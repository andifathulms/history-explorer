In 541 the Goths, beaten and down to a strip of the north, elected a young man
called Totila. He spent the next eleven years taking Italy back from the empire,
and he did it by making an offer to people nobody had thought to ask.

Procopius disliked the regime he served and by this stage was writing about it
with his teeth set. He gives Totila more credit than he gives almost anyone on
his own side, and he does it while recording the things Totila did that were
ruinous.

## The letter to the senate

Totila's first move on Rome was not an assault. It was a letter to the senate,
and its argument is that the Roman aristocracy had betrayed a government that had
treated it well.

Men who wrong their neighbours out of ignorance, he writes, or because some
forgetfulness has come upon them, may fairly be forgiven by their victims; a man
who does wrong out of deliberate intent has nothing left to plead. So consider,
he tells them, what defence you will be able to make for what you have done to
the Goths. Are you ignorant of the benefactions of Theoderic and Amalasuintha,
or have time and forgetfulness effaced them in you? They were not small favours,
and they were not long ago, and they were shown to you yourselves.

Then the comparison: having learned by report or by experience what Greek rule is
worth to its subjects, did you really decide to hand over to them the affairs of
the Goths and the Italians? He tells them to remember how well they entertained
those guests, and what kind of guests and friends they got in return, and leaves
them to recall what the imperial commanders and the imperial soldiers had been
like.

It is an accurate letter. The senators had spent forty years being governed
carefully by a Gothic king and then six years being governed by an occupying
army that had to live off the country.

## The offer

The thing that actually won Italy back is one sentence in Procopius's account of
the Lucanian campaign, and it is the most consequential sentence about this
kingdom that anybody wrote.

Totila had taken Rome, and had the senators in his hands. The countryside of the
south was in arms against him, organised by a local landowner named Tullianus,
and the fighting there had gone badly for the Goths. So he had the captured
senators send their own household servants down to their Lucanian estates, with
a message to their tenants: leave off their present machinations, and till the
fields as they were accustomed — for they would have the property of their
masters.

The peasants detached themselves from the imperial army and went quietly back on
to the land. Tullianus fled. The whole territory south of the Adriatic except one
port became Gothic again without another battle.

That is a Gothic king dissolving the Roman landowning class's hold on its own
estates by telling the men who worked them that the land was theirs — and doing
it through the mouths of the landowners themselves, who were his prisoners. Two
generations earlier the Ostrogothic settlement had been built on leaving the
Roman aristocracy in possession, and had lasted as long as it did because of it.
Totila took Italy back by promising to dispossess them.

## The letter about Rome

He was not sentimental about the city. Having decided to march south, he
determined to raze Rome first: he pulled down the fortifications in many places
until about a third of the defences were gone, and he was on the point of burning
the finest of the buildings and making Rome a sheep-pasture.

Belisarius wrote to him. The letter Procopius gives is the best-known thing in
the Gothic War and deserves its reputation.

> Among all the cities under the sun Rome is agreed to be the greatest and the
> most noteworthy. For it has not been created by the ability of one man, nor
> has it attained such greatness and beauty by a power of short duration, but a
> multitude of monarchs, many companies of the best men, a great lapse of time,
> and an extraordinary abundance of wealth have availed to bring together in
> that city all other things that are in the whole world, and skilled workers
> besides.

To damage such a place, he goes on, would properly be considered a crime against
the men of all time, because it robs past generations of the memorials of their
ability and future generations of the sight of their works. And then the
practical half, which is the half that persuades: one of two things must happen.
Either you beat the emperor or he beats you. If you win and you have dismantled
Rome, you will have destroyed your own city and not another man's. If you lose,
having saved Rome you would be assured of the victor's gratitude, and having
destroyed it you will have left yourself no plea for mercy at all — and no
benefit from the deed either.

Procopius says Totila read the letter over many times, came to understand the
advice exactly, was convinced, and did Rome no further harm.

Then he emptied it. He kept the senators with him and sent everybody else, with
their wives and children, into Campania, refusing to allow a single soul to
remain, and left the city entirely deserted.

Rome had been the largest city in the world. For a season there was nobody in it.

## How it ended

Totila held most of Italy, offered terms repeatedly, and was refused, because
Justinian's position throughout was that there was nothing to negotiate. In 552
Narses came west with an army the empire had not previously been willing to pay
for, and killed him at Taginae. His successor Teia was killed the year after.

What Procopius leaves on the page is a king who argued in writing that the Roman
senate had wronged a government that deserved better, who spared Rome because a
letter told him what posterity would say, who emptied it of every inhabitant in
the same month, and who won a country back by promising its farm labourers the
estates they worked on. None of it was inherited from Theoderic's settlement.
It was the opposite of that settlement, and it very nearly worked.
