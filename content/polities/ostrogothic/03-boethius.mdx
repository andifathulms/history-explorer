In 524 the *magister officiorum* — the head of the civil administration, the
senior Roman in the kingdom — was arrested on a charge of treasonable
correspondence with Constantinople, imprisoned at Pavia, and clubbed to death.
His father-in-law, the head of the senate, followed him. The pope died in
custody the following year after a failed embassy to the emperor.

Boethius wrote the *Consolation of Philosophy* in the cell. It became one of the
most widely read books in medieval Europe, was translated into English by Alfred
the Great and into Middle English by Chaucer, and is the reason a Gothic king in
Ravenna is a figure in the history of Western thought at all.

## Whether there was a conspiracy

Unknowable, and it is worth leaving contested rather than settled.

<Figure id="pavia-san-pietro" />

The case against: Boethius's own account, which is the fullest, is written by the
accused and is a work of consolation rather than a defence brief. There was
real correspondence between Italian senators and the eastern court. The eastern
emperor Justin had begun persecuting Arians in 523, which threatened the Gothic
regime's church directly, and a Roman aristocracy that had spent thirty years
being loyal to an Arian king now had a Catholic emperor to be loyal to instead.
From Ravenna it would have looked like the beginning of something.

The case for the prosecution being paranoid: nothing came of it, no rising
followed, and Theodoric had ruled for thirty-one years without needing to kill
senators.

## Why it matters

Because it is the moment the arrangement in the first chapter is shown to be
conditional.

Two peoples, two functions, one king holding the position of both a Gothic
monarch and a Roman official — that works as long as the Roman half has nowhere
else to look. From 476 to 518 there was, in practice, nowhere: the eastern empire
was theologically estranged from Rome and militarily uninterested in Italy. From
518 the schism was healed and from 527 there was Justinian, and the calculation
for an Italian senator changed.

Theodoric died in 526. Justinian's armies were in Italy nine years later, the
war ran for eighteen, and it left the peninsula in a condition neither power
could pay to hold.

## What was lost with it

Sixty years is a short life for a kingdom, and this one left remarkably little
that can be counted. No script. No faith carried outward — the Goths were Arians
and made no attempt to spread it. No later dynasty invoking its name, because
the Lombards who arrived fifteen years afterwards had no interest in it and the
empire's official position was that it had never been legitimate in the first
place.

What it did was keep the Roman administration of Italy running for sixty years
after the empire in the west had stopped, and hand the *Consolation of
Philosophy* to the Middle Ages by killing the man who wrote it. Neither of those
is the kind of thing anybody thought to measure.
