The Ostrogothic kingdom was destroyed in a war that ran from 535 to 554 and
ruined Italy more thoroughly than any barbarian invasion had. The Goths
disappeared as a people almost immediately afterwards; there is no Ostrogothic
successor, no exile claimant, no later dynasty claiming the name.

The one thing that came out of it lasted, and it was done by a retired civil
servant on the toe of Italy.

## The retirement

Cassiodorus went home to his native province of Bruttii around 538, while the
war was still in its early stages, and outlived the kingdom he had served by
something like forty years.

At Squillace, on the coast between the town and the sea, he founded a monastery.
He called it Vivarium, after the fishponds cut into the rock there. And he did
something with it that nobody had done in quite that way before: he made the
copying of manuscripts a central duty of the monastic life, wrote a handbook
setting out what ought to be read and in what order, and had the community
assemble a library of Christian and classical texts and reproduce them.

His stated purpose was the education of monks. The effect was a working
argument, in a country in the middle of a twenty-year war, that a religious
house should be a place where books are preserved and multiplied.

## Why that is the afterlife

Because it is the only part of the Ostrogothic enterprise that outlived the
enterprise, and because of what it was preserving.

The Gothic settlement had been an attempt to run Italy as a going Roman concern
with a Germanic army — the senate meeting, the consuls appointed, the aqueducts
repaired, the correspondence conducted in the full formal Latin of the imperial
chancery. Its administrator spent thirty years drafting that Latin. When the
arrangement collapsed he retired and spent the rest of his life ensuring that
somebody would still be able to read it.

The Variae themselves survived by the same route: copied, in monasteries, by
people whose profession he had helped define, until the eleventh and twelfth
centuries when Italian and German scholars started looking for records of what
the sixth century had been.

## The vindication

There is a last thing worth saying about why the letters exist at all.

Cassiodorus collected and published them, on his own account, to show that for
more than thirty years the influence he had wielded in the councils of the
Gothic sovereigns had been exerted on the side of law and order and just
government — directed equally, in his own phrase, at the repression of Teutonic
barbarism and the punishment of Roman venality.

That is a man defending a career spent serving a regime that had just been
destroyed and would be remembered, by the historians of the winning side, as an
occupation. He wanted it on record that the government had been a government.

The defence worked. Almost everything anybody knows about how the Ostrogothic
kingdom actually functioned comes from the file he assembled to prove he had
done his job properly, kept safe in a monastery he built after there was nobody
left to prove it to.
