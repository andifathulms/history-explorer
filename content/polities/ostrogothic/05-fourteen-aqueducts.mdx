In March 537 a Gothic army sat down in front of Rome and cut the water. Procopius
was inside the walls, working as Belisarius's secretary, and what he wrote about
the next year is the most detailed account of a siege to survive from antiquity.

The aqueducts of Rome, he explains, were fourteen in number, made of baked brick
by the men of old, of such breadth and height that a man on horseback could ride
along inside them. The Goths tore open all of them, so that no water at all
should enter the city. Belisarius then blocked every channel with masonry for a
considerable distance, in case anybody tried to come in the same way. Between
them, in a few days, the two armies ended the thing that had made Rome possible.

The city had about five thousand imperial troops in it.

## What stopping the water actually did

Procopius is precise about the consequences, and they are not the ones a modern
reader expects.

Drinking water was not the problem: people who lived far from the river drew
from wells. The sewers were not a problem either, since they all discharged into
the Tiber and could not be used against the city. What failed was the grain.

All the mills of Rome stood on one hill across the river, turned by water brought
up by aqueduct and dropped down the slope, and the ancient Romans had walled the
hill and bridged the river precisely so that no enemy could ever destroy them.
The Goths did not have to reach the mills. They only had to cut the supply above
them, and every mill in the city stopped. There were no draught animals to spare
— the garrison could barely feed its horses.

So Belisarius built floating mills. He stretched ropes from bank to bank just
below the bridge, where the water comes out from under the arch with the greatest
force, moored two boats two feet apart, set a mill on each and slung the
machinery between them, and then attached further boats behind, one to the next,
with wheels between them all the way down. The current turned every wheel in
turn, and the city had flour.

The Goths learned of it from deserters and answered it. They gathered large trees
and the bodies of Romans newly killed and threw them into the river upstream, and
the current carried them down between the boats and broke the wheels off. So
Belisarius hung long iron chains across the Tiber above the bridge; everything
the river brought down collected against them, and details of men pulled the
trees and the corpses out and carried them ashore. He admits, in Procopius's
telling, that he did it less for the mills than because he had begun to fear the
Goths might come through under the bridge in boats and be in the middle of the
city before anyone knew.

The Romans kept their mills. They lost the baths.

## The statues

The other scene worth having whole is at the far end of the city.

The tomb of the emperor Hadrian stands outside the gate by the river, a
stone's throw from the walls, and Procopius describes it as a very noteworthy
sight: Parian marble, the stones fitted so closely that there is nothing at all
between them, four equal sides each about a stone's throw long, higher than the
city wall — and above, statues of the same marble, men and horses, of wonderful
workmanship. Long before, the Romans had decided the tomb was a fortress
threatening the city and had run two walls out to it, making it part of the
defences.

The garrison there was small, because that stretch was reckoned the least
assailable. The Goths came at it anyway, hidden under the colonnade that runs
out to the church of St Peter, and appeared at close range with shields so large
that the engines on the wall — which could only shoot straight out — were useless
against them. They were getting their ladders up and working round both flanks
of the defenders when the men on the tomb, by common agreement, broke the statues
in pieces and threw the fragments down on their heads with both hands.

That is the whole war in one paragraph: a Roman garrison, holding an emperor's
tomb against a Gothic army in order to keep a city for another emperor, destroying
the sculpture to get something to throw.

## Why they left

Not because of anything that happened at Rome. A Roman commander named John took
Ariminum, close to Ravenna, on the calculation that the Goths would abandon
everything else the moment they feared for their capital, and he was right.

Procopius adds one detail he plainly enjoyed. Matasuntha — Theoderic's
granddaughter, whom Witigis had married by force to give his election a
pedigree — heard that John was at Ariminum and was, in the translation,
absolutely overcome with joy, and opened secret negotiations with him about
marriage and the betrayal of the city. The dynastic marriage that was supposed
to legitimise the war king was being used, within two years, to hand over his
capital.

## What the siege settled

It was about the spring equinox, and one year had been spent in the siege and
nine days in addition, when the Goths burned their camps and set out at
daybreak. Belisarius held Rome, which is what the campaign is usually scored on.

What the year did to the city is the part that lasted. The aqueducts were not
repaired, and Rome went on for the rest of the war being fought over by armies
that cut whatever the previous one had mended. A city built to be supplied by
fourteen brick rivers was now a city of wells, and it never again held a
population of the size those rivers had been built to water. The Gothic kingdom
had spent forty years keeping the machinery of Roman Italy running — the
aqueducts repaired, the grain moved, the offices staffed. The war fought to
restore Roman Italy broke the machinery in its first campaigning season.
