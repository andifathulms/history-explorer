Theodoric was born about 454, spent ten years of his childhood as a hostage at
Constantinople, and came out of it a Roman patrician and a consul who was also
the king of a Gothic army camped in the Balkans and expensive to keep there. In
488 the emperor Zeno sent him to Italy with a commission to remove Odoacer, the
commander who had deposed the last western emperor twelve years earlier and been
governing as a patrician ever since.

It took five years. In 493, after a three-year siege of Ravenna, the two agreed
to rule Italy jointly, and ten days later Theodoric killed Odoacer at the banquet
celebrating the agreement — reportedly with his own hands, and reportedly
remarking afterwards that the man seemed to have no bones in him.

## `investiture`, in a region that had not carried it

Theodoric's claim to rule rested on two things at once, and the second is what
makes the kingdom unusual: descent from the Amal royal house, and a commission
from the emperor at Constantinople.

That second kind of claim is common enough on the eastern frontier — a Jafnid
phylarch holding Roman rank from Justinian, a Nasrid king wearing a Persian
diadem, Herod voted a kingdom by the Roman senate — and rare in the post-Roman
west, where the successor kings mostly stopped pretending. Theodoric did not
stop pretending. He was sent by an emperor. He struck coin in the emperor's name and not
in his own. He sought imperial confirmation for his rule and, later, for his
successor. He used the title *rex*, never *imperator* or *augustus*, and his own
chancery describes his position as holding Italy on the emperor's behalf. The
arrangement is deliberately ambiguous and the ambiguity was the point: a Gothic
king who was also a Roman official could be obeyed by both halves of the
population.

## Two peoples, two functions

The division of labour was explicit.

The Goths were the army. Gothic families were settled on assignments of land or
of tax revenue and owed military service; Romans were barred from bearing arms.
That is the same arrangement the Vandals had in Africa, with one difference that
the third chapter comes back
to: in Africa the Vandals took the land, and in Italy the Goths largely took a
share of the tax on it, which left the Roman landowning class intact and much
less hostile.

The Romans were the government. The praetorian prefecture went on functioning,
the senate went on meeting, consuls went on being appointed, the land tax went on
being assessed and collected by the same officials as before 476. Cassiodorus,
who served as the king's minister, left twelve books of the official
correspondence, and it is Roman bureaucratic Latin about grain shipments, aqueduct
repairs and tax remissions — a state whose administrative record is
indistinguishable from an imperial province's, headed by a Gothic king.

Sixty years of this produced an Italy governed better than it had been for a
century. The second chapter is about the flaw in it.
