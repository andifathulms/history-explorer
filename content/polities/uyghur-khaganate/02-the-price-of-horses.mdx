In 755 An Lushan revolted and the Tang empire came apart. Chang'an fell,
Luoyang fell, the emperor fled to Sichuan, and the dynasty survived because it
bought help.

The Uyghurs sold it.

## What the cavalry cost

Uyghur horsemen fought for the Tang against the rebels and were decisive in the
recovery of both capitals. What they were paid, and how they behaved while
being paid, is recorded in the Chinese sources with an unusual amount of
feeling — a dynasty writing down the terms of its own rescue is not a
flattering genre.

They took an agreed right to plunder a recaptured city. They took silk in
quantity. And, permanently, they took a trade: horses for silk, at a fixed
ratio, in quantities the Uyghurs set.

The horse-and-silk exchange is the heart of the ninety-six years. Tens of
thousands of horses a year went east; hundreds of thousands of bolts of silk
came west. The Chinese sources say the horses were often poor and the ratio
never favourable, and that the court could not refuse — partly because it needed
cavalry, partly because the alternative was a khaganate with a grievance on the
northern frontier.

## What silk is for

A steppe empire does not want silk to wear. It wants silk because silk is
currency.

A bolt of Chinese silk is portable, durable, divisible, universally valued from
the Yellow River to the Mediterranean, and — crucially for a qaghan — it is
something he can hand to a subordinate chief in front of witnesses. Steppe
authority is held together by distribution: the ruler who can pay is the ruler
who is obeyed, and the ruler who runs out of things to hand over stops being
one.

So the Tang subsidy was not tribute in the sense of humiliation money. It was
the raw material of Uyghur politics, and the khaganate needed it in bulk,
annually, in order to remain a khaganate at all.

That is why the terms could not be softened. A ruler who accepted a smaller
consignment would be distributing less at home.

## The merchants who came with it

Converting silk into everything else required people who knew the roads, and
those people were Sogdians.

Sogdian merchants from Samarkand and Bukhara had run the Silk Road caravan
network for centuries, with colonies from the Crimea to Chang'an. Under the
Uyghurs they moved into the khaganate itself: as traders, as moneylenders to
the Chinese court on Uyghur account, and as the literate class the khaganate
had not previously possessed.

They brought a script — the Sogdian alphabet, which the Uyghurs eventually
adopted in place of the runiform letters. They brought commercial law and
accounting. And they brought a religion, which is the subject of the next
chapter and the strangest decision any steppe ruler ever made.

## The shape of the bargain

By about 780 the Uyghur khaganate was the richest steppe polity that had ever
existed, with a walled capital, a foreign merchant class, a literate chancery,
and an annual income denominated in Chinese silk.

Every one of those advantages was a hostage. The capital could be found and
burned. The merchants would go wherever the money was. And the income depended
on the continued existence of a Chinese dynasty that resented paying it, on a
route that ran through country the khaganate did not control.
