The Uyghurs did not arrive from outside. They had been inside the Türk
khaganate the whole time, as one of the Tokuz Oghuz — the Nine Oghuz — and the
Orkhon inscriptions name them among the peoples that kept rising against the
qaghan who was addressing them.

Bilge Qaghan's own words, cut in the 730s: *The Tokuz-Oguz people was my own
people. As Heaven and Earth were in a turmoil they rose against us. Within one
year we fought five times.*

That is the last generation of Türk rule describing, on a monument, the people
who would end it fourteen years later.

## 744

The second Türk khaganate came apart in the 740s in the ordinary way — a
disputed succession, then a coalition. The Uyghurs, the Basmil and the Karluks
combined against the reigning qaghan; the Basmil took the title first; the
Uyghurs then took it from the Basmil and drove the Karluks west.

By 744 the Uyghur leader held the Ötükan — the mountain forest Bilge had told
his people never to leave — and the Türk khaganate was finished.

## What they inherited, and what they changed

They took the ground, the title, the runiform script and the political
vocabulary. Their early inscriptions are in the same alphabet, on the same kind
of stone, in the same idiom.

They also took the strategic position, and read it differently. Bilge's advice
had been to stay in the forest, trade outward, and treat Chinese generosity as a
weapon. The Uyghurs did the opposite on purpose: they went into the Tang
relationship as deeply as they could get, and made it pay.

That decision is the whole of their history and it has two halves. It made them
rich, literate and — eventually — a settled people with towns. It also made them
dependent on a neighbour, which is exactly what the stones on the same river had
warned against, and the warning was correct.

## Karabalgasun

The clearest evidence of the difference is that they built a city.

The Türks had no capital. The Uyghurs put up Ordu-Baliq at Karabalgasun on the
Orkhon: walls, a citadel, watchtowers, workshops, a quarter for foreign
merchants, and a palace complex. The ruins cover several square kilometres and
they are the first permanent capital anybody built on the Mongolian steppe.

A steppe empire that builds a walled capital has decided something. It has
decided that what it holds is worth defending in one place, that the wealth
coming in is worth storing rather than distributing, and that the people who
manage it need somewhere to live. Every one of those is a break with the way
the Türks had governed the same ground from the same valley.
