Bögü Qaghan's own cousin murdered him in 779 for the same reason the
khaganate had converted to Manichaeism in the first place: the
Sogdians at court had too much influence, and a faction around the
throne had had enough of it. What followed was fifteen years of anti-
Sogdian reaction, a brief and undistinguished reign, and then a
completely different royal line taking the title from a general who
had spent his whole career failing to hold the western frontier.

## The cousin who killed the convert

Tun Bagha Tarkhan, Bögü's chief minister and first cousin, opposed the
invasion of China the Sogdian faction had talked Bögü into launching
during a period of Tang court mourning. When Bögü refused to back
down, Tun Bagha Tarkhan struck first - killing Bögü and a number of his
Sogdian advisers and seating himself on the throne. He spent his own
reign undoing what his cousin had built: favouring native Turkic cults
over Manichaeism, and, by most readings, reducing Sogdian influence at
court generally, even if the new faith itself survived him.

Tun Bagha Tarkhan died in 789. His successor lasted barely a year. With
the throne weakened twice in a decade, real authority at court settled
on the chief minister, a general remembered in the Chinese sources as
Kutlugh - the same man, most likely, who had just presided over the
loss of Beshbalik to the Tibetans on the empire's western frontier, an
embarrassment that would have ended a lesser man's career rather than
setting up his enthronement.

## A new clan, an old title

In 795 the khaganate's royal line changed outright. Power passed from
the Yaghlakar clan, which had held the title since the khaganate's own
founding, to the Ediz clan, and Kutlugh - the general who had just
lost a war - became khagan of an entirely new dynasty. It should have
been the moment the empire came apart. Instead it produced one of the
khaganate's strongest reigns: Beshbalik was retaken from the Tibetans,
Uyghur influence reached as far west as Ferghana, and the Sogdian and
Manichaean establishment Tun Bagha Tarkhan had tried to suppress
recovered fully, if anything stronger than it had been under Bögü
himself.

## Two more reigns before the winters came

Kutlugh died in 808 and was followed by Bai Qaghan, whose own reign
was prosperous enough that a Manichaean hymn-book completed under him
still lists Beshbalik, Kocho, Kucha and Karashahr as Uyghur
dependencies - but the same reign saw the Tibetans grow confident
enough, by its end, to think they could take the Uyghur capital
outright if they chose to try. Bai Qaghan died in 821 and was
succeeded by Chongde Qaghan, whose own accession was marked the same
way Bögü's family had once marked its own alliances: a Tang princess,
Taihe, sent to Karabalgasun to marry him.

It was the last marriage of its kind the khaganate would arrange.
Chongde inherited a court more addicted to the Manichaean establishment's
luxury than to governing, a war already open with the Kirghiz to the
north, and, within a generation, the run of bad winters and internal
plots that would finally bring the whole empire down.
