The khaganate ended in about three years, and the sequence is a textbook case of
what happens when every one of a state's advantages is also a dependency.

## The three winters

The 830s went wrong on the ground first. The sources record severe winters,
disease among the herds, and famine — the *zud* that periodically empties the
Mongolian steppe of animals and, with them, of the wealth that holds a
confederation together.

Then the politics. A succession dispute at the top produced the ordinary thing:
a faction, a murdered qaghan, and a defeated commander who went and found
someone else to fight with.

## The Kirghiz

The Yenisei Kirghiz had been subjects of the Türks and then of the Uyghurs, in
the forested country north-west of the Ötükan, and they had been fighting the
khaganate on and off for two decades. In 840 an Uyghur general defected to them
and brought them down the valley.

They took Karabalgasun, killed the qaghan, and burned the city.

Then they did not stay. The Kirghiz had no interest in ruling the Orkhon and no
intention of running an empire from a walled town on it; they went back north
with what they had taken, and the Mongolian steppe was left without a central
power for the first time in three hundred years and stayed that way until the
Mongols.

## Why a capital was the wrong asset

The Türks had nothing to burn. Their qaghans lived where the grass was, and a
Chinese army that marched into the Ötükan found a valley.

The Uyghurs had put everything in one place: the treasury, the chancery, the
merchant quarter, the temples, the workshops, the stored silk. It made them
formidable while it stood and it made the war a single objective. One city, one
season, one result.

That is the sharpest lesson of the ninety-six years, and it was not lost on
anybody afterwards. When the Mongols built a capital at Karakorum four hundred
years later, they put it about twenty-five miles from these ruins, in the same
valley, for the same reasons — and Karakorum was burned too.

## The scattering

The population went in three directions.

Some went south to the Chinese frontier and were massacred or absorbed. Some
went west into the Gansu corridor and founded a kingdom at Ganzhou. The largest
group went south-west into the Tarim basin, to the oasis towns around Turfan
and Kucha, and founded the kingdom of Qocho.

That third group stopped being nomads. Within a couple of generations they were
farming irrigated land, living in walled towns, writing in the Sogdian-derived
alphabet, and — increasingly — Buddhist rather than Manichaean.

They are the reason the end of this khaganate is not the end of the story. What
was destroyed in 840 was a steppe empire. What survived it was a literate
settled people who spent the next four centuries being indispensable to
everybody who came after.
