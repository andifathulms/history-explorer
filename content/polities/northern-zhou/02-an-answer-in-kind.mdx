Northern Qi, the eastern half of the same Wei dynasty Zhou had split from
forty years earlier, had its own dark inheritance to answer for. Its
founding house had once massacred the entire royal family of the old,
united Wei — seven hundred and twenty-one people, all but a single survivor
— and Parker suggests it may be from that atrocity that the later Chinese
custom of exterminating the heirs of every deposed dynasty took hold as
settled practice rather than occasional excess.

<Figure id="nz-flask" />

## Zhou's turn to collect

In 577 Zhou armies took Northern Qi's capital. The Zhou emperor did to the
Qi ruling house exactly what the Qi ruling house had once done to the
undivided Wei: its entire family stock was put to death. Parker records the
act without elaboration, but the symmetry needs none - a debt from one
Toba-descended dynasty to another, paid back in the same coin two
generations after it was incurred, by a court that had spent its own
formative decades watching the precedent set and had evidently drawn the
obvious lesson from it.

<Figure id="nz-wudi" />

## What the conquest actually settled

Politically, the victory did what the Wei split of 534-5 had never allowed:
it put the whole of northern China back under a single throne, ending four
decades in which two rival courts, each claiming the same broken dynasty's
inheritance, had faced each other across a border neither had the strength
to erase. Zhou had finished the job Wei's own quarrel had left unfinished.
What it had not settled, and could not have known it was setting up, was
who would actually get to keep the throne it had just spent twenty years
consolidating.
