Zhou's own throne had been built on a regency that quietly outgrew the
dynasty it was supposed to serve, and the mechanism that had carried the
Yuwen family to power carried them out of it again on exactly the same
terms. A minor succeeded to the throne, a senior official close to the
imperial family took the regency, and within a short span of years the
regent's own position had become, in substance, indistinguishable from
sovereignty - the identical sequence, run by a different family, that had
converted a Western Wei regency into an independent dynasty a generation
earlier.

## Yang Jian's position

Yang Jian held exactly the kind of standing the precedent required: he was
already tied to the imperial family by marriage, already trusted with real
authority under the reigning emperor, and positioned, when a child inherited
the throne, to become regent for him as a matter of course rather than by
any dramatic seizure of the office. Nothing about his rise to that position
broke any rule Zhou's own history had established. It followed the pattern
almost exactly.

## What Zhou's own history had already taught him to do next

What came after followed the pattern too, only faster. Where the Yuwen had
waited two years after their decisive conquest to formalise a regency into
a throne, Yang Jian took the throne outright in 581 and moved immediately
to exterminate the entire Zhou ruling family - the same fate Zhou's own
emperor had inflicted on the house of Northern Qi four years before, now
visited on Zhou in turn by a regent who had, by every available account,
learned the method from watching the dynasty he served use it on somebody
else. Yang Jian took the name Sui for his own new house. The Wei line that
had split itself in two in 535, fought itself to a single throne by 577,
and reunited the north under Zhou's own name for less than half a decade,
ended having taught its own executioner everything he needed to know to
finish it.
