When the Wei dynasty split into rival eastern and western courts in 534 and
535, the western half kept a member of the old Wei house on its throne in
name while a general held the actual power behind it. In Wei's case, as in
so many of the northern dynasties that had come before it, the distance
between "regent" and "emperor" was a matter of years rather than kind.

## Twenty-two years of governing in someone else's name

The regent's own family, the Yuwen, spent a generation running Western
Wei's government and army as though it were already theirs, without moving
to formalise the fact. Their reward came in 555, still ruling in the puppet
emperor's name, when Western Wei's forces destroyed the remaining territory
of the Chinese Liang dynasty on the middle Yangtze — a conquest that gave
the regency both the resources and the reputation to make the throne worth
taking outright rather than continuing to govern through someone else's
name.

## The formality, finally observed

Two years later, in 557, the regent's son deposed the last Western Wei
figurehead and founded his own dynasty, taking the name Zhou. It changed
remarkably little about how the state was actually run - the same family,
the same army, the same administration that had already been running
things for a generation - and remarkably much about what it was now
permitted to become. A regency has a ceiling built into its own logic: it
governs in someone else's name, for someone else's dynasty, and can be
undone by whoever controls the figurehead next. An emperor answers to no
name but his own.
