Three and a half centuries after Stephen's own deathbed settlement with
the Ottoman Porte, Moldavia's separate existence ended the same way
Wallachia's did: through a legal maneuver rather than a battle. The 1858
Paris Convention, negotiated among the European powers with the Porte's
own participation, granted Moldavia and Wallachia the right to elect
their own princes independently of each other - a right the Convention's
own authors assumed would produce two separate rulers, not one.

## An assembly acts first

In January 1859, Moldavia's own elective assembly moved first, choosing
Alexandru Ioan Cuza as its prince. Weeks later, Wallachia's assembly
elected the same man to its own throne - a coincidence of timing that
was, in fact, no coincidence at all, but a coordinated exploitation of a
loophole neither the Convention's own drafters nor the Ottoman Porte had
anticipated or wanted. Nothing in the letter of the Convention had been
broken. Each principality had simply elected, entirely within its own
rights, the same candidate.

## A tribute that finally stopped mattering

The Porte's own response, a firman issued in December 1859, recognized
Cuza's double rule only as a one-time personal exception rather than a
permanent union, an attempt to preserve, at least on paper, the
fiction that Moldavia and Wallachia remained separate tributary states.
In practice, the recognition changed nothing about what had already
happened. Full administrative union of the two principalities' governments
followed in 1862, and the state that resulted from it would, within a
few more years, take the name Romania - closing out, in an assembly hall
rather than on a battlefield, a separate Moldavian political history that
had run from an exiled noble's 1359 seizure of Dragoș's own frontier
march through fifty years under a prince the Ottomans themselves could
never quite finish conquering.
