Stephen ruled for nearly fifty years, longer than any other prince of
the medieval Romanian lands, and spent almost the whole of that reign at
war - against Ottomans, Poles, and Hungarians in turn, rarely all at
once, and with a consistency of purpose his own court physician credited
directly for Moldavia's unusual prosperity under near-constant fighting.
He was, by his own calculation, always going to face a final Ottoman
reckoning eventually. What his decades of victories had actually bought
was the right to choose roughly when, rather than the ability to avoid
it forever.

## Advice that reversed a lifetime of resistance

On his deathbed in 1504, Stephen gave his son and chosen successor,
Bogdan, counsel that broke sharply from the pattern of his own reign: make
peace with the Ottoman Porte rather than continue the resistance that had
defined his father's rule. It was not sentimentality. Stephen had spent
nearly five decades reading the balance of power in the region as
accurately as any ruler of his generation, and his own deathbed judgment
was that continued defiance would cost his successor far more than a
negotiated tribute would.

## Securing the succession by the same hand that had won every battle

Stephen did not leave his son's throne to chance any more than he had
left his own battles to it. In the same act of arranging the succession,
he ordered the immediate execution of nobles he suspected of plotting
against Bogdan's own claim - a final, characteristically ruthless
measure from a ruler whose reputation for severity had always run
alongside his reputation for victory. Vlad III of Wallachia, who had
once helped install Stephen on his own throne, and Stephen himself,
who spent his life defending his country by every method available to
him, were in this respect products of exactly the same era: one in which
a ruler's own survival, and his kingdom's, depended on a willingness to
act as decisively against internal rivals as against foreign armies.

## A tribute finally paid in full

Stephen's own father had already paid the Ottomans tribute as far back as
1456, so his deathbed advice did not open an entirely new relationship
with the Porte - but it did formalize what earlier tribute payments had
left ambiguous. Bogdan concluded a fuller arrangement with the sultan in
1513, promising an annual payment along with military support when
called on, in exchange for guarantees against Turkish settlement inside
Moldavia and the country's own right to elect its future princes. What
Stephen's decades of battlefield victories had won was not permanent
independence, in the end, but the terms on which submission finally
came - a distinction his own reign had spent its whole length treating
as worth fighting for.
