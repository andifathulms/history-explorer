The century between Moldavia's 1359 break from Hungary and Stephen the
Great's 1457 accession produced, by Seton-Watson's own count, some
sixty-five separate reigns across both Danubian principalities - most
of them too brief and too obscure to leave any real trace. Alexandru
cel Bun, "the Good," who held Moldavia's throne from 1401 to 1431, was
one of the rare exceptions: a ruler capable enough, in Seton-Watson's
own assessment, of giving his country real stability at the exact
moment Bulgaria and Serbia to the south were being extinguished by the
same Ottoman advance Moldavia's own geography had so far kept at a
remove.

## A kingdom that looked north rather than west

Where Wallachia's rulers spent the same period maneuvering between
Hungary and the Ottoman Porte, Alexandru built his own security on a
different axis entirely: Poland. He married a relative of the Polish
king Władysław Jagiełło as his third wife, and the alliance was no
mere formality - Alexandru sent Moldavian troops to fight under the
Polish banner at the great battle of Tannenberg in 1410, one of the
largest engagements medieval Europe had yet seen. The relationship cut
both ways. Władysław and his own brother-in-law, the Hungarian king
Sigismund of Luxembourg, separately negotiated a secret treaty
providing for Moldavia's own eventual partition between them - a
reminder that even Alexandru's most reliable foreign alliance carried
its own quiet threat.

## What one stable reign bought a fragile state

Alexandru's own domestic reputation rested on exactly the kind of
unglamorous, cumulative work that rarely survives in colorful detail:
consolidating a still-young principality's administration, maintaining
its church, and holding its borders without the dramatic sieges and
battlefield reversals that mark so much of both Danubian principalities'
history. He died in 1431 having ruled longer than any Moldavian prince before
him - thirty years against an average, across both principalities in
this period, of barely seven. Whatever instability followed him
through the reigns Seton-Watson judges not worth individually
recounting, Alexandru had already shown that a Moldavian throne could
hold steady for a generation at a time. It was that possibility, more
than any single battle, that Stephen the Great would spend his own
much longer reign proving could be sustained.
