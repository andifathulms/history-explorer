By the early 1470s, Stephen's own raids into Wallachia to install a ruler
friendlier to an anti-Ottoman alliance had convinced Sultan Mehmed II
that Moldavia needed dealing with directly. The stakes went well beyond
one principality: control of the Black Sea trade and the mouth of the
Danube ran through fortresses Moldavia held, and Mehmed himself is said
to have remarked that so long as those ports remained in Christian
hands, his conquest of the region would stay incomplete. In the depths of
winter, an unusual season for Ottoman campaigning given the strain it put
on supply and transport, Mehmed dispatched Hadim Suleyman Pasha with an
army redirected from a siege in Albania to end the Moldavian problem for
good.

## Ground chosen by a boy who had watched a battle before

Stephen met the invasion at a site his own father, Bogdan II, had chosen
to defeat a Polish-backed rival claimant a quarter century earlier -
ground Stephen himself had witnessed as a child. A marshland along the
Bârlad river, ten kilometers long and hemmed by densely wooded hills,
gave the Ottoman cavalry no room to outflank the Moldavian position and
forced Suleyman Pasha's army to attack directly into a defended line at
its narrowest point. Stephen held the bulk of his own forces on high
ground overlooking the marsh, ready to counterattack once the Ottoman
advance committed itself to the single approach available.

## A battle won by terrain and by nerve

The Ottoman vanguard took the bait, charging into the defended crossing
in a deafening rush that, by report, startled even Stephen's own
soldiers standing at the rear - until an elderly boyar knelt before him
and told him plainly that the army would stand with him regardless.
Stephen answered with sustained cannon and arrow fire into the committed
Ottoman force, then led a direct charge into its exposed flank. The
result was total: even Mara, widow of the late Sultan Mehmed's own father,
is recorded as saying no Ottoman army had ever suffered a comparable
defeat, and Italian observers spread word of the first open-field victory
any Christian army had won against the Ottomans in living memory.

## A victory Europe praised and did nothing to follow up

Writing to European courts from Suceava on 25 January 1475, Stephen
described the battle in his own words: his forces had "prevailed upon
them and trampled them underfoot," and warned that if Moldavia's own
resistance failed, all of Christendom stood in danger behind it. The
historian Nicolae Iorga would later call the battle "our own
Thermopylae" - a comparison that captures both the scale of the victory
and its limits, since like the Spartans at Thermopylae, Stephen's
triumph bought admiration abroad rather than a lasting alliance. The
sultan returned the following year with a fresh chastising expedition of
his own and came back from it just as unsuccessful as before - proof
Vaslui had not been a fluke, even if the European coalition Stephen's
letters had appealed for never materialized in any form that outlasted
the immediate crisis.
