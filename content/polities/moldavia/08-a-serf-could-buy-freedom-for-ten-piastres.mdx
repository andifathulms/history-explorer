Between Cantemir's flight and the century's own collapse, Moldavia's
throne changed hands thirty-six times under appointees from twelve Greek
and Hellenized families - and out of that turnover came the one
Phanariote prince later Romanian reformers would still name alongside
their own, and the crisis that finally ended the regime.

## A prince who ruled six times in one country and four in the other

Constantin Mavrocordato, son of the same Nicolas Mavrocordato the Porte
had installed after Cantemir's exile, held the Wallachian throne six
times and the Moldavian four, though his combined reigns added up to
only twenty-two years across both. He governed at a moment when peasant
flight had become an emergency rather than a nuisance: a rough census in
1741 counted some 147,000 peasant families in Wallachia alone, and within
four years the tax collectors' and landlords' exactions had driven off
half of them, fleeing across the Danube to Ottoman territory, north into
Transylvania, or west into the Banat - anywhere a master's grip on their
labor was looser than at home. Between 1743 and 1746 Mavrocordato
answered the crisis in Wallachia with genuine reform: abolishing the
taxes that fell heaviest on the peasantry, dividing tenant farmers into
three classes by the oxen they owned and guaranteeing each a minimum of
land, free pasture, and firewood rights, and - most consequentially -
letting any unfree peasant buy his own freedom outright for ten
piastres. In 1749 he extended the same emancipation of the vecini, as
Moldavia's own bonded peasantry were known, to the throne he had by then
moved to. The Moldavian assembly that ratified his decree recorded its
own justification in blunt terms: that some landowners had taken to
selling their peasants as slaves outright, separating children from
parents and trading them like property.

## A reformer's fall, and a reform undone

Mavrocordato's reforms cost him the loyalty of the boyars whose revenue
they touched. The Porte recalled him from Wallachia within the year, and
though he was restored twice more to that throne, in 1756 and 1761, his
final deposition in 1763 left him stripped of his estates and imprisoned
in the Seven Towers. What his reforms had loosened, later princes let the
boyars tighten again: by 1766 a Moldavian decree, signed reluctantly by
Grigore II Ghica, had already begun expanding the forced-labor
obligations Mavrocordato had tried to cap, and the pressure to deepen
peasant servitude continued for the rest of the Phanariote century
regardless of who held the throne.

## The crossing that started, and did not finish, in Moldavia

The regime's own end began on Moldavian soil. On 20 February 1821,
Alexander Ypsilanti - a Russian-service officer and son of a former
hospodar - crossed the Pruth at the head of the Greek revolutionary
Hetairia, hoping to rally the Romanian principalities behind a war for
Greek independence. Moldavia's own reigning prince, Mihail Șuțu, backed
the movement openly, subsidizing it and petitioning the Tsar on its
behalf; Moldavia's boyars did not. They appealed to the neighboring
Ottoman pashas for help against Ypsilanti's men, whom they described
plainly as brigands, and the movement found no popular following among
a peasantry that, by a contemporary British consul's own account, met
even ordinary oppression with numb resignation rather than revolt.
Ypsilanti pressed on into Wallachia, where a separate and far more
consequential rising was already under way, and Moldavia watched the
resulting collapse - Ypsilanti's rout at Drăgășani, his flight into
Austrian custody - from the sidelines of a revolution its own prince had
funded and its own nobility had opposed from the start.

## A native prince restored

The Ottoman Porte, eager to separate the Romanian and Greek causes and
free its forces for the larger revolt still burning in the Peloponnese,
granted Moldavian boyars the same concession Wallachia's received in the
same month: in June 1822, Ioniță Sandu Sturdza was installed as prince,
the first Moldavian-born ruler since Cantemir's flight eleven years
earlier. He inherited a country the Phanariote century, Pasvantoğlu's
raids, Russian requisitions and the Hetairist invasion had left in ruins
- Jassy itself burned by retreating Janissaries that August, over two
thousand houses lost - and a boyar class already split between
reformers pressing a limited constitutional council and emigres who
would use Russian pressure to block him at every turn.
