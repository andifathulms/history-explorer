Moldavia's own founding legend is considerably more modest than
Wallachia's battlefield origin story. As the power of the Tatars
weakened in the region during the reign of Hungary's King Louis I, the
Hungarian crown established a frontier march east of the Carpathians to
hold the approach against any renewed threat from the steppe, placing a
voivode named Dragoș from Maramureș in charge of it. Tradition holds that
Dragoș, out hunting, named the river running through his new territory
Moldova after his own hound, Molda, who drowned chasing game across it -
and gave the land the same name in turn.

## A frontier post that outgrew its own purpose

What Hungary had built as a defensive outpost did not stay a Hungarian
outpost for long. Dragoș's own successor was banished by a rival noble -
himself previously exiled from Maramureș by the Hungarian crown - and in
1359 that rival took the march's own scepter and broke it from Hungarian
control outright. The same year, in Wallachia, Prince Nicolae Alexandru
founded a metropolitan seat under the authority of the Patriarch of
Constantinople - two Romanian-speaking principalities, on opposite sides
of the Carpathians, asserting their own independence from Catholic
Hungary within months of each other, one politically and one
religiously.

## Two states, one shared position

Moldavia's own geography gave it a different relationship to its
neighbors than Wallachia's had. Sitting further from the Ottoman
heartland but directly exposed to Poland and Hungary on its other
borders, Moldavia spent its own early history balancing threats from
three directions rather than one - a position that would eventually
produce, in Stephen the Great, the single most successful balancing act
either principality's history has to show.
