Stephen's own diplomacy could be as combative as his battles. When King
John Albert of Poland announced he intended to march against the
Ottomans, Stephen suspected the declared purpose concealed a different
one - an attempt on Moldavia itself - and sent two of his own men, the
scribe Tăutu and the treasurer Isaac, to greet the Polish king and take
his measure. The suspicion proved justified. According to the chronicler
Grigore Ureche, the Polish court received the Moldavian envoys warmly
enough at first, but once the army crossed the Dniester, "they
discovered all their cunning things and their hidden deeds" - Tăutu and
Isaac were seized, placed in manacles, and sent to prison at Lviv.

## A battle that answered the insult directly

Poland's actual invasion followed the arrest of Stephen's own envoys, and
it fared no better than the sultan's own campaigns had. At Codrii
Cosminului, on 26 October 1497, Stephen's forces inflicted a crushing
defeat on the Polish army - a second major victory, alongside Vaslui, in
which a ruler surrounded on three sides by larger powers turned each
threat back in turn rather than being ground down by any one of them.
Isaac the treasurer, freed from his own captivity in the aftermath,
marked the occasion by having a Slavonic gospel manuscript bound later
that same December - a small, personal gesture of gratitude for an
imprisonment that had ended considerably better than it began.

## A ruler who built as readily as he fought

Stephen's own reign left a religious legacy as durable as his military
one. He founded the Episcopate of Vad, near Dej, and tradition credits
him as well with founding the church at Feleac, near Cluj, which became
the seat of the Transylvanian Metropolitan Church for nearly seven
decades afterward - support for Orthodox communities living under a
Catholic Hungarian crown, extended across a border Stephen's own wars
never crossed as an invader. The church at Feleac itself, a structure
blending Byzantine painting with Gothic vaulting, stands as a physical
record of exactly the position Moldavia occupied throughout Stephen's
reign: a Christian Orthodox state literally built from both the Eastern
and Western architectural vocabularies available to it, refusing to
commit fully to either side of the frontier it sat on.
