Stephen took the Moldavian throne in 1457 with backing from an unlikely
source: Vlad III of Wallachia, the same neighboring ruler whose own
reputation for cruelty would come to rival, and in some tellings exceed,
Stephen's. The debt did not bind Stephen to permanent loyalty. In 1462,
with Vlad occupied fighting off Mehmed II's invasion, Stephen attacked
Radu - the Ottoman-backed brother who had just displaced Vlad in
Wallachia - and drove him temporarily into exile in Hungary, bringing
Wallachia briefly under Moldavian influence before Radu's own restoration
reversed the gain. It was an early sign of how Stephen intended to run
his own long reign: aiding whichever side served Moldavia's own interests
in the moment, kinship and prior obligation notwithstanding.

## A defeat severe enough to name a valley after it

Stephen's own fortunes did not run one direction only. A major defeat at
a battlefield later called Valea Albă - "the White Valley," for the bones
of the Moldavian dead left bleaching there - came only a year after a
victory impressive enough to draw a special envoy from Venice and a papal
letter praising Stephen as a defender of Christendom. Popular tradition
holds that when Stephen, disheartened by the scale of the loss, wavered
over whether to keep fighting, his own mother refused him shelter and
sent him back to his army instead - a story a later Romanian poet turned
into verse, imagining her urging her son to fall in battle rather than
retreat and live. Whatever actually passed between them, Stephen returned
to the field, rebuilt his forces within a few years, and drove the
Ottomans back out of Moldavia.

## A ruler who fought his wars one at a time

What made Stephen's nearly fifty-year reign so unusual, by the account
of his own court physician, was not merely that he fought constantly -
nearly every prince of the era did - but that he fought with real
consistency of purpose, dealing with Ottoman, Polish and Hungarian
pressure separately rather than letting them combine against him at
once. Confident that a full Ottoman invasion of Moldavia was only a
matter of time regardless of what he did, Stephen made a deliberate
choice to force the reckoning during his own lifetime rather than leave
it to a less capable successor - a wager that would be tested directly,
and decisively, at Vaslui in 1475.
