Dimitrie Cantemir was not the obvious choice to end native Moldavian
rule. The Ottoman Porte had installed him on the throne in 1710
specifically to counter the ambitions of Wallachia's own prince,
Constantin Brâncoveanu - a scholar who had spent years studying the
Ottoman state's own origins and had concluded, well before most of
his contemporaries, that Turkish power had passed its peak and that
Russia was the coming force in the region. When Tsar Peter the Great
marched into Moldavia in 1711 hoping to rally the Christian Balkans
against the Porte, Cantemir did not hedge the way Brâncoveanu chose
to. On 13 April 1711 he signed a secret treaty guaranteeing Moldavia's
own independence and his family's hereditary right to the throne, then
defied the grand vizier's standing orders and threw his support openly
behind the Russian army once it reached Iași.

## A bet that collapsed within a season

The gamble did not survive contact with Ottoman numbers. Peter's own
army, overextended and outnumbered roughly five to one, was trapped at
Stanilesti on the Pruth on 11 July 1711; only frantic bribery of the
Ottoman high command by the tsar's envoy secured a Russian withdrawal
rather than a capitulation, and Moldavia was left to face the
consequences of a war its own prince had just publicly joined on the
losing side. Cantemir fled with the retreating Russian army, reportedly
smuggled out wrapped in blankets in the tsarina's own carriage, and
never returned. In exile he went on to help found the Academy of
Sciences in St. Petersburg and to win real standing in European
intellectual circles, elected to the Prussian Academy and corresponding
with scholars abroad - a genuine second career built on the ruin of
his first.

## The throne Cantemir left behind

The Porte did not restore native rule once Cantemir was gone. It
installed Nicolas Mavrocordato, a Greek administrator from
Constantinople's own Phanar district who had already held the
Moldavian throne briefly the year before, ending direct Ottoman trust
in locally rooted boyar dynasties for good. Wallachia would follow the
same path three years later, after Brâncoveanu's own hedging failed to
save him from a similar fate. What began as one scholar-prince's
calculated bet on a foreign power became, within a few years, the
model the Ottomans applied to both Danubian principalities at once:
government by appointees who owed Constantinople everything and the
local nobility nothing.
