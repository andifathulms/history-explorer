Long before Mayapan existed, the Itza people had already made their own
long migration across the Yucatán peninsula - settling first at
Chakanputun on the Gulf coast, driven from it around the start of the
thirteenth century, and moving on to found a new home at Chichen Itza,
already a site of deep religious importance built up by earlier Maya and
Toltec builders. Chichen Itza flourished under them for a time, but its
own great period did not last.

## A departure the chronicles remember by name

Colonial-era Maya sources are specific about what happened next: a
figure named Kukulcan left Chichen Itza and established another city,
which he called Mayapan. Modern scholarship treats this less as the
biography of one man than as the folk-memory of a migration - a group of
Itza relocating from the old religious capital to found a new political
one nearby, in the Maya calendar's own reckoning, in the katun that
correlates to somewhere between A.D. 1263 and 1283. The new city, unlike
Chichen Itza, was built with an unusual feature for a lowland Maya
capital: a massive stone wall running for miles around its core, dotted
with gateways, enclosing the temples and the residences of the lords who
would come to dominate it.

## A city that outgrew its own founding legend

Whatever the precise mechanics of Kukulcan's departure from Chichen
Itza, Mayapan did not stay a modest daughter settlement for long.
Within a century or so it had eclipsed the city it grew out of entirely,
becoming the dominant power of the whole northern peninsula and drawing,
eventually, the lords of provinces far beyond its own immediate
territory into a single system of government centred on the walled
city itself.
