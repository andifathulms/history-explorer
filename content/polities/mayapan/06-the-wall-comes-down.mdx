Sometime within the Maya katun that correlates to the years 1441 to
1461, the resentment the Cocom's own foreign garrison had held in check
for decades finally broke into open violence. Landa's account states
the immediate cause plainly: the nobles, worn down by a ruler they
regarded as a tyrant, joined the party of the Xiu family and conspired
together to put the Cocom to death.

## Fighting for control of the city's own wall

Mayapan's chronicles describe the fighting itself in terms that point
to the city's most distinctive feature: stone-throwing combat over
possession of the great defensive wall that ringed the whole capital,
followed by the wall's own deliberate destruction. Breaking down a
structure that ran for miles around the city was, by any account, a
formidable task - one that would have made little sense unless the
attackers meant to ensure the joint government housed inside it could
never be reconstituted behind those same defences again.

## A ruling family destroyed almost to the last name

The Cocom ruling family was killed in the uprising, by the tradition's
own account, down to nearly every member of the line. A single son
survived only because he happened to be away on a trading expedition in
Honduras at the time; on his return, he gathered enough surviving
relatives and loyal followers to found a new state at Sotuta, carrying
the Cocom name forward in a smaller territory rather than at the head of
a peninsula-wide capital. The Canul, allies of the Cocom who had
garrisoned the city alongside them, fared better than their patrons: they
were permitted to withdraw to a territory of their own in western
Yucatán, remembered afterward, in their own chronicle, as a people who
came to be loved by the towns they settled among rather than feared.
