title: A tyranny the lords endured

The other noble families compelled to live inside Mayapan's own walls
had every reason to resent a Cocom ruler backed by foreign soldiers, but
for decades they had no practical way to act on it. The Xiu, a family
that had itself only recently settled in Yucatán after some time at the
ruined city of Uxmal, are singled out in the tradition as never having
consented to the arrangement the Cocom had imposed - a standing
objection that carried no weight so long as the Cocom held the loyalty,
or the fear, of foreign troops billeted in the same city as every rival
lord's own household.

## A grievance later generations argued over

Whether the Cocom ruler's conduct amounted to genuine tyranny is not
something even the surviving Maya sources agree on cleanly. The version
that reached the Spanish missionary Diego de Landa, drawn chiefly from a
Xiu informant with an obvious stake in the story, describes outright
oppression and enslavement of commoners. Other evidence suggests a less
dramatic possibility: that what later chroniclers called tyranny may
have been ordinary enforcement of tax obligations and military service
against families and provinces that had simply stopped paying or
serving - a routine exercise of a ruler's own authority remembered, once
his family had been destroyed, as monstrous by the descendants of the
people who destroyed it.

## Held hostage in the same city they resented

Whatever its true character, the arrangement left the other noble
houses in an uncomfortable position: it was in the same capital they
were required to reside in, under the same roof as the family they had
come to resent, that the eventual reckoning would have to happen. There
was no province to retreat to and organize a rebellion from at a safe
distance - if the Cocom were to be overthrown, it would have to happen
inside Mayapan's own walls, among neighbours who had lived beside each
other for a generation.
