What made Mayapan more than the largest city in northern Yucatán was a
system of government unlike anything else recorded among the Maya
lowlands. Rather than each province governing itself under its own
resident lord, the noble families of the surrounding territories were
required to keep their own households inside Mayapan's own walls,
leaving the day-to-day administration of their home districts to
appointed deputies while they themselves lived at the capital -
permanently, not on periodic visits.

## A visitor's description that survived the city's own fall

Nearly two centuries after Mayapan's own destruction, a Spanish
missionary named Antonio de Ciudad Real visited the crumbling remains
of the site and recorded what older informants still remembered of how
it had once functioned: a very populous city, he wrote, in which, as if
it were a royal court, all the caciques and lords of the province of
Maya resided, and there they came with their tribute. Whatever the
precise legal mechanism behind the arrangement, its effect was to turn
the whole of Yucatán's own provincial nobility into permanent residents
of one city, answerable to whichever family held the capital's own
leading office.

## An arrangement built on leverage rather than consent

A city built to hold every province's own ruling family inside one set
of walls was, in effect, a city built to hold every province hostage to
whoever controlled it. For a century or more this arrangement produced
real stability across the peninsula - a broad political order,
archaeologists would later conclude from the site's own remains, falling
between periods of conflict on either side of it. But the same
concentration of hostages that gave Mayapan its power also gave whichever
family dominated its own government an unusually direct means of
coercing every other noble house in the land, a temptation the city's
own ruling family would not resist indefinitely.
