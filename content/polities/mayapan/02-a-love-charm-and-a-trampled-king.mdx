---
title: A love charm and a trampled king
drafted_from: mayapan-1962

Maya chronicles preserve, in several overlapping versions, a story of
treachery that marks Mayapan's own rise against the city it had grown
out of. A ruler remembered as Hunac Ceel - identified in some accounts
as the Cocom lord of Mayapan itself, in others as a ruler of the allied
Canul people - is said to have worked a love charm with the plumeria
flower that caused Chac Xib Chac, the ruler of Chichen Itza, to desire
the bride of the ruler of the nearby city of Izamal. Chac Xib Chac,
invited to the wedding, was induced to smell the enchanted flower and
abducted the bride in front of the assembled guests - compounding an
older grievance, since Izamal's own people were already said to be
sending their sons in tribute to feed a serpent god kept at Chichen
Itza.

## A city destroyed to settle the insult

What followed differs by source. One tradition, preserved among the
Itza who eventually settled at Lake Petén, holds that Chichen Itza's own
people, fearing retribution, abandoned the city outright and fled south
by sea. The traditions of northern Yucatán tell a harder story: that
Hunac Ceel, backed by a group of followers bearing Mexican names,
attacked Chichen Itza directly, trampled its ruler underfoot, and
scattered its population into the forest. Either way, the outcome was
the same - a city that had dominated northern Yucatán for generations
lost its people, and the city that had grown out of it, Mayapan,
inherited the standing Chichen Itza could no longer hold.

## A legend too tangled to date precisely

Later chroniclers themselves could not agree on exactly when this
happened, nor could they always agree on Hunac Ceel's own identity - a
sign, more than anything, of how thoroughly the episode had already
passed from remembered history into founding legend by the time Spanish
missionaries began writing any of it down. What the story consistently
explains is not a date but a fact: that Mayapan's own claim to primacy
over the peninsula rested, in the memory of the people who told it, on
the ruin of the older and more sacred city it had once been part of.
