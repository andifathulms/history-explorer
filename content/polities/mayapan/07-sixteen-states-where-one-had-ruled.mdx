Destroying the Cocom family and tearing down Mayapan's own wall did not
hand power to any single successor. No account makes clear whether the
Xiu, who had led the conspiracy, actually took over the supreme office
at the capital, or whether the revolt simply ended with the city's own
destruction and nothing built to replace what had governed from it.
What is clear is that the joint government of resident provincial lords,
the arrangement that had bound the whole northern peninsula together
for the better part of a century, ceased to exist at the same moment the
city that housed it did.

## A peninsula without a centre

In the vacuum, Yucatán broke apart into roughly sixteen independent
provinces, each under its own ruling family, with no capital and no
shared institution left to coordinate them. Some of these successor
states, like the Xiu's own territory around Mani and the Cocom's
refounded state at Sotuta, carried forward names and lineages the
Mayapan period had made prominent; others formed around noble houses
that had played smaller parts in the joint government the city once
housed. The friendly relations some of these provinces maintained with
each other, and the old hostilities that persisted between others,
followed lines drawn as much by the family loyalties of Mayapan's own
final years as by any new logic of their own.

## A landscape ready-made for later conquest

This fragmented Yucatán is the one that survived largely intact for
close to a century, until Spanish expeditions arrived to find not one
Maya kingdom to negotiate with or defeat, but sixteen separate
provinces that had to be approached, allied with, played against each
other, or conquered one at a time - a slower and more piecemeal
conquest than the single decisive campaign that had ended the Aztec
Empire, and one that took Spanish forces the better part of the
sixteenth century to complete.
