---
title: O ravening dog named Timur

Timur took Baghdad on 20 June 1401 and this time he did not leave a governor in
it.

The siege had cost him a number of senior officers, and the massacre was
explicitly revenge for them. Every soldier was ordered to bring in a head; Ibn
Arabshah says the army numbered twenty thousand and the order was two heads
each. Sharaf al-Din Yazdi, writing for the conqueror's own house and therefore
not inclined to minimise it, describes the day in the register of a market
report: the market of retribution became so brisk that the broker of death sold
at one price the old man of eighty and the child of eight, and the oven of wrath
was so enkindled that it consumed the corporeal vestiture of the wealthy
plutocrat and the wretched pauper alike. Browne's summary of what was left is
three words: a smoking charnel-house.

Sultan Ahmad was not in it. He had gone west again, as he had gone to Cairo in
1393, and this time to the Ottoman sultan Bayezid — and what followed is the
best-documented thing in the whole Jalayirid record, because the letters survive.

Timur wrote to Bayezid in Arabic demanding that no shelter be given to Qara
Yusuf the Turkmen or to Sultan Ahmad, in terms Browne calls peremptory, and
warning him against disobedience. Bayezid's reply, also in Arabic, opens after
the doxology: *Know, O ravening dog named Timur.* It then dares him to advance.
Browne, who did not much like the Ottomans, notes that refusing to surrender a
guest was a thing the Turks had always done, and that this particular refusal was
phrased in a manner little calculated to calm anybody.

Ahmad's own letter is in the same collection, written in Persian to Bayezid. In
it he reports what he has done since losing Baghdad and the two Iraqs: he
withdrew to Malatya and Sivas as instructed, waited for Qara Yusuf, and the two
of them together attacked the Uzbek troops forming Timur's vanguard and
annihilated them. He adds that he expects the main army to come for them as soon
as the news reaches it. Bayezid's answer says that on the strength of this report
he has made peace with the Byzantine emperor and moved up to Tokat to help hold
the invasion.

That is a dispossessed sultan, living on other people's ground, successfully
persuading the Ottoman empire to break off the siege of Constantinople and turn
east. It worked, in the sense that Bayezid came. At Ankara in 1402 Timur
destroyed the Ottoman army and took Bayezid prisoner, and Byzantium got another
fifty-one years out of it.

Timur died in February 1405 and Ahmad came back and retook Baghdad, and then,
with Qara Yusuf, Tabriz — and then quarrelled with Qara Yusuf over which of them
Tabriz belonged to, marched against him in 1410, and was defeated and killed.

The proportions are worth holding. Over nine years one man was driven out of his
capital twice, sheltered by two empires, corresponded with a third, contributed
materially to the destruction of the Ottoman army and the survival of
Constantinople, recovered his city, and was then killed in a dispute with the
Turkmen chief who had been his fellow refugee — and whose confederation had been
hired cavalry to Ahmad's father thirty years earlier.
