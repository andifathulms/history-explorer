The Jalayirids matter for a small number of books.

Persian painting before them is the painting of the Ilkhanid workshops: the
great *Shahnama* manuscripts, Chinese-derived landscape, figures arranged in
registers, an art of illustration in the strict sense. Persian painting after
them is the Timurid and then the Safavid tradition — the high horizon, the
deep gold-flecked sky, the architecture drawn so that the reader looks into and
around it, figures who occupy a place rather than stand in front of one. The
change happens in Jalayirid Baghdad and Tabriz in the last quarter of the
fourteenth century.

Two manuscripts carry most of the argument. The *Khamsa* of Nizami made in the
late 1380s, and the *Divan* of Khwaju Kirmani copied at Baghdad in 1396 for
Sultan Ahmad, whose paintings are signed by Junayd — one of the earliest Persian
painters whose name is attached to his own work rather than inferred.

Lentz and
Lowry's catalogue treats the Jalayirid court as the direct ancestor of the
Timurid *kitabkhana* rather than as a preface to it: the personnel, the formats
and the pictorial conventions move from Baghdad and Tabriz to Herat and
Samarkand, in some cases with the painters themselves, when Timur takes the
cities and deports the craftsmen.

<Figure id="add18113-malikshah" />

That deportation is the transmission mechanism, and it should be stated plainly
rather than admired. Samarkand was built by craftsmen taken from Delhi and
Damascus; the same policy was applied to Baghdad and Tabriz. The Timurid court
style that three later empires worked from was assembled substantially out of
workshops that had belonged to somebody else and were moved at swordpoint.

Sultan Ahmad, who commissioned the 1396 *Divan*, was himself a poet and by
several accounts drew. He spent the last twenty years of his life losing cities.
Political weight and cultural output come apart everywhere; this is the version
where the output was physically carried off by the winner.
