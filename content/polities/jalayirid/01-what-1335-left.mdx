title: What 1335 left

This site's Ilkhanate page ends in 1335 with `fragmentation` and an empty list
of conquerors, because there was no conqueror. Abu Sa'id died without an heir,
the Mongol state in Iran stopped functioning, and what remained was its army:
Mongol and Turkmen amirs commanding contingents, holding revenue assignments,
and each in possession of a piece of a machine that no longer had an operator.

None of them could take the throne. The rule that had governed Central Asian
politics since Chinggis still held — only a descendant of Chinggis could be
khan — and none of these men was one. So they did the only thing available:
each found a Chinggisid, put him on a throne, ruled in his name, and fought the
others. There were at least half a dozen such puppets in the two decades after
1335, several of them raised and deposed by the same amir.

The two houses that mattered were the Jalayir and the Chobanids. The Jalayir
were a Mongol tribe that had come west with Hulegu; Hasan Buzurg — Big Hasan, so
called to distinguish him from his rival Hasan Kuchak, Little Hasan of the
Chobanids — was their leader. The two Hasans fought over Azerbaijan through the
late 1330s, and Hasan Buzurg lost.

Losing Azerbaijan is why the Jalayirids exist. He went south, and in 1340 took
Baghdad, which nobody else particularly wanted: it had been sacked in 1258, it
was no longer a capital of anything, and it was a long way from the pastures the
amirs were fighting over. What it had was the tax revenue of Iraq. Within
fifteen years the Chobanids were finished and the man who had taken the
consolation prize was the strongest power in the region.

That sequence — an amir who cannot be khan, a rival who wins the prestigious
ground, and a state built out of the settled revenue nobody was contesting —
is worth holding on to. Timur is thirty years away and will do the second half
of it on a much larger scale.
