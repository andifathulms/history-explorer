---
title: The nearest thing to a restoration
drafted_from: chi-6
phase: peak
---

Shaykh Uways succeeded his father in 1356 and was, by the assessment of the
sources and of modern scholarship, the ablest ruler the dynasty produced. In
1360 he took Tabriz from the last Chobanid and held it, which put the
Ilkhanate's two capitals — Baghdad and Tabriz — under one man for the first
time since Abu Sa'id died.

For the fourteen years he lived, the Jalayirid state was the nearest thing to a
restoration of the Ilkhanate that anybody managed. It ran Iraq and Azerbaijan,
took tribute from Shirvan, campaigned into eastern Anatolia and against the
Muzaffarids of Fars, and it did so with the Ilkhanid divan still functioning
underneath it: the tax registers, the Persian secretarial class, the assignment
system. This is the reason this page's institutions read the way they do. The
Jalayirids did not build a state. They inherited the working parts of one and
kept them running for three generations, which nobody else who picked over the
Ilkhanate managed for more than a decade.

Uways also dropped the puppet. After about 1356 the Jalayirids stopped raising
Chinggisid figureheads and ruled as sultans in their own name, which is a
straightforward admission that the rule everyone had been observing since 1335
had stopped meaning anything in Iran. It had not stopped meaning anything in
Transoxiana, where Timur was at that moment building his career around
observing it scrupulously — the difference between the two halves of the former
Mongol world in the same decade, and one worth noticing.

Tabriz in this period is the largest commercial city in western Asia, the point
where the routes from Iran and Central Asia met the routes to Trebizond and the
Mediterranean, and Genoese and Venetian merchants were resident in it. The
customs on that traffic are half the reason the dynasty could afford what the
next chapter is about.
