Sultan Ahmad ibn Uways lost Baghdad three times, spent years as a refugee in
Cairo and in Anatolia, was restored twice, and was finally killed by Qara Yusuf
in 1410. He is also the only ruler of his century whom the sources describe as a
practising artist.

## What he was

Browne's description of him is a list of accomplishments, given in passing while
discussing somebody else: Sultan Ahmad ibn Uways the Jalayirid, the accomplished
Ilkhani ruler of Baghdad, himself a poet, a musician, a painter and an artist.

Set that against his political record, which is a catalogue of losing cities to
Timur, and the two do not obviously belong to the same man. They belong to the
same court, though, and the court is the point. Between about 1380 and 1400 the
workshops at Baghdad and Tabriz were producing manuscripts — his own divan among
them, illustrated in 1396 — in which Persian painting stops being a diagram and
becomes a landscape with weather in it.

## The one thing he could not buy

He wanted Hafez, and he could not get him.

Ahmad wrote repeatedly to Shiraz inviting the poet to Baghdad. Hafez declined
every time, and the refusal became one of his best-known couplets: the
zephyr-breeze of Musalla and the stream of Ruknabad do not permit him to travel
or wander afield.

He did send verses. He praised the sultan's justice, called him a khan and the
son of a khan and a king of kingly descent, and added a line that must have been
maddening to receive — that no rose-bud of delight had bloomed for him from the
earth of Fars, and that he sighed for the Tigris of Baghdad and its spiritual
wine.

A ruler who was himself a poet, a musician and a painter wrote to the greatest
lyric poet in the language and was answered with a compliment, a lament for
Baghdad, and a refusal.

## What Timur took

The afterlife proper is the part Ahmad had no say in.

When Timur took Baghdad and Tabriz, the workshops went east with everything else
portable. The Timurid court style that was assembled at Samarkand and then at
Herat — the style three later empires worked from, in Iran, in Turkey and in
India — was built substantially out of craftsmen and manuscripts taken from
Jalayirid cities.

So the transmission ran through a defeat. Everything the Jalayirids had made
reached the fifteenth century in somebody else's collection, credited to
somebody else's patronage, and the dynasty that had paid for it held Basra for
another twenty-two years and then stopped.

Ahmad's own divan, illustrated in his workshop in 1396, is in the British
Library. It is the earliest surviving manuscript in which the landscape does
what Persian painting afterwards always did, and the man who commissioned it
spent the last fourteen years of his life being chased around western Asia.
