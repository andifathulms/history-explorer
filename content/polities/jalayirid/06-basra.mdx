The house did not end in 1410. What was left of it held Basra and the lower
Euphrates for another twenty-two years, under a succession of claimants who
appear in the record mainly when somebody attacks them, until Husayn II was
killed in 1432 and the Kara Koyunlu took the last of it.

So the dynasty has two ending dates, twenty-two years apart, and both are
defensible: 1410 is when the Jalayirids stopped being a power and 1432 is when
they stopped existing. That gap is the ordinary fate of these houses. They are
rarely exterminated. They are reduced, over decades, to whatever could not
conveniently be besieged — a delta, an island, a fortress on a rock — and then
somebody finally bothers.

It is also a standing trap for anybody writing about the period. Qara Yusuf took
Baghdad and killed Sultan Ahmad in 1410, and it is easy and wrong to say that he
finished the Jalayirids, because for another twenty-two years there was a
Jalayirid at Basra.

What they leave is the transmission. The Ilkhanid administrative
apparatus reached the Turkmen confederations and then the Safavids through them
rather than directly, and Persian painting reached Herat the same way.
Ninety-seven years, two capitals, nobody afterwards claiming to be their heirs,
and a manuscript in the British Library that changed what the rest of the
tradition looked like.
