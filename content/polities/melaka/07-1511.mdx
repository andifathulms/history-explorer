Afonso de Albuquerque took Melaka in August 1511 with about fifteen ships and
twelve hundred men.

He had come for it specifically. Portuguese strategy in Asia was to hold the
points at which the spice route passed through a narrows — Hormuz, Goa, Aden,
Melaka — and Melaka was the one that mattered most, because everything moving
between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea went through the strait.
Taking it was supposed to give Portugal the trade.

<Figure id="albuquerque" />

It gave Portugal a customs house.

The trade did not stop and it did not stay. It went to Aceh in northern
Sumatra, to Johor at the tip of the peninsula, to Banten in western Java, to
Brunei and to Patani — and the Portuguese spent the following century holding a
fortified city on a strait whose commerce had rerouted around them. What Melaka
had sold was order, and order was the one thing a garrison could not seize.

<Figure id="famosa" />

The dynasty was not extinguished either. Mahmud Shah withdrew up the peninsula
and died on Bintan; in 1528 his son established himself on the Johor river with
the Melakan regalia and the Melakan court offices, and the claim that this was
the same sultanate in a different place.

That court then commissioned the *Sejarah Melayu*, the chronicle that supplies
the founding story of Melaka — written a century after the city fell, by the
successor, to prove that it was one.

The line held for another hundred and seventy-one years. It ended in 1699, when
Johor's own nobles killed the last man of Melaka's dynasty on his way to the
mosque, three hundred years after a prince out of Palembang stopped under a
tree at a river mouth.
