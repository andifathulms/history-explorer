---
title: Iskandar Shah
drafted_from: reid-1988

Around 1414 the ruler of Melaka married a princess of Pasai in northern
Sumatra, converted, and took the name Iskandar Shah. The date and the identity
of the ruler are both disputed, but the direction is not.

Islam had been present in the archipelago's ports for centuries, and Pasai
itself had been Muslim since about 1267 — the first Muslim throne in Southeast
Asia, absorbed by Aceh two centuries after this marriage. What Melaka did was
make Islam the religion of the archipelago's dominant court, and then export it
along the trade network it already controlled. Melakan merchants carried it.
Melakan princesses married into the ruling houses of ports that then converted.
Over the following century Islam reached Java's north coast, Brunei, Sulu, the
Moluccas and the southern Philippines, and the sequence follows the shipping
routes rather than any frontier.

What is remarkable is that it happened without a single campaign of conquest.
This was conversion by commerce, and it is rarer than the sheer extent of the
result makes it look — the Swahili coast carried Islam the same way and did not
carry it nearly as far.

There is a second thing Melaka spread and did not invent. Malay was written
here in Jawi, the Arabic script with letters added for sounds Arabic has no
signs for, and Melaka made Jawi the standard written form of Malay across the
archipelago. It did not create that script; it made it the region's. Four
centuries later, a Malay letter from Sulu to Sumatra would still be legible at
both ends because a port on this strait had settled how the language was
written.
