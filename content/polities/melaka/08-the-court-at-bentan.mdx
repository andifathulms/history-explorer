On the night before the Portuguese assault, the young nobles of Melaka sent to
Sultan Ahmad asking for the *Hikayat Muhammad Hanafiah* to be read to them, so
that they might take courage from it.

Ahmad sent back the *Hikayat Amir Hamzah* instead, with a message: we would
give you the story of Muhammad Hanafiah, did we not fear that the bravery of
the gentlemen of our court falls short of the bravery of Muhammad Hanafiah —
but it may be that their bravery is such as was the bravery of Hamzah.

The nobles were silent. Then Tun Isak Berakah answered: tell the ruler he has
spoken amiss. *If he will be as Muhammad Hanafiah, we will be as war-chief
Bania'.* The message went back. The king smiled, and sent them the story of
Muhammad Hanafiah after all.

That is a court negotiating, on the last night it existed, over which epic it
deserved.

## The day

The Franks landed at dawn. Ahmad mounted his elephant Juru Demang, the Sri
Awadana on the elephant's head, Tun 'Ali Hati steadying the king on the
packsaddle. The Portuguese onslaught broke the Malay line and left the king
isolated on his elephant, fighting pike to pike, and he was wounded through the
palm of the hand.

He held it up. *"See this, Malays!"* — and the war-chiefs came back and fought.

Tun Salehu'd-din was struck in the chest and killed, and twenty of the leading
war-chiefs with him. The Sri Awadana was wounded in the groin, and the elephant
was made to kneel so he could be carried off; a doctor probed the wound with
the point of a sireh leaf and said it could be treated, but that half a rice
grain deeper would have killed him.

And Melaka fell.

## What the dynasty did next

Ahmad fled to Pahang and then to Bentan, with his father. His conduct in exile
offended Mahmud, who had him murdered and resumed the throne himself — the son
who had fought on the elephant, killed by the father who had abdicated to let
him do it.

Mahmud then set up a new administration at Bentan and named as his successor
his son by Tun Fatimah — the daughter of the bendahara he had executed, whom he
had taken after the execution. The son he displaced to do it was Raja Muzaffar
Shah.

## The empire dissolving

What follows in the chronicle is a court with a claim and no city, watching its
vassals work out that the claim was all it had.

Sultan Abdullah of Kampar refused to appear before Mahmud at Bentan or to
acknowledge him any longer, and — fearing what would follow — asked the
Portuguese at Melaka for protection. They gave it. Mahmud sent a fleet against
Kampar and the Portuguese beat it, and the Malays took refuge up the Indragiri
river.

The end of that story is the shape of the whole century in one sentence:
Abdullah was taken prisoner by the Portuguese, and sent first to Goa and then
to Portugal.

Mahmud died on Bentan. In 1528 his second son established himself on the Johor
river with the regalia and the court offices, and the line held another hundred
and seventy-one years — long enough to commission the *Sejarah Melayu*
itself, written to argue that nothing had ended at all.
