Melaka was a river mouth with a hill behind it, and within a century it was the
port whose fall would date the arrival of European power in maritime Asia and
the polity through which Islam reached the eastern archipelago.

The founder, in the *Sejarah Melayu* — the *Malay Annals*, the court chronicle
composed at Johor a century after the city fell — is a prince of Palembang
named Parameswara, driven out, moving up the peninsula, and stopping at a river
mouth where he saw a mouse-deer turn and face his hunting dogs.
He took it as
an omen of a place where the weak stand against the strong, and named the
settlement after the tree he was standing under.

<Figure id="melaka-river" />

The chronicle then traces his line back through Palembang to the kings of
Srivijaya, and beyond them to Alexander the Great.

The two halves of that genealogy are worth separating. Palembang is a real
memory: the founder came from there, and Srivijaya had run the same strait on
the same terms seven hundred years earlier. Alexander is not — it is the
standard furniture of Malay royal descent, and almost every dynasty that could
afford a chronicler acquired some.

What matters is that Melaka claimed it at all. A new settlement with no army
and no rice land needed to be somebody, and the somebody it chose was the last
power that had made this water pay.
