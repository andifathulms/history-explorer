---
title: The bendahara
drafted_from: brown-1970

Mansur Shah reigned from 1459 to 1477, and under him Melaka's authority
extended over most of the peninsula and the Sumatran coast opposite. The *Malay
Annals* treat his court as the model against which everything later is
measured, which is what a chronicle written in exile after the fall does.

The figure the chronicle actually turns on is not the sultan. It is the
*bendahara* — the chief minister, an office held for most of the fifteenth
century by a single family, and the man the *Annals* show choosing between
claimants, restraining rulers, and in one celebrated episode being executed on
a false accusation by a sultan who then regretted it for the rest of his reign.

So how did the throne actually pass? The sultanate was hereditary, and the
decisive intervention in at least three successions was the bendahara's. Nobody
wrote that down as a rule, and it probably never was one — an office that
repeatedly determines who rules, without any right to, is a habit rather than a
constitution.

It is a particular kind of unknowable. The sources do not describe chaos here;
they describe a man, and describing a man is not the same as describing how a
system works. A century of successions turned on the judgement of whoever held
one office, and when the fifteenth-century bendahara family lost its footing
the sultanate had nothing underneath it.
