title: No rice
drafted_from: reid-1988

Melaka lived on tolls and on nothing else, because the city had no agricultural
hinterland worth the name. The peninsula's west coast at that latitude is
mangrove and jungle; the settlement was a river mouth with a hill behind it.
Melaka fed itself by importing rice, and most of the rice came from Java.

Majapahit was the exact inverse: a Javanese state with a granary that wanted
the straits. The two arrangements faced each other across the Java Sea for a
century, and the one without the rice won — because what mattered on this water
was not what a polity produced but what passed it.

What Melaka sold was order. Four *syahbandars* — harbourmasters, one each for
the Gujaratis, for the merchants of southern India and Burma, for those of Java
and the eastern islands, and for the Chinese — handled the traders of their own
communities. Duties were fixed and published. Weights and measures were
standardised, debts were enforceable, and a written commercial code, the
*Undang-Undang Laut Melaka*, governed shipping.

The *orang laut* — the sea peoples of the straits and estuaries — held the rest
of it. They attached themselves to Parameswara and raided for Melaka rather
than against it.

That is precisely the arrangement Srivijaya had run, with the same people, on
the same water, five hundred years earlier. Nothing about it had been written
down and handed on; the geography simply produced the same answer twice. A
power on this strait cannot garrison the coastline, so it hires the only people
who can, and it keeps hiring them for as long as it can pay.
