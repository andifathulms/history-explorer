Melaka's `revenue_basis` is `trade-toll` and nothing else. That makes it the
ninth record in this corpus coded that way alone, and the second in this region.

The reason is that the city had no agricultural hinterland worth the name. The
peninsula's west coast at that latitude is mangrove and jungle; the settlement
was a river mouth with a hill behind it. Melaka fed itself by importing rice,
and most of the rice came from Java.

This corpus's Majapahit record has a chapter called "Rice and sea" about a
Javanese state that had a granary and wanted the straits. Melaka is the exact
inverse: a state that had the straits and bought its food from the granary. The
two arrangements faced each other across the Java Sea for a century, and the one
without the rice won — because what mattered on this water was not what a
polity produced but what passed it.

What Melaka sold was order. Four *syahbandars* — harbourmasters, one each for
the Gujaratis, for the merchants of southern India and Burma, for those of
Java and the eastern islands, and for the Chinese — handled the traders of their
own communities. Duties were fixed and published. Weights and measures were
standardised, debts were enforceable, and a written commercial code, the
*Undang-Undang Laut Melaka*, governed shipping.

The *orang laut* — the sea peoples of the straits and estuaries — held the rest
of it. They attached themselves to Parameswara and raided for Melaka rather than
against it, which is why this record's `military_basis` is `client-levy`. It is
the same arrangement, with the same people, on the same water, that this
corpus's Srivijaya record carries five hundred years earlier. Two records, five
centuries apart, identical in that field for identical reasons: the strait was
safe because the people who could have made it unsafe were on the payroll.
