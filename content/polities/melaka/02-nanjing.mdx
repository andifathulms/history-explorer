A new settlement on a strait, with no army and no rice land, between Ayutthaya
to the north — which claimed the peninsula — and Majapahit to the south, which
had claimed everything.

In 1411 Parameswara sailed to China. He went in person, with his wife and son
and a party of several hundred, was received by the Yongle emperor at Nanjing,
and came home with recognition as king of Melaka, a seal, a set of robes, and a
stone tablet for a hill in his city.

This corpus's Ming record carries Zheng He's voyages as an outbound fact — seven
fleets, an enormous state effort, and a policy abandoned within a generation.
This is the same system seen from the receiving end, and it is the clearest case
on this site of it working as designed. Melaka got a guarantee that made
Ayutthaya's claim unenforceable, and the Ming got a secured waypoint at the
narrowest point of the route to the Indian Ocean. The fleets called at Melaka
repeatedly and used it as a depot.

The `legitimation` field on this record carries `investiture` for it. That value
was added to this site's vocabulary earlier in this corpus's life for a set of
records — mostly Islamic — where a ruler held authority granted by an external
sovereign and said so publicly, and where the existing `caliphal-investiture`
named the wrong grantor. Melaka is now the record that shows the value was
right to be general: the grantor here is a Chinese emperor, the transaction is
identical, and a value naming a caliph could not have held it.
