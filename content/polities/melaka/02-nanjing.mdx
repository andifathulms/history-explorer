A new settlement on a strait, with no army and no rice land, between Ayutthaya
to the north — which claimed the peninsula — and Majapahit to the south, which
had claimed everything.

In 1411 Parameswara sailed to China. He went in person, with his wife and son
and a party of several hundred, was received by the Yongle emperor at Nanjing,
and came home with recognition as king of Melaka, a seal, a set of robes, and a
stone tablet for a hill in his city.

This is Zheng He's system seen from the receiving end, and it is the clearest
case anywhere of it working as designed. Melaka got a guarantee that made
Ayutthaya's claim unenforceable. The Ming got a secured waypoint at the
narrowest point of the route to the Indian Ocean, and the treasure fleets
called there repeatedly and used it as a depot.

It is worth noticing what kind of transaction that is. Melaka's ruler did not
claim descent from a Chinese line, and the Ming did not administer anything in
the strait. What passed between them was recognition: an emperor who had never
seen the place declared its ruler a king, and every neighbour who might
otherwise have taken the town had to weigh what that meant. The seal was worth
more than a garrison, and considerably cheaper.

The arrangement lasted about as long as the fleets did. When the Ming abandoned
the voyages within a generation, Melaka kept the title and lost the guarantee,
and had to hold the strait on its own terms — which, for another century, it
did.
