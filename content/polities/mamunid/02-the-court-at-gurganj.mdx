When Mahmud of Ghazna annexed Khwarazm in 1017 he took its scholars back to
Ghazna along with everything else that could be moved. This is the court he took
them from.

The Ma'munids displaced an older line of Khwarazmshahs at Gurganj in 995 and
lasted twenty-two years. Khwarazm is the delta where the Oxus reaches the Aral
Sea — irrigated, dense, rich for its size, physically separated from everywhere
else by desert, and sitting on the route by which furs, amber and slaves came
south out of the northern forests. It was a small state that could afford
things.

What it bought was scholars. At Gurganj under Ma'mun II there were assembled,
at the same time: al-Biruni, then in his thirties, already an astronomer of the
first rank; Ibn Sina, the physician and philosopher whose *Canon* was taught in
European medical faculties for five centuries; Abu Nasr Mansur, al-Biruni's
teacher and a mathematician who did foundational work on spherical
trigonometry; Abu Sahl al-Masihi, a Christian physician; Ibn al-Khammar,
another; and al-Tha'alibi, the anthologist to whom a great deal of what
survives of tenth-century Arabic poetry is owed.

Later tradition calls it an academy, which overstates the institution and
understates the concentration. There is no evidence of a formal foundation with
statutes and endowments. There is evidence of a ruler who paid well, a small
capital where these men were in each other's company, and correspondence
between them that survives — including an exchange of questions and answers
between the young Ibn Sina and the young al-Biruni, in which they disagree
sharply about Aristotle and neither gives ground.

Fifteen years, in a delta town, and two of the half-dozen most consequential
minds of the medieval world were arguing with each other in it.
