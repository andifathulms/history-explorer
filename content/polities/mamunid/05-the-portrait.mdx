The dynasty lasted twenty-two years and its afterlife is a scattering of men.
The best account of how they scattered is in the *Chahar Maqala*, written a
century and a half later, and Browne translates it.

## The letter

Shortly before the annexation, Mahmud of Ghazna sent Ma'mun II a letter by the
hand of one of his nobles. Browne gives its text, and its politeness is the
whole of its menace:

> I have heard that there are in attendance on the Khwarazmshah several men of
> learning, each unrivalled in his science, such as So-and-so and So-and-so. You
> must send them to my Court, so that they may have the honour of being
> presented thereat. We rely on being enabled to profit by their knowledge and
> skill, and request this favour on the part of the Prince of Khwarazm.

It was a command and Ma'mun understood it as one. He called the men in and put
it to them straight: the Sultan is strong, he has a large army out of Khurasan
and India, and he wants this country. I cannot refuse to obey his order.

What say ye on this matter?

## Who went and who ran

Three of them — al-Biruni, Ibn al-Khammar and one other — had heard about
Mahmud's generosity and were willing to go.

Ibn Sina and Abu Sahl al-Masihi were not, and with Ma'mun's connivance they got
out privately. A dust-storm caught them in the desert and al-Masihi died in it.
Ibn Sina, after what Browne calls terrible hardships, reached Abiward, and made
his way to Tus, then Nishapur, and finally to Gurgan on the Caspian, where Qabus
b. Vushmgir was ruling.

Now the detail that makes the episode. Of all the scholars he had demanded, the
one Mahmud actually wanted was Ibn Sina; so on learning that he had escaped, the
sultan had a portrait of him copied and circulated through the lands.

At Gurgan, Ibn Sina restored a favourite kinsman of Qabus to health and was
summoned before the prince — who recognised him immediately from the portrait,
and did not hand him over. He kept him honourably in his service instead, until
the philosopher moved on to Rayy and eventually became a minister at Isfahan.

## What happened to the ones who went

Al-Biruni spent the rest of his life at Ghazna and wrote the great book about
India there, so the arrangement was not a disaster. It was not comfortable
either.

The *Chahar Maqala* has Mahmud, sitting in a four-doored summer-house in the
Garden of a Thousand Trees, asking al-Biruni to predict by the stars which door
he would leave by. Al-Biruni wrote his answer and put it under a quilt. The
sultan had a hole knocked in a wall and went out through that instead, then
called for the paper. It read: the King will go out by none of these four doors,
but an opening will be made in the eastern wall by which he will leave the
building.

Mahmud, who had wanted to make him look foolish, had him thrown off the roof. A
mosquito-curtain broke the fall. Asked whether he had foreseen that, al-Biruni
produced a notebook in which he had written, under the day's date, that he would
be cast down from a high place and reach the ground unhurt. He was imprisoned in
the citadel for six months.

When the prime minister eventually interceded — pointing out that the man had
made two exact predictions and been given fetters for them — Mahmud's answer is
the one worth keeping:

> Know, my lord, that this man is said to have no equal in the world save
> Avicenna; but both his predictions were opposed to my will, and Kings are like
> little children: in order to receive rewards from them, one should speak in
> accordance with their opinion.

He then ordered a horse with gold harness, a robe, a satin turban, a thousand
dinars, a slave and a handmaiden.

## The measure of a small court

Set that beside what Gurganj had been. A prince who paid these men, let them
argue with each other, and connived at the escape of the two who would not go.

He was killed by his own commanders for temporising over one sentence in the
Friday prayer, and the men he had assembled were divided between a sultan who
threw one of them off a roof and a road across the desert that killed another.
Twenty-two years, and the only thing the delta could not keep was the reason
anybody remembers it.
