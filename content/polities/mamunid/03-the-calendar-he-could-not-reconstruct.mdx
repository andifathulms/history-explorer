---
title: The calendar he could not reconstruct
drafted_from: chi-4
phase: aside
---

Around the year 1000, in exile at the Ziyarid court at Gurgan on the Caspian,
al-Biruni finished a book called *al-Athar
al-Baqiya*, the remaining traces of past centuries. It is a comparative study
of calendars and eras: how the Persians, Greeks, Romans, Jews, Christians,
Sabians, Zoroastrians, Arabs before Islam, and the people of his own homeland
reckoned time, with the festivals attached to each and the intercalation rules
that made them work.

It is one of the most original books of the Middle Ages. Nobody had written
comparative chronology before, and doing it required a method he had to invent
— establishing what each system's epoch was, converting between them, and
treating each community's account of its own past as evidence to be weighed
rather than accepted or dismissed.

The part that belongs here is Khwarazm. Al-Biruni was a Khwarazmian,
writing about the Khwarazmian calendar, in the decade after his homeland's old
dynasty had been overthrown — and he keeps having to say that he cannot recover
things. He gives the month names and the festivals and the era, and he explains
that the written material was destroyed and the men who could read the old
script were gone, so parts of it are irrecoverable.

That is a scholar marking the limits of his own evidence, a thousand years ago,
about his own country. He does not fill the gap with a plausible reconstruction.
He says what is missing and why, and stops.

Two things it leaves. The first is that most of what is known about pre-Islamic
Khwarazm — the calendar, the era, the script's existence — is known because one
man who happened to be from there wrote it down while it was going, and the
archaeology of the twentieth century has largely confirmed him.

The second is how rare that is. Archives get burned and replaced by travellers'
tales; conquests get written up by men on the conqueror's payroll; traditions
get compiled a century after the events by people with a case to make. What
almost never happens is this: the record is destroyed, and somebody who knew
what had been in it writes down what he can and marks the rest as lost.
