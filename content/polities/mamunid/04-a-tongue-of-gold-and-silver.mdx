The three years between the demand and the murder are recorded in unusual
detail, because Bayhaqi copied them out of al-Biruni's history of Khwarazm,
which is otherwise lost. The man who wrote down what could not be recovered
about the Khwarazmian calendar also wrote down, from inside the palace, how the
delta was talked out of its independence.

The demand was made deniably. In 1014 Mahmud's vizier, Ahmad ibn Hasan
Maymandi, remarked to the Khwarazmshah's envoy — as though on his own
initiative, and adding that the sultan knew nothing about it — that it would be
well if the Friday sermon in Khwarazm named the sultan. Ma'mun understood
perfectly that a vizier does not say such things on his own initiative, and
hesitated anyway. The vizier repeated it, less politely.

So Ma'mun called in his commanders and the leading men of the country, put the
request in front of them, and said he intended to agree, because otherwise he
and the country would be destroyed. They refused absolutely: they walked out of
the palace, unfurled the standards, drew their swords and stood in the open
cursing. To calm them the Khwarazmshah had to announce that no such request had
ever been made and that he had been testing their loyalty. It then fell to
al-Biruni — Barthold quotes the phrase, "with a tongue of gold and silver" — to
talk the nobles into apologising to their sovereign for their insolence.

The same adviser's next suggestion was to find allies. Two Karakhanid princes
were fighting each other near Uzgand; Ma'mun offered to mediate, made peace
between them in 1016, and concluded an alliance. Mahmud sent an embassy from
Balkh to say he was displeased. The Karakhanids answered blandly that they
regarded the Khwarazmshah as the sultan's friend and brother-in-law, that their
treaty with him was merely a supplement to their treaty with the sultan, and
that if there were any misunderstanding they would be glad to mediate that too.
No answer came. They told Ma'mun about the embassy; he proposed that they and he
send detachments into Khurasan from different directions to skirmish, on the
understanding that civilians were not to be touched and the whole thing was to
be understood as a means of restoring peace. They declined to provide troops and
repeated the offer to mediate, which he accepted. In the winter of 1016 Mahmud
received their envoys at Balkh and sent them home with the assurance that there
was no serious disagreement between himself and the Khwarazmshah, and that what
little there had been was now entirely removed.

The letter arrived immediately afterwards.

It is worth having at length, because it is the clearest statement anyone made
in that century of what a sultan thought a client ruler was. In the matter of the
sermon, it says, the Khwarazmshah submitted to our will, knowing how it might
otherwise turn out for him, but his people did not leave him a free hand. I do
not use the words *guards and subjects*, because men who are in a position to
tell a king do this and do not do that cannot be called guards or subjects. This
is plain weakness and an incapacity to rule; and such it was. Angered by these
people, I have stayed a long time here at Balkh and have collected a hundred
thousand horse and foot and five hundred elephants, to punish the traitors who
set themselves against their sovereign's will and put them back on the right
road. At the same time we shall rouse the amir, our brother and brother-in-law,
and show him how a kingdom is ruled; a weak amir is not fit for the work.

Three terms followed, and although the letter offered them as alternatives, the
Khwarazmshah read them — correctly — as a list. He was to introduce the sermon
in the sultan's name, willingly and in full obedience. He was to send money and
presents worthy of a sultan, which would afterwards be secretly returned, since
the sultan had no need of surplus money and his lands were already tottering
under the weight of gold and silver. And he was to send his nobles, imams and
jurists to petition for mercy.

Ma'mun complied with all of it. The sermon was changed first at Nasa and Farawa
in his Khurasanian territory, then in the other towns, and finally everywhere
except the two capitals, Kath and Gurganj. He sent eighty thousand dinars, three
thousand horses, and a party of shaykhs, qadis and notables.

His army was at Hazarasp at the time, under the chief chamberlain, watching the
hundred thousand men at Balkh. It did not march against them.
