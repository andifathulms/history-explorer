Krum ruled from about 803 to 814 and did three things that between them turned a
frontier khanate into a power.

He destroyed what was left of the Avar khaganate, in the same years Charlemagne
was destroying it from the other side — this corpus's Carolingian record has the
Frankish half, the ring in Pannonia and the treasure taken out of it. Bulgaria
took the eastern half and doubled in size, and the Avars, who had been the
dominant power in the middle Danube for two hundred and fifty years, stopped
existing. It is the only occasion in this corpus where two records converge on
the destruction of a third polity from opposite directions and neither of them
gets an edge for it, because the Avars have no page.

He issued a law. The Suda, a tenth-century Byzantine lexicon, preserves a
summary: penalties for false accusation, for theft, for giving shelter to a
thief, and an order that the vineyards be uprooted, which is either a
prohibition on drink or a scorched-earth measure depending on which reading you
take. It is the earliest legislation attested from this state and it is attested
by an enemy's dictionary, which is a fair summary of the evidence situation the
first chapter describes.

And he killed an emperor.

## 811

Nikephoros I marched on Pliska in 811 with a large army, took the capital,
sacked it, and turned for home through the mountains. Krum blocked the passes
with palisades and destroyed the army in the Varbitsa defile. The emperor was
killed, his son was carried away mortally wounded, and Krum had the skull lined
with silver and used it as a drinking cup.

Very few Roman or Byzantine emperors were killed in battle across the fifteen
hundred years the two records cover, and this corpus carries the other two as
turning points of their own: Valens at Adrianople in 378, on the Roman record,
and Constantine XI on the walls in 1453.

## What it settled

Not the war, which went on. What it settled is that the Balkan question could not
be closed by a campaign.

Byzantium had been trying since 681 — the treaty in this record's first chapter
was a concession made under exactly that pressure — and 811 established that a
Bulgarian state in the mountains could absorb the loss of its capital and destroy
the army that took it. The empire's answer for the next two centuries was
diplomacy, subsidy, mission and the patient work this corpus's Byzantine record
calls its most effective instrument, and it did not attempt a war of conquest
again until Basil II, two hundred years later.

Krum himself besieged Constantinople in 813 and died the following spring,
reportedly of a haemorrhage, while preparing another attempt. This record's
`military_basis` field reads `tribal-levy, conscript` and the second half of that
pairing is the reason the sieges failed: an army of Bulgar horse and Slav levies
could destroy a field army in a pass and had no way of assaulting the walls of
Constantinople, which nobody found a way through until 1204 and then only from
the water.
