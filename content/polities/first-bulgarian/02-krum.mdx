Krum ruled from about 803 to 814 and did three things that between them turned a
frontier khanate into a power.

He destroyed what was left of the Avar khaganate. Charlemagne was destroying it
from the other side in the same years — the Frankish armies took the fortified
ring in Pannonia in 795 and 796 and carried the accumulated Byzantine subsidies
of two and a half centuries west in ox-carts — and Bulgaria took the eastern
half and roughly doubled in size. The Avars had been the dominant power on the
middle Danube for two hundred and fifty years. Between the Franks and the Bulgars
they stopped existing inside a decade, and there is nobody left who wrote in
their language.

He issued a law. The Suda, a tenth-century Byzantine lexicon, preserves a
summary: penalties for false accusation, for theft, for giving shelter to a
thief, and an order that the vineyards be uprooted, which is either a
prohibition on drink or a scorched-earth measure depending on which reading you
take. It is the earliest legislation attested from this
state and it survives only because an enemy's dictionary happened to summarise
it, which is a fair description of how most of what is known about early Bulgaria
reaches us.

And he killed an emperor.

## 811

Nikephoros I marched on Pliska in 811 with a large army, took the capital,
sacked it, and turned for home through the mountains. Krum blocked the passes
with palisades and destroyed the army in the Varbitsa defile. The emperor was
killed, his son was carried away mortally wounded, and Krum had the skull lined
with silver and used it as a drinking cup.

Across roughly fifteen hundred years of Roman and Byzantine emperors, three died
at enemy hands in the field: Valens at Adrianople in 378, Nikephoros in the
Varbitsa pass in 811, and Constantine XI on the walls of his own city in 1453.
The middle one was killed by a Balkan khanate a hundred and thirty years old.

## What it settled

Not the war, which went on. What it settled is that the Balkan question could not
be closed by a campaign.

Byzantium had been trying since 681 — the treaty of that year was itself a
concession made under exactly this pressure — and 811 established that a
Bulgarian state in the mountains could absorb the loss of its capital and destroy
the army that took it. The empire's answer for the next two centuries was
diplomacy, subsidy and mission, which were the instruments it was best at, and it
did not attempt a war of conquest again until Basil II, two hundred years
later.

Krum himself besieged Constantinople in 813 and died the following spring,
reportedly of a haemorrhage, while preparing another attempt. The sieges failed for a
reason no Bulgarian ruler ever solved. An army of steppe cavalry and peasant
levies could destroy a field army in a mountain pass and had no means whatever of
assaulting the walls of Constantinople, which nobody got through until 1204, and
then only from the water.
