In 893 a council at Preslav moved the capital there from Pliska, made Slavonic
the language of church and state, and expelled the remaining Greek clergy.

The literary schools that followed — Preslav in the east under Naum, Ohrid in the
west under Clement — produced translations of the Greek fathers, original
Slavonic homilies and hagiography, a treatise on the alphabet by a monk called
Khrabr defending Slavonic letters against the argument that only Hebrew, Greek
and Latin were fit for scripture, and the script.

## Which alphabet

Two alphabets were involved and they are commonly confused, which matters,
because only one of them was made here and it is not the one named after the man
who came.

Cyril devised **Glagolitic** for the Moravian mission: a genuinely new alphabet,
not obviously derived from anything, invented to write Slavonic sounds that Greek
had no letters for. It came to Bulgaria with his pupils.

**Cyrillic** was developed afterwards, at Preslav, and it is a different object —
Greek uncial letters used for the sounds Greek had, plus a set of Glagolitic
letters kept for the sounds it did not. It is easier to write, it looks like
Greek, and it displaced Glagolitic within a century in the east while Glagolitic
survived in Croatia for another eight hundred years.

It is written today by something over two hundred and fifty million people, and
the name commemorates a man who did not make it.

## Whose alphabet it is

Cyrillic was made in Bulgaria, by scholars a Bulgarian ruler had rescued and
employed, in a programme whose whole purpose was to build a church
Constantinople could not run.

And its parent is Greek. That is not a quibble: the letters are Greek uncials,
used for the sounds Greek already had, with a handful of Glagolitic characters
kept for the sounds it did not. Anyone who can read Greek capitals can read half
of a Cyrillic page without being taught. The alphabet designed to make a church
independent of Constantinople looks, at a glance, exactly like Constantinople's.

That is the joke buried in the thing, and it was not an accident. Glagolitic —
Cyril's invention, owing nothing visible to any existing script — was the
genuinely independent alphabet, and Bulgaria had it, and Bulgaria dropped it.
What Preslav wanted was not a script that looked like nobody else's. It was a
script that looked like the prestige script of the Christian east, and could be
written quickly by a scribe trained in Greek, and carried a Slavonic liturgy.

Glagolitic survived where the pressure was different. In Croatia, on the
Adriatic, in the shadow of Latin rather than of Greek, priests went on writing it
for another eight hundred years, precisely because it did *not* look like the
alphabet of the power next door.

Two alphabets for one language, and each of them survived in the place where
resembling the neighbour was the more useful option.

## What went out of Preslav

Bulgaria did not invent Slavonic Christianity. It is the reason Serbia and the
Rus have it.

What went out of Preslav and Ohrid was a complete package — a liturgy, a
scriptural corpus, a body of law, a hagiography and an alphabet, all in a
language a Slav speaker could follow. Serbia took it. The Rus took it: the
conversion of about 988 was supplied from Bulgaria, in Bulgarian books, by clergy
trained in Bulgarian schools, and the literary language of Russia until the
eighteenth century descends from what was written at Preslav.

Obolensky's term for the result is the Byzantine commonwealth, and his point is
that Byzantium supplied the model and never controlled the thing. Bulgaria is
where the model stopped being Byzantine.
