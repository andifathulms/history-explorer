In the summer of 968 the ambassador of the Holy Roman Emperor sat down to dinner
in Constantinople and found a Bulgarian placed above him. He wrote a report about
it that is one of the angriest documents of the tenth century, and in the course
of being furious he recorded exactly what Bulgaria's standing was.

## The seating

Liudprand of Cremona had come to negotiate a marriage between Otto I's son and a
Byzantine princess, and the embassy was going very badly. He was housed in what
he calls a detestable dwelling, kept under guard, and argued with at length. Then
he was invited to the imperial table for the feast of the Apostles:

> I found placed above me on my side of the long narrow board the Bulgarian
> envoy. He was a fellow with his hair cut in Hungarian fashion, girt about with
> a brazen chain, and, as I fancy, just admitted into the Christian faith: the
> preference given to him over me was plainly meant as an insult to you, my
> august masters.

He left the table. Two court officials came after him — the emperor's brother,
who was marshal of the court, and the chief secretary — and explained, in words
Liudprand reproduces because he thought they proved his case:

> When Peter king of the Bulgarians married Christopher's daughter, a mutual
> agreement was sworn to on both sides, to the effect that envoys of the
> Bulgarians should with us be preferred, honoured and esteemed above the envoys
> of all other nations. What you say is true: the Bulgarian envoy over there has
> his hair cut short, he has not washed himself, and his girdle consists of a
> brass chain. But nevertheless he is a patrician and we are definitely of
> opinion that it would be wrong to give a bishop, especially a Frankish bishop,
> the preference over him.

They then refused to let him go back to his lodgings and made him eat with the
emperor's servants at an inn.

## What that was worth

Liudprand thought he was recording an outrage. What he recorded is a treaty
provision.

The marriage is the peace of 927 — Peter's, to the granddaughter of Romanos
Lekapenos, which bought Bulgaria the title of tsar and its own patriarch. What
Liudprand's evening adds is that the settlement had a protocol clause nobody else
records, and that forty-one years later it was still being enforced at the
imperial table, against the ambassador of the western empire, by officials who
regarded his objections as beside the point.

A treaty term about seating is not a small thing. Precedence at that court was
the public statement of where every foreign power stood, and Bulgaria had
negotiated first place in it and kept first place in it for two generations.

Liudprand's own contempt is the best evidence of how binding it was. The
Byzantines agreed with him about the man's hair, his washing and his belt. They
seated him above the Frankish bishop anyway, because he was a patrician of the
empire and the agreement said so.

## The rest of the evening

The emperor, having been told Liudprand valued his table, sent a consolation
dish: a fat goat which he had eaten from himself, stuffed with garlic, onion and
leeks, and swimming in fish sauce. Liudprand wished his masters could have seen
it, being sure the sight would have banished any doubts they had about Byzantine
luxury.

Eight days later, when the Bulgarians had left the city, he was made to dine at
the same table again.

## The date

The passage is worth dating precisely, because of what was about to happen.

Liudprand was in Constantinople in June 968. In that same year the emperor whose
officials had insisted on the seating paid Sviatoslav of Kiev to come down the
Danube and attack Bulgaria. Peter died the following January, and what the Rus
began the Byzantines finished themselves.

So the last recorded occasion on which the peace of 927 was honoured is a
bad-tempered Italian bishop being placed below an unwashed Bulgarian at a feast,
in the months between the decision to destroy the kingdom and the arrival of the
army hired to do it.
