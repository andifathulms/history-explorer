In 680 the emperor Constantine IV took an army and a fleet north to deal with a
group of Bulgars who had crossed the Danube into imperial territory. The
campaign failed; the emperor left, the army panicked, and the Bulgars followed
them south. In 681 Byzantium signed a treaty recognising the new state and
agreeing to pay it an annual subsidy.

The manner of it matters. This is not a migration into a vacuum. It is a
province of the eastern Roman empire taken by force and then conceded by treaty,
some forty-five years after the Arab conquests had stripped the empire of Syria
and Egypt, by a state that could not fight on two frontiers and knew it.

## Two populations

The Bulgars were a Turkic group out of the steppe north of the Black Sea, with
the cavalry and the political vocabulary of one: a khan, a clan aristocracy, and
the institutions of the western steppe.

The population they came to rule was Slav, agricultural, and had been settling
across the Balkans for a century as imperial control receded.

The army was both of them: Bulgar horse and Slav levy. So was the state's
central problem for two centuries, which was that a Turkic cavalry aristocracy
and a Slav peasantry had no reason to think of themselves as one people. It was
eventually solved by converting, in a language the Slavs spoke, which dissolved a
distinction that neither war nor administration had.

## The subsidy

The state's second-largest source of income was the empire it had taken a
province from.

Byzantium paid Bulgaria an annual subsidy under a series of treaties from 681
onward. Withholding it is the stated cause of at least two wars, including the
one in which Nikephoros I lost his army and his skull. From Constantinople's side
this was ordinary business: a rich state paying people to go away is cheaper than
a poor state fighting them, and the empire did it wherever it could. From this
side it was structural. A polity founded on a treaty and funded partly by an
annuity from its principal enemy will spend two centuries in a peculiar
relationship with that enemy, and it did.

## What the sources are

Almost everything written about Bulgaria for its first two centuries was written
by Byzantines: by the enemy, in Greek, mostly after the fact. Fine is careful
throughout about the difference between what they say and what can be
established, and the difference is large.

The state itself is not in doubt. The interior of it is. There are Bulgar
inscriptions — a set of stone texts in Greek letters recording treaties, building
works and lists of officials, which are the only substantial thing the state
wrote about itself before the conversion — and after 893 there is a literature.
The two centuries between are Byzantine chronicles and archaeology.

It is why nobody can say how the throne was supposed to pass. The eighth century
is a sequence of khans deposed and killed, recorded by outsiders who did not care
why, and no source anywhere states a rule about who was eligible. A pattern of
violence is not a constitution, and it should not be read as one.
