title: A province taken

In 680 the emperor Constantine IV took an army and a fleet north to deal with a
group of Bulgars who had crossed the Danube into imperial territory. The
campaign failed; the emperor left, the army panicked, and the Bulgars followed
them south. In 681 Byzantium signed a treaty recognising the new state and
agreeing to pay it an annual subsidy.

That is where this record begins, and the manner of it matters. This is not a
migration into a vacuum. It is a province of the eastern Roman empire taken by
force and then conceded by treaty, forty-five years after the Arab conquests
this corpus's Byzantine record's fourth chapter describes, by an empire that
could not fight on two frontiers.

## Two populations

The Bulgars were a Turkic group out of the steppe north of the Black Sea, with
the cavalry and the political vocabulary of one — a khan, a clan aristocracy, and
a set of practices this corpus's Khazar and Gokturk records will look familiar
to.

The population they came to rule was Slav, agricultural, and had been settling
across the Balkans for a century as imperial control receded.

This record's `military_basis` reads `tribal-levy, conscript` and that is the
two of them: Bulgar horse and Slav levy. It is also the state's central problem
for two centuries, and the second chapter is about the man who solved it by
dissolving the distinction.

## The subsidy

`revenue_basis` reads `land-tax, tribute`, and the second value is the unusual
one because of who was paying whom.

Byzantium paid Bulgaria an annual subsidy under a series of treaties from 681
onward. Withholding it is the stated cause of at least two wars, including the
one in which Nikephoros I lost his army and his skull. This corpus's Byzantine
record's first chapter argues that a rich state paying people to go away is not
weaker than a poor state fighting them, and this record is the other end of that
transaction — a state whose second-largest source of income was the empire it
had taken a province from.

## What the sources are

Almost everything written about this record for its first two centuries was
written by Byzantines, and Fine — this record's source, and chosen for it — is
careful throughout about the difference between what they say and what can be
established.

That produces an evidentiary situation this corpus keeps meeting. The Nasrid
record in the Eastern Mediterranean is described almost entirely by the Sasanian
enemy's neighbours; the Median record's own page says it is disputed whether
there was a state; the Mitanni record has one coded institution because nobody
has found its capital.

Here the state is not in doubt and the interior of it is. There are Bulgar
inscriptions — a set of stone texts in Greek letters recording treaties, building
works and lists of officials, which are the only substantial thing the state
wrote about itself before the conversion — and after 893 there is a literature.
The two centuries between are Byzantine chronicles and archaeology.

That is why this record's `succession_rule` is null. The eighth century is a
sequence of khans deposed and killed, recorded by outsiders who did not care why,
and no source states a rule about who was eligible. A pattern of violence is not
a constitution, and coding rule 7 will not take one for the other.
