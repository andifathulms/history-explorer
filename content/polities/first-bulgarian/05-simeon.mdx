Simeon had been sent to Constantinople as a boy and educated there, in Greek, at
the imperial school. The Byzantines called him *hemiargos* — half-Greek — and
meant it as a description rather than an insult. He had been intended for the
church and came home when his brother was deposed to take a throne instead.

He spent thirty-four years at war with the empire that educated him, and what he
wanted from it was not territory.

## The title

Simeon wanted to be emperor, in Constantinople, crowned by the patriarch. Not an
emperor of Bulgaria: the title he used was *basileus of the Bulgarians and the
Romans*, and the seals survive with it on them.

That is a claim on the Byzantine imperial office itself, and it is the earliest
of the three serious ones. The Latin emperors took the title with the ceremonial
in 1204 and Mehmed II took it with the city in 1453. Simeon took it with
neither. He never held Constantinople and was never crowned in it, and he
organised thirty years of foreign policy around being the man who would be —
which is the point worth holding onto about the whole business. What made the
office worth claiming was not that it could be won. It was that everybody in the
eastern Mediterranean agreed it was the office.

## Why he could not take it

He beat Byzantine field armies repeatedly — at Bulgarophygon in 896, at
Acheloos in 917, which was one of the worst defeats the empire suffered in the
period — and reached the walls of Constantinople more than once. In 913 the
regency brought him inside the city, the patriarch put something on his head that
the Bulgarian sources call a crown and the Greek sources call a headpiece, and
the arrangement was repudiated within months.

The walls are the answer, and they always were. A land army without a fleet
cannot take Constantinople. Simeon understood this perfectly and sent to the
Fatimids in 922 to hire one; the embassy was intercepted at sea by the
Byzantines, who then bought the Fatimids off. Bulgaria had cavalry and it had
levies and it never had ships, and that single absence is the exact limit of what
the state could achieve in three hundred and thirty-seven years.

## 927

Simeon died in 927 with the war unwon and his son Peter made peace within months.

The terms gave Bulgaria almost everything the thirty years had been about,
except the throne: Byzantium
recognised the title of *tsar* — the Slavonic form of caesar, and a rank below
basileus but above every other ruler Constantinople dealt with — recognised an
autocephalous Bulgarian patriarchate, and sent a princess.

It is the high point of the state and it is the end of its expansion. Peter
reigned forty-two years in peace and the sources, which are Byzantine and hostile
and later Bulgarian and nostalgic, agree on almost nothing about them except that
nothing much happened.

What was happening underneath those forty-two quiet years is the one thing this
state produced that outlived it by four centuries and reached the south of
France.
