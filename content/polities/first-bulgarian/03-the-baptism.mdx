Boris was baptised in 864 with the Byzantine emperor standing as his godfather,
and then spent four years trying to get out of the consequences.

The problem was not the faith. It was that a church is an administration, and an
archbishop appointed from Constantinople is a Byzantine official inside a
Bulgarian state. Boris understood this immediately and asked for a patriarch of
his own. Constantinople refused. He wrote to Rome instead.

## The hundred and six questions

What survives from that manoeuvre is one of the more extraordinary documents in
this corpus's medieval half. In 866 Boris sent Pope Nicholas I a list of
questions about how to be a Christian country, and the papal reply — the
*Responsa ad consulta Bulgarorum*, a hundred and six answers — survives entire.

They are not theology. They are a new state asking what it is now allowed to do.
May we go on wearing trousers. May we eat before communion. What do we do about
our custom of a man having several wives. May we continue to punish a soldier
who is unready for battle. What should be done with the ancestral stone we swear
by. May we still consult a book of divination before a battle. What happens to
our ancestors, who died unbaptised.

Nicholas answered all of them, at length, and mostly with unexpected latitude —
trousers were a matter of indifference, the stone was not — and the tone
throughout is of a man who knows he is competing for a kingdom and does not want
to lose it over clothing.

## What Boris got

He played the two churches against each other for four years and got, in 870, a
Bulgarian archbishopric under Constantinople with a great deal of autonomy — less
than the patriarchate he wanted and considerably more than the Greek bishopric he
had been offered.

Then in 886 the pupils of Cyril and Methodius arrived. The Moravian mission this
corpus's Byzantine record's seventh chapter describes had been broken up by
Frankish clergy, and its survivors were sold as slaves or expelled; Boris took
them in, gave them land and pupils, and set them to work at Preslav and at Ohrid.

That is the decision this record turns on, and the next chapter is what came of
it. A Bulgarian church in Greek would have been a Byzantine institution in
Bulgaria. A Bulgarian church in a language the population spoke was something no
empire had a mechanism for, and Obolensky's argument — that what Byzantium built
in eastern Europe was a commonwealth held together by liturgy and script rather
than by authority — begins in the gap between those two facts.

## Abdication, and the return

Boris retired to a monastery in 889 and left the throne to his eldest son
Vladimir, who spent four years trying to reverse the conversion and restore the
old cult.

Boris came out of the monastery, deposed him, blinded him, put his younger son
Simeon on the throne, and went back. Nothing in this corpus's `succession_rule`
vocabulary describes that, and this record's field is null, which the data
comment attributes to the absence of any stated rule rather than to the presence
of episodes like this one. Both are true. A state in which a retired monk can
depose and blind a reigning khan and install a different son has not written
down who inherits, and the reason it has not is not an accident of survival.
