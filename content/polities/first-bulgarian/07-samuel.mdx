In 971 John Tzimiskes took Preslav, captured the tsar Boris II, brought him to
Constantinople and had him publicly stripped of the imperial insignia in a
ceremony designed to end the Bulgarian empire. The eastern half of the state was
annexed and the patriarchate was suppressed.

That should have been the ending, and this record runs another forty-seven years,
because the western half did not accept it.

## The comitopuli

Four brothers, the sons of a provincial governor, held the mountains of Macedonia
and went on holding them. Three of them died over the following decade and the
fourth, Samuel, ruled until 1014 — first in somebody else's name and then in his
own, with a patriarch he re-established, a capital at Ohrid, and a state running
from the Adriatic to the Black Sea at its widest.

Whether this is the same polity is a real question and this record answers it in
the affirmative, following Fine. Samuel used the imperial title, restored the
patriarchate, was recognised as tsar by a pope, and was understood by
Constantinople to be the Bulgarian empire rather than a rebellion inside it.
The Bulgarian tradition treats the continuity as unbroken; some Byzantine and
some modern accounts treat the western state as a different object.

The `capitals` field on this record carries the move — Pliska, Preslav, Ohrid —
and the third entry is that argument in a data field.

## Forty years

Basil II came to the throne in 976 and spent essentially his entire adult reign
on Bulgaria. Not in one campaign: in annual expeditions across four decades,
taking a fortress at a time, buying the local aristocracy, and returning the next
year to do it again. This corpus's Byzantine record's chapter on him describes the
method as attrition rather than battle, and it is the least dramatic and most
effective thing any emperor in that record does.

Samuel fought it competently for most of it and won some of it — he destroyed a
Byzantine army at the Gates of Trajan in 986, in a pass, in the manner of Krum a
hundred and seventy-five years earlier, and Basil escaped with difficulty.

## Kleidion

In 1014, at Kleidion, Basil trapped the Bulgarian army in a defile and destroyed
it.

The Byzantine account says he blinded the prisoners — fourteen thousand of them —
and left one man in a hundred with a single eye to lead the rest back to Samuel,
who is said to have died of the shock two days later.

This record marks the turning point `contested: true`, and the reason is on the
last chapter rather than here, because it is a statement about the evidence and
not about the war. What belongs here is what the war was.

Four decades of annual campaigning by the richest state in the Mediterranean
against a mountain polity that could not be reached quickly and would not stop.
Basil never married, had no children, and left no dynasty; what he had instead
was Bulgaria, and this corpus's Byzantine record notes that the empire's
territorial peak and its succession crisis arrive in the same decade because the
man who produced the first spent the years that would have produced the second in
Macedonia.

Bulgaria surrendered in 1018 and the empire had a Balkan frontier on the Danube
for the first time in three hundred and thirty-seven years. Fifty-three years
later it lost Anatolia at Manzikert, and this site's Great Seljuk record carries
that. The two facts are usually told separately.
