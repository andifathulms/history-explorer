Some time in the reign of Peter, in the middle of the tenth century, a priest
called Bogomil began preaching in Bulgaria that the visible world was made by
the devil.

The doctrine that grew from it is dualist: two principles, a good God who made
souls and an evil one who made matter, with the material world — bodies,
buildings, property, the cross, the sacraments, the church itself — belonging to
the second. Its practical programme followed: no churches, no icons, no priests
beyond the initiated, no baptism by water, and a general refusal of the
authority of anyone whose authority rested on things.

A presbyter named Cosmas wrote a treatise against them which is the main source,
and his complaint is revealing. The Bogomils, he says, teach their followers not
to obey their lords, revile the wealthy, hate the tsar, ridicule the elders,
condemn the boyars, regard those who serve the emperor as loathsome, and forbid
every serf to work for his master.

## It went a long way

Bulgaria persecuted it. Byzantium persecuted it — Alexios I had the Bogomil
leader Basil burned in the hippodrome around 1100, and Anna Komnene describes it.

It spread anyway: through Byzantine Thrace and Anatolia, into Serbia, into Bosnia
where the local church was accused of it for two centuries, across the Adriatic
into northern Italy, and into Languedoc, where the Cathars held a recognisably
related doctrine and where the Albigensian Crusade was launched to destroy them
in 1209.

How direct that transmission was is argued about and the argument is not settled.
What is not in doubt is that a set of ideas that begins in tenth-century Bulgaria
is being burned for in southern France two hundred and fifty years later.

## What it was for

Cosmas's list is the useful part, because it says what the doctrine was doing
rather than what it taught.

A tenth-century Bulgarian peasant had a landlord, a tax collector, a priest and a
tsar, and a body of teaching arrived which held that the visible world and
everything commanding obedience in it belonged to the devil. Refusing the church
followed from the theology; refusing the boyar followed from the same premise
without any further argument being needed. That is why it kept spreading through
persecution, and why every state it reached persecuted it. It was a complete
answer to authority that did not require an army.

The state's own position is worth being clear about. Bulgaria did not export
this. Bulgaria hunted it, Byzantium hunted it, Serbia hunted it, and it travelled
anyway — along trade routes, in the baggage of refugees, through exactly the
channels no government controlled. It is the one thing a polity cannot take
credit for and cannot be blamed for either: an idea that got out.

## The reach of it

Two hundred and fifty years after a priest in the reign of Peter began preaching
in a Balkan village, the pope was raising an army to destroy people in Languedoc
who believed something very like it, and the Cathar perfecti of Toulouse and
Albi were refusing the sacraments on the same grounds Cosmas had complained
about.

Whether there is a continuous line between them is argued about and has not been
settled. The resemblance is close and the geography is plausible; a shared
doctrine is not the same as a documented transmission, and the case for one has
been made and contested for a century.

What is not in doubt is that the most consequential thing this state produced was
something it spent a century trying to suppress.
