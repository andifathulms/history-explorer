Some time in the reign of Peter, in the middle of the tenth century, a priest
called Bogomil began preaching in Bulgaria that the visible world was made by
the devil.

The doctrine that grew from it is dualist: two principles, a good God who made
souls and an evil one who made matter, with the material world — bodies,
buildings, property, the cross, the sacraments, the church itself — belonging to
the second. Its practical programme followed: no churches, no icons, no priests
beyond the initiated, no baptism by water, and a general refusal of the
authority of anyone whose authority rested on things.

A presbyter named Cosmas wrote a treatise against them which is the main source,
and his complaint is revealing. The Bogomils, he says, teach their followers not
to obey their lords, revile the wealthy, hate the tsar, ridicule the elders,
condemn the boyars, regard those who serve the emperor as loathsome, and forbid
every serf to work for his master.

## It went a long way

Bulgaria persecuted it. Byzantium persecuted it — Alexios I had the Bogomil
leader Basil burned in the hippodrome around 1100, and Anna Komnene describes it.

It spread anyway: through Byzantine Thrace and Anatolia, into Serbia, into Bosnia
where the local church was accused of it for two centuries, across the Adriatic
into northern Italy, and into Languedoc, where the Cathars held a recognisably
related doctrine and where the Albigensian Crusade was launched to destroy them
in 1209.

How direct that transmission was is argued about and the argument is not settled.
What is not in doubt is that a set of ideas that begins in tenth-century Bulgaria
is being burned for in southern France two hundred and fifty years later.

## And this record does not count it

`religions_carried` on this page is 1 and the item is Orthodox Christianity in
Slavonic. Bogomilism is not the second item and cannot be.

Coding rule 2 counts propagation *by the polity* — mission, endowment, patronised
conversion, or conquest followed by institution-building, with a source
connecting the polity to the spread. What happened here is the opposite: the
state persecuted the movement continuously, and the movement spread through
exactly the channels the state did not control.

That is a real distinction and not a technicality. The rule exists to measure
what a polity's power did to everywhere else, and a heresy travelling along trade
routes and refugee paths in spite of the state is not that. If it were counted,
the axis would stop measuring anything specific — every polity would score for
whatever was invented within its borders and got out.

So the most durable idea this state produced is a paragraph, and the count stands
at one for the thing the state actually did.

## The pattern this page belongs to

This corpus keeps arriving here. The Nasrid record at al-Hira has the pre-Islamic
Arabic poetic canon assembled at a court maintained as a military convenience,
and no field for it. The Venetian record has double-entry bookkeeping, marine
insurance and quarantine, and three influence counts of zero. The Norman Sicilian
record has a trilingual chancery and scores nothing. The Attalid record has a
library and a near miss on parchment.

The rules are narrow on purpose, because a count that can absorb *was culturally
important* will absorb anything and stop being comparable across a hundred and
ninety records. What the site does instead is put the thing in a chapter and say
plainly that the fields do not hold it — which is this paragraph, and the fourth
or fifth time it has been written, and it is worth writing every time rather than
letting a reader conclude the site did not notice.
