Basil II spent the years from 976 to 1018 destroying Bulgaria, and he did it
almost annually, by attrition: a fortress a season, the local aristocracy bought
where it could be bought, and a return the following year to do it again. Very
little of it makes a good story. All of it worked.

The state he was destroying was not the one Simeon had ruled. The eastern half
had been annexed in 971 and the imperial insignia publicly stripped off its tsar
in Constantinople; what Basil spent forty years on was the western half, run from
Ohrid by a family of provincial governors who had declined to accept that the
empire was over.

## Kleidion

In 1014 Basil trapped a Bulgarian army in a mountain pass at Kleidion and
destroyed it. According to the Byzantine account he then blinded the prisoners —
some fourteen thousand of them — leaving one man in a hundred with a single eye
to lead the rest home, and Samuel died two days after they arrived.

<Figure id="fb-samdeath" />

That story needs handling, and not out of squeamishness.

The figure is unverifiable and comes from a chronicle written a century after the
event. The epithet Basil is universally known by — *Boulgaroktonos*, the
Bulgar-slayer — is not contemporary either: it first appears in the twelfth
century, well after he was dead, and it was then taken up with enthusiasm by
nineteenth-century Greek nationalism and, from the opposite direction and for
opposite purposes, by Bulgarian.

So the single most famous fact about this war is a number nobody can check,
attached to a nickname nobody used at the time, kept alive because two modern
nationalisms both had a use for it. What is not in doubt is that a battle was
fought at Kleidion, that the Bulgarian army was destroyed in it, that Samuel died
within months, and that the state surrendered four years later.

Something atrocious happened in that pass. The specific atrocity everyone can
name may be a later century's contribution.

## The surrender

Ivan Vladislav was killed outside Dyrrachium in 1018 and the resistance ended
without a final battle. Basil went through Bulgaria receiving submissions,
Samuel's widow and family came in, and the aristocracy was given Byzantine titles
and taken to Constantinople.

<Figure id="fb-sarc" />

Three hundred and thirty-seven years after Constantine IV conceded a province he
could not hold, the empire had its Danube frontier back.

It had taken the whole adult reign of its most capable emperor to get it, and it
kept it for a hundred and sixty-seven years.
