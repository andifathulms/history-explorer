Goguryeo's reigning king died in 392 and his son Tam-dok took the
throne with none of his predecessors' old agreements considered
binding any longer. He spent the next twenty years doing what no
earlier Goguryeo king had managed for long: winning decisively on
every frontier at once.

<Figure id="gg-stele" />

## A fortress taken by seven paths

Tam-dok opened his reign by attacking Baekje outright, seizing ten of
its towns before turning north to punish the Khitan tribes and then
swinging back south again for the fortress of Kwang-nu - an
almost unapproachable stronghold on a sea-girt rock. Goguryeo's
soldiers besieged it for twenty days before finding seven separate
paths up the cliffs, then stormed it by simultaneous assault from all
seven at once. When word of the fortress's fall reached the Baekje
court, resistance in the field was abandoned outright; Baekje's king
could do nothing but bar his own capital's gates and wait.

## A decree that made a kingdom Buddhist

Tam-dok was as devoted to Buddhism as his father had been - Goguryeo
had formally adopted the religion as a government undertaking twenty
years earlier, in 372 - and he pushed the commitment further than any
king before him, decreeing that all the people of Goguryeo should
adopt the Buddhist faith and building nine more monasteries in the
capital at P'yŭng-yang in the years that followed. Between the
building program and the military campaigns, his reign fixed both the
kingdom's religious character and its territorial reach for the
generation after him.

## A frontier drawn seven hundred li wide

Not every year of the reign went Goguryeo's way. In 399 the northern
kingdom of Yŭn, suspicious of Goguryeo's growing strength, sent an army
of thirty thousand men that seized the frontier fortresses of Sin-sŭng
and Nam-so and pushed the border back by some seven hundred li,
carrying off thousands of households as captives in the process. It
was a serious loss, but not a fatal one - Goguryeo absorbed it without
the kind of collapse that had followed earlier defeats, a measure of
how much stronger the kingdom Tam-dok commanded had already become
compared to the one his father had left him.

## A son invested by the emperor he had once defied

Tam-dok died in 413, after twenty-one years on the throne, and was
succeeded by his son Kö-ryŭn. Where Tam-dok had built his reign on
independent military success, Kö-ryŭn opened his by formally accepting
investiture from the Chinese emperor - a symbolic submission that
nonetheless kept the peace between Goguryeo and its powerful neighbor
for as long as it was observed, and the same courtesy China extended to
Baekje's own king two years later.
