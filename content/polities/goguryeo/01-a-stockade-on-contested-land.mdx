Jumong, the archer-son whose own flight from Buyeo this region's Buyeo
record follows in detail, gathered new followers as he travelled south
and settled at last in Jol-bon, where in 37 BC he built a capital
enclosed by a heavy stockade, with storehouses and a prison to match. He
named his new kingdom Goguryeo, joining his own family name to that of
a mountain in the homeland he had left behind.

## A kingdom built on land China had already claimed

The territory Jumong settled belonged, at least on paper, to China: it
formed part of a province the Han emperor had established more than
forty years earlier. Rather than seize it all at once, Jumong's young
kingdom absorbed the province gradually, and China - which held these
frontier lands only loosely - appears to have tolerated a native ruler
capable of keeping the peninsula's wilder inhabitants in check, so long
as he continued to acknowledge Chinese suzerainty in form if not always
in substance.

## Five clans, one throne

Goguryeo's own people traced their origin to five distinct groups - the
So-ro-bu, Chŭl-lo-bu, Sun-no-bu, Kwan-no-bu and Kye-ro-bu - of whom the
So-ro-bu supplied the earliest kings before the Kye-ro-bu line took
over the throne in later generations. Jumong himself secured his own
position peacefully rather than by further conquest, marrying the
daughter of Jol-bon's own ruling family and leaving the existing social
order largely undisturbed once he held it.

## A frugal people with a violent code

The kingdom's early customs, as later observers recorded them, combined
severity with ceremony: thieves paid twelvefold restitution, ordinary
criminals were executed on the spot with their families enslaved, and
the death of a great man was sometimes marked by burying servants alive
alongside him. Within two years of his own founding, Jumong turned this
same disciplined society outward, beginning in 35 BC a series of
conquests that would grow his stockaded capital into a kingdom capable
of resisting the full weight of Chinese power for the next seven
centuries.
