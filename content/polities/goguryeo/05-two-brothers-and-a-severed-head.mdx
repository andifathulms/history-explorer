Yeon Gaesomun - a man of formidable build and reputation, said by
tradition to wear five swords at once and to ride over the prostrated
bodies of his own subjects in state - assassinated Goguryeo's reigning
king in 642, cutting the body in two and installing a compliant nephew,
Chang, in his place. He first invited to a feast every official he
suspected of plotting against him and killed them there before turning
on the king himself, securing for his own family the real power behind
a throne that would answer to no one else for the following quarter
century.

## An iron chancellor's death opens the field

For years Yeon Gaesomun's own force of will held Goguryeo's defenses
together against renewed pressure from Tang China. When he finally died
of natural causes, the kingdom lost the one figure capable of directing
its war effort - and inherited, in his place, two sons who shared their
father's ferocity without any of his restraint. Nam-seong, the elder,
assumed his father's office, only to have his younger brother Nam-geun
seize it while he was away on other business.

## A brother's defection opens the gates

Nam-seong fled to the Yalu River and, rather than accept his own
displacement, placed himself at the head of the border tribes under his
command and defected outright to the Tang emperor, who made him
governor-general of Liao-tung and set him to work turning his own
former countrymen's tribal allies into instruments of Chinese revenge.
The defection cost Goguryeo its one great frontier ally and handed
China an implacable new enemy who knew the kingdom's own defenses
intimately.

## A month-long siege, a suicide, and twenty thousand captives

Combined Tang and Silla forces converged on P'yongyang, and after a
month of siege the king's own officials came out under a flag of truce
to negotiate surrender. Nam-geun, knowing what fate awaited the man
responsible for his father's legacy crumbling, made one final attempt
to break the besieging army by force; when it failed, he cut his own
throat rather than be taken. The victorious Chinese general carried the
king, his two sons, a number of officials, many of Nam-geun's own
relatives and a large company of P'yongyang's own residents back to
China - twenty thousand captives in total, closing a kingdom that had
held the frontier against China for seven hundred and five years under
twenty-eight kings.
