Luwu's own account of its origins belongs to the La Galigo cycle, the
vast body of Bugis oral literature centered on the hero Sawerigading.
In the tradition, a prince of the Upper World named Batara Guru
descends to marry a princess of the Lower World, and their
descendants become the ruling line of Luwu. Sawerigading, their most
celebrated descendant, conquers a rival chiefdom called Cina to win
the hand of his own cousin, Wé Cudai; the daughter of that marriage
and the son of Sawerigading's twin sister become, in turn, the couple
whose union founds Luwu's kingship, while every other figure in the
story returns to the Upper or Lower World from which they came.

## A first king with a name that means "Lion Man"

A separate but related text, the Lontara Simpurasia, names Luwu's
actual first ruler as Simpurasia - a name drawn from Sanskrit meaning
"Lion Man." Several Bugis genealogical traditions treat Simpurasia as
an archetypal founder-figure more broadly, but the same analysis that
identifies him this way also identifies him as a character out of the
La Galigo cycle rather than an independently attested historical
person. The two traditions, in other words, are not really separate
evidence for the same events; they are versions of the same
foundational story serving the same purpose.

## Myth as a political argument

That purpose becomes clearer once it is set beside how other Bugis
chiefdoms told their own origins. Most of them trace their first
rulers to a tomanurung, a figure who descends directly from heaven to
found a particular kingdom - a founding moment placed, in the logic of
Bugis political tradition, later and more historically grounded than
the La Galigo age. By claiming descent from the La Galigo world
instead, Luwu's own elite reached further back than any rival could
match, asserting a primordial legitimacy no tomanurung-founded
neighbor could claim for itself. Whether or not the underlying stories
preserve any genuine memory of real events, the political use to which
Luwu put them is itself a real and traceable historical fact - and,
fittingly, one that grew in importance precisely as the kingdom's own
actual territory contracted.
