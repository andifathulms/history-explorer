Luwu's own economy ran on a partnership between two very different
landscapes: the iron-bearing highlands around Lake Matano and the
Rongkong hills, and the sago-swamp lowlands that fed the population
without needing the rice paddies its southern Bugis neighbors relied
on. Archaeological survey has traced iron smelting at sites like Nuha
and Sukoyu on Matano's own shores back to the early second millennium,
with charcoal dates placing the earliest confirmed smelting debris
between roughly 1000 and 1250 - centuries before Luwu had matured into
the chiefdom its own political rise represents.

## Iron, gum and hardwood moving downstream

Iron was never Luwu's only export. Forest produce collected in the
highlands and carried down to the coast alongside it included damar
resin, rattan, and fine timbers such as gharu and ebony - a bundle of
goods that made the same river network serve multiple trades at once
rather than depending on iron alone. Fragments of iron ore found well
away from any known smelting site suggest the raw material itself
moved as cargo before ever being worked, implying collecting points
along the trade routes handling unprocessed ore alongside finished
tools and weapons.

## Beads and coins from very far away

What came back upriver in exchange tells its own story about how far
Luwu's own trade network actually reached. Glass beads appear in the
archaeological record as early as the late first millennium, well
before Luwu's own political emergence, and single Chinese cash coins
turn up at more than one site - small, portable evidence of a trade
network stretching, indirectly, all the way to a Chinese economy that
Luwu's own inhabitants likely never dealt with face to face. A kingdom
remembered today mainly for a legendary poetic cycle and a slow
political decline was, for centuries before either of those defined
it, simply a very effective conduit between a highland ore body and
the wider trading world of the archipelago.
