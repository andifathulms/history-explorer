Cremation, not burial in the ground, was the dominant way Luwu's own
pre-Islamic population treated its dead - a practice shared with the
Bugis more generally, though the archaeological record suggests Luwu
had likely adopted the custom well before it spread more widely. The
cemeteries at Malangke, the pre-Islamic palace center itself, show
cremation sites dating from as early as the thirteenth or fourteenth
century through to the sixteenth, running in parallel with the
kingdom's own political rise and its slow territorial contraction.

## A coastal community that buried its dead differently

Not every community within Luwu's own orbit treated the dead the same
way. At a site called Tampinna, inhumation - burial of the body whole,
rather than cremation - was the only mode found, a pattern the
excavators associate with the Bajo, a seafaring people distinct from
the Bugis population that dominated the interior settlements. The
difference in burial custom marks a real cultural boundary within
Luwu's own territory, between an agrarian and administrative Bugis
population centered on Malangke and a maritime Bajo community whose
own customs the wider kingdom did not simply absorb or erase.

## Islam arriving in the grave goods before it arrived in name

The clearest evidence of religious change comes not from any written
conversion narrative but from the graves themselves. At burial sites
east of Tampinna, grave markers carved in shapes drawn directly from
Hindu religious symbolism - forms recognizable as yoni and linga -
appear alongside burial goods datable to the seventeenth and
eighteenth centuries, well after Luwu's own official conversion to
Islam in 1605. The dead, in other words, were still being marked with
older religious imagery generations after the kingdom's rulers had
formally adopted a new faith - a reminder that an official conversion
recorded in a chronicle and an actual community's religious practice
can move at very different speeds, and that Luwu's own countryside
carried older forms long after the court had moved on from them.
