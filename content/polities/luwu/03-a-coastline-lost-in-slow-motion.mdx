By the fifteenth century, Luwu's own reach extended well beyond its
own river valley. A later observer, writing during the great
seventeenth-century wars that would remake the whole region, recalled
that the entire east coast of the South Sulawesi peninsula had once
answered to Luwu. It was the high point of a dominance the same
sources that record it cannot fully explain - whether Luwu held this
territory through raiding, through control of trade, or through some
combination of both is not something the surviving evidence settles.

## Two neighbors, and a slow retreat

That dominance did not survive contact with agrarian neighbors better
suited to hold territory than a river-mouth trading power was.
Around 1500, Wajo and Bone - the two Bugis chiefdoms nearest to Luwu,
both built on the kind of intensive wet-rice cultivation Luwu's own
sago-based economy never matched - forced Luwu to give up the central
territories of the peninsula it had held. Luwu did not collapse all
at once. It kept its grip on Cenrana, the river mouth that had once
anchored its wider trade network, for more than sixty years longer,
finally losing it in 1563.

## A conversion that changed the terms, not the trend

By the time Luwu's own ruler converted to Islam in 1605 - months
ahead of Gowa's own conversion the same year - the kingdom's practical
power had already been receding for over a century. The conversion
did not reverse that trend; if anything, it folded Luwu into the same
religious and political order Gowa was about to impose by force on
every other Bugis chiefdom in the region, Luwu included. A kingdom
whose own prestige had once rested on holding an entire coastline
entered the seventeenth century holding a name, a lineage claim
reaching back to a mythical age, and comparatively little else.
