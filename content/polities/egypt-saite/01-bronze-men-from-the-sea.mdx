When Ashurbanipal's Assyrian army sacked Thebes in 663, it did not rule
Egypt directly afterward. It left the country in the hands of local Delta
princes who had already been governing under Assyrian oversight, the most
capable of them a man named Necho, prince of Sais, whose son Psamtik
inherited both the position and the same nominal obligation to an
overlord two thousand miles away.

## Twelve kings, or one waiting his turn

The Greek historian Herodotus, writing a century and a half later from
material collected in Egypt itself, describes the period just before
Psamtik's rise as a rule by twelve princes dividing the country between
them — a dodecarchy — bound by an oracle's promise that whichever of them
poured a libation from a bronze vessel would rule all of it. Psamtik, the
story goes, did so unwittingly, using his helmet after his own ceremonial
cup was short one for the twelve, and the others drove him out rather than
honour the prophecy against themselves.

Herodotus is not always reliable on Egyptian chronology, and the number
twelve looks more like a folk-tale convention than a headcount of actual
Delta rulers. What the story preserves accurately enough to matter is the
shape of the real situation: multiple local Egyptian princes, none strong
enough to rule the whole country, all nominally subordinate to a foreign
power distracted by problems of its own.

## The prophecy that actually came true

A second oracle, from the goddess of Buto, told the exiled Psamtik that he
would recover the throne by means of "bronze men come from the sea." Not
long after, a fleet of Ionian Greek and Carian raiders — bronze-armoured
in a way no Egyptian army was — was driven onto the Egyptian coast by
weather. Psamtik hired them.

Whatever the oracle stories owe to hindsight and later embellishment, the
mercenaries were real, and Psamtik's use of them was decisive: with a
force loyal to him personally rather than to any Egyptian faction or
Assyrian appointment, he defeated the rival Delta princes one by one and,
while Assyria's own attention was consumed by a civil war against its
governor in Babylon, simply stopped behaving as anyone's vassal. No source
records a battle against Assyria itself. Psamtik's independence was won by
being useful to nobody's notice at the exact moment nobody could afford to
notice.
