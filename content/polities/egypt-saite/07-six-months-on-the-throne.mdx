Amasis died in his own bed in 526, still king after forty-four years, and
was succeeded by his son Psamtik III without incident — the dynasty's most
ordinary transfer of power in over a century, and its last. The Persian
king Cambyses was already marching on Egypt when Amasis died, and Psamtik
inherited a war rather than a kingdom at peace.

## A defection before a single battle

Herodotus attributes part of what followed to Phanes of Halicarnassus, a
Greek commander formerly in Egyptian service who defected to Cambyses and
briefed him on how to cross the waterless approach to Egypt's eastern
frontier — securing safe passage and water for the Persian army through an
Arabian intermediary before the campaign had reached Egyptian territory at
all. Whatever the exact truth of Phanes' role, the desert crossing that had
turned back or worn down every earlier invader approaching Egypt from the
east cost Cambyses nothing.

## Pelusium

The armies met at Pelusium, on the coast at Egypt's eastern edge, in the
spring of 525. The Egyptian line — Psamtik's mixed force of native
troops and the same Greek and Carian mercenary establishment this dynasty
had depended on since Psamtik I's own reign — was broken. What was left of
the army fell back on Memphis, which Cambyses then besieged and took.

## What ended and what did not

Psamtik III was captured, treated for a time as a Persian prisoner with
some ceremony, and executed some months later after apparently being
implicated in a plot to raise a revolt. Egypt was made a Persian
province — the twenty-seventh dynasty, in the traditional Egyptian
king-list convention, though the throne that dynasty inherited had nothing
Egyptian about the family holding it.

What the conquest did not do was end Egyptian kingship as an institution
the country's own elite still understood and, eventually, reasserted:
Cambyses' own successors would rule Egypt for a century as pharaohs in the
full Egyptian style, and Egypt itself would throw off Persian rule
entirely within another century and a half, showing that the office this
dynasty had spent a hundred and forty years rebuilding out of two-
thousand-year-old fragments had outlasted the family that rebuilt it.
