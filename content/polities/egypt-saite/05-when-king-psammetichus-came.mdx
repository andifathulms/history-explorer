In 592, Psamtik II sent an army south into Nubia, deep enough to threaten
the old Kushite heartland around Napata itself — a strike, most likely,
against a dynasty that had ruled Egypt within living memory and had never
stopped using the full pharaonic titulary from its own new capital
further south. The campaign's official record is unremarkable as these
things go.

<Figure id="kalabsha-stela" /> What survives beside it is not.

## Soldiers who could write

On the leg of one of the colossal seated statues of Ramesses II at Abu
Simbel — already thirteen centuries old when this happened — a group of
Greek and Carian mercenaries serving in Psamtik's army carved a message in
their own alphabet, hacked into the stone with an axe rather than
professionally cut. It reads, in translation:

> When king Psammetichus came to Elephantine, this was written by those who
> sailed with Psammetichus the son of Theocles, and they came beyond Kerkis
> as far as the river permits. Those who spoke a foreign tongue were led by
> Potasimto, the Egyptians by Amasis.

Two of the men who carved it signed their own names beside it.

## What five lines happen to prove

Nobody commissioned this text. It is not a royal monument, follows no
temple's decorum, and was carved by soldiers for reasons that can only be
guessed at — pride, boredom, or the same impulse that puts a name on any
wall a traveller expects never to see again. Its value has nothing to do
with what it says about the campaign, which the official record covers
adequately on its own, and everything to do with what it proves by simply
existing: foreign mercenaries serving this dynasty could read and write
their own language, expected other literate people to encounter what they
wrote, and travelled far enough up the Nile to leave it a thousand
kilometres from the Mediterranean coast they had presumably arrived from.

It is also, by a wide margin, among the earliest substantial pieces of
Greek writing found anywhere outside the Aegean world — a language with no
business at Abu Simbel at all, standing on a monument older than anyone
who carved it could have reckoned, put there by men whose employer this
dynasty's own reunification had depended on from its very first
generation.
