Necho II, Psamtik's grandson, pursued two projects that together say more
about how this dynasty understood Egypt's place in the world than any
battle it fought. Both involved water going somewhere no earlier Egyptian
king had tried to send it.

## A canal toward the Red Sea

Necho began cutting a canal linking the Nile, through the Wadi Tumilat, to
the Red Sea — a route that would let a ship sail from the Mediterranean to
the Arabian and East African trade without going overland at all. Ancient
sources differ on whether he finished it in his own reign or abandoned the
project unfinished on a priestly warning that it would benefit foreigners
more than Egypt; the route was certainly usable within a century, since the
Persian king Darius I later completed or restored a working version of it
and commemorated the fact on stelae along its banks. Either way, the
ambition itself — physically joining Egypt's two seas rather than treating
the isthmus between them as a fixed boundary — belongs to Necho.

## A voyage nobody in Egypt is recorded taking credit for

The second project is stranger and, on the evidence, real. According to
Herodotus, Necho commissioned a Phoenician crew to sail from the Red Sea
around the entire African continent and return to Egypt through the
Mediterranean — a voyage the Greek historian reports took three years,
with the sailors putting ashore twice to sow and harvest grain before
continuing.

Herodotus himself did not believe the sailors' most specific claim: that
partway through the voyage, the sun stood on their right hand rather than
their left. To a Mediterranean sailor, the sun stands to the north at
midday, on the left if facing west — Herodotus records the detail
specifically to dismiss it as beyond belief. He had the geography backward.
South of the equator, at the relevant time of year, the noon sun genuinely
appears in the northern sky, meaning it would fall on the right for a ship
sailing west along Africa's southern coast — a fact no Mediterranean
observer of the period had any way to know unless somebody had actually
been there to see it. The detail Herodotus rejected as the least credible
part of the story is, to a modern reader, the strongest evidence that the
voyage happened.

## What the two projects share

Neither the canal nor the circumnavigation added an acre of territory to
Egypt. Both were investments in movement itself —
in what could reach Egypt, and what Egypt could reach — from a king whose
own dynasty had come to power a hired fleet at a time and clearly never
stopped thinking about what the sea could still deliver.
