Bone's own chronicle opens not with a battle or a migration but with a
single figure appearing, by tradition, from outside the ordinary world
entirely: a tomanurung, a "descended one," known by the name
Matasilompo'é. The chronicle's own internal reign-lengths, checked
against later genealogical records kept at Bone, place the start of
this founding reign somewhere in the first half of the fourteenth
century - the two surviving attempts at absolute dating differ by
several decades, but converge on roughly the 1330s to 1360s as the
period a recognizable kingship began at Bone.

## A marriage between two founders

Matasilompo'é's own reign is defined in the chronicle less by conquest
than by alliance: he married another tomanurung, a figure descended in
the same way at the neighboring settlement of Toro'. Two rulers, each
claiming a heavenly rather than an ordinary human origin, were joined
by marriage rather than one absorbing the other by force - a founding
pattern of negotiated union rather than conquest that set a tone later
Bone rulers did not always follow.

## A line the chronicle takes pains to trace precisely

What follows across the chronicle's own structure is a sequence of ten
named rulers before the kingdom's own submission to Gowa in 1611:
Matasilompo'é, his son La Ummasa', and the long-reigning Kerrampélua'
after him, followed by seven more rulers whose reigns the chronicle
treats with the same careful attention to genealogy, marriage and
succession. The chronicle's own author was plainly less interested in
recording victories for their own sake than in tracing exactly how
power passed from each ruler to the next - a concern that shapes every
reign the work records, including the crisis that opens the third
reign.
