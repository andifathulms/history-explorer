Arung Palakka's own life began under exactly the subordination his
generation had inherited: a Bugis prince of Bone whose own people Gowa
had conquered and set to forced labor. He escaped that condition,
spent years in exile at the Dutch trading settlement of Batavia, and
came back to South Sulawesi with something no earlier Bone ruler had
been able to muster - a foreign company's fleet, willing to lend its
ships and money to a war he had every personal reason to want fought.

## Whose war it actually was

The conflict that followed, from 1666 to 1669, is often filed simply
as a Dutch conquest of Makassar, and the Dutch company's contribution
was real: ships, siege engineering, and money that no Bugis prince
could have raised alone.
But the war itself, and the reasons behind
it, were Bone's own. This was a South Sulawesi conflict the company
joined rather than a colonial expedition that South Sulawesi merely
suffered - the difference between who supplied the cause and who
supplied the hardware.

## A treaty, and then a siege

The Treaty of Bongaya, signed in 1667, stripped Gowa of its foreign
allies and its fortifications and installed Bone as the region's
paramount power on paper. It did not end the fighting. Gowa's own
ruler signed the treaty and kept fighting anyway, and it took another
twenty months of war before the fortress at Sombaopu - its walls four
meters thick, too strong to breach and requiring mining instead - fell
in June 1669. What Bongaya had promised on paper, Sombaopu's fall made
real on the ground.
