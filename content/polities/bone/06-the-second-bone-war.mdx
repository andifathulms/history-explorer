Bone did not stop being a working kingdom the moment Arung Palakka
died in 1696. For more than a century and a half afterward it kept
the governing structure he had inherited and refined: a council of
seven nobles, the Dewan Hadat, drawn from the same seven founding
wanua that had first united under the tomanurung Matasilompo'é, with
the power to choose each new ruler from among those of royal descent
and, when a king's own conduct required it, to depose him. Beneath the
council sat the Tomarilalang, a chief-minister's office powerful
enough that a nineteenth-century European visitor called its holder
"the sole medium of communication with the king," and a war-command,
the punggawa, raised only when the ruler's own summons, the
bila-bila, called the kingdom's five regional forces to arms.

## A treaty signed only under protest

That structure did not save Bone from its own rulers' quarrels with
Batavia. Queen Saleha, who held the throne from 1823 to 1835, refused
for over a decade to sign a renewed Treaty of Bongaya the Dutch
pressed on her, and only her successor, Arung Panyili, put his own
name to it in August 1838. Succession disputes and factional rivalry
between the Dewan Hadat and the Tomarilalang's office continued
through the following reigns - Arung Pugi (1845-57), Besse Kajuara
(1857-60) - each new ruler reshuffling the punggawa and chief-minister
posts to favor their own kin, weakening exactly the institutions that
had once given the kingdom its stability.

## A kingdom reduced to a loan

The reckoning came in 1859-60. Dutch forces defeated Bone outright in
what Dutch administrative records call the Second Bone War, and the
kingdom that had spent a century and a half asserting an ever-wider
authority over its own neighbors was reduced to a leenvorstendom - a
"loan-kingdom" - under direct Dutch oversight. Bone lost Sinjai,
Bulukumba and Bantaeng outright; Lamuru, a dependency the Dutch had
also seized, was eventually "loaned" back rather than restored as an
integral possession. The council, the chief minister and the war
command all survived the defeat in name, much as Gowa's own sultans
had gone on reigning after 1669 without a kingdom that answered their
summons - but Bone after 1860 governed under a Dutch administration
that could, and did, redraw its own offices at will. Bone's own history did not end there either - the kingdom continued
into the twentieth century under still-tightening Dutch control - but
the sources drawn on here close with the kingdom already a dependency
in substance, waiting only for the paperwork to catch up.
