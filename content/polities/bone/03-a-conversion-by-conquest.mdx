Islam had already been present on South Sulawesi's coasts for
generations, carried by Malay traders and missionaries, when Gowa's
own rulers took it up in 1605. What made Gowa's conversion different
was not timing but consequence: within a year, the newly Islamic
kingdom turned its own religious change into a campaign of conquest
against its Bugis neighbors, rather than waiting for the faith to
spread through the ordinary channels of marriage and trade that had
carried it elsewhere in the archipelago.

## A kingdom that fought rather than followed

Bone, along with the neighboring Bugis kingdoms of Soppeng and Wajo,
was not a minor target. All three were established states with their
own courts, their own histories, and their own reasons to resent a
coastal upstart's demand that they adopt its religion on its own
schedule rather than their own. Their resistance turned what might
have been a diplomatic formality into six years of real warfare - a
measure of how seriously Bone, in particular, took the question of
whether it would be told what to believe.

## A submission that did not mean acceptance

By 1611 the resistance had failed, and Bone's own submission left
Gowa the unrivaled dominant power of the entire South Sulawesi
peninsula. But submission was not the same as reconciliation. Bone
never fully accepted the subordination the war had imposed on it, and
the resentment lingered for two generations - long enough to survive
into a moment when a single Bugis prince of Bone would find outside
allies willing to help him finally settle the account.
