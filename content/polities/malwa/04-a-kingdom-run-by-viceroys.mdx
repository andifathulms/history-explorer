Bahadur Shah's conquest did not settle Malwa so much as open it to a
decade of men who ruled it without ever quite owning it. Mallu Khan, a
former officer of the fallen Khalji kings, used the confusion of
Bahadur's later wars against Humayun to reassert control over Malwa's
fief-holders from his own base at Mandu and crowned himself Qadir Shah —
a self-made sovereign with no dynasty behind him and no real claim
beyond having been the strongest man left standing in the wreckage.

## An insolent reply, and an invasion that followed

Qadir Shah's undoing came from the direction he least expected. Sher
Khan Sur, still consolidating his own power in the east and pressed for
resources, demanded assistance from him in terms Qadir Shah found too
peremptory to tolerate, and sent back an insolent refusal. Sher Shah,
by then king of Delhi in his own right, marched into Malwa in 1542;
Qadir Shah submitted at Sarangpur rather than fight, was given a
consolation post in Bengal instead of the province he had just lost, and
fled his new patron's camp shortly afterward out of fear for his own
safety. Sher Shah left Malwa in the hands of viceroys rather than
restoring any king to it at all.

## The massacre at Raisen

The bloodiest single episode of this viceroy period belonged to Puran
Mal, a local Rajput lord who had massacred the population of Chanderi
after occupying it and assembled a harem of two thousand women, Hindu
and Muslim alike, at his fortress of Raisen. Sher Shah besieged him there
in 1543 and lured him into surrender with promises of safe conduct, then
attacked his disarmed followers with war elephants the moment they were
in his power. Rather than accept the massacre that followed, Puran Mal's
Rajputs carried out the rite of jauhar and died fighting rather than be
taken — one more act of violence in a province that had not had a king
of its own, secure enough to prevent such things, in more than a decade.

## A viceroy who stopped calling himself one

Malwa's last viceroy under this arrangement, Shuja'at Khan, survived a
falling-out with Sher Shah's own son and successor and an assassination
attempt carried out with that successor's tacit approval, and by the time
Humayun recovered the Mughal throne in 1555, Shuja'at Khan had simply
stopped acknowledging any overlord at all, governing Malwa in every
practical respect as an independent sovereign in his own right — the
quiet reappearance, after a lost battle, two invasions and a massacre,
of a throne with no dynasty behind it and nothing to legitimise it beyond
one man's refusal to keep answering to anyone else.
