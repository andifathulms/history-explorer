---
title: A mercy that did not last
drafted_from: haig-1928

By the reign of Mahmud II, Mahmud Khalji's line had fallen under the
influence of its own Rajput minister, Medni Rai, whose faction had grown
powerful enough at Mandu that the king's Muslim nobles were purged,
dismissed or driven from court outright. Mahmud fled his own capital in
1517 to escape his minister's grip and appealed to Muzaffar II of
Gujarat for help — help that came, restored him to his throne, and left
Malwa more dependent on Gujarat's goodwill than it had ever been on its
own resources.

## Defeated, captured, and set free

The Rajput faction Mahmud had escaped was not finished. In 1519, backed
by Rana Sangrama of Mewar, it drew Mahmud into a battle near Gagraun that
went badly wrong: his army, exhausted by a forced march, was cut to
pieces before it could properly form up, and Mahmud himself, wounded
while fighting on with real courage, was captured and brought before the
Rana. Sangrama could have annexed the broken kingdom outright. He chose
instead to receive his defeated rival with what Haig calls the
chivalrous courtesy a Rajput knows how to show a beaten foe, exacted only
the surrender of Mahmud's crown jewels, and sent him home under armed
escort to reclaim his own throne — a mercy so widely remarked upon,
Muslim and Hindu chroniclers alike, that one of Gujarat's own officers
was said to have named a dog after the Rana out of sheer irritation at
hearing his generosity praised.

## The debt that finally came due

Mahmud repaid none of it. When Bahadur Shah ascended Gujarat's throne in
1526, Mahmud sheltered his rival brother and openly encouraged him to
contest it; three years later, hearing of Sangrama's death, he raided the
territory of the very Rana who had spared him. Sangrama's successor
retaliated, and Mahmud, having exhausted the goodwill of both the man who
had once shown him mercy and the man he was supposed to be allied with,
found himself with no protector left when Bahadur Shah's own army
arrived at Mandu on 17 March 1531. The fortress fell, and the Khalji
dynasty — founded in one poisoning, sustained through a century of real
conquest, and preserved once already by an enemy's clemency — ended in a
siege its own king's ingratitude had done as much to bring about as any
army had.
