Shuja'at Khan died in 1555 still ruling as an undeclared sovereign, and
his son Miyan Bayazid, known to history as Baz Bahadur, took the throne
over the objections of his father's adopted heir, Daulat Khan Ajyura.
Baz Bahadur settled the rivalry the way most of Malwa's rulers before him
had settled theirs — he lulled Daulat Khan's suspicions with a proposed
partition of the kingdom, then seized and killed him once his guard was
down, and assumed the royal title cleanly on his own.

## A defeat that ended his ambitions early

His one serious military campaign, against Rani Durgavati of the Gonds
at Garha-Katanga, went badly from the start: the queen defeated him
decisively and drove his army back across his own frontier, a setback
Baz Bahadur never attempted to reverse. He withdrew instead into the
company of his celebrated mistress, Rupmati, at Mandu — a retreat
substantial enough, and famous enough even in his own lifetime, that a
pavilion built for her survives at the fortress to this day, and that
Haig records history remembering Baz Bahadur less for anything he
governed than for the romance that occupied the years he might otherwise
have spent doing so.

## An emperor's officers, not an emperor

Akbar did not need to come himself. In 1561 his officers invaded Malwa,
and Baz Bahadur, already discredited by his defeat at Durgavati's hands
and with no real military reputation left to rally support around, was
driven from his kingdom with comparatively little resistance. Malwa
became a province of the Mughal empire — a kingdom whose founding
dynasty had begun with one son poisoning his father for the crown, and
whose independent existence ended with a king remembered chiefly for the
woman he shared his final years with rather than for anything he had
managed to hold onto.
