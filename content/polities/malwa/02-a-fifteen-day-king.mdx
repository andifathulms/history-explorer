Hushang Shah's own line did not hold the throne for long after him. His
grandson Ghazni Khan ascended in 1435 and was poisoned within the year
by his own cousin, who seized the throne in his place and took the title
Mahmud Khalji — founding, in the same stroke of murder that had put
Hushang on the throne a generation earlier, the Khalji dynasty under
which Malwa would reach the widest extent it would ever hold.

## Warlike and, on balance, effective

Haig describes Mahmud Khalji as an active and warlike prince, and the
thirty-three years of his reign bore the description out. He campaigned
against neighbouring Gujarat and against the Rajput states along Malwa's
borders, fought Rana Kumbha of Mewar to a costly standstill more than
once, and by the time of his death on 31 May 1469 had built a kingdom
substantial enough that Gujarat, Malwa's most persistent rival, never
managed to reduce it to a client state during his own lifetime, whatever
it would go on to do to his descendants.

## A throne no less violent for being royal now

The paradox of the Khalji line was that it never wholly escaped the
method by which it began. Ghazni Khan's own murdered claim to the throne
had lasted only fifteen days; Mahmud Khalji's own usurpation of it was,
by the standard of the dynasty he founded, simply the one attempt at
seizing Malwa's crown that happened to succeed and then hold. The
kingdom he built was real, and its wars were fought by a state with
genuine resources behind it rather than a pretender's improvised
following — but the throne underneath all of it had been won by the same
tool that had put both his predecessors there before him.
