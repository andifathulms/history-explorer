Uzun Hasan spent a decade conducting a foreign policy towards western Europe,
and it is the strangest thing any Turkmen confederation ever did.

He married Theodora Komnene, daughter of the emperor of Trebizond — Despina
Khatun to the Persian sources — whose own page records the marriage as that
empire's last attempt to buy a protector. It did not work: Trebizond fell to
Mehmed II in 1461 and Uzun Hasan did not come.

<Figure id="hagia-sophia-trabzon" />

He negotiated with Venice. The Venetians were at war with the Ottomans, wanted a
second front in the east, and Uzun Hasan wanted what the Ottomans had and he did
not, which was artillery and men who could work it. Ambassadors went back and
forth for years — Caterino Zeno, who was married to a niece of Uzun Hasan's
wife, and Josafat Barbaro, whose account of the court is one of the better
European descriptions of fifteenth-century Iran. Guns and gunners were promised
repeatedly. A small quantity arrived too late and in the wrong place.

That is the diplomatic shape of the whole eastern Mediterranean in the 1460s and
1470s. Venice, Trebizond, the Ak Koyunlu and the Mamluks all wanted the Ottomans
contained, and none of them could coordinate a campaign across that distance
with that technology. The Ottomans could fight them one at a time and did.

When Uzun Hasan met Mehmed II in 1473 he had the finest Turkmen cavalry in the
region and essentially no firearms. The promised alliance had produced a decade
of embassies and nothing on the field.
