---
title: What Uzun Hasan hired
drafted_from: woods-1999
phase: peak
---

Having taken Iran, Uzun Hasan did what the successful pastoralists in this
corpus do with a settled country, and this site has now recorded it for the
Ghaznavids, the Seljuks, the Karakhanids, the Ilkhanids and the Timurids. He
hired the Persian bureaucracy.

The senior civil offices went to Iranian officials and the divan ran in Persian.
He promulgated a law code — the qanunname of Uzun Hasan — regulating taxes and
dues across the territory, which is the substantive act on this page and is the
reason the revenue field is coded `land-tax` from a source rather than left
null. The code outlived the state comprehensively: the Safavids kept it, and the
Ottomans were still applying "the law of Hasan Padishah" in eastern Anatolia
generations after the confederation that produced it had gone.

Woods' three-word subtitle is the argument — clan, confederation, empire — and
the third stage is this. A clan builds a confederation by distributing pasture
and marriages; a confederation becomes an empire by taking a settled revenue and
then acquiring the apparatus to collect it. What Woods is careful about is that
the stages do not replace each other. Uzun Hasan had a Persian chancery and a
tax code and he still ruled a coalition of Turkmen leaders whose loyalty was
personal and whose troops went home, and the second fact eventually decided the
polity's fate regardless of the first.

The court also produced art of a quality out of proportion to the state's
thirty years at the top. The Tabriz atelier under Uzun Hasan and his son Ya'qub
produced illustrated manuscripts in what is called the Turkman style, and its
painters and its book-making went straight into the Safavid workshop when the
Safavids took the city. Ya'qub built the Hasht Behesht palace, which Venetian
visitors described at length and which is gone.
