Ya'qub held the confederation together for twelve years after Otlukbeli, and the
Ottoman historian Munajjim-bashi gives him in a line: disposed to drink and a
merry life, and very fond of poetry, with poets gathering to his court from all
quarters and composing resonant *qasidas* in his praise. A Venetian merchant
resident in Persia in those years describes the palace Uzun Hasan had built
outside Tabriz, and then describes how Ya'qub died, and the second account is
the one that decided what happened to Iran.

His wife had arranged with a kinsman of his that she would make him king, since
in the absence of children he would have the right to it. She prepared the
poison while Ya'qub was in the bath. He had gone in, as he usually did, with his
son of eight or nine, and stayed from the twenty-second hour until sunset; and
because it was his habit to drink something on coming out, she was waiting at
the door of the harem with a cup and a gold vase. She made more of him than she
usually did, and could not govern her face, and went very pale. Ya'qub had
already begun to distrust her. He told her to drink first. She knew what it was
and drank it anyway, because she could not refuse. Then she handed him the cup
and he and the boy finished it. By midnight all three were dead.

Everything after that follows from three people dying in one night in 1490. The
great lords quarrelled; for five or six years, says the Venetian, all Persia was
in civil war, with first one noble and then another becoming sultan. Ya'qub's
son Baysunqur reigned a year and eight months before his cousin Rustam replaced
him. Rustam marched against the Timurids, turned back to deal with a revolt at
Isfahan, chased the governor to Qum and killed him, and sent an expedition to
Shirvan that celebrated its success in the manner of the previous century, by
building pyramids of skulls.

And then, needing men against Baysunqur, Rustam did the thing that ended his
house. He released the Safavid prisoners held at Istakhr — the sons of Shaykh
Haydar, who had been killed by Ya'qub — and sent them out to avenge their
father. They did: they defeated and killed Baysunqur at Ahar. Rustam, finding
that Sultan Ali's following was now larger than was comfortable, sent four
thousand horse after him. The Safavids were seven hundred and fought, in the
words of another Venetian who was there, like lions, and were destroyed, and
Sultan Ali was killed after naming his young brother Isma'il as his successor.

Isma'il went into hiding in Gilan, where the governor protected him and a Shi'i
propaganda was worked among the population with great success.

The White Sheep took a boy out of prison, armed him against their own rival, and
were surprised by what he did with it. Eleven years later his brother took
Tabriz.
