The best surviving portrait of Uzun Hasan was written by a Venetian who had come
to sell him a war, and Browne — who read everything the Persian and Turkish
historians wrote about the man — says flatly that nothing to compare with it
exists in any eastern source he knows.

## Contarini at table

Ambrogio Contarini was with him in 1474 and 1475, at the end of the decade of
embassies, after Otlukbeli, when the alliance against the Ottomans had already
failed. What he wrote down is a man at dinner.

He always drank wine with his meals. He appeared to be a good liver, and took
pleasure in inviting his guests to share the dishes in front of him. There were
constantly present a number of players and singers, and he commanded whatever he
wished to be played or sung; His Majesty appeared to be of a very merry
disposition.

He was tall and thin, with a slightly Tartar cast of countenance and a constant
colour in his face. His hand trembled as he drank. He seemed about seventy.

He was fond of amusing himself in a homely manner, Contarini adds — but when too
far gone was sometimes dangerous.

*Take him altogether, however, he was a pleasant gentleman.*

## What the eastern sources have instead

They have the virtues, in a list.

Munajjim-bashi calls him a wise, just, brave, pious, religious and devout king,
a friend of learned and godly men, charitable, a public benefactor, and a builder
of many pious foundations; and notes that with a small army he overcame two such
mighty kings as Jahan Shah and Abu Sa'id, took tribute from Georgia, and ruled
Azerbaijan and the two Iraqs.

Every word of that is probably true and none of it tells you what it was like to
be in the room.

Ramusio, introducing another of the Venetian narratives, put his finger on why.
It is to be regretted, he wrote, that some Eastern kings, great in power and
intellect, have not had historians to celebrate their deeds — since among the
sultans of Egypt and the kings of Persia there have been men worthy of
comparison not only with famous barbarian kings but with the great Greek and
Roman commanders. What struck him particularly was the career: a poor nobleman,
the weakest of many brothers, with a small castle and not more than thirty
soldiers, who raised himself to the point of disputing the empire of all Asia
with the Ottoman house.

## Why the portrait is European

Because the Ak Koyunlu lost, and because the people who came to look at them
came for a reason that had nothing to do with them.

Venice wanted a second front. It sent ambassadors across Asia to a Turkmen
confederation it could not have found on a map ten years earlier, and those men
wrote up everything — the roads, the towns, the prices, the ruler's face, his
drinking, his trembling hand — because their masters needed to know whether this
was a power worth an alliance.

The war never happened. Uzun Hasan was beaten at Otlukbeli in 1473 and did not
fight the Ottomans again, and his great-grandson destroyed the dynasty
twenty-eight years later. What the embassies produced instead is the fullest
description of fifteenth-century Iran by anybody, written by men who were not
interested in Iran, preserved because Venice kept its diplomatic papers.

A dynasty is lucky to be described at all. This one was described by strangers,
in the wrong language, for the wrong purpose, and it is the only reason anybody
knows that Uzun Hasan liked music at dinner and that his hand shook.
