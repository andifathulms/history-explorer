---
title: What gunpowder settled
drafted_from: woods-1999
phase: contraction
---

In August 1473, at Otlukbeli in eastern Anatolia, Mehmed II destroyed the Ak
Koyunlu army. The Ottomans had field artillery and janissaries with handguns;
Uzun Hasan had the finest Turkmen cavalry in the region and the Venetian
firearms he had spent a decade being promised had not come. His son was killed
in the battle.

The immediate consequence was the frontier. Eastern Anatolia was Ottoman from
that point in a way it had not been before, and the eastern boundary of Ottoman
power stopped being a question of which Turkmen confederation was strongest.
Uzun Hasan lived another five years and did not fight the Ottomans again.

The longer consequence is the one this site is interested in, and it is best
seen by dating Otlukbeli against three other entries in the corpus. Otlukbeli in
1473, Chaldiran in 1514, Marj Dabiq and Ridaniya in 1516 and 1517. In
forty-four years the same combination — cannon in a fixed line, drilled infantry
with firearms, cavalry used to exploit rather than to decide — settled who ruled
eastern Anatolia, Iran's western frontier and the Arab lands, and in every case
the losing side was mounted archers commanded by men who understood exactly what
had beaten them and could not reproduce it in time.

The Ak Koyunlu were the first polity in this corpus to lose to it, and they were
the first to try to buy it. Uzun Hasan's decade of embassies to Venice was an
attempt to acquire the technology by alliance rather than to build it, which is
the strategy the Mamluks tried at Diu and the Safavids tried at Chaldiran, and
it did not work for any of them. What the Ottomans had was not guns. It was a
standing paid infantry that could be drilled to use them, and none of these
polities had a revenue system that could support one.

After Otlukbeli the confederation held together for two decades on Uzun Hasan's
reputation and his son Ya'qub's competence, and then came apart on schedule.
