title: Seventy years of being the smaller one

The White Sheep were Turkmen of the Bayandur, one of the twenty-four Oghuz
clans, pasturing in the country around Diyarbakır in the upper Jazira. For the
first three-quarters of their history they were the junior of the two
confederations, and the page usually skips it.

Woods' study is about that period as much as about the famous one, and his
argument gives the polity a shape it otherwise lacks. The Ak Koyunlu were not a
tribe that suddenly produced a conqueror. They were a clan — the Bayandur —
which built a confederation around itself by marriage, patronage and the
distribution of pasture, and which carried a genealogical claim the whole time:
descent from Bayandur Khan, grandson of Oghuz Khan, which made the ruling house
the legitimate leadership of the Oghuz. That claim was in the chancery
documents and on the coins, and it is why this site's legitimation field for the
polity carries `descent` alongside `conquest`.

The politics of the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries were determined by
having the Black Sheep as neighbours and rivals for the same grazing. When Timur
came west the Ak Koyunlu took his side, and Qara Yuluk Uthman Beg was confirmed
in Diyarbakır for it — the standard reward for a confederation that backs the
winner, and the reason the White Sheep survived the period that scattered the
Black.

Then Timur died and Qara Yusuf came back, and the White Sheep spent another
fifty years in Diyarbakır watching their rivals hold Tabriz. Qara Yuluk was
killed fighting them in 1435. His sons fought each other. The confederation was
still there in 1452 because it had a genealogy, a pasture and a fortress, which
was enough to persist on and not enough to do anything with.
