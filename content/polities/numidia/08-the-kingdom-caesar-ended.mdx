What happened to Numidia's own throne in the two generations after
Jugurtha's death is not well documented: it passed first to his
half-brother Gauda, and the line resurfaces clearly only with Juba I,
found already reigning deep in Rome's own civil war between Caesar and
Pompey. Juba had no love for Caesar, and
after Pompey's defeat at Pharsalus in 48 BC his kingdom became a
refuge for the losing side: Cato and Scipio arrived with what survivors
they could gather, joining an army already commanded by Labienus,
Petreius and Afranius, the provincial governor Attius Varus among
them. Juba's own Mauretanian neighbors, Bocchus and Bogud, backed
Caesar instead - the same split loyalty, on opposite sides, that had
decided the war against Jugurtha two generations before.

## A town that shut its own gates

The war turned at Thapsus on 6 April 46 BC, where Caesar broke the
combined Pompeian and Numidian forces outright. Juba's own defeat
proved total and immediate: when he approached the town of Zama, the
capital his ancestors had built their power around, hoping to gather
what men and treasure he still could, the townsfolk refused to answer
him at all and left him to retreat to a country estate with nothing
but a handful of horsemen and his companion Petreius. Zama's own
envoys, rather than wait for the king's return, sent word to Caesar at
Utica asking for protection instead.

## A duel meant to look like courage

Six days after Thapsus, Cato took his own life at Utica rather than
accept Caesar's clemency. Juba, outlawed by every town in his own
kingdom and with no path left to safety, chose to die by his own hand
as well - but not alone. After dining together, he and Petreius fought
each other with swords, so that both men's deaths might look like a
soldier's rather than a fugitive's. Juba, the stronger of the two,
killed Petreius easily; when he then tried to turn the blade on
himself and found he could not finish the stroke, he asked a slave to
do it for him, and the slave obliged.

## A kingdom sold at auction

Caesar wasted no time settling what remained. At Zama he had the royal
property auctioned off, confiscated the estates of every Roman citizen
who had borne arms against the republic under Juba's banner, rewarded
the town for having shut its own gates against its king, and organized
the whole kingdom into a new Roman province, Africa Nova. He left it in
the charge of a proconsul with full military authority: Gaius
Sallustius, the same man who would go on to write the history of
Jugurtha's own war against Rome a half-century earlier, now governing
the very throne that war had first won for his ancestors.
