Micipsa succeeded his father Massinissa without contest - his two
brothers had already died of disease - and raised his own two sons,
Adherbal and Hiempsal, alongside a nephew he had taken into the
palace on equal terms: Jugurtha, the illegitimate son of Micipsa's own
dead brother Mastanabal, born too far outside the succession for
Massinissa to have troubled with him. Jugurtha grew into a man
powerful in frame and remarkable in ability, excelling at riding,
marksmanship and the hunt while staying, unlike most young men of his
rank, notably modest about his own achievements. Micipsa was delighted
at first, certain his nephew's merit would add lustre to the throne.

## A general's letter changes everything

As Jugurtha's reputation grew and his own sons remained young, Micipsa's
delight curdled into fear - not of any act Jugurtha had committed, but
of what a man so admired by the Numidians might do once ambition
caught up with opportunity. Unable to move against him without risking
revolt, Micipsa sent him instead to a war he might not survive: command
of a Numidian contingent serving Rome at Numantia in Spain. Jugurtha
returned a hero. The Roman general Scipio Aemilianus, dismissing the
allied troops, took him aside privately to warn him against buying
Roman friendship rather than earning it - then sent him home with a
letter praising his merits so highly that Micipsa, his plan for a
quiet death in battle undone, adopted Jugurtha outright and named him
joint heir with his own two sons.

## A seat no one wanted to yield

Micipsa died worn out by age a few years later, having first summoned
Jugurtha and his sons to his deathbed and adjured them, at length, to
hold together as brothers rather than let ambition break what he had
built. Jugurtha's private thoughts were already elsewhere. At the
princes' first meeting after the funeral, Hiempsal - proud, headstrong,
and openly contemptuous of Jugurtha's illegitimate birth - took the
seat that would have forced Jugurtha into the middle, the place of
honor among Numidians, and yielded it only with visible reluctance
when Adherbal appealed to his own seniority. A later remark of
Hiempsal's, needling Jugurtha's own recent claim to authority, sank
deeper than anyone realized at the time.

## A key copied, a hiding place found

Jugurtha's schemes against Hiempsal moved slowly at first, but his
resolve never wavered. When the princes divided to oversee the
partition of Micipsa's treasury, Hiempsal lodged in the town of
Thermida, in a house belonging to one of Jugurtha's own longtime
attendants. Jugurtha bribed the man to copy the house keys and pass
them along, then struck at night with a body of soldiers who cut down
Hiempsal's household as they slept, tore the building apart room by
room, and finally found the young prince hiding in a slave woman's
hut. His severed head was sent to Jugurtha as proof the deed was done.
