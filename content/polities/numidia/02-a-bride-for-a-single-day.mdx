Fortune turned. After Scipio burned the Carthaginian and Numidian
camps in a single night attack, and won a further victory at the
Great Plains, the task of hunting down Syphax fell to the man Syphax
had exiled. Massinissa's pursuit was as fast as it was thorough: the
Massaesylians were broken, and Syphax himself, along with his
beautiful wife Sophonisba and his capital at Cirta - a city built in
the most romantic and impregnable of situations - fell into the
conqueror's hands.

<Figure id="n-cirta" />

## An old love, briefly answered

Massinissa's own heart, so the story went, had been touched by
Sophonisba's charms years before fortune threw her to his rival
instead. Now his turn had come. He married her at Cirta without delay
- and when Scipio, alarmed at the political complications a
Carthaginian bride might create, ordered him to give her up, Massinissa
sent her a cup of poison instead, the only wedding gift, he said, a
bridegroom in his position could still offer. Sophonisba drank it
without hesitation, remarking only that her death would have suited
her better had it come less immediately after her wedding.

<Figure id="n-sophonisba" />

## A title no foreign king had held before

Massinissa's Roman patrons rebuked him gently for atoning for one rash
act with another, then consoled him with what Rome had never before
granted anyone who was not a Roman citizen: the royal title, and Roman
robes bestowed on him in person by Scipio. Syphax's whole former
kingdom was his by that same reward, joined now to the hereditary
Massylian throne he had been driven from years earlier - two rival
Numidian peoples united, for the first time, under a single king whose
power rested on a Roman alliance he had gambled everything to secure.
