Kung-ye's descent from eccentric faith into outright madness accelerated
in his final years. When his own wife tried to dissuade him from his
Buddhist pretensions, he heated an iron to white heat and used it on her
body, accusing her of adultery; he went on to kill both of his sons and
a number of the people closest to him. He was, the source says, "hated
as thoroughly as he was feared" — and by 918 he sensed it himself, aware
that his own army had transferred its loyalty entirely to Wang-gön.

## An accusation turned into a compliment

Fear made Kung-ye reckless rather than cautious. He summoned Wang-gön to
the capital and accused him outright of conspiracy, claiming to know it
through his own Buddha-nature. When Wang-gön, tipped off by a
sympathetic clerk who deliberately dropped a pen beside him, confessed
to a plot that had never existed, Kung-ye took the false confession as
proof of his own supernatural insight and rewarded him with gifts
instead of punishment — a king so lost in his own delusion that he
could not recognize the danger standing in front of him even when it
confessed to his face.

## A throne seized rather than offered

Kung-ye's own officials had less patience than his general. Certain that
they would share the fate of his murdered wife and sons if nothing
changed, they begged Wang-gön to seize power himself; when he refused
on principle, his own wife joined the officials in overruling him, and a
group of them carried him bodily into the palace and proclaimed him king
over his own objections. Kung-ye fled the city he had built, naked and
alone, and was run down and beheaded at Pu-yang before the day was out.

## A prophecy read backward

Tradition later supplied the change with an omen to match: a mirror,
bought from a Silla man by a Chinese merchant and eventually presented
to Kung-ye himself, was said to bear an enigmatic verse that a court
scholar decoded only after the fact — "the green forest," Song-do,
where a king would rise, and "black metal," Ch'ŭl-wŭn, where a king
would fall. Whether the mirror's riddle came before the coup or was
fitted to it afterward, its solution matched the outcome exactly: a
kingdom built on one man's claim to divinity ended the moment his own
army stopped believing him.
