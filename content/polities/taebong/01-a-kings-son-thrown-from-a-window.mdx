title: A king's son thrown from a window

Kung-ye was born to King Hŏn-gang of Silla by a concubine, on what
tradition holds to be the least auspicious day of the year, already
possessed of teeth at birth. The king ordered the infant destroyed and
had him thrown from a window; a nurse caught him and raised him in
hiding, accidentally blinding one of his eyes in the process. He grew up
a monk, ill-suited to monastic life, and drifted into the camp of a
bandit chief named Ki-hwŭn before finding his true calling under a
second bandit leader, Yang-gil, at Puk-wŭn.

## A lieutenant who outgrew his master

Kung-ye's own talents found room to grow under Yang-gil in a way they
never had as a monk. He overcame the districts of Ch'un-ch'ŭn, Nă-sŭng,
Ul-o and O-jin, and by 896 was operating on a far larger scale, seizing
ten districts near Ch'ŭl-wŭn and putting a young lieutenant named
Wang-gön in charge of them. Success bred ambition: he styled himself
"Prince," began appointing his own prefects, and in 901 proclaimed
himself king outright — driving the point home by slashing with his own
sword a painted image of Silla's king that hung in a monastery.

## A capital, a name, a kingdom

Kung-ye ruled four more years as a self-declared king without a state to
match the title. That changed in 905, when he built a capital at
Ch'ŭl-wŭn, named his kingdom Ma-jin and distributed formal offices among
his own followers — the acts that turned a warlord's growing following
into an administered state. By this point his reach already extended
across most of the north and east, close enough to Silla's own capital
that the court there had nothing left to do but hope its two rebel
neighbors, Kung-ye in the north and Kyŏn Hwŏn in the south, might
destroy each other before either reached Kyŏngju itself.
