The Frankish king Chlodomer, one of Clovis's own sons ruling from
Orléans, moved against Burgundy in 523, within a year of Sigistrix's
killing. Gregory of Tours frames the invasion as the Franks finally
collecting on Chlothild's own decades-old grievance against the
Burgundian royal house, but the timing argues for a more opportunist
reading: a kingdom whose own king had just murdered his heir and gone
into penance for it was a far easier target than a Burgundian throne
secure in its own succession would have been.

## A campaign too fast for Burgundy to answer

Chlodomer's attack was swift rather than prolonged. Sigismund, still
reeling from the crisis he had created himself, was overrun, captured,
and handed over to the Franks along with his wife and remaining
children. Chlodomer had them all thrown down a well - a single brutal
disposal of an entire royal household rather than a negotiated
surrender or a formal execution, and a death that left the Burgundian
throne as unsettled by Frankish action as Sigismund's own killing of
Sigistrix had already left it unsettled from within.

## A martyr's cult built on a murdered king's bones

Sigismund's remains did not stay in a Frankish well. They were recovered
and carried back to his own foundation at Agaune, where they became the
object of a cult that would make him, in time, the first royal saint of
the early medieval west - his murdered son's death and his own murder at
Frankish hands together transformed, within a generation, into the
story of a martyred king rather than the story of a dynasty that had
destroyed itself from the inside before a neighbour ever finished the
job.
