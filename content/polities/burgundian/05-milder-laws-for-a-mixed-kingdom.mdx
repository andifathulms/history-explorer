Gundobad's restored kingdom, once Godegisel was dead and the Franks paid
off, was the largest and most secure the Burgundians had held since the
Rhône settlement began. He spent his last years building the legal
foundation his son would finish: a code of law, later called the Liber
Constitutionum, that set out in detail how land, dependents and woodland
were to be divided between Burgundian settlers and the Gallo-Roman
population they lived among, and that aimed - as Gregory of Tours states
plainly - to keep the one people from oppressing the other under a single
crown.

## A king out of step with his own family

Gundobad's own faith sat oddly against the kingdom he ruled. Nearly
every other Burgundian on record, from the fifth century onward, appears
as a Catholic Christian - including, by most accounts, Gundobad's own
niece Chlothild, who would go on to marry Clovis. Gundobad himself,
however, held to the Arian creed, the same faith as his uncle by
marriage, the Roman kingmaker Ricimer, in a kingdom whose Church was
otherwise overwhelmingly Catholic. He is recorded as having seriously
considered converting to the Catholic faith himself, a step his own son
would complete, but he died still formally Arian - a king whose own
personal religion had, by the end of his reign, become the exception
inside his own family and his own court rather than the rule.

## A throne built to outlast one dynasty's own faith

What Gundobad left behind mattered more than which creed he personally
held. A law code addressed to both Burgundians and Romans, a Rhône valley
kingdom secure enough to survive a Frankish invasion with its throne
intact, and a son already positioned to align the crown with the
Catholic majority his own father had never quite joined - together, they
gave the Burgundian kingdom a stability at Gundobad's death in 516 that
neither the doomed foundation at Worms nor Godegisel's own rebellion had
ever come close to matching.
