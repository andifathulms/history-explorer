Sigismund succeeded his father Gundobad in 516 already converted to the
Catholic faith his father had only flirted with - a change that aligned
the Burgundian throne, for the first time in a generation, with the
church most of his own kingdom's population and even his own extended
family already belonged to. The year before his accession he had founded
a monastery at Agaune, on the site associated with Saint Maurice and the
Theban Legion, organized around an unusual liturgical practice: monks
divided into shifts so that praise of God, the laus perennis, continued
without interruption day and night - an arrangement later ages would
treat as a model for royal monastic patronage generally.

## A council, and a code, in the same year

In 517 Sigismund brought two of his father's unfinished projects to
completion within months of each other. A council of the kingdom's
bishops met at Epaon to settle matters of church discipline and to
dismantle what remained of the Arian church structure his father had
tolerated - a plainly Catholic house-cleaning by a plainly Catholic king.
In the same year the law code Gundobad had begun, the Liber
Constitutionum, was formally promulgated, setting out in detail the
obligations and protections owed to Burgundian settlers and their
Gallo-Roman neighbours alike under one set of rules.

## A concord that did not survive its own king's temper

The alignment of throne and church did not make Sigismund's own reign
untroubled. Within a short time of the Epaon settlement, the kingdom's
bishops threatened to suspend Sigismund from communion over his defense
of a royal official implicated in a case of incest, and forced him,
under threat of their own mass withdrawal to a monastery, to accept
their judgment rather than his own. The same king who had aligned his
crown with the Catholic episcopate so completely could still find
himself disciplined by it - a warning, in hindsight, of how little his
own authority over his own household would prove to be worth only a few
years later.
