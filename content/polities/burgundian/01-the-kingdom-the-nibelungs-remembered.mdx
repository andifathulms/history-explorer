title: The kingdom the Nibelungs remembered

The Burgundians had been neighbours of Rome for centuries before they
had a kingdom of their own inside its borders - Pliny already knew of
them, and the emperor Valentinian I enlisted their warriors against the
Alamanni as early as 369. Like other peoples crossing into Roman Gaul in
the chaos after 406, some Burgundians took the opportunity that
collapsing frontier control offered. In 412 their king, Guntiarius,
joined Goar, a leader of the Alans, in raising a Gallo-Roman aristocrat
named Jovinus to a rival emperorship - a gamble on the losing side of a
Roman civil war that nonetheless left Guntiarius established, within a
few years, as king of a Burgundian settlement on the Rhine centred on the
city of Worms.

## An annihilation Rome itself arranged

That kingdom did not last a generation. By the mid-430s Roman policy in
Gaul, directed by the general Aetius, had turned against the Burgundians
on the Rhine, and around 436 an assault - one a contemporary chronicler
recorded simply as a war "in which almost the whole people with their
king were destroyed" - annihilated Guntiarius's kingdom outright.
Centuries later, German legend would transform this catastrophe into the
fall of the Nibelungs, the doomed treasure-holding dynasty whose
destruction became one of the great stories of the medieval Germanic
world - a memory of an actual kingdom's actual end, refracted through
generations of retelling into epic.

## Survivors, not survivors of nothing

What the destruction of 436 did not do was end the Burgundians as a
people. Enough of them survived - families, warriors, whatever
leadership structure could still function - to be resettled by Rome only
a few years later, in territory far to the south of the kingdom the Huns
had just erased. That resettlement, not the doomed foundation at Worms,
is where the Burgundian kingdom that would last for a century actually
began.
