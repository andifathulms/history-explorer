Thakombau's early years as Vunivalu brought Bau to the edge of the
same kind of ruin his father had once suffered. War with Rewa, whose
king Ratu Nggara pressed the fight with real fury, left Bau
beleaguered and its own subjects wavering. Even inside the town,
loyalty was for sale: Koli, chief of the Lasakau fishermen who lived
on part of the island, was quietly bribed by the rebel chief Mara and
by Rewa itself - canoes, land, and women from one side, a hundred
canoes promised from the other - to fence off his own quarter of Bau
and open a second front against his king. Only the missionary
Calvert's own intervention, a mixture of blunt warning and a
well-timed gift of hatchets and axes, talked Koli out of the revolt at
the last hour, and Bau survived a conspiracy that could have cost
Thakombau his life and, with it, every established authority in Fiji.

It was in this extremity that Thakombau's own converted faith was
tested past performance into conviction. Urged by Calvert to flee
while escape was still possible, the King refused: "I cannot do that.
If evil comes, I must die. But I think the Lord will deliver me. I am
lotu." Bau held out against odds that impressed even its enemies -
the King of Rewa himself was heard to say that if Thakombau's faith
were hypocrisy it would only feed the fire against him, but if it were
real, Rewa would never take him at all.

## A death that ended the war no battle could

The war Bau could not win by force was ended instead by an
enemy's death. On the 26th of January, 1855, Ratu Nggara, Rewa's
implacable king, was carried off by dysentery before he was able to
leave the instructions for continuing the war that Fijian custom
required of a dying leader. Thakombau moved at once, sending an
ambassador with an offer of peace framed entirely in the language of
his new faith: "Tell the Rewa people to become Christian, and let us
establish a peace that shall be lasting... I am Christian, not because
I am weak or afraid, but because I know it to be true."

The offer nearly unravelled when a holdout faction under the chief
Mara, excluded from the settlement, gathered fresh support for another
rebellion - but King George of Tonga, visiting Fiji at the time and
drawn into the conflict against his own wishes, brought that final
resistance to a swift end. Some seventy towns returned to their
allegiance to Bau, and Thakombau, meeting the defeated rebel chiefs
aboard a British warship lying off Ovalau, reproved them for their
past folly and urged them toward the pursuits of peace rather than
further war. The coalition that had once driven his father into exile
and nearly killed him in his own capital now answered to Bau more
completely than it ever had before.
