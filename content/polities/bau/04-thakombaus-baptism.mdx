Victory over Rewa did not by itself settle the question that had
hung over Thakombau since the darkest days of the war. At the
commencement of 1857 he dismissed the many wives that were among the
clearest marks of a Fijian chief's wealth and standing, and was
publicly married to his chief Queen alone - a sacrifice the source
records as one strangers to Fiji could hardly appreciate the size of.
With that difficulty removed, the Vunivalu and his Queen were publicly
baptized on the 11th of January, 1857.

The missionary who performed the ceremony left a description of the
congregation that gathered to watch it: husbands whose wives
Thakombau had dishonoured, widows whose husbands he had killed,
relatives of people he had ordered strangled, and children descended
from men he had eaten and who had sworn to avenge them. Before this
same assembly, a man who had once accepted near-divine honours as a
living god stood and confessed, in his own words, that he had been a
bad man, that he had disturbed the country, and that he had once told
the missionaries he would go on fighting rather than embrace their
religion. He took the baptismal name Ebenezer, in acknowledgment,
he said, of God's own hand in preserving him through the fighting of
1854 and 1855 - the war that had nearly cost him Bau itself. His Queen
took the name Lydia.

## An old order's last king

Thakombau's baptism did not end Fijian politics, and it did not end
his own rule; he remained Vunivalu of Bau, and the towns that had
returned to his allegiance the previous year stayed returned. What it
closed instead was the world Bau's own rise had been fought in - a
world of priest-sanctioned war, cannibal feasts celebrating a father's
restoration, and a young man's name taken from the terror of a single
night's arson. The king who once
answered a missionary's offer of escape with "I am lotu" had, by
January of 1857, made that declaration in front of the very people his
old religion and his old wars had most directly harmed.
