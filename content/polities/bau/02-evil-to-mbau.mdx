The rebels who held Bau during Tanoa's exile were not unanimous about
what to do with his son. The old chief of Viwa, one of the revolt's
own leaders, warned that Seru was a clever youth and ought to be
killed before he could do them any harm; the others judged a mere
boy no threat, and let him stay. It was the last miscalculation the
rebellion made.

Seru spent the time he was allowed quietly building a following. He
won over men of real influence to his exiled father's cause, among
them his own close friend Verani, who happened to be nephew to the
same Viwa chief who had wanted Seru dead. When the moment came, the
plan was executed with a single night's work: the quarter of Bau held
by Tanoa's remaining loyalists was fenced off from the rest of the
town, and the rebel quarter beside it was set alight. The rebels fled
in confusion to the mainland, where they were eventually delivered up
to Tanoa - restored, by his son's own hand, to the throne the
rebellion had taken from him.

## A name that meant terror

Bau's welcome home for its returning king matched the violence of the
coup that had won it back. Captured rebel chiefs were killed and
eaten to celebrate Tanoa's restoration - two bodies were still in the
ovens when the first Wesleyan missionary reached the island in
1837 - and Seru himself took a new name from the episode: Thakombau,
meaning evil to Bau, for the destruction and terror his coup had
caused the very town it saved.

Tanoa reigned nearly fifteen years more after his restoration, growing
old, proud, and no gentler than before - vain enough to blacken his
grey hair with powder, and still capable of the mutilations and
executions that had marked his rule from early on. Age eventually
left the active work of government to a son serving as regent in all
but name. When Tanoa died on the 8th of December, 1852, it was
Thakombau - the boy once thought too harmless to kill - who succeeded
him as Vunivalu of Bau.
