Nizam al-Mulk was assassinated in 1092 and Malikshah died a month later, and
the empire never had a single undisputed ruler again. What it had instead, for
sixty-five years, was Sanjar.

He is the most consequential figure the dynasty produced after Malikshah and
the least written about, because his career is a slow one and ends badly.

## What he actually did

Sanjar was made king of Khurasan by his brother Barkiyaruq, held it from 1096,
and was senior in the house from 1118 until his death in 1157. Browne, reading
the Seljuk chroniclers, reports Rawandi's assessment plainly: in length of life
and in brilliant achievement Sanjar surpassed all the other Seljuk monarchs.

The record supports it. In forty years he made nineteen conquests. He took
Ghazna and installed Bahramshah on its throne, on terms of a thousand dinars a
day. He captured Ahmad Khan, the Karakhanid ruler of Samarkand, in 1130. He
subdued Sistan and Khwarazm. Khurasan, through the fratricidal wars that
convulsed the rest of the family, stayed largely out of it, because he governed
it firmly.

And none of that arrested the decline, which is the point.

## Why not

Browne lists the reasons and they are structural rather than personal.

The fratricidal wars at the beginning of the period had already dispersed the
authority. The revolts of turbulent amirs were constant. And two or three
rival powers were always in the field: the kings of the mountains of Ghur in
the east, whose rising power would shortly finish the house of Ghazna; and, from
Atsiz's accession in 1127, a new dynasty of Khwarazmshahs who became formidable
rivals inside a generation — and who were the Seljuks' own appointees.

Then in 1141 Sanjar took an army east against the Qara Khitai and was destroyed
at the Qatwan steppe outside Samarkand. Transoxiana passed under the protection
of a Buddhist steppe empire, and the news of a Muslim sultan beaten by a
non-Muslim power from beyond the Oxus travelled west and became, in Europe, the
story of Prester John.

## The Ghuzz

The end is worse than the defeat.

In 1153 Sanjar was beaten by the Ghuzz — Turkmen of the same stock his own
family had come from a century and a quarter earlier, and whom his officials had
been mishandling. Tus and Nishapur were sacked, and many of their inhabitants
killed, including, Browne notes, some of the most celebrated of them for
learning and piety.

Sanjar himself was taken. For three years he was practically a prisoner in the
hands of the Ghuzz: treated, outwardly, with some respect, and unable to go
where he wished or to protect his own people while the province he had governed
for sixty years was ruined around him.

In the autumn of 1156 a few of his old retainers bribed his custodians and got
him out, and brought him safely to Merv, where he began to collect an army.
Grief at the desolation of his country, with old age, killed him a few months
later. He was buried at Merv, in a building he had put up himself, beside his
grandfather Alp Arslan.

## What was left

Browne's judgement is that with Sanjar's death the epoch of the Great Seljuks
came to an end, and the political record bears him out: what continued for
another thirty-seven years was a title, contested among cousins in western Iran,
while the atabegs held the provinces and Khwarazm grew.

The devastation the Ghuzz did to Khurasan was frightful, Browne says, and was
eclipsed some seventy years later by the Mongols. That is the honest scale of
it: the worst thing to happen to eastern Iran in the twelfth century, and a
rehearsal.
