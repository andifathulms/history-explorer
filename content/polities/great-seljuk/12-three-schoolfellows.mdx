The most famous story about the Seljuk age is that Nizam al-Mulk, Omar Khayyam
and Hasan-i Sabbah were at school together.

It appears in the preface of almost every edition of FitzGerald's *Rubaiyat*,
which is how most of the English-speaking world encountered Persian history at
all. It is a very good story. It is also, on the dates, impossible.

## The story

Browne prints the oldest surviving form of it, from Rashid al-Din's universal
history, composed in the first quarter of the fourteenth century — two hundred
years after all three men were dead:

> Now the cause of the enmity and mistrust which existed between the Nizam
> al-Mulk and Hasan-i Sabbah was that they and Omar Khayyam were at school
> together in Nishapur, and there, in boyish fashion, conceived for one another
> a devoted friendship which culminated in their partaking of each other's blood
> and registering a solemn vow that whichever of them should attain to high rank
> and lofty degree should protect and help the others.

Nizam al-Mulk becomes prime minister. Omar comes to claim the promise, is
offered the governorship of Nishapur, and refuses it — being, the text says, a
great man and withal a philosopher and a man of sense — asking instead for a
pension, and receiving ten thousand dinars a year from the treasury of Nishapur
without deduction. Then Hasan-i Sabbah arrives to claim his share, and the rest
of the story is the intrigue that ends with the vizier assassinated by the
grand master's men.

## Why it cannot be true

Nizam al-Mulk was born in 1017. Hasan-i Sabbah and Omar Khayyam both died
between 1123 and 1124, at ages nobody records.

For the three of them to have been schoolfellows, both of the younger men would
have had to reach a hundred — and even then, Browne points out, they would still
have been considerably younger than the vizier, who appears to have finished his
education and entered public life early. The odds against two named individuals
both living to a hundred are not good. The objection was pressed by Houtsma and
is, on any reading, fatal.

Houtsma's own suggestion is the elegant part. The schoolfellow was probably not
the famous Nizam al-Mulk at all, but Anushirvan b. Khalid — a later and much
less famous minister, to a Seljuk prince who reigned from 1117 — whose own
chronicle says, in the passage where the Assassins first appear, that in his
youth he had studied with some of their chief leaders, and particularly with a
man of Ray who travelled about the world and whose profession was that of a
secretary.

That is Hasan-i Sabbah, described by somebody who had actually known him. Two
centuries later the anecdote had been transferred to the celebrated vizier, and
a third schoolboy had been added.

## What that is an afterlife of

This is the fifty-year state's real inheritance, and it is not the roads or the
madrasas.

Everything about the Great Seljuks that a general reader knows arrives through
later Persian writers with a shape to impose: Nizam al-Mulk as the wise Persian
vizier, Hasan-i Sabbah as the sinister organiser, Omar Khayyam as the sceptical
astronomer who wanted a pension rather than power. It is a very satisfying
triangle — administration, subversion and detachment, the three things one can
do about a state — and it was assembled two hundred years afterwards out of an
anecdote about a different man.

The empire itself was a family holding that came apart within a generation of
its best sultan. What survived it in working order was the vizier's book about
how to run one. What survived it in general circulation was a story about three
boys in Nishapur, which is not true, and which is the version that reached
England.
