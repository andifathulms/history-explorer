In 1055 Tughril entered Baghdad, arrested al-Malik al-Rahim, and ended a
hundred and ten years of Buyid control. The caliph al-Qa'im received him and
granted him the sultanate.

<Figure id="tughril-dinar" />

Read that next to 945 and almost nothing has changed. A military power from
outside Iraq takes the city, leaves the caliph in place, and receives from him a
formal grant of the authority it already possesses. Same building, same office,
same transaction.

Two things had changed, and both mattered. The new masters were Sunni, so the
grant could be presented as a restoration rather than a captivity — the caliph
delivered from heterodox amirs by an orthodox sultan. And the title was
*sultan*, which under the Seljuks stopped being an honorific attached to a
particular strongman and became a standing office, with a chancery, a
succession, and a defined relationship to the caliphate: the caliph holds
religious authority, the sultan holds temporal power, and each legitimates the
other.

That division of labour outlived the Seljuks by centuries and was copied by
almost everybody who came after them. It is the most durable thing the dynasty
produced, and it is not a piece of territory.
