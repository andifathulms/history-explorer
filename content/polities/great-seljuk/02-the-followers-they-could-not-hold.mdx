title: The followers they could not hold

The Seljuk family won at Dandanaqan with an army of Oghuz Turkmen and spent the
next fifty years trying to work out what to do with them.

The problem is structural and it is the opposite of every other military
problem in this thread. The Samanids, Ghaznavids and Buyids had armies they had
bought or hired, which could be paid, quartered and — as those pages all
record — eventually turned on their employers. The Seljuks had a following.
Turkmen pastoralists had come across the Oxus with their households and their
flocks because the grazing was better and the leadership was Seljuk, and they
expected pasture, plunder and a chief who behaved like one. They were not
soldiers in a chancery's sense and could not be discharged.

What a settled state needs from that is exactly what it cannot have. Tughril
and his successors set about becoming the thing they had beaten: a Persian
bureaucracy, a tax base in irrigated agriculture, an alliance with the urban
Sunni establishment, and cities that had to be protected from raiding — which
meant protected from their own following. Within a generation the sultans were
recruiting slave soldiers of the usual kind and paying them with the *iqta*
described later on this page, because a professional guard obeys and a tribal
confederation negotiates.

The Turkmen were therefore surplus, and they went where the grazing and the
raiding were: west, into the Armenian highlands and the Byzantine frontier
districts, in numbers and largely on their own initiative. Seljuk policy was
less to direct them than to point them at somebody else and be relieved when
they went.

That is the background to the next chapter but one, and it is why the battle
there mattered so much more than its scale. The Turkmen were already on the
frontier in force. What Manzikert removed was the thing that had been keeping
them out of the country behind it.
