---
title: A child on the throne
drafted_from: bar-hebraeus-budge
phase: contraction
---

Turkan Khatun got what she had wanted and it lasted about two years.

Bar Hebraeus, who calls her a woman of intelligence and traces her descent to
the first king of the Turks, says she administered the kingdom herself after
Malikshah's death and had her son Mahmud proclaimed sultan at Baghdad at the age
of five. He preserves what the court made of the boy. The caliph's men dressed
him in royal apparel and set him on the throne, and he put out neither a hand nor
a foot, did not move his eyes, did not bend or salute, and moved no part of
himself at all; he sat so exactly like a stone that everyone present marvelled at
him. It is offered as evidence of the child's intelligence. It reads now as a
description of a frightened five-year-old who had been told not to move.

Other Turks raised his half-brother Barkiyaruq, a son of Malikshah by his
cousin. Barkiyaruq had ten thousand men and Mahmud twenty thousand, and when the
two armies met, Mahmud's nobles crossed over in the middle of it. Then the
dead sultan's brother, Tutush, who held Mosul, Nisibis, Mayyafariqin, Syria and
Damascus, came up into Azerbaijan; his nephew went to meet him and the two of
them talked in a village near Tabriz called Sa'idabad, after which Tutush went
back to Damascus and two of his amirs took service with the nephew. Barkiyaruq
went on to Baghdad, where the caliph dressed him in royal apparel, wrote him a
patent and proclaimed him sultan — the same ceremony, in the same room, for the
third time in forty years, performed now for one claimant among several while the
others were still in the field.

In 1094 Turkan Khatun died, and her son had nothing left but Isfahan. Barkiyaruq
came to the city with a small escort; Mahmud's officers shut the gates against
him, then reconsidered, decided it would be better to let him in and seize him,
and opened them. He had been inside one day when his seven-year-old brother
caught a violent fever and died of it the same day. So the officers made peace
instead and proclaimed the survivor king of Isfahan.

Bar Hebraeus reports the fever without comment, which is his habit and is
probably the right treatment. What the sequence establishes is not who poisoned
whom but what the succession had become. A sultanate that had been handed from
Tughril to Alp Arslan to Malikshah without a war was now decided by which
faction's amirs changed sides during the battle, and by whether a child in a
besieged city lived through the week.

Barkiyaruq held the title for twelve more years and spent almost all of them
fighting relations for it. What his father had ruled was never again ruled by
one person.
