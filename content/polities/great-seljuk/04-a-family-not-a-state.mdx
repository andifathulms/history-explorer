title: A family and not a state

The Seljuks held their empire as a family possession. Territory was appanaged
to brothers, sons and nephews, each with an *atabeg* — a Turkish commander
appointed as guardian to a young prince, running the province in his name.

The arrangement worked while there was a strong sultan to arbitrate. After 1092
there was not, and the mechanism inverted with total predictability: the
atabegs kept the provinces and the princes became the formality. The Zangids in
Mosul and Aleppo, the Eldiguzids in Azerbaijan and the Salghurids in Fars all
begin as guardians of Seljuk children.

Meanwhile the eastern frontier produced the same story in its purest form. The
Seljuk sultans appointed a governor to Khwarazm; the office became hereditary;
by the 1190s the Khwarazmshahs were stronger than the sultans who had appointed
them. In 1194 Tughril III, the last Great Seljuk, was killed in battle against
Ala al-Din Tekish of Khwarazm.

The Seljuks are the only polity in this corpus destroyed by an office it created
itself. The Ghaznavids were ended by a vassal house they had not made; the
Samanids by a slave-general who had gone east on his own initiative. Khwarazm
was a Seljuk appointment from beginning to end, and it took the sultanate's
titles along with the sultanate.

Two branches outlived the centre — Rum in Anatolia until 1308, and Kirman —
which is why the sultanate went on being a thing that could be claimed long
after there was no longer anyone to claim it from.
