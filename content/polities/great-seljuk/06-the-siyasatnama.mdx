Nizam al-Mulk wrote the *Siyasatnama* — the book of government — for Malik-Shah
towards the end of his thirty years running the empire, and it is the most
revealing document any polity in this corpus produced about itself.

It is a mirror for princes, the genre this site's Karakhanid page describes
Yusuf of Balasaghun working in a few years earlier, and it belongs to a
tradition reaching back through Persian and Arabic advice literature to
Sasanian models. What makes it different from most of the genre is that it was
written by the man who was actually doing the job, about the machine he had
actually built, for the sultan he had actually managed.

So it is specific where the genre is usually pious. It explains how to run a
postal and intelligence service and why a ruler who does not have one will be
lied to. It explains that assignment-holders must be inspected because a man
collecting his own revenue will ruin the district — which this site's Buyid
page describes happening to the Sawad a century earlier, and which the
*iqta* chapter before this one describes Nizam al-Mulk systematising anyway. It
argues for salaried judges, for audiences at which subjects can reach the
sultan without an intermediary, and for keeping soldiers of several different
origins so that no one group can dictate. It also devotes long stretches to
heretics — meaning, in the 1080s, the Isma'ilis of Alamut on this site's Nizari
page, whose emissary is traditionally credited with killing him in 1092.

The strain in the book is the interesting part. Nizam al-Mulk was a Persian
administrator serving a Turkish military family, and he is arguing throughout
for the bureaucratic, sedentary, tax-collecting order against the instincts of
the people he served: for records against improvisation, for salaried officers
against tribal claims, for the Sunni scholarly establishment his madrasas
produced against everything else. He is not describing the Seljuk state so much
as recommending it.

He was assassinated in 1092 and Malik-Shah died a month later. The empire never
again had a single undisputed ruler, and the arrangement in this book was
running the Islamic world for the next four centuries anyway — which is the
peculiar thing about the Great Seljuks. Their state lasted about fifty years in
working order. What their vizier wrote down outlasted every polity on this page.
