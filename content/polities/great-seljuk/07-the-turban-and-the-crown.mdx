title: The turban and the crown

Nizam al-Mulk was dismissed before he was killed, and the dismissal is the part
worth having, because it explains the twenty years better than the thirty do.

The pressure came from inside the household. Malikshah's favourite wife, Turkan
Khatun, wanted the succession for her small son Mahmud; the vizier was known to
favour the elder Barkiyaruq, a boy of twelve or thirteen. Around this ran the
ordinary complaint of a long ministry, which was that the vizier's relations
held the best posts in the empire. One of his grandsons, governing Merv, behaved
badly enough that a victim took the case to the sultan directly, and Malikshah
sent Nizam al-Mulk an angry message asking, with heavy irony, whether he was his
partner in the throne or his minister.

The old man answered rashly and it became the most quoted sentence of the reign:
he who gave you the Crown placed the Turban on my head, and the two are bound
together inseparably. Browne, who is more careful with such things than the
authors he is reading, notes that the words come from the *Rahat al-Sudur* and
that the fullest account of the quarrel is Ibn al-Athir's, and observes that a
remark like that, reported to a sultan with the usual improvements, is not one
its author would have made in a calmer hour. Nizam al-Mulk was dismissed in
favour of Taj al-Mulk, Turkan Khatun's man, along with a set of other
replacements that a court poet promptly turned into a lament: if you were tired
of Nizam and Kamal and Sharaf, see what Taj and Majd and Sadid have done to you.

He did not long survive it. On 14 October 1092, travelling with the sultan from
Isfahan to Baghdad, he halted near Nahavand — where the last Sasanian army had
been destroyed four and a half centuries earlier — broke his fast after sunset,
and was walking to his family's tents when a young Daylami came up to him in the
posture of a petitioner and stabbed him. The killer belonged to the new order of
*fida'is* that Hasan-i Sabbah had just instituted, and this is generally called
their first stroke, though Ibn al-Athir records an earlier murder of a muezzin at
Isfahan and thinks the vizier's execution of a carpenter suspected in it was what
marked him. It was also widely said that Taj al-Mulk, the rival who had replaced
him, had arranged it; if so the arrangement did not pay, because Nizam al-Mulk's
servants killed Taj al-Mulk four months later.

Malikshah outlived him by three weeks. He went hunting on 6 November, either
took a chill or ate something that disagreed with him, was bled, developed a
fever and died on the 19th, not yet forty. Bar Hebraeus, writing from other
sources, says his own slave gave him poison. The poet Mu'izzi produced the couplet
everyone remembers — one month the aged minister was translated to heaven, the
next month the young king followed him, and what impotence the power of God
brings upon earthly power — having already written, on the dismissal, that the
sultan had set the accursed Taj over his dominions and jeopardised for his sake
both Crown and State.

Browne then does the thing he is most useful for. There is a famous set of
verses supposed to have been written by the dying vizier and sent to Malikshah:
thirty years cleansing the earth of tyranny, a warrant signed by the sultan to
carry to the Angel of the Throne, ninety-six years of life ended by violence near
Nahavand, and a son commended to the king's care. Browne shows it is fabricated.
The last verse is demonstrably by Burhani, Malikshah's own laureate, written to
recommend his son Mu'izzi to the royal favour; the *Chahar Maqala* says Nizam
al-Mulk thought little of poets because he had no skill in the art; he was about
seventy-five, not ninety-six; and his sons had, as everyone complained, already
been given more than enough. A minister who had spent three decades making the
state's institutions was handed, within a century of his death, the deathbed
poem of somebody else.
