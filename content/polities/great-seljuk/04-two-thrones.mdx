---
title: Two thrones, one of them lower
drafted_from: bar-hebraeus-budge

Bar Hebraeus watched the arrangement being made physical, and described the
furniture.

Tughril rode to the caliph's palace and waited outside on his horse until the
gate was opened. He crossed the courtyard; the caliph's nobles came out to meet
him; slaves lifted one curtain and then another, and behind the second al-Qa'im
was sitting on a throne raised about seven cubits off the ground, in a black
headcloth and a black cloak — the Abbasid colours, as Bar Hebraeus is careful to
note, the Fatimids in Egypt having taken white — with a gold sceptre in his hand
and a eunuch on either side. The sultan bowed to the ground and did homage
several times. Then the caliph gave an order and the slaves raised him onto a
second throne, lower than the first, about the height of a standing man.

The caliph did not speak to him directly. He spoke to a dragoman, who said to
the sultan: the Commander of the Faithful praises your solicitude, applauds your
labours, is comforted by your presence and rejoices in you; and behold, he hands
over to you all the provinces and countries which God has handed over to him;
therefore fear God, do justice in your dominion, and give thanks. The sultan
stood, bowed to the ground again, and said that he was the slave and the subject
of that command.

Then he was dressed. Seven black robes, one over another; a black headcloth shot
with gold; on top of that a crown set with two jacinths, each with fifteen very
fine pearls above it, and gold bands at the ends. The collar of gold he would
not put round his neck: it was laid on his shoulders instead, and a eunuch
carried it in front of him. When he wanted to prostrate himself a third time he
found he could not, because the crown was too heavy for him to bend. He was
given three purple banners lettered in gold, and a royal seal, and the heralds
proclaimed him King of the East and the West. On leaving he asked the caliph to
put out his hand, kissed it twice, and passed it across his forehead.

Everything in that room is doing the same work. One throne is higher and one is
lower; the higher man does not address the lower man in his own voice; the lower
man is given the provinces in a sentence that says God gave them to the man
handing them over; and the lower man is dressed in so much of the higher man's
authority that he physically cannot bow again.

And the man on the lower throne had the army, and both of them knew it. The
arrangement worked for two centuries precisely because neither party tested it.
The caliph's grant was the only thing that made a Turkmen chief the lawful ruler
of Iraq, and the sultan's soldiers were the only thing that kept a caliph in
Baghdad at all. Bar Hebraeus, who was a Christian bishop with no stake in either
of them, describes the ceremony as a foreigner describes a rite: attentively,
and without saying what it meant.
