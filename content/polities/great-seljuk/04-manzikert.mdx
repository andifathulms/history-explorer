title: Manzikert

On 26 August 1071, near Manzikert north of Lake Van, Alp Arslan met a Byzantine
army under the emperor Romanos IV Diogenes and destroyed it. Romanos was
captured — the first Roman emperor taken alive by a Muslim army — brought
before the sultan, treated with public courtesy, given terms, and sent home.

Alp Arslan had not wanted the battle. He was campaigning towards Syria and
Fatimid Egypt, which was the direction the Seljuk state's ambitions actually
ran; the Byzantine expedition was an interruption he turned to deal with. He
died the following year in Transoxiana. Nothing in Seljuk policy after 1071
suggests that anyone in Isfahan regarded Anatolia as the prize.

The consequences are nonetheless the largest of any single day in this corpus.

What broke was not the Byzantine army so much as the Byzantine settlement.
Romanos returned deposed and was blinded; the empire spent the following decade
in civil war, and the rival claimants hired Turkmen bands against each other and
opened the passes to do it. The frontier system that had held the Anatolian
plateau — the themes, the local levies, the border lords — dissolved in that
decade rather than on that afternoon, and the Turkmen who had been pressing on
it since the 1050s moved in behind. Within twenty years there was a Turkish
sultanate at Nicaea, a hundred miles from Constantinople. That is this site's
Sultanate of Rum, on its Anatolia page, and it lasted two hundred and thirty
years.

Three of the largest facts in this corpus run through the gap that opened here.
The Byzantine empire on this site, having lost the manpower and revenue of the
plateau, appealed west and got the First Crusade, which arrived in 1095 and took
Jerusalem in 1099 from the Fatimids — recorded on their page as the loss of the
coast. Anatolia became Turkish-speaking and Muslim, which it had not been, and
remained so. And the Seljuk dynasty acquired a branch in the west that outlived
the trunk by a century.

None of that was the plan. The sultanate that won at Manzikert was facing the
other way.
