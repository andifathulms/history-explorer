In about 1332 Ibn Battuta came ashore on the southern coast and walked across
what had been the Sultanate of Rum. His itinerary is the best surviving
description of what replaced it, and the striking thing about it is that he
records who ruled every single place, because it was different every time.

Alanya: Turkmen, under Yusuf Beg son of Karaman. Milas: Orhan Beg son of
Menteshe, whom he calls a very excellent prince. Birgi: Muhammad ibn Aydin, who
showed him a meteorite. Konya and Larande: Badr al-Din ibn Karaman. Aksaray,
Niğde, Kayseri, Sivas, Amasya, Erzincan, Erzurum: all of them, he says, subject
to the king of Iraq — meaning the Ilkhan, eight hundred miles away at Tabriz.

Not one sultan of Rum. A generation after the last Seljuk stopped being
mentioned, a traveller crossing the peninsula met half a dozen rulers and a
foreign tax administration, and the country's name was still Rum.

## Konya

He found it large and handsome, abounding in water and gardens, and belonging to
Karaman — though the king of Iraq, he notes, has occasionally had the rule of it
too.

He lodged with the qadi, a member of the brotherhood of youths, in a beautiful
cell with a great many disciples who traced the authority for their legal
opinions back to Ali. And he went to the tomb of the Shaykh Jalal al-Din, *better
known by the title of Mawlana*, who was, he says, very highly esteemed.

Then he tells the story he was told there, which is not the story anybody tells
now.

## The sweetmeat seller

The Shaykh, Ibn Battuta was informed, had at first been an ordinary lecturing
doctor with a large number of pupils. One day a stranger came into the
lecture-room with a basket of sweetmeats on his head, which he was selling. The
Shaykh said: bring your basket here. The man took out a piece and gave it to
him, and he ate it.

Then the man went out — nobody else having tasted the sweetmeat — and the Shaykh
became agitated and went out after him, abandoning his reading and leaving his
pupils sitting there expecting him back. They eventually went looking and could
not find where he had gone.

Some years later he returned with his mind deranged, speaking nothing but Persian
verses. His pupils followed him about writing down what he said, and published it
under the title of the *Mathnawi*, a book, Ibn Battuta adds, highly esteemed in
these parts.

That is Shams of Tabriz sixty years on, remembered in Konya as a man with a tray
of confectionery. It is a folk version and it is wrong in every detail, and it
has the shape of the thing exactly right: a stranger came in, the teacher ate
something and followed him out, and the man who came back was not the man who
left.

## What was actually holding the country together

Not a sultanate, and not the Ilkhanate either.

In every Turkmen town, Ibn Battuta says, there was a brotherhood of young men —
the *akhis* — and no people on earth were more courteous to strangers, more ready
to supply them with food, or more opposed to oppressors. A tradesman around whom
the young men of his craft and any destitute foreigners collected would be made
their president. He built a cell and put a horse and a saddle in it. In the
evening the members brought in everything they had earned that day, and it was
sold to buy food and fruit for the house. A traveller who arrived was taken in
and kept until he left the country.

Ibn Battuta was fed by them from one end of Anatolia to the other, which is why
his account of the beyliks is so detailed: he was passed along a network that
existed to receive people like him.

## The carpets

Sixty years before him, Marco Polo had crossed the same country going the other
way, and his chapter on it is one page long.

There are three sorts of people in Turcomania, he says. The Turkomans, who keep
cattle in the mountains and downs and breed excellent horses and very valuable
mules. And the Armenians and the Greeks, who live mixed among them in the towns
and villages, occupying themselves with trade and handicrafts, and who *weave the
finest and handsomest carpets in the world*, and quantities of rich silks. The
chief cities are Konya, Sivas and Kayseri. The people are subject to the Tartar
of the Levant.

He was writing while a Seljuk sultan was still nominally on the throne at Konya,
and he does not mention that there is a sultan. What he mentions is the
suzerain, the towns, and what was for sale.

Between the two travellers the whole afterlife is there. The state disappeared
without anybody outside noticing much. The country went on: three populations
living mixed in the same towns, a craft network feeding strangers, a shrine at
Konya, and the best carpets in the world.
