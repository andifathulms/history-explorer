Between roughly 1200 and 1240 the Sultanate of Rum was one of the better-run
states in the eastern Mediterranean, and almost nothing in the popular picture
of medieval Anatolia reflects it.

The sultanate had spent its first century as a frontier polity among rivals —
Danishmendids to the east, Byzantines to the west, crusading armies passing
through twice — and it emerged from that with Konya, most of the plateau, and
under Kayqubad I from 1220 a coastline: Antalya on the Mediterranean and
Sinop on the Black Sea, taken deliberately, because a landlocked plateau state
that controls two ports controls the route between them.

The two ports are the whole of the strategy and they were taken in that order
and for that reason. Sinop in 1214 gave the sultanate the Black Sea and the
Crimean trade beyond it. Alanya on the Mediterranean, taken in 1221 and rebuilt
with a dockyard and a tower still standing, gave it the other end. Antalya had
come in a few years earlier. What lay between them was two hundred and fifty
miles of plateau that the sultanate already held.

A landlocked state with two coasts is not landlocked; it is a bridge, and it can
charge for the crossing. Everything the sultanate is remembered for materially —
the roads, the caravanserais, the customs revenue, the treaties with Venice and
with the Italian communes of the Crimea — follows from a decision to buy both
ends of a line and then make the line worth using.

## And he saw them coming

Kayqubad also did the one thing that mattered in the 1220s, which was to take the
Mongols seriously before he had to.

He fortified. He took Erzurum in 1230, which put a garrison on the eastern
approach. He fought and then negotiated with the Khwarazmian remnant under Jalal
al-Din, who was the wreckage of the last state the Mongols had destroyed and was
now on his frontier. And he sent an embassy east, which is the act of a ruler
who has understood that the thing coming towards him is not a raid.

He died in 1237, four years after the Mongols reached Azerbaijan. The
arrangements he had made were tested six years after that, by his son, and did
not survive the test.
