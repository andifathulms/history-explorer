The two ports and the fortified frontier were the state seen from its treasury
and its garrisons. Seen from its own court, the same forty years produced
something the customs revenue alone does not explain: a Turkish dynasty
running a Persian-speaking government over a Greek- and Armenian-speaking
country, and doing it well enough that a scholar's family fleeing the
Mongols chose to settle there rather than anywhere else available to them.

What it looked like by 1230 was a court.

## A Persian state in a Greek country

The administration worked in Persian and the chancery style was Iranian. The
sultans took their throne-names out of the *Shahnama* — Kayqubad, Kaykhusraw,
Kaykavus, the names of legendary Iranian kings, borne by Turks ruling in
Anatolia. They married Byzantine, Georgian and Armenian princesses, and several
of them had Christian mothers.

None of that is decoration. A Turkish family adopting a Persian bureaucratic
order is the standard move of the period — the Great Seljuks had done it in Iran
two generations earlier — but here the order was carried into a country that had
been Greek-speaking and Christian for a thousand years, and it did not displace
what it found. Greeks and Armenians stayed in the administration. Christian
officials held serious office. Byzantine formulae turn up on the coinage.

The result is a state whose ruling family were Turks, whose paperwork was
Persian, whose taxpayers were Greek and Armenian, and whose sultans were named
after Iranian heroes out of a poem written three centuries earlier and two
thousand miles east.

## Why it is not remembered

Partly because it did not last, and partly because of what came next.

The century after 1243 gave Anatolia a Mongol tributary regime, then a dozen
squabbling beyliks, then the Ottomans, and the Ottoman account of its own origins
had no particular use for a Seljuk state that had governed the peninsula
competently and then been beaten in an afternoon. Rum survives in the popular
picture mainly as the thing the crusaders marched past and the thing the Mongols
knocked over.

What it actually was is on the ground: several dozen caravanserais, the mosques
and medreses of Konya, Sivas and Divriği, a working coinage, treaties with
Venice, and a court that could attract a Khurasani scholar's family fleeing the
Mongols and give them somewhere to teach for fifty years.
