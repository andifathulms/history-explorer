On 28 November 1244, a man in coarse black felt walked into Konya.

Nicholson gives the date precisely and then admits that almost nothing else
about Shams of Tabriz is fixed: his parentage is disputed, his birthplace is
disputed, and the traditions about him contradict each other on nearly every
point. He calls him a weird figure who flits across the stage for a moment and
disappears tragically enough. What Shams did in Konya was to take a
forty-something professor of religious law, with a chair and a following and a
reputation, and comprehensively unmake him.

The sultanate had lost its army at Kösedağ seventeen months earlier.

## The professor

Jalal al-Din's family had left Balkh in Khurasan ahead of the Mongol advance and
travelled west, arriving in Anatolia in the 1220s while Kayqubad was recruiting
scholars. He taught at Konya for most of his life and died there in 1273. He is,
on most estimates, the most widely read Persian poet who ever lived, and the
name the world knows him by — Rumi, the man of Rum — is the name of the
sultanate.

After Shams, and after Shams disappeared, he wrote. The lyrics were published
under Shams's name rather than his own, which is either the most extravagant
act of attribution in Persian literature or a statement about who was speaking.
One couplet Nicholson quotes puts the break at a specific age:

> Forty years did Reason plunge me in care;
> At three score and two I was made a prey.

## What he wrote in a country of four languages

The best-known of the poems is a list of everything he says he is not:

> I am neither Christian, nor Jew, nor Gabr, nor Moslem.
> I am not of the East, nor of the West, nor of the land, nor of the sea;
> …
> I am not of India, nor of China, nor of Bulgaria, nor of Saqsin;
> I am not of the kingdom of 'Iraqain, nor of the country of Khorasan.
> …
> My place is the Placeless, my trace is the Traceless.

It is worth reading that where it was written rather than as a devotional
generality. The man setting down that he is not of the East nor of the West was
a refugee from Khurasan, living in a Greek-speaking city, in a Turkish
sultanate, whose administration ran in Persian, under the protection of Mongols
who had not yet become Muslim, in a household where the audience for a public
lecture would include Turks, Persians, Greeks and Armenians. The *Masnavi* has
lines of Greek and Turkish in it because the people in the room spoke them.

A poem announcing that its author belongs to no nation and no confession was
composed in about the only place in the thirteenth-century world where that
would have sounded like a description rather than a provocation.

## The inversion

The other thing worth putting on the record is the timing, because it is the
opposite of what one expects.

The decades that produced Rumi, the historian Ibn Bibi, the caravanserais, the
Konya and Sivas architecture, and the Mevlevi order that grew out of Rumi's
funeral are the decades in which the sultanate was a Mongol tributary being
administered from Tabriz. The political collapse and the cultural peak are the
same period. Whatever relation there is between a state's strength and what gets
made under it, this is not an example of it.

The order that formed around his memory became an institutional fixture of
Anatolia and then of the Ottoman empire for six hundred years — a longer run
than the sultanate that housed him managed, by a factor of four.
