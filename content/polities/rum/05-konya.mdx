title: What was written at Konya
drafted_from: peacock-yildiz-2013

Jalal al-Din Rumi came to Konya as a boy, taught there for most of his life,
and died there in 1273. He is, on most estimates, the most widely read Persian
poet who ever lived, and he wrote in a Turkish sultanate in a Greek-speaking
country under Mongol suzerainty.

His family had left Balkh in Khurasan ahead of the Mongol advance — the
displacement this site's Khwarazmian page describes — and travelled west,
arriving in Anatolia in the 1220s when Kayqubad was recruiting scholars.
Rumi's Persian is Khurasani; his subject was a Sufism at home anywhere; his
audience at Konya included Turks, Persians, Greeks and Armenians, and the
*Mathnawi* has passages of Greek and Turkish in it because the people around
him spoke them.

The order that formed around his memory, the Mevlevis, became one of the
institutional fixtures of Anatolia and then of the Ottoman empire for six
hundred years, which is a longer run than the sultanate that housed him
managed.

Two things this puts on the record. The first is that the century the previous
chapter describes as a political collapse is the century of Rum's cultural
peak — Rumi, the historian Ibn Bibi, the caravanserais of the chapter before
last, and the Konya architecture — most of it produced while the state was a
Mongol tributary. The correlation this corpus keeps assuming between political
strength and cultural production does not hold here, and the Ilkhanate page
records the same inversion a generation later.

The second is about what this site can carry. Rum's rating panel has no cited
extent, no cited population, and a religion count of one. It has no field for
the fact that the most-read poet in the Persian language wrote here, in a
sultanate whose administration worked in Persian, in a country where most
people spoke Greek, under the protection of Mongols who were not yet Muslim.
That sentence is the polity, and it is entirely prose.
