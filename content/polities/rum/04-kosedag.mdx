On 26 June 1243, at Kösedağ in eastern Anatolia, a Mongol army under Baiju met
the forces of Kaykhusraw II and destroyed them in a few hours. The sultan fled
before the fighting had finished. There was no siege, no long campaign, and no
second battle.

What followed is why this record types the ending `gradual absorption` and
dates it sixty-five years later. The Mongols did not occupy Anatolia or
depose the dynasty. They imposed tribute and took the sultanate as a vassal,
and the Seljuk house went on sitting at Konya for another two generations while
Ilkhanid governors collected the revenue and decided, increasingly openly, who
would sit there. Rival sultans were installed by rival Mongol factions; the
country was partitioned between brothers by Mongol arbitration; a rebellion in
the 1270s was put down by a Mongol army. By the time the last Seljuk of Rum
disappears from the record around 1308, the sultanate had not governed anything
for decades.

Two consequences run out of that, and both are on this site.

The Ilkhanate on the Mongol World page is the polity that inherited the
revenue, and its page describes the Persian bureaucratic order it took over
wholesale — the same order the previous chapter describes Rum importing into
Anatolia.

And the Turkmen did what Turkmen in this corpus do when central authority
weakens. The frontier marches filled with independent beyliks — a dozen or more
of them, each with a warband and a stretch of country — because the sultanate
could no longer restrain them and the Ilkhanate did not care to. One of those,
in the north-west, on the Byzantine frontier, was the one this site's Ottoman
page begins with.

Manzikert opened Anatolia and this sultanate organised it. Kösedağ broke the
organisation and left the country full of small Turkish states, which is the
condition the Ottomans grew out of. The two battles are a hundred and seventy
years apart and the second one made the first one's consequences permanent.
