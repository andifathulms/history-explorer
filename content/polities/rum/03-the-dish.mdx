The sultanate became the only Turkish power in Anatolia under Kilij Arslan II,
who ruled for nearly forty years, absorbed the Danishmendids, beat the Byzantine
emperor at Myriokephalon and outlived every rival. Bar Hebraeus, writing from
Melitene, records one episode from the middle of that process, and it explains
the method better than the battles do.

The neighbours had had enough of him. Kilij Arslan had attacked Melitene, failed
to take it, and carried the country people off into slavery on his way back to
Kayseri. So a coalition formed: Nur al-Din of Aleppo, the Artuqid lord of Mardin,
the lord of Hisn Kayfa, the Armenians of Cilicia, and the Danishmendid lord of
Sivas. They assembled at Sivas and marched to the gates of Kayseri.

Kilij Arslan did not come out. He asked for terms, and gave back the captives he
had taken from Melitene. Then the allies made a further demand: he was holding
four of his own nephews in prison, and they wanted them released.

What he did, Bar Hebraeus writes, was *a most accursed thing.* He killed one of
the four, roasted him, arranged him on a dish, and sent him to the boy's father.
And he swore that if they went on asking for the other three he would treat all
of them in exactly the same way.

They left him and went their way.

It is worth being careful about what that story does and does not establish. Bar
Hebraeus is a Syriac Christian bishop writing a century later from a city Kilij
Arslan had raided, and the account is meant to horrify. But the shape of it is
politically exact, and the shape is what matters: a coalition of five powers
brought a sultan to the walls of his own capital, took back what he had stolen,
and then discovered that there was nothing further they could make him do,
because he was willing to do something none of them were. They went home.

That is how the Sultanate of Rum ended up owning Anatolia. Not by winning a
decisive campaign against the coalition — he had just lost to it — but by
outlasting the coalition and everybody in it. Nur al-Din died in 1174. The
Danishmendid line at Sivas was absorbed within a few years and the last of it
extinguished in 1178. The Byzantine army was destroyed at Myriokephalon in 1176.
The Artuqids stayed small. Kilij Arslan lived until 1192.

He then divided his territories among eleven sons, which undid a good deal of it,
and the sultanate spent a decade putting itself back together before the century
that the caravanserais and Konya belong to. But by then there was no longer a
second Turkish power in Anatolia, and the reason there was not is in this
chapter: a ruler who could be beaten and could not be coerced, in a region where
everybody else could be.
