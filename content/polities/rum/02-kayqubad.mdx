Between roughly 1200 and 1240 the Sultanate of Rum was one of the better-run
states in the eastern Mediterranean, and almost nothing in the popular picture
of medieval Anatolia reflects it.

The sultanate had spent its first century as a frontier polity among rivals —
Danishmendids to the east, Byzantines to the west, crusading armies passing
through twice — and it emerged from that with Konya, most of the plateau, and
under Kayqubad I from 1220 a coastline: Antalya on the Mediterranean and
Sinop on the Black Sea, taken deliberately, because a landlocked plateau state
that controls two ports controls the route between them.

That is what the caravanserai network in the next chapter is for. It is also
why the court looked the way it did. The administration worked in Persian, the
chancery style was Iranian, the sultans took Persian throne-names out of the
*Shahnama* — Kayqubad, Kaykhusraw, Kaykavus, which are the names of legendary
Iranian kings — and married Byzantine, Georgian and Armenian princesses. This
site's Great Seljuk page describes a Turkish family adopting a Persian
bureaucratic order; here that order was carried into a country that had been
Greek-speaking and Christian for a millennium, and it went on absorbing the
people already there rather than replacing them.

Kayqubad also did the one thing that mattered in the 1220s, which was to notice
the Mongols. He fortified, took Erzurum, negotiated with the Khwarazmian
remnant under Jalal al-Din, and sent an embassy east. He died in 1237, four
years after the Mongols reached Azerbaijan, and the state he had built was
tested six years later.
