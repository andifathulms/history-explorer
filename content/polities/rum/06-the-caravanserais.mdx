Under Kayqubad I the sultanate took the Black Sea port of Sinop and the
Mediterranean port of Alanya, which gave it both ends of a route across Anatolia,
and then built the route: a chain of stone caravanserais spaced roughly a day's
march apart, with free lodging, stabling, a bath and often a mosque and an
infirmary, endowed as charitable foundations and open to merchants regardless of
religion.

Several dozen survive. They are the most substantial physical inheritance of the
sultanate, and they represent a specific economic strategy — a landlocked
Anatolian state making itself the cheapest and safest transit between two seas,
and insuring merchants against loss on its roads.

The population being governed was largely Greek and Armenian Christian
throughout, and the sultanate's practice was accommodation rather than conversion:
Christian officials in the administration, dynastic marriages with Byzantine and
Georgian houses, and a court culture that was Persian in language and literature
while the countryside was not. Jalal al-Din Rumi wrote in Persian at Konya under
Mongol overlordship, which is why the world calls him after this sultanate.

That mixture is the ground the Ottomans grew from. What made the Byzantine
frontier productive for a small Turkish principality in 1300 was two centuries of
Turkish, Greek, Armenian and Persian coexistence in Anatolia that this sultanate
had organised, and that nobody had to invent again.
