On 26 June 1243, at Kösedağ in eastern Anatolia, a Mongol army under Baiju met
the forces of Kaykhusraw II and destroyed them in a few hours. The sultan fled
before the fighting had finished. There was no siege, no long campaign, and no
second battle.

What followed is the strange part, and it is why the sultanate has two possible
end dates sixty-five years apart.

The Mongols did not occupy Anatolia and did not depose the dynasty. They imposed
tribute and took the sultanate as a vassal, and the Seljuk house went on sitting
at Konya for another two generations while Ilkhanid governors collected the
revenue and decided, increasingly openly, who would sit there. Rival sultans were
installed by rival Mongol factions. The country was partitioned between brothers
by Mongol arbitration. A rebellion in the 1270s was put down by a Mongol army.

## What a defeat like that actually costs

Not territory, at first. Authority.

A sultanate whose sultan is chosen at Tabriz cannot discipline anybody, because
everybody it might discipline can appeal over its head. That is the mechanism,
and it worked downwards through the whole structure. Provincial commanders
stopped remitting revenue. The Turkmen of the marches — who had always been the
least governable element and had been held in place by a combination of
patronage and force — found that neither was available any more.

The sultanate could no longer restrain them and the Ilkhanate, whose interest was
the revenue of the settled districts, did not care to. So the frontier filled
with independent beyliks, a dozen or more of them, each with a warband and a
stretch of country, and among them one in the north-west on the Byzantine march
that was too poor to be worth anybody's attention.

## Two battles

Manzikert opened Anatolia to the Turks and this sultanate organised what came
in. Kösedağ broke the organisation and left the country full of small Turkish
states.

The two battles are a hundred and seventy-two years apart, and the second one is
what made the first one's consequences permanent. Without Kösedağ there is a
Seljuk sultanate governing Anatolia from Konya, with a frontier it polices;
after it there is a peninsula of competing warbands with a Mongol tax farm
behind them, which is a much better environment for a small aggressive
principality with nothing to lose.
