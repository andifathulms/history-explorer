The last Seljuk sultan of Rum disappears from the record around 1308, and nobody
recorded how.

There is no deposition, no execution anybody bothered to write down, and no
successor. The line simply stops being mentioned, in a country where the
mentions had been getting thinner for two generations, and the Ilkhanid governor
went on governing exactly as before.

## Sixty-five years of a state that was not one

That is the interval between the battle and the last name, and it is the reason
the ending here is a slow absorption rather than a conquest.

The Mongols had every opportunity to abolish the sultanate in 1243 and chose not
to. A dynasty with a name people obeyed was a cheaper instrument than a garrison:
it collected, it legitimised, and it could be replaced when it stopped being
convenient, which it repeatedly was. What that produced was a throne occupied by
men who had been put there from Tabriz, competing with brothers who had been put
somewhere else from Tabriz, in a country whose actual revenue was being assessed
by Persian officials working for a khan in Azerbaijan.

By about 1280 the sultanate was a formality. By 1308 it was not even that, and
the fact that nobody can say what happened to the last of them is the most
accurate possible statement of how much the office was worth by the end.

## What replaced it

Not one thing. A dozen.

The beyliks that had formed in the marches while the centre could not restrain
them became, in the fourteenth century, the political map of Anatolia: Karaman
at Konya itself, claiming to be the heirs of Rum and asserting it for two
hundred years; Germiyan, Menteshe, Aydin, Saruhan and Hamid across the west and
the Aegean coast; Candar on the Black Sea; Eretna and then the Ottomans.

Karaman's claim is the one worth pausing on, because it shows what the Seljuk
name was still worth. In 1277 Karaman took Konya while the sultanate was an
Ilkhanid tributary and put a man it said was a Seljuk on the throne. The attempt
collapsed within weeks. The claim to be the heirs of Rum was maintained for the
next two centuries anyway, by a dynasty that had briefly held the capital for
long enough to say so — and it was maintained specifically against the Ottomans,
who had no such claim and did not need one.
