Lorenzo de' Medici died in 1492, and his son Piero proved catastrophically
unequal to holding what his father and great-grandfather had built. When
a French army under Charles VIII invaded Italy in 1494, Piero
surrendered a string of Florentine fortresses to Charles entirely on his
own authority, without so much as consulting the government he
supposedly led. The news triggered an immediate popular uprising. Piero
fled the city, and sixty years of Medici control over Florentine
government ended in a matter of days.

## A vacuum filled by a preacher, not a politician

What replaced the Medici was not, at first, any clearly organized
faction at all. Girolamo Savonarola, a Dominican friar who held no civic
office and had built his own following purely through sermons in the
Duomo warning of coming judgment, emerged as the only figure commanding
enough public trust to help fill the gap. He negotiated with Charles
VIII directly on the city's behalf, helped design a new constitution
built around a broadly representative Great Council, and used his own
pulpit to shape Florentine politics as thoroughly as any elected
magistrate could have - all without ever holding a single formal
position in the government he was busy remaking.

## A bonfire built for an entire city's vanity

Savonarola's influence reached beyond politics into the texture of daily
Florentine life. For the Carnival of 1496 - traditionally a season of
licence the friar found intolerable - he organized bands of boys, dressed
as angels, to go door to door collecting "vanities": cosmetics, fine
dress, gaming equipment, secular books, and artwork depicting the nude
human form. A huge pyramidal scaffold was built in the Piazza to burn
everything collected. The painter Fra Bartolommeo, swept up in the
fervor, burned his own studies from life; the artist Lorenzo di Credi did
the same. Vasari records that "the loss, especially in painting, was very
considerable" - a single sermon's worth of moral urgency destroying
finished works and studies by some of the city's own most talented
artists, offered up voluntarily by their own creators.

## A theocracy with no army behind it

What Savonarola's movement never solved was the same problem every
Florentine government before it had faced: how to hold power without
either broad consent or force to back it up. His faction, the
"Piagnoni," held genuine popular devotion for a season, but built no
independent security for their own leader beyond that devotion - a
vulnerability that a resurgent opposition, and an increasingly hostile
papacy, would prove entirely willing to exploit once his own popularity
began, even slightly, to waver.
