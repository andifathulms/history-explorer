The Medici returned to Florence in 1512 and were expelled again in 1527,
in the general chaos following the Sack of Rome, giving the city one
final experiment in republican government - and one final war to defend
it. Pope Clement VII, himself a Medici, was determined this time to
settle the question permanently, and secured the backing of Emperor
Charles V's own army to do it. A combined papal and imperial force laid
siege to Florence in the autumn of 1529.

## A hero who could not save the war he had already lost

The Republic's own defense produced at least one genuine feat of arms:
Francesco Ferrucci held out in the field with real skill and daring for
months, tying down forces that might otherwise have pressed Florence
directly. His death in battle in August 1530, alongside the rout of the
army he commanded, effectively ended any realistic hope of relief from
outside the walls - though the city's own defenders, by every account,
remained willing to fight on rather than surrender.

## A commander who killed the messenger sent to remove him

What actually ended the siege was treachery within the city's own
command, not the collapse of its will to resist. Malatesta Baglioni, the
condottiere general entrusted with Florence's own defense, had been
secretly negotiating his own terms with the besiegers for some time. When
the Signory, learning of his disloyalty, sent an envoy to formally strip
him of command, Baglioni stabbed the man to death on the spot, declaring,
"Go back to your masters, and tell them that they shall in spite of
themselves make submission to the Pope!" He then seized the city gates
by force, replaced the guards with his own men, and opened the way for
the besieging army - accomplishing by betrayal what two years of siege
warfare had failed to achieve outright.

## Terms preserved, and a republic that ended anyway

The capitulation that followed was, on paper, remarkably gentle: its
very first article promised that Florence's own form of government would
be settled within four months by the Emperor, "it being always
understood that liberty is to be preserved." No one who had actually
witnessed the siege believed such promises would be honored by an
adversary, in the words of one contemporary account, "whom no promises
or treaties, howsoever solemn, could bind." Alessandro de' Medici was
installed as the city's hereditary ruler under the settlement's own
terms - not through any single dramatic revocation of Florence's
constitution, but through the quiet, total substitution of hereditary
rule for the elective one that had, with every interruption along the
way, run for more than four centuries.
