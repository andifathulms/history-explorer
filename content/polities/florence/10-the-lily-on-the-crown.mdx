The republic's own constitutional forms did not end with the siege.
For ten months after the surrender the city was governed through
Baccio Valori, the Pope's own representative, with the Signoria left
nominally in place around him. Alessandro de' Medici arrived from Rome
the following July, styled for the time being only "head of the
Republic" while a fortress was built at the centre of the northern
wall to garrison the troops his coming rule would need. Only once that
fortress was ready, on the first of May 1532, did Alessandro summon
the Signoria and read out the Emperor's order: the Signoria abolished,
the Republic ended, himself sole ruler of Florence. The great bell
known as La Vacca, which had rung from the tower of the Palazzo della
Signoria for generations to summon Florentines to exercise their own
collective will, was pulled down that same day and broken to pieces.
A contemporary diarist recorded the reason plainly: it was taken so
that the city would no longer hear the sound of liberty.

When Alessandro was murdered by a cousin in 1537, the councillors who
chose his successor picked an eighteen-year-old kinsman, Cosimo, on
the calculation that a boy with no independent power base would be
easy to manage. He spent the next three decades proving the
calculation wrong.

## A diploma, then a crown

Cosimo's first move was not to seize authority the city had never
formally given him but to have an outside power confirm it. He
represented himself to the Emperor Charles V as a loyal vassal, useful
against French interests in Italy, and received in return an imperial
diploma granting him "all the authority formerly borne and exercised
by Duke Alessandro." The document never used the word duke outright -
Cosimo simply began signing himself "Duca di Fiorenza" afterward, and
no one with the power to object did. A fireplace inscription from his
own rooms in the Palazzo Vecchio records how he styled himself in
these years: not Cosimo I, but "Cosimus Flor. Dux II" - the second
Duke of Florence, counting Alessandro as the first and treating his
own rule as a continuation rather than a fresh grant.

Each following step used the same method: marriage alliances upward,
first to the Viceroy of Naples's daughter and then, for his own son
Francis, to a Habsburg archduchess - the most exalted match the Medici
had yet made, and one Cosimo hoped would eventually support a claim to
something more than a ducal title. By 1569 the moment he had been
building toward for years arrived. France was consumed by civil war,
Spain was fighting in the Netherlands, and no power in Europe had the
attention to spare for objecting to what came next: Pope Pius V, citing
Cosimo's standing and his services to the Papal See, issued a bull
naming him Grand Duke of Tuscany. Spain and the Empire refused to
recognise the new rank at first; France and England accepted it
immediately, and the rest of Europe followed within a few years.

## What the crown looked like

The bull specified the crown's design in detail: radiated like an
eastern king's rather than shaped like the crowns of France or Spain,
its points curving outward to suggest the blades of an iris, and at
its centre, set as the crown's principal jewel, a large red Florentine
lily - making the crest of the old republic itself the chief jewel of
the new royal crown. Cosimo was crowned in Rome the following
February with full ceremony. The city that had once elected its own
magistrates by lot and exiled families for reaching too far above their
station now had a hereditary sovereign wearing its own civic symbol as
a crown jewel, and the Medici Grand Dukes who followed him governed
Tuscany under that same title for another century and a half.
