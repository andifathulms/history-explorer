By 1478, Lorenzo de' Medici, Cosimo's grandson, ruled Florence with a
good deal less of his grandfather's own careful diplomacy toward rival
families. Where Cosimo had married a Pazzi bride into his own house and
restored the family to civic honors after decades of exclusion, Lorenzo
froze the Pazzi out of every office in the Commonwealth for sixteen
straight years and treated their commercial rivalry with his own bank as
a genuine threat to be crushed rather than managed. Jacopo de' Pazzi,
head of the family, and his nephew Francesco found allies willing to go
further than exclusion: Pope Sixtus IV himself, angered by Lorenzo's own
obstruction of papal ambitions in the Romagna, quietly backed a plot to
remove both Lorenzo and his brother Giuliano by murder.

## A plan reshuffled twice by simple bad luck

The conspirators' original plan, to strike during a banquet at the
Medici villa at Fiesole, collapsed when Giuliano stayed home feeling
unwell. A second attempt, planned for a dinner following mass at the
cathedral, fell through the same way when word reached the plotters that
Giuliano would attend the mass but skip the banquet. With time running
out and the secret already spreading dangerously wide, the conspirators
settled on the only setting left to them: the cathedral itself, during
mass, with the elevation of the host as the signal - a moment chosen
because everyone present would be kneeling, silent, and utterly
predictable in their positions.

## A hired soldier who would not kill within sight of Christ

Even this final plan nearly collapsed at the last moment. Giovannibattista
di Montesecco, the professional soldier assigned to kill Lorenzo, refused
outright, declaring he would commit murder in a private house without
hesitation but could not bring himself to strike where Christ himself
would witness it. Two priests were hurriedly recruited in his place -
men with no similar scruple, but far less skill with a blade. At the
signal, Francesco de' Pazzi and an accomplice fell on Giuliano and
stabbed him nineteen times, Francesco's own fury driving the blade so
wildly that he wounded his own thigh in the process. The two
substitute priests attacking Lorenzo managed only a shallow cut to his
neck before he threw his cloak around his arm, fought clear, and barred
himself inside the cathedral's own sacristy.

## An uprising that never found its crowd

Jacopo de' Pazzi rode into the Piazza at the same moment, calling on the
people to rise for their own liberty - and found, in Machiavelli's own
account, that "the one was made deaf by the liberality and the good
fortune of the Medici, and the other was unknown in Florence." No one
answered the call. Archbishop Salviati, sent to seize the Palazzo
Pubblico in a coordinated second act of the plot, was cornered by the
sitting Gonfaloniere the moment his own confused, unconvincing story gave
him away - and the uprising that was supposed to seize the city collapsed
before Lorenzo had even emerged from his own hiding place in the
sacristy.
