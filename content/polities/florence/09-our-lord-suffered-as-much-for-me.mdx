Savonarola's open defiance of Pope Alexander VI - refusing to stop
preaching after his own excommunication, and denouncing the papacy's own
corruption from the pulpit - cost him support in Rome long before it cost
him support at home. What finally turned Florentine opinion against him
was a challenge his own faction could not walk back from: a proposed
ordeal by fire, in which a Franciscan and one of Savonarola's own
followers, Fra Domenico, would each enter flames to prove whose faith
was genuine. When the ordeal collapsed in confused argument over its
own terms before either man ever stepped near the fire, the crowd that
had gathered to watch it, denied its spectacle, turned its anger on
Savonarola instead.

## A trial that answered to politics rather than evidence

Savonarola was arrested within days and subjected to a process that made
little pretense of impartial justice - a body largely composed of his
own political enemies, working from confessions that his own supporters
maintained were extracted under torture. He was condemned along with two
of his closest followers, Fra Domenico and Fra Silvestro, to be hanged and
burned in the Piazza della Signoria, the same square where his bonfires
of confiscated luxuries had once drawn cheering crowds.

## A composure that outlasted the crowd's contempt

Contemporary accounts describe a crowd permitted to jeer and insult the
condemned men as they walked to the scaffold, and all three meeting the
abuse with a composure their tormentors plainly had not expected. Fra
Domenico chanted the Te Deum the entire way. When one bystander offered
Savonarola a word of comfort, he answered simply, "In the hour of death,
God only can comfort his creatures." Asked by another how he found the
courage to face what was coming, he replied, "Our Lord suffered as much
for me." Even the executioner, according to the account that survives,
added deliberate cruelty to the process in an apparent bid for the
crowd's own favor.

## Ashes thrown in the river, and a movement that would not quite die

The bodies were burned and the ashes, on the Signory's own explicit
order, collected and scattered from the Ponte Vecchio into the Arno - a
precaution meant to deny his followers any relic to venerate. It did not
fully work. Savonarola's own Piagnoni continued to treat him as a martyred
prophet for years afterward, and the theocratic experiment his own
preaching had improvised, rather than the man himself, left behind a
Great Council and a constitution that would structure Florentine politics
for another three decades - the institutional residue of a friar who had
never once held office, outlasting by far the popularity that had let
him build it.
