By the late thirteenth century, Florence's wealthy merchant class - the
"popolani grassi" - had grown frustrated with a government still
adulterated, as they saw it, by the old feudal nobility mixed in among
them. In February 1293, a group of the discontented secured the election
to the Priorato of Giano della Bella, a wealthy trader who had his own
grievance against the aristocracy: he had recently suffered a public
insult from a member of the Frescobaldi family and was, by every
account, thoroughly sincere in his outrage at the abuses he believed
noble privilege had produced.

## A code built to exclude, not merely to punish

Under Giano's influence, the city enacted an entirely new body of law,
the Ordinances of Justice, meant not to replace existing legislation but
to stand alongside it as a special instrument for controlling the
nobility specifically. Every family with a knight among its members was
barred from holding office. A new magistracy, the Gonfaloniere di
Giustizia, was created to preside over the Priori and given command of a
thousand armed men - the "gonfalone," the standard he carried, giving
the office the name by which it would be known for the rest of the
Republic's history.
Any noble who killed a commoner, or who procured
someone else to do so, faced beheading, the destruction of his house,
and the forfeiture of his entire estate to the Commune. Seventy-two
noble families were named and permanently barred from any future office
in the city.

## A reformer undone by the very mob he empowered

The proscription did not sit quietly. A wave of conspiracies and street
violence followed, and Giano della Bella himself, ironically, became one
of its earliest casualties. A brawl between two nobles - one of them
Corso Donati, an especially unpopular figure - ended with a Podestà
ruling unjustly in Donati's favor, provoking a riot in which the crowd
nearly killed the Podestà outright. Giano went out among the rioters
hoping his own influence would calm them; instead he barely escaped with
his life, and by the next day, nobles who had been quietly stoking
popular anger against him found the "popolani grassi" themselves turning
on their own champion. Giano fled the city rather than wait to be
formally exiled, and died in France not long after - proof, in the
judgment of the historian recounting it, of how frequently the makers of
great reforms become the reforms' own first victims.
