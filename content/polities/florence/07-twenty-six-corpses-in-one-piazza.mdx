The Gonfaloniere Cesare Petrucci, a Medici loyalist raised from obscurity
entirely by the family's own patronage, did not wait for any trial once
he understood what had happened. Within half an hour of the conspiracy's
collapse in the cathedral, he had a rope thrown around Archbishop
Francesco Salviati's neck and hanged him, still in his archbishop's
vestments, from a window of the Palazzo Pubblico overlooking the Piazza
- without a moment allowed for confession or absolution.

## A hunt that did not distinguish the guilty from the merely related

What followed was less a legal reckoning than a citywide manhunt.
Francesco de' Pazzi, too badly wounded by his own frenzied attack on
Giuliano to flee, was dragged from his sickbed naked and bleeding and
hanged beside the Archbishop's own body. Within the same half hour,
twenty-six corpses lay in the Piazza and several more dangled from the
Palazzo's windows - everyone who had entered the building alongside
Salviati, save one man discovered four days later hidden among a
woodpile, more dead than alive. Jacopo de' Pazzi himself was captured and
executed shortly after. Members of the family who had no part in the
plot at all were swept up in the same purge: Giovanni de' Pazzi,
innocent of any involvement, spent the rest of his life imprisoned in
the tower of Volterra and died there in 1481, his own wife imprisoned in
turn for trying to help him escape.

## A city more loyal to the Medici for having tried to kill them

What the conspiracy had been designed to accomplish - the destruction of
Medici power in Florence - it achieved in precisely the opposite
direction. Rather than exposing Lorenzo as vulnerable, the failed attempt
and its bloody aftermath left Florence's own citizens more firmly
attached to the family than before, their sympathy hardened by an
assassination carried out during mass and directed at two young men, one
of whom had died a horrific and very public death for no clearer offense
than being his brother's own brother. Sixteen years of frozen exclusion
from office, a papal-blessed murder plot, and a bloodbath of reprisal had,
between them, accomplished what Cosimo's own patient diplomacy toward
rival families had spent a lifetime trying to avoid needing: they made
Medici rule in Florence look less like one family's private ambition and
more like the settled will of the city itself.
