Florence's twelfth-century prosperity did nothing to spare it the
factional violence that split most of Italy's communes into rival
camps, and the chroniclers traced the whole Guelph-Ghibelline divide
in Florence back to a single broken marriage promise in 1215.

## A jilted bride and a proverb about a deed done

Buondelmonte de' Buondelmonti, a young nobleman celebrated for his
good looks, had agreed to marry a niece of Oddo Arringhi de' Fifanti
as compensation for wounding Oddo in a brawl. Two days before the
betrothal, Monna Gualdrada de' Donati lured him to her house, showed
him her own beautiful daughter, and taunted him for marrying out of
fear rather than choice. Buondelmonte broke his engagement publicly
and betrothed himself to the Donati girl instead - an insult the
Amidei family's allies met in council with a warning from Mosca de'
Lamberti that became proverbial in Florence ever after: "Beat him or
mark him as you will, but if you stop there, you may as well dig your
own graves. Let him die - a deed done has a head." On Easter Sunday,
as the wedding party crossed the Ponte Vecchio, Schiatta de' Lamberti
struck Buondelmonte from his horse with an iron mace and Oddo himself
cut his throat. His bride rode the funeral bier back through the city
with his head in her lap. "On that day," wrote the chronicler Villani,
"began the ruin of Florence" - the noble families split permanently
into the Guelph faction, which backed the Buondelmonti's kin and papal
authority, and the Ghibelline faction, loyal to the emperor.

## A battle that colored a river red

The feud simmered through the century as Florence's own consular and
then podestà-led government tried, with limited success, to keep both
factions inside one city administration. It broke into open war after
the death of Emperor Frederick II left his illegitimate son Manfred
contesting Sicily and the Ghibelline cause across Italy. In 1260, a
Florentine-led Guelph coalition of more than three thousand horsemen
and thirty thousand foot marched into Sienese territory to confront a
Ghibelline army stiffened by German mercenaries and exiled Florentine
nobles under the general Farinata degli Uberti. At the river Arbia,
near a hill called Montaperti, the Florentine standard-bearer had his
sword arm hacked off by Bocca degli Abati - a Ghibelline riding
disguised in the Guelph ranks - who then galloped over to the enemy
with his fellow conspirators, an act of betrayal Dante later punished
with a permanent place among history's traitors in the Inferno. The
Guelph army broke. More than 2,500 were killed and 1,500 captured; the
sacred war-cart, the Carroccio, was lost with the old guardian who died
defending it and three generations of his own family beside him. "On
that day," Villani wrote of Montaperti in turn, "was broken and
destroyed the old popular government of Florence" - the Guelphs driven
into a mass exile from which the city's politics would not fully
recover for another generation, until a reformer named Giano della
Bella finally locked the old feudal nobility out of government for
good.
