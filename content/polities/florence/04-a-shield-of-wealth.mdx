By the 1430s, no single family carried more practical weight in Florence
than the Medici, and no one recognized the danger that posed more
clearly than Rinaldo degli Albizzi, leader of the rival aristocratic
faction. In 1433, with the Signory under his own faction's control,
Rinaldo had Cosimo de' Medici arrested and confined in the Palazzo, his
life genuinely at risk, while his brother, cousin, and other relatives
were sent into exile across Italy.

## A blow that missed its mark

Rinaldo's own faction chose exile over execution for Cosimo himself - a
decision that, in hindsight, proved the single greatest miscalculation
of the whole affair. Florence, in Machiavelli's own phrase, found itself
"widowed" of a citizen many still deeply admired, and the shock briefly
stunned both sides of the conflict equally. But Cosimo's wealth followed
him into exile at Venice more effectively than any weapon could have,
and it was immediately clear to observers that he remained, in practical
terms, more powerful outside Florence than Rinaldo was inside it. Rinaldo
had struck at his rival without the force to make the blow permanent,
and in doing so had guaranteed his own eventual ruin instead.

## A homecoming compared to a conqueror's triumph

The reversal came within a year. A new government hostile to Rinaldo's
own faction took power, and Cosimo's recall followed as, in Machiavelli's
words, "a matter of course." His actual return, on the 1st of October,
1434, was met with a welcome contemporaries described as unlike anything
given to any citizen in living memory - "every man spontaneously saluted
him as the benefactor of the people and father of his country." Rinaldo
and the whole of his own faction were exiled in turn, in numbers so much
larger than the previous year's proscriptions that, as one chronicler
put it, "there was scarcely any town in Italy" without a Florentine exile
now living in it.

## A constitution left standing, and quietly hollowed out

Cosimo made no attempt to alter Florence's own republican constitution
after his return. The Priori continued to be elected, the guilds
continued to hold their nominal place in government, and no new office
was created to formalize what had actually changed. What had changed was
simpler and more durable than any office could have made it: for the
rest of his life, and for six decades after his death, no Florentine
government would be assembled without the Medici family's own consent -
a form of rule that left every republican institution standing exactly
where it had always stood, while quietly deciding, behind the scenes,
who would ever sit in it.
