Even the Ordinances of Justice had not extended political rights to
Florence's own lowest ranks of labor - the wool-carders and combers whose
work sustained the city's most important industry, but who belonged to
no guild with any voice in government at all.
In the summer of 1378,
after a government crackdown on their attempts to organize, these
workers - contemptuously nicknamed the "Ciompi" - rose in open revolt.

<Figure id="baptistery" />

## A palace surrendered without a fight

The uprising reached its climax when the ruling Signory itself lost its
nerve. Two Priors who had sworn to remain at their posts in the Palazzo
regardless of danger found, when they went looking for their colleagues,
that every other member of the government had already slipped away to
the safety of their own homes. Concluding the cause was lost, the two
remaining Priors handed the palace keys to a tavern-keeper acting as the
mob's own representative and went home themselves. The gates were thrown
open, and the crowd surged in.

## A wool-comber given the city outright

Among those who entered was Michele di Lando, a foreman in a wool
workshop, bare-legged and shoeless, carrying a standard seized from a
government office.
He walked straight to the Signory's own audience
chamber and stood there - and the crowd, by simple acclamation, declared
him Gonfaloniere and Lord of Florence. For more than twenty-eight hours,
by a contemporary chronicler's own astonished reckoning, an ordinary
wool-comber held absolute power over the city. He used it, remarkably,
to begin restoring order rather than to entrench himself: he had a new
Signory elected by the ordinary constitutional process of balloting, with
three Priors drawn from the greater guilds, three from the lesser, and
three - for the first time in Florence's history - from the ranks of the
"popolo minuto," the common people who had never before held office at
all.

## A revolution that consumed its own gains within weeks

The new arrangement did not last. When some of Lando's own former
comrades continued occupying the streets around the Piazza rather than
disperse, Lando himself, now the city's own Gonfaloniere, charged them on
horseback and scattered them by nightfall. His successor as Gonfaloniere,
another wool-comber named Jacopo Baraccio, proved an embarrassment even
to the very workers who had helped install him - contemporaries could
not stomach the sight of a man who had been carrying wool bales the day
before dressed in the robes of state. Within weeks, an assembly of the
guilds deposed Baraccio, barred anyone outside the twenty-one recognized
guilds from future office, annulled nearly everything enacted since the
revolt began, and beheaded two of its ringleaders in the same Piazza
where Lando had once stood, briefly, as Lord of Florence.
