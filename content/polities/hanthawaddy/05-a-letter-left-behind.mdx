Tabinshwehti of Toungoo turned on Lower Burma in 1535, drawn by the
wealth its maritime trade had built up over two and a half centuries
of Mon kingship. His forces reduced the western provinces of Bassein
and Myaungmya without much trouble, drove off both the Talaing war
canoes and a Portuguese trading vessel that happened to resist him
outside Pegu town, and pressed on toward the capital itself. But Pegu
would not fall to direct assault, no matter how many times
Tabinshwehti tried.

## A forged letter and a false quarrel

So he turned to stratagem. King Takayutpi's two ablest commanders
had written to Tabinshwehti proposing friendly relations; he ignored
their letter in his reply but treated the two envoys themselves with
conspicuous honour, then wrote to the commanders by name promising
each of them a city to govern once "the matter you arranged with me"
was finished. The men who carried this letter had instructions to
provoke a quarrel with the local Mon officials by demanding food for
nothing, then to flee, leaving the letter behind them where it would
be found.

## Pegu falls without a fight

The scheme worked exactly as planned. The local officials forwarded
the letter to Takayutpi, who took it as proof that his own most
capable commanders were traitors and had them put to death. Deprived
of its best leadership, the Mon army lost heart and began deserting
in numbers; Takayutpi himself fled toward Prome, and Tabinshwehti
walked into Pegu in 1539 without striking a blow. Two hundred and
fifty-two years after Wareru had won the same country in single
combat on an elephant's back, Mon kingship at Pegu ended not in
battle but in a letter its own king had been tricked into believing.
