By general consent the throne passed in 1453 to Shinsawbu, daughter
of the great king Razadarit, after palace massacres had left no male
heir of his line alive - the only instance in Burmese history of a
major state ruled by a woman. Daughter, sister, wife and mother of
kings, she governed well enough that four centuries later Mon
writers, groping for the highest praise they could offer Queen
Victoria, could think of nothing better to call her than Shinsawbu
reborn.

## Two monks and an almsbowl

After seven years on the throne, Shinsawbu decided to retire, and
faced a choice between the two monks who had once helped her escape
captivity at Ava. Unable to decide between them herself, she left
the matter to providence: she hid a layman's robe and small models
of the five royal regalia in one monk's almsbowl among the morning
offerings, prayed that the lot would fall on the worthier man, and
waited. The bowl fell to Dhammazedi. He left the monastic order,
married her daughter, and took the government - a choice the court's
lords resented at first, murmuring that he was not of royal blood,
until Shinsawbu had a common wooden beam carved into a Buddha image
and asked them whether they did not now bow before what had, only
the day before, been trodden in the dust beneath their own feet.

## The years the source calls happiest

Dhammazedi ruled from 1472 to 1492, continuing the building programme
at the Shwedagon that Shinsawbu had begun - she alone had beaten out
her own body weight in gold for gilding its dome - and widened the
river at Pegu after a boat-racing accident on its narrow waters
convinced him the channel needed enlarging. The years from 1423 to
1539 were, by one careful reckoning, probably the happiest the
kingdom ever knew - a peace and prosperity built as much on a
queen's providence-tested succession as on any king's conquest.
