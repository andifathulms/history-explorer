The event that made Kokand a state happened two hundred miles east of it and had
nothing to do with anybody in Fergana. The Qing destroyed the Dzungars.

## 1750

Qing armies came over the Tian Shan in pursuit of the Dzungars and then of the
khojas of the Tarim, and in 1750 the general Zhao Hui detached officers to deal with
the Kirghiz of the mountains.

Erdeni Bek entertained these officers at Kokand, and when they left he sent one of
his own officials after them to tender his submission to the Qianlong emperor.

The other beks of Fergana followed him — Tokhto Muhammad of Andijan, Has Ping-li of
Marghilan — and in 1760 they sent embassies and tribute to Beijing, Tokhto Muhammad
going in person. Tashkent had submitted two years earlier.

Among the gifts sent to the emperor were *horses that sweat blood*, the argamaks of
Fergana that Chinese writers had been describing since the Han; great eagles and
falcons for hunting; and plates of the fountain of the dragon.

## What submission bought

Not subjection. Erdeni went on doing as he liked: he invaded Osh in 1762 and was
ordered by the Qing governor-general to withdraw his troops, and raided the Kirghiz
again in 1763 and was blamed by an imperial decree. He died in 1770.

What the submission bought was standing. The Chinese geographer records that the
beks of all the other towns in Fergana were under Erdeni's rule and obeyed his
orders, and that is the first statement in the record that Fergana had one ruler.
Tendering tribute to Beijing, and being recognised by Beijing as the man who spoke
for the valley, settled among Fergana's own quarrelling towns a question they had
not been able to settle themselves.

## The frontier that opened

The other consequence was commercial and enormous.

The Qing conquest of the Tarim opened the passes. Kashgar and Yarkand — which the
Dzungars had governed for eighty years as tributaries — were now part of an empire
that wanted trade, and the shortest road to them from the west ran over the Terek
Dawan out of Fergana. Kokandi merchants took it, and Kokandi merchant colonies
established themselves in the Tarim cities and became, in time, an interest the
khanate would fight about.

At the same moment Russia was coming down the Syr Darya from the north-west. For
about a century Kokand sat between the two advancing empires with a monopoly of the
road between them, which is exactly how a valley of quarrelling towns became the
largest and best-peopled of the three khanates — three million people, by Vámbéry's
estimate, more than Bukhara and far more than Khiva.
