Khudayar Khan ruled Kokand off and on for thirty years, was driven out three times,
and spent his last years so frightened that he could only sleep to the sound of his
servants talking.

## The plots

Schuyler's account of 1873 to 1875 reads like a court chronicle written by an
insurance assessor, and the detail is the point.

The insurrection of 1873 died out with the cold weather and the khan retook his
cities with little opposition. In 1874 it began again as a plot to put his second
son on the throne, prepared by an uncle — and given away, it is said, by the young
man's own talkativeness. The uncle and sixteen conspirators were summoned to the
palace and never came out; it was supposed they were drowned in a pond inside it.
The mekhter was poisoned by the khan's order for not having warned him.

That same mekhter had earlier been suspected of embezzling the customs funds and
put to an ordeal: bound onto a thin lattice thrown across a deep ravine, over which
a horse was galloped several times, the whole thing threatening at every moment to
break. He came out alive and was therefore held innocent — at least, Schuyler adds,
of that offence.

The Kirghiz and Kipchaks then looked for another claimant and found a boy of sixteen
living at Khojend who is said not to have known his own descent; at Khudayar's
request the Russians moved him to Tashkent under strict surveillance.

## The fear

The failure of the plots did not restore the tranquillity Khudayar had previously
enjoyed. He became sombre and distrustful. His bodyguard of four hundred picked men,
trained to the trust from infancy, no longer inspired the same confidence. He felt
menaced everywhere and at all times and saw dangers where there were none, and for a
long time did not leave his palace at all.

The door of his room was watched day and night by a slave named Nasim Toga, who was
blindly attached to him and had orders to let nobody in — not his wives, not his
children — without consulting him.

And he could only be lulled to sleep by the voices of three of his most faithful
servants, ordered to stay in the next room and to keep themselves constantly audible,
as a palpable proof that watch was being kept.

His eldest son was put under surveillance. The system of espionage across the
khanate was carried to its utmost limits; his best spy was a rich Kokand merchant
with agents inside the Russian possessions as well as his own, who naturally played
his master's affairs to build his own fortune.

## 1875

In 1875 General Kaufmann, wanting a bargain with the khan, handed back the boy
pretender and sent him to Kokand.

While the Russian embassy was still in the city the rebellion broke out again, and
its leader was the Aftobachi — the son of the regent Musulman Kul, who had been
waiting since 1842 to avenge his father's murder.

Everything had been prepared. The army deserted in a mass. Khudayar's own brother
and his sons went straight over to the rebels. He fled for the third time, with all
his treasure, to Tashkent, where the Russians received him well and then sent him to
live at Orenburg.

His eldest son Nasreddin was proclaimed khan, allowed himself to be drawn into a war
against Russia, and lost the throne.

## Ferghana

The khanate was annexed — and, alone of the three, abolished.

Schuyler's own phrase for what replaced it is worth keeping, because of what it
assumes: Kokand was annexed to Russia *under the historic name of Ferghana*. The
dynasty was removed, the title extinguished, and the province given the name the
Greeks and the Chinese had used for the valley two thousand years before the Ming
tribe arrived in it.

Bukhara and Khiva kept their rulers until 1920 because their rulers could still
control their own subjects when the Russians came. Kokand's could not, and so there
was nothing to leave standing.
