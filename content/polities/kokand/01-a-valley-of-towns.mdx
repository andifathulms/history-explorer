Fergana is the best land in Central Asia: a bowl a hundred and fifty miles long
between the Tian Shan and the Alai, watered by the head of the Syr Darya, with more
towns in it than the rest of Turkistan put together. It was the last part of the
region to become a state, because there was too much of everything and no obvious
centre.

## No history

Schuyler opens his account of the khanate with an admission worth quoting, because
every date that follows depends on it.

Fergana had been part of Chagatai's share of the inheritance and shared the fate of
Transoxiana; it was an appanage of Samarkand in Babur's time; after that it was
sometimes rebellious, sometimes conquered, sometimes in one prince's hands and
sometimes another's, and it does not emerge as a separate and independent country
until toward the beginning of the nineteenth century. No written historical account
by a native author had ever seen the light. What was known came from tradition and
oral report, strengthened here and there by coins and documents.

The khanate of Kokand is the only one of the three that never wrote itself down.

## What tradition says

Popular tradition and the Chinese records agree that in the middle of the eighteenth
century Fergana was not under one rule but divided among separate cities, provinces
and clans, each with its own bek or khoja.

The founding story, from a Kokandi informant quoted by Ritter, is brief and typical.
Some time in the last century, Shahrukh Bek came with some of his countrymen from
the Volga region to Fergana, married the daughter of Ediger Khoja, the ruler of
Khurram-Sarai, and settled with his Uzbeks at Kukan, twelve miles west of the present
city. Then he murdered his father-in-law and made himself ruler of the district, and
profiting by the dissensions and weakness of his neighbours he soon extended his
sway.

His eldest son succeeded him, then his brother Abd al-Karim, who built the present
city of Kokand and moved his residence there. Then Erdeni, his nephew.

## The Ming

The dynasty belonged to the Ming, one of the Uzbek tribes — not Chinggisids, and
not, unlike the Manghits at Bukhara and the Qungrats at Khiva, a family that had
spent generations as ministers to a legitimate line before taking it.

They simply took a district and grew. That is why Kokand's rulers never agonised
over titles the way their neighbours did, and also why nothing ever settled the
question of who should hold the throne. A dynasty that begins by murdering its
father-in-law has no argument to offer the next man who tries it.

Schuyler's narrative of the khanate's last forty years is, in consequence, almost
entirely a list of pretenders.
