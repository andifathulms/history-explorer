The war with Bukhara over the Syr Darya towns ran for twenty years and ended with
the khanate intact and unable to govern itself, which is the worst of both outcomes.

## The loop

Muhammad Ali held Kokand from 1822, and the quarrel with the emir Nasrullah ran on a
loop.

The emir marched; Muhammad Ali retreated; when the retreat could go no further he
was beaten at Khojend and the city was taken. He fell back again, found himself
still pursued and his own capital threatened, and sent a flag of truce. Peace was
made at Kohna Badam: Kokand ceded Khojend and much besides.

Then Nasrullah could not resist an extra turn of the knife and appointed as governor
of the ceded province Muhammad Ali's own brother and rival, who had taken refuge at
Bukhara. Their mother reconciled the two brothers, and before the emir had heard
anything about it Khojend and the other towns had rejoined Kokand, and he had two
enemies where he had had one.

## 1842

His fury, Vámbéry says, knew no bounds.

He added ten thousand Tekke and Salor Turkmen to his thirty thousand horse, marched
so fast that Muhammad Ali had to fly from his own capital, caught him near Marghilan
and executed him ten days later in Kokand with his brother and his two sons. Most of
their supporters went to the executioners after them and the property was
confiscated. To excuse it, Nasrullah put about that Muhammad Ali had married his own
mother and had therefore been put to death.

The emir went home loaded with booty, leaving a governor and two thousand men.

## The three months

The Kipchaks, who had stayed neutral all through, grew tired of the Bukharans, took
the city, made the garrison prisoners and put Muhammad Ali's son Shir Ali on the
throne.

Then, so as not to be surprised again, the Kokandis decided to wall off the quarter
containing the khan's residence — and built the wall with the forced labour of the
captured Bukharan garrison.

Nasrullah sent fifteen thousand men back under a Kokandi pretender he had been
keeping. On the march the pretender made his peace with his countrymen, the gates
were opened to him, and his first act was to turn on the Bukharans who had escorted
him there and put them to flight. A fifth army got as far as Ura-Tepe and stopped,
because the emir had fallen ill at Samarkand; he went home to Bukhara and died.

## What was left

Kokand had survived the strongest neighbour it had, lost its khan and most of its
ruling family, and come out of it with a new wall.

It had also learned the lesson that destroyed it. The Kipchaks had made the khan.
The regent Musulman Kul who came out of that settlement ruled in the boy's name and
was eventually killed for it; his son spent thirty years waiting to avenge him, and
led the rebellion that finished the khanate. The pattern set in those three months —
a nomad faction installs a khan, governs through him, is destroyed, and produces the
next claimant — ran without interruption until the Russians ended it.
