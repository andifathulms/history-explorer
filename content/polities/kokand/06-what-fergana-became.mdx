The province outlasted the khanate by a long way, and it is worth being precise
about what the annexation actually changed.

## The three fates

Set the three Uzbek khanates beside each other one more time.

Bukhara lost Samarkand and its river head in 1868, signed a treaty, kept its emir,
its army, its courts and its taxes, and lasted until 1920. Khiva lost its capital in
1873, signed, kept its khan, and lasted until 1920. Kokand was annexed in 1876 and
its dynasty ended.

The difference was not military. Kokand was the largest of the three, with three
million people to Bukhara's two and a half and Khiva's far fewer, and the richest
agricultural land of the lot. What it did not have was a ruler who could hold his
own army together for one campaign, and an empire that acquires a territory in the
middle of a civil war does not leave the civil war's losers in charge of it.

## What was kept

Nearly everything else. The Russians in Turkistan governed as the Dzungars had
governed the Tarim and as Bukhara was now being governed: garrisons in the important
towns, the internal administration left largely alone, the local courts and the local
elders in place, and the revenue collected through people who had been collecting it
before.

Tashkent, taken from Kokand in 1865, became the capital of Russian Turkistan, which
is what it has remained. The Fergana valley became a cotton province, because
irrigated land that could grow cotton was the thing the empire most wanted from
Central Asia.

## The last of the arrangement

It is worth noticing what ended with this khanate, because it was older than the
khanate itself.

Since the Mongol conquest the political grammar of Central Asia had been the same:
a nomad or tribal military class taking the revenue of an irrigated, urban,
Persian-and-Turkic-speaking population, and legitimating itself by descent from
Chinggis Khan or, when that failed, by piety. The Shaybanids ran it. The Janids ran
it. The Manghits and Qungrats ran it without the descent. The Dzungars ran it in the
Tarim. Kokand ran it in Fergana with Kirghiz and Kipchak cavalry.

In 1876 the military class was replaced by one that came from two thousand miles
away, did not live on the land, and had a state behind it that could supply an army
across a desert. The towns went on paying. The men who had been paid stopped being
paid.

## The name

Kokand's own name went off the map at the moment the khanate ended. There was no
successor calling itself the khanate, no claimant, no dynasty in exile.

What there was instead was a valley, given back a name two millennia older than the
state that had just been removed from it — which is as neat a statement as the
nineteenth century ever made about what it thought it was doing in Asia.
