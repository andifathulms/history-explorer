Alim Khan opened his reign by executing his own brother and closed it
fleeing his own capital with twenty horsemen. In between he did what
his father had spent thirty years preparing for: made Kokand a khanate
in name as well as fact, and started the fort-building push up the Syr
Darya that would define the state for the rest of its existence.

## A title his father never claimed

Narbuta's eldest son Alim had his rival brother Rustem and several
other relatives put to death the moment he took power, then did what
no Khokandi ruler had done before him - struck coin in his own name and
had the khutbeh read for himself rather than for Bukhara's amir. He
finished the war with Tashkent his father had started, capturing the
city outright by 1805 and completing its conquest under his own
brother Omar; subdued the Kazakh clans along the northern steppe; and
pushed, without full success, against Uratepa. He was, by every
account that survives him, a genuinely frightening ruler - he tested a
miracle-working sheikh's claims by making him walk a rope over a pond
and had him beaten to death when he fell in, and put entire orders of
dervishes to forced labour as camel-drivers for annoying him.

## The mutiny

His cruelty finally reached his own army. Ordered to finish putting
down a Kazakh submission with a massacre in the depth of winter, Alim's
brother Omar and the officers under him judged the order both
impossible and needless - the Kazakhs were already beaten, Muslim, and
scattered across a desert no army could sweep in that season - and his
own maternal uncle told the assembled troops plainly that they could
not go on obeying a tyrant. They swore allegiance to Omar on the spot
and marched on Kokand. Alim, deserted by every follower but a handful,
sent his young son ahead to Bukhara with what money he could spare,
and was shot in the back within sight of his own capital's walls in
1809, abandoned even by the retainer who might have given him a fresh
horse.

## Omar's turn

Omar Khan's own reign was, by contrast, remembered as mild -
Khokand became, in the phrase that survives him, a resort for
merchants rather than a court people feared to enter. He finished what
his father and brother had started: took Uratepa's own ruler prisoner,
conquered Turkestan and the towns around it, and drove out the last
claimant of the old Kazakh khan's line. He put down one palace
conspiracy by inviting the plotters to a feast and having them
strangled before the meal was over, and opened the diplomatic and
trade relationship with Russia that brought Colonel Nazarov's mission
to Kokand in 1813 - the first sustained contact between the khanate and
the empire that would eventually end it. When Omar Khan died, the
throne passed to his own son Muhammad Ali, already a young man rather
than an infant regency, inheriting a state that stretched from the
Kazakh steppe to the Tarim frontier and called itself, for the first
time without qualification, a khanate.
