Vámbéry never reached Kokand. He compiled his account of it in Bukhara and
Samarkand from people who had been there, which makes it hearsay — and his verdict
on its people is the harshest thing in his book, and the most useful.

## Who lived there

Uzbeks formed the settled population, and were of a type quite distinct from the
Uzbeks of Khiva or Bukhara. The name had by then become a social category rather
than a descent: because the Uzbeks had been the dominant race in Turkistan for
centuries and had adopted the institutions of Islam earlier than the other nomads,
the name carried prestige, and Kirghiz, Kipchaks and Kalmyks who settled in the
cities generally dropped their own nationality and called themselves Uzbek. Half of
those who did so in Kokand, Vámbéry reckons, were really a mixture of the nomad
peoples.

Next came the Tajiks, more compact here than anywhere else — whole towns and
villages of them, Khojend entirely, and Namangan, Andijan and Marghilan said to be
largely so.

Then, outside the towns and above them, the Kirghiz and the Kipchaks.

## The judgement

> Judging from his outside appearance, as he presents himself in his clumsy loose
> clothes, the Ozbeg of Khokand seems a very helpless person. We had many
> opportunities of witnessing his unexampled cowardice, and, had it not been for the
> protection of the nomads, his cities would have long fallen under the dominion of
> China, Russia, or Bokhara.

That is a European gentleman-traveller being contemptuous about townsmen, and it
should be read as such. But the political observation underneath it is exactly
right, and it is the key to the whole khanate.

## Who actually held the state

The settled valley produced the revenue. The nomads provided the fighting.

The Kirghiz of the mountains and the Kipchaks of the steppe were not the khan's
subjects in the sense the farmers of Fergana were. They were a constituency he had
to keep, with their own leaders, their own grievances and the ability to walk away
or to march on the capital. In 1842 it was the Kipchaks who retook Kokand from the
Bukharan garrison and put a new khan on the throne; in 1875 it was the Kirghiz whose
advance the cities were sending messages to hurry along, promising to rise against
the khan when they arrived.

The three khanates had the same structural problem and solved it differently.
Bukhara hired Turkmen mercenaries at four thousand tilla a year rather than arm its
own tributaries. Khiva exempted its warriors from the land tax and lived alongside
Turkmen it could not coerce. Kokand made the nomads the army and then discovered it
could not stop them choosing the government.

Vámbéry's sentence has the causation the wrong way round. It was not that the
nomads protected the cities. It was that the cities had handed them the state.
