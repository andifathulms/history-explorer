The general who had let the Manchus into China in 1644 spent the next
quarter-century as one of the dynasty's own great satraps, ruling the
south-west with an authority Boulger describes as independent of Manchu
control in everything but name. He kept a large standing army, held vast
revenues in his own hands, and by the time the young Kangxi came to the
throne, Wu Sangui's independence had begun to look, to the court that owed
him its capital, less like loyalty than like a threat the dynasty had never
actually disarmed.

## A summons Wu Sangui would not obey

In 1671 Kangxi invited Wu Sangui to Beijing — a reasonable enough request,
since he had not visited the capital in years and his professions of loyalty
had come only informally, by deputy. Wu Sangui's own son lived at court as a
hostage for his father's good conduct, married into the imperial family
himself, and — aware of the intrigues building against his father — sent a
private warning advising him not to come. It was an act of filial devotion
that, in Boulger's judgement, showed very little understanding of the
politics involved: Wu Sangui took the advice, declined the summons on the
grounds of his age, and sent his son the formal powers to perform the act of
allegiance by proxy instead.

Kangxi was not a ruler to be satisfied with a proxy. When two trusted
officials arrived to press the point in person, Wu Sangui received them with
every courtesy until they raised the actual purpose of their visit, and then
dropped all pretence at once: "Do they think at the court that I am so blind
as not to see the motive in this order of summons? I shall, indeed, present
myself there, if you continue to press me, but it will be at the head of
twice forty thousand men." He cut off the queue that marked Manchu
subjection, discarded the Manchu calendar, and declared open rebellion.

## A conspiracy that failed in Beijing

While the father raised the south-west, the son ran a parallel plot inside
the capital itself: a planned massacre of the imperial family and chief
officers of state, timed for the New Year's festival, built around a secretly
sworn body of enslaved Chinese eager to buy their freedom by striking first.
The scheme very nearly worked. On its eve, one of the conspirators - a slave
named Matsi, anxious to save the life of the Manchu master he served - warned
his owner, who carried the information straight to Kangxi. The son and most
of his fellow plotters were arrested, tried and executed before the plan
could be carried out, leaving Kangxi to face the father's rebellion without
also having to survive the son's.

## A common blaze of insurrection

Kangxi moved carefully rather than rashly, ordering the disbandment of the
native armies that Wu Sangui and other Chinese commanders in the south still
held under their own control — an edict aimed at every regional viceroy
alike, which had the effect of forcing every one of them to declare a side.
Wu Sangui's example proved infectious: from Fujian and Guangdong to the
borders of Tibet and Burma, the whole south rose in what Boulger calls one
common blaze of insurrection against the Manchus, and for a time the danger
to the dynasty Wu Sangui had helped install was as real as any it had faced
since 1644. Kangxi held his ground, wore the rebellion down province by
province over the years that followed, and by the time it collapsed for good
the throne he kept was, for the first time since his grandfather's
generation had ridden through Shanhaiguan, entirely and irreversibly his
own.
