Charles Gordon took command of the Ever Victorious Army on the 24th of
March, 1863, inheriting a force more likely, by Boulger's estimate, to
disobey its new English commander than to follow him. Two previous leaders,
Ward and Burgevine, had already come and gone, the war against the Taiping
had dragged on inconclusively for two years, and the five regiments of
foreign-officered, Chinese-manned troops under Gordon's nominal control were,
in the wrong hands, as capable of carving out a private dominion for
themselves as of fighting for the government that paid them. Unlike his
predecessors, Boulger notes pointedly, Gordon wanted nothing for himself.

## A campaign that began well

Gordon moved fast. Three days after reaching his headquarters at Songjiang he
was back in Shanghai buying ammunition; within a week his relief force had
broken the Taiping siege of Changshu by seizing an unoccupied walled town,
mounting guns on its ramparts, and shelling the rebel stockades until they
broke — the garrison's ex-rebel commander and his Chinese and French
defenders relieved without further loss. It was, in Boulger's judgement, a
demonstration to every wavering Taiping leader that abandoning a hopeless
cause and rallying to the government carried no automatic death sentence
with it, which mattered enormously for what came next at Suzhou.

## Soochow surrenders

The siege of Suzhou turned, in the end, less on Gordon's guns than on a
negotiation. Several of the city's Taiping commanders — the Wangs, as their
titles styled them — favoured coming to terms with the Qing forces besieging
them; only one, Mow Wang, wanted to fight to the last, and he was murdered by
his own colleagues to clear the way for surrender. Gordon personally
negotiated the terms with one of the surviving Wangs, Lar Wang, and pressed
on both his Chinese counterparts, General Ching and Li Hung Chang, the
absolute necessity of treating the surrendered rebel chiefs leniently and
honourably. Both men agreed with everything Gordon said. General Ching even
swore a personal oath of brotherhood with Lar Wang to seal it.

## What the oath was worth

Li Hung Chang had no intention of keeping either man's word. The surrendered
Wangs represented exactly the kind of able, experienced rebel commander he
had no wish to see absorbed into government service, and with Gordon's own
force withdrawn to Kunshan after a pay dispute, the moment to be rid of them
arrived unwatched. They were invited to an entertainment aboard the
governor's boat; Gordon's own lieutenant later found nine headless bodies on
the far side of a creek near the governor's quarters. Li Hung Chang did not
deny responsibility when the question was put to him afterward: "I accept
myself the full and sole responsibility for what has been done. But also
tell Gordon that this is China, not Europe... Therefore what was done was
inevitable."

Gordon, by Boulger's account, was prepared to become the murdered men's
avenger with his own hand, and was only prevented by circumstance from
getting the opportunity. He refused to serve any longer alongside men who had
broken faith so completely, and the breach very nearly ended his command of
the Ever Victorious Army altogether — over a promise the dynasty's own
generals had never regarded as binding on them in the first place.
