Yuan Shikai spent two months, on the throne's own commission, negotiating
with the representatives of the republican armies that had followed
Wuchang's lead across the southern and central provinces. Neither side would
yield to the other, trade stayed interrupted, and the fighting dragged on
precisely because the one question that mattered — what form the government
would take — remained unsettled. By February 1912 the court's own reading of
the situation, set down in the edict that ended it, was that the balance had
already tipped: the south had declared for a republic first, the northern
generals had since pledged their support to the same idea, and "from the
preference of the people's hearts, the Will of Heaven can be discerned."

## The edict

On the 12th of February, 1912, the Empress Dowager Longyu, regent for the
child emperor, issued the decree that closed the dynasty. It did not read
like a surrender. "How could We then bear to oppose the will of the millions
for the glory of one Family?" it asked, and answered its own question by
vesting sovereignty in the people and declaring in favour of "a republican
form of constitutional government" — instructing Yuan Shikai, already
elected Premier, to organise a provisional republican government in
consultation with the southern representatives, uniting north and south and
the five peoples of the empire, Manchu, Chinese, Mongol, Muslim and Tibetan,
in the one republic that would follow. A companion decree, issued the same
day in the young emperor's own name, asked only for the courteous
continuation of the treatment the throne had always received, so that the
imperial household might live out its retirement "free from responsibilities
and cares" while watching the republic it had just authorised come into
being.

## What ended, in the language it used to end

Kent, writing within months of the abdication, reaches for the phrase
Chinese tradition itself supplied: the Ta Ching dynasty — the House of Great
Purity — had "exhausted the Mandate of Heaven." He does not soften the
verdict on what came before it. Corrupt, unscrupulous and lacking in
political discrimination, the dynasty's end had been hastened rather than
caused by the folly of its last generation, and Kent allows that its
extinction was not undeserved. But he does not leave the judgement there
either. "The House of Nurhachi had once been great," he writes — an obscure
clan from the shadow of the Long White Mountain that had made itself a
conquering race, subjugated Manchuria from the Palisade to the Yalu, imposed
its authority on Mongol princes descended in the direct line from Genghis
Khan, and seated itself on the Dragon Throne by a combination of statecraft
and arms few frontier peoples in history ever managed to repeat.

## Two centuries, judged both ways

What followed the conquest, in Kent's own accounting, was neither simple
triumph nor simple tyranny. A warrior race skilled in war, the Manchus had
the wisdom to recognise that in the arts of peace the Chinese were their
masters, and rather than rule as an occupying garrison indefinitely, they
governed for more than two centuries largely through Chinese institutions
and Chinese officials — well enough, Kent notes, quoting the sinologist
Samuel Wells Williams, that the population "has been better governed by the
Manchus than under the princes of the Ming Dynasty; there has been more
vigour in the administration of government, and less palace favouritism and
intrigue in the appointment of officers, more security of life and property
... in a word, the Manchu sway has well developed the industry and resources
of the country." But the same conquerors who had learned to rule through
Chinese institutions learned, in the generations that followed, to be
undone by Chinese luxury as thoroughly as any native dynasty before them —
two centuries at the wealthiest court in Asia finishing what the hard
lessons of camp and battlefield had first built, and could no longer sustain
by the time a colonel's forced conscription into a rebellion in Wuchang put
the question of the Mandate of Heaven to the test one last time.
