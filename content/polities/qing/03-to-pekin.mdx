The Ming did not fall to the Manchus. They fell first to Li Zicheng, a rebel
of, in Boulger's phrase, most ignoble origin whose principal object appeared
to be plunder, and whose army took Beijing after only a few days' attack in
1644. The last Ming emperor was dead, and for a brief moment Li Zicheng
looked like the master of China.

## A general with a choice of evils

One force alone stood between Li Zicheng and total control: the veteran
army of Wu Sangui, holding the fortresses of Ningyuan and Shanhaiguan on the
Manchu border under orders to defend against the very enemy he was about to
invite in. Recalled too late to save the capital, Wu Sangui learned of
Beijing's fall and the emperor's death while still completing his march
south. Li Zicheng sent him lavish offers of support; Wu Sangui rejected all
of them. He judged — correctly, as it turned out, though not in the way he
intended — that asking Manchu help to punish a rebel was the lesser of two
evils compared with becoming a rebel's partner in ruling what remained of
the empire.

## The invitation

The Manchus had watched Wu Sangui's fortresses for decades without finding a
way past them. His letter asking their court to send an army against Li
Zicheng handed them, without a fight, the Great Wall and the two fortresses
that had blocked every previous attempt at conquest. The request was granted
before the ink of his letter had properly dried, and the cry went up through
Manchu camps that had waited generations for exactly this: "To Pekin!"

## Yungping and Chingting

Wu Sangui moved first, without waiting for his new allies to arrive, and
destroyed a force Li Zicheng had sent to negotiate rather than fight —
twenty thousand rebels left on the field. Li Zicheng himself then marched out
with sixty thousand men, the pick of his army, taking Wu Sangui's own father
as a hostage. At Yungping, Li's larger force nearly surrounded Wu Sangui's
army entirely before a Manchu contingent arrived and turned an apparent
Li Zicheng victory into a rout; thirty thousand of his best men fell, and Li
escaped with a few hundred horsemen. When Wu Sangui reached Beijing's walls
in pursuit, he found his father's head displayed on the ramparts — Li's
answer to his refusal, and the point past which any reconciliation between
the two men became impossible.

Li Zicheng fled Beijing with as much plunder as he could carry, was overtaken
and lost another ten thousand men at the bridge of Likao, and made one final
stand near Chingting with an army swollen to two hundred thousand by the
arrival of eighty thousand fresh Manchu troops and Chinese levies flocking to
what now looked like the winning side. Li lost forty thousand men in a single
night's fighting and fled west; his own troops eventually refused to make a
further stand for him, and he was run down and killed by a local militia
while foraging for food with a handful of followers, his head carried in
triumph to the nearest official.

## What the invitation actually bought

Wu Sangui had asked for help chastising a rebel. What he received was a new
dynasty on the throne he had helped clear. The Manchus who entered Beijing as
his allies in 1644 did not leave it, and the fortresses that had kept them
out of China for three decades — the ones his own letter had opened —
became, from that year forward, simply the road into the capital rather than
the wall around it.
