Qianlong came to the throne in October 1735, sixty years after his
grandfather Kangxi had broken Wu Sangui's rebellion and secured the dynasty
his own grandfather Nurhaci had never lived to see rule an inch of China. By
the time Qianlong abdicated in 1796 — a year early, by his own choice, so
that his reign would not run longer in the record than Kangxi's — the empire
he handed to his son controlled, in Boulger's words, territory stretching
"from Siam to Siberia and from Nepaul to Corea." No military feat remained
for the loftiest ambition to accomplish. It was, as Boulger puts it flatly,
the pinnacle of Manchu power.

## An ambassador who would not kneel

The clearest single measure of how that power was regarded abroad arrived in
1793, in the person of Lord Macartney, the first British ambassador granted
audience with a Qing emperor. Every European envoy before him had performed
the kowtow — three kneelings and nine prostrations — as the price of
admission to the imperial presence. Macartney refused, and managed the
refusal with enough tact that Qianlong himself took no offence, rising from
his own seat at a banquet to pour Macartney a glass of wine with his own
hand. The Board of Rites was considerably less forgiving, declaring that
dispensing with the ceremony had sullied the lustre of a long and glorious
reign and that nothing but future humiliation could be expected from so
proud and unbending a nation.

The embassy failed at everything it had actually been sent to accomplish —
no resident minister, no new ports, no change to the terms on which Britain
traded at Canton. But the audience itself, granted without the ceremony that
had subordinated every earlier European visitor, was read on both sides as
proof of something else: that the court granting it could afford, at its own
height, to be generous about a formality that a weaker court could not have
survived conceding.

## What outlasted the pinnacle

Boulger is explicit about how briefly the height lasted. With Qianlong's
death the stern qualities that had sustained a widely extended empire departed
Chinese history along with him, and the vigour that had built for a century
and a half over three long reigns gave way, in his account, to a decline as
consistent and as rapid as the rise had been. The generation that inherited
Qianlong's empire had, in Boulger's own judgement, only to retain what he had
won. It would not manage even that.
