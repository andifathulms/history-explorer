For three decades after the Taiping rebellion was finally crushed, the Qing
court did something it had never seriously attempted before: it tried,
deliberately and at real expense, to build the military and industrial
machinery of a modern power. The 1894 war with Japan is usually told as a
foregone conclusion. It was, in fact, the test of a genuine reform effort -
and its failure is more instructive for that.

## Arsenals and foreign drill

The clearest commitment came in the military sphere. English officers
drilled Chinese troops in a permanent camp at Fengwang; Sir Halliday
Macartney built and organised a major arsenal at Suzhou, later relocated to
the more convenient site of Nanjing, which was in full working order by
1866. Plans were made to replace the foreign gunboats that had been
withdrawn to Europe with new vessels supplied from America - though Chinese
delay, the record notes dryly, postponed the actual start of that fleet for
a full decade. The customs revenue Robert Hart's Maritime Customs Service
had built into a reliable two million taels a year, rising as the Taiping
wars ended and trade along the Yangtze grew safer, gave the court a genuine
financial base to work from that earlier reforms had never had.

## What the reformers could not sell

Railways and telegraphs met a different reception entirely. When the
Shanghai-Wusong line - a short stretch of track, barely worth the name of a
railway by European standards - was finally built and half-opened in the
summer of 1876, ordinary Chinese excitement at the novelty was, for a few
weeks, as visible as anything the foreign engineers had hoped for. Mandarin
hostility followed almost immediately, threatening mob violence serious
enough that the British minister asked the operating company to suspend
service. The settlement that followed let the Chinese government buy the
line outright and take over its management after a fixed period - and once
that period arrived, officials neglected it deliberately, with the explicit
aim of destroying it, before finally having the rails and rolling stock torn
up and shipped to Formosa. It was the single clearest demonstration
available of the gap between the reform program's military wing, which the
court funded and defended, and its civilian half, which the same court's own
provincial officials worked to sabotage the moment it threatened local
authority or ordinary superstition about disturbing the land.

## A program that was real, and real enough to lose

None of this was cosmetic. Arsenals were built and stayed built; a customs
service genuinely modernised; military drill genuinely improved under
foreign instruction; and by the 1880s and 1890s the resulting fleet and
army were substantial enough, on paper, that neither Chinese nor foreign
observers expected the humiliating collapse that followed when Japan
actually tested them in 1894. That is exactly what makes the defeat worth
taking seriously rather than treating as inevitable. China had genuinely
tried what Japan had tried in the same decades - foreign drill, foreign
arsenals, a modern fleet - and applied real money and real effort to it for
thirty years. What it never managed was Japan's other half of the
programme: an administration willing to let reform actually redistribute
authority rather than simply purchase new equipment for the old one to
command. The Woosung railway's own fate was the whole story in miniature,
years before Weihaiwei made it a national one.
