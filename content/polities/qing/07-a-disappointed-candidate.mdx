Hong Xiuquan was the son of a small farmer near the North River, a district
associated with the Hakka minority whose exclusion from the ordinary
privileges of Chinese life extended, in practice if not in law, to the civil
service examinations that were every ambitious commoner's one route upward.
His friends claimed he placed high in each of the periodic examinations he
sat; Boulger is skeptical of the claim, but the outcome is not in doubt —
Hong failed to obtain his degree, was shut out of the public service, and
carried the injury of it, sharpened rather than dulled by his own friends'
flattery, for the rest of his life. Denied the chance to serve the
government, he found, with what Boulger calls a shrewd and revengeful
astuteness, that he could instead become its enemy.

## An uncertain origin, a rapid spread

Where the movement's specific ideology came from is a question Boulger
declines to settle outright — there was a tradition, which he treats with
open reservation, that it had been conceived inside a Buddhist monastery
whose members had old scores to settle with the government, and had gone
looking through the provinces for the most disaffected ground they could
find to plant it in. Whatever its origin, no province offered more fertile
conditions than Guangxi, and by the summer of 1850 the rebels there had grown
strong enough to besiege the town of Wuzhou with a force rumour placed as
high as fifty thousand men. The Guangxi governor was too afraid of the
consequences to even report the scale of the crisis to Beijing.

## Neither reform nor restraint

The rebels' own proclamations claimed to speak for the Chinese people against
Manchu usurpers — "the present dynasty are only Manchus, people of a small
nation... why then are troops causelessly sent against us?" — but their
conduct in the field matched no such claim to legitimacy. They plundered and
massacred without regard to age or sex wherever they went, sparing only
those women who could be put to use, and an imperial force ambushed near
Canton was wiped out to the last man in a single engagement. When the main
body finally settled on a single leader and gave him the royal title of
Tianwang, the Heavenly King, the movement's political weight increased
overnight — it could no longer be dismissed, by Beijing or by anyone else,
as a matter of banditry that would burn itself out.

## What a failed examination produced

A man excluded from the ordinary machinery of Chinese advancement had found
the one avenue still open to him, and used it on a scale the examiners who
turned him away could never have imagined answering for. The rebellion Hong
Xiuquan's disappointment set loose would take the Qing dynasty the better
part of fifteen years, several provincial armies, and eventually a
foreign-led mercenary corps to put down.
