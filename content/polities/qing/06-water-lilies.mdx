Qianlong left his son an empire at the height of its own reputation abroad
and its own exchequer at home. Within a few years of Jiaqing's accession,
both were visibly in decline, and the reign that followed spent itself
answering a threat that never came from outside China at all.

## Eighty million taels

The first crisis was entirely domestic. Heshen, Qianlong's own favourite
minister for the final decades of his reign, had held the keys to the
treasury and the disposal of Imperial favour largely unsupervised, his aging
master's confidence in him effectively unlimited. Once Qianlong died,
Heshen's enemies moved immediately, and Jiaqing signed the order for his
execution on evidence that left, in the record's own judgment, little room
for doubt: a confiscated fortune reckoned at some eighty million taels,
roughly twenty-five million pounds sterling, extracted from a state Jiaqing
himself would spend the rest of his reign describing as chronically short of
money.

## The Water-lily sect

The money did not go toward fixing the problems it might have solved. Real
danger to the dynasty came instead from a secret society, the Pe-leen-keaou
or White Water-lily sect, first denounced by the court in connection with an
1803 assassination attempt on the emperor himself, in the streets of the
capital. Whether or not the society had actually been behind that particular
plot, the government's proscription of it did nothing to weaken it. Driven
underground and rebranded under a succession of new names - the Society of
Celestial Reason, and later the Triads - the movement spread through
Shantung and three neighboring provinces, no longer content with secrecy but
in open hostility to Qing rule. Jiaqing himself later described eight
unbroken years of military operations against the resulting unrest, though
almost none of the campaign's actual details survive in the record.

## An emperor twice nearly killed by his own subjects

The society's boldest strike came in 1813, when roughly two hundred rebels
breached the walls of the Forbidden City itself - by a gate, according to
some accounts, or by climbing over, according to others - and fought their
way toward the emperor's own chambers. Jiaqing survived only because his
second son, Prince Minning, showed a courage nobody had expected from so
retiring a young man: seizing a gun, he shot two of the intruders himself,
while a nephew of the emperor's dispatched a third. It was the second time
in a decade Jiaqing had escaped assassination by his own subjects rather
than by any foreign hand, and his gratitude was immediate - Minning was
proclaimed heir apparent on the spot, with full ceremony, and would go on to
reign as the Daoguang emperor.

## What twenty-five years produced

Jiaqing died in 1820 having governed, by the record's own unsparing verdict,
in a way that did almost as much harm to his dynasty in twenty-five years as
his father had done good across sixty. He inherited a court whose
foundations his father's own long reign had left secure, and left his own
successor a diminished treasury, a weakened administration and a population
that had watched secret societies grow bold enough to nearly reach the
throne twice. Nothing in the reign came from a foreign power - the whole of
it was domestic corruption, domestic rebellion, and a domestic failure to
convert one into a remedy for the other. It set the terms, financial and
institutional both, that the dynasty would still be operating under twenty
years later when foreign pressure finally did arrive at Canton.
