Nurhaci's clan ruled a valley so small that its length did not exceed twelve
miles, a stockade in the hills below the Long White Mountains where the
Jurchen — the Niuche, as Chinese writers of the day called the scattered
descendants of the Jin dynasty the Mongols had broken three centuries
earlier — lived under Ming tolerance rather than Ming rule. It was, Boulger
writes, well adapted to be the cradle of a race of conquerors, on much the
same pattern as the valley that had cradled the Mongols themselves, only
smaller and closer to the Chinese frontier.

## The murder that made him

In 1583 a rival chief named Nikan induced the Chinese commander in Liaodong
to help him attack a neighbour — a neighbour who happened to be married to
Nurhaci's cousin. Nurhaci's grandfather and father marched to the man's
defence, and the Chinese, unable to take the defended town by force, resorted
to treachery instead: they lured a large part of the garrison outside the
walls under a pretence of negotiation and massacred them. Nurhaci's
grandfather and father were among the dead.

Vengeance became, in Boulger's phrase, the principal object of Nurhaci's
life, and to the murder of his kinsmen must be attributed the origin of the
danger that would eventually cost the Ming their throne. He spent three years
manoeuvring to get Nikan into his power, by force or through the Chinese
authorities who had made Nikan chief of all the Jurchen districts specifically
to check Nurhaci's own rising influence. In 1586 the Chinese, tired of
propping up a client who could not defend himself, handed Nikan over. Nurhaci
killed him at once.

## Building something that would outlast a feud

With his father's murder avenged, Nurhaci turned to the wider ambition Nikan
had been reaching for before him: uniting the scattered Jurchen clans into a
single confederacy. He built a walled capital, drew up a simple code of laws
suited to his people, drilled a small standing army, and — while doing all
of this — kept faith with the Chinese border officials, accepting their
titles and gifts and giving them no pretext to intervene against him a second
time. In 1591 he annexed the Yalu river district in a sudden, nearly
unresisted stroke, and the ease of that success told every neighbouring chief
what kind of ambition they were now dealing with. Seven of them combined
against him, bringing thirty thousand Jurchen and Mongol troops into the
field to undo what a border feud had started and a single-minded vendetta had
built into something much larger than a feud.

## Not yet an empire

Nurhaci beat the coalition, absorbed most of what remained of it, and spent
the next quarter-century doing the same to whoever was left. He declared his
own dynasty in 1616 — the year his descendants would use, ever afterward, to
date the House that eventually ruled China, though in 1616 it ruled nothing
south of the Great Wall and would not for another twenty-eight years. What it
already had, by then, was everything a conquest would later need: a standing
army, a functioning administration, a capital, and a founder for whom the
debt owed to the Ming had never once, in thirty years, been allowed to go
unpaid.
