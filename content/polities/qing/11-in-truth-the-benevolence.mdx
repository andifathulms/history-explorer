Port Arthur and Weihaiwei fell to Japanese forces in what Boulger calls
ignominious surrender, and by January 1895 the war had made plain what
neither side had been fully prepared to believe when it began: a state the
Qing court had never regarded as a serious military rival had beaten it
decisively on land and sea both. On paper China's navy had looked formidable.
In practice, after the fall of its strongest fortress, no foreign observer
believed it capable of contesting the peace terms Japan intended to impose.

## An old statesman's wound

Li Hung Chang, the only Chinese statesman with the standing and diplomatic
skill to negotiate a peace worth having, reached Shimonoseki on the 20th of
March, 1895, as China's plenipotentiary. Four days later, returning from a
conference with the Japanese negotiator Count Ito, he was shot in the cheek
by a would-be assassin. The wound was not serious, but the outrage produced
a wave of sympathy in Japan that worked entirely in China's favour: the
Emperor sent his own physician, the Empress prepared the dressings with her
own hands, and Li Hung Chang, never a man to waste a diplomatic opening,
answered with a line calculated to keep the sympathy flowing — "In truth the
benevolence of Her Majesty the Empress of Japan is profound as the sea."
Japan granted an armistice and softened several of its harshest original
terms as a direct consequence.

## The terms

The Treaty of Shimonoseki, signed on the 17th of April, cost China Formosa
and the Pescadores, the Liaodong peninsula and the fortress of Port Arthur,
an indemnity of two hundred million taels payable in eight instalments, and
new commercial concessions opening China's rivers and lakes to Japanese
shipping — while formally guaranteeing, in its first article, the complete
independence of Korea from Chinese suzerainty. Given the scale of Japan's
victory, Boulger judges the terms severe but not excessive. What made them
unsustainable was not their weight on China but their effect on the wider
balance of power in the region.

## A triumph reversed by everyone but the victor's actual enemy

Russia could not tolerate a Japanese foothold on the Manchurian mainland
commanding the approach to Beijing, and moved with a speed and coordination
Boulger calls unprecedented in the East: the loose Franco-Russian
understanding was converted into a formal alliance, Germany's cooperation
secured alongside it, and within weeks of the treaty's signing Japan found
herself confronted by all three powers demanding, in polite language that
left no real room for refusal, the surrender of her claim to Port Arthur and
the Liaodong peninsula. Japan gave way, exchanging the peninsula for a
further six million pounds of indemnity.

## What the rescuer charged for the rescue

Li Hung Chang travelled to Moscow the following year as China's envoy to the
Tsar's coronation, and while there signed a secret convention settling
Russia's own price for having forced Japan out of Liaodong: control of the
very peninsula Russia had just finished rescuing from Japanese hands, Port
Arthur and Dalian included. China had paid an indemnity to recover territory
from one power only to hand its actual control to another that had never
fired a shot in the war at all — a transaction Boulger declines to
characterise in the terms it deserved, on the grounds that the events were
still too recent for the judgement to be written without a charge of
partisanship, but that he clearly expected history to make in his place.
