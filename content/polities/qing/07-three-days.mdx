Opium had been banned in China for decades before Commissioner Lin arrived
at Canton, and banned to no visible effect: the Kiaqing emperor's own edicts
against it had failed to stop either the smoking or the steady outflow of
Chinese silver that paid for the trade. By the 1830s the court's patience
with half-measures had run out, and Lin Zexu was sent south with the
authority, and the reputation for courage and capacity, to finally settle
the question.

## An edict of remarkable power

Lin made his formal entry into Canton on the 10th of March, 1839, and within
a week produced an edict Boulger admits was written with real literary power
and, he allows, "breathing the noblest moral sentiments" — addressed to the
foreign merchants in terms of pure superiority, commanding them to listen
and obey "with trembling." He forbade any foreigner to leave Canton until
the matter was settled, and made unmistakably clear, within days of his
arrival, that what he wanted was not a negotiated compromise but the
complete humiliation of the trade and the people running it.

His most consequential proclamation demanded the surrender, within three
days, of every chest of opium held by foreign merchants at Canton. After a
brief standoff, and mostly through individual decisions rather than any
collective vote of the merchant community, more than a thousand chests were
handed over to be destroyed. Captain Elliot, the British superintendent of
trade, responded by ordering British ships to assemble at Hong Kong and
prepare to resist any further aggression by force — the moment, Boulger
notes, at which a collision that had been building for years finally became
unavoidable.

## Two governments that could not understand each other

Neither side found the other's position remotely legible. The Chinese
mandarins could not conceive of the foreigners as anything other than
traders who existed on Chinese sufferance; Elliot spoke, and expected to be
treated, as the representative of a power as proud as the Emperor of China
himself. When an apparent attempt was made to seize the merchant Mr. Dent
under cover of an invitation, the Canton settlement found itself surrounded
by an armed crowd of soldiers and coolies, and for a moment the British
community feared a massacre. The London government's own position, meanwhile,
had already been made plain in advance: it would not defend the opium trade
as such, and would raise no objection if the Chinese government chose to
seize and destroy it. What London would not tolerate was the detention of
British subjects and the closure of British trade generally — a distinction
the Chinese authorities, focused entirely on the one commodity they had
banned, never fully registered.

## What three days of defiance cost

Lin's edict was meant to end a trade the court had spent decades failing to
suppress by half-measures. It ended instead with a war fought over terms Lin
himself had never set: not whether opium would be sold, but whether a
foreign power's subjects and shipping could be detained, confined and
threatened by a government that regarded them as beneath the ordinary
courtesies of diplomacy. China had the moral argument. It did not have the
navy, and the treaty that followed the war would be written by the side that
did.
