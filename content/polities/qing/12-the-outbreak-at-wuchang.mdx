An explosion in a rented house in the Russian concession at Hankou, on the
afternoon of the 9th of October, 1911, betrayed a revolutionary bomb factory
that had no business drawing attention to itself yet. The raid that followed
turned up finished bombs, bomb-making materials, revolutionary pamphlets,
white sleeve badges, maps of Wuchang, plans for attacking the city gates, and
— worst of all for the conspirators — what looked like a full membership
roll. Two of the ringleaders escaped arrest, one of them with half his face
blown off by the blast; thirty others were seized, and three were executed
outside the Viceroy's residence before dawn the next day.

## A rising forced before it was ready

The revolutionary plan had never been complete, and everyone involved knew
it. But with a membership list already in government hands, delay meant
being hunted down piecemeal, and Liu Kung — one of the two leaders still at
large — spent the following night persuading soldiers of the New Army's own
ranks that rising immediately was their only real chance of survival. The
attack began around eight o'clock that evening, ahead of the midnight the
plotters had originally set, when a troop of army engineers turned on their
own camp outside the city. Within half an hour the barracks had fallen; most
of the other regiments proved half-hearted in their loyalty once they
recognised an organised movement rather than an isolated mutiny, and threw in
with it rather than against it.

## The Viceroy's flight

Viceroy Jui Cheng, a Manchu who understood better than almost anyone what
the loss of Wuchang would mean for the dynasty he served, judged the
situation hopeless almost as soon as the attack began and fled with his
family to a gunboat on the river rather than attempt to hold his own
residence. It was, on the evidence of what happened next, the choice that
saved his life: revolutionary artillery had already taken Serpent Hill,
which commands the whole city, and the yamen he abandoned was overrun,
looted and partly burned within hours by soldiers in no mood to spare any
Manchu they found.

## A colonel who did not want the command

The soldiers who had just destroyed the old order in Wuchang needed someone
to stop them destroying everything else along with it, and settled on
Colonel Li Yuanhong — a forty-seven-year-old Hupei man, trained at the
Tientsin Naval College and in Japan, whose real talent had gone unrewarded
for years under superiors more skilled at flattering their Manchu patrons
than at soldiering. Li refused the command when it was first offered him.
His own more liberal sympathies argued for accepting it; his caution argued
against gambling his life on a rising that might yet fail. The
revolutionaries settled the argument for him: he was told plainly that
refusal would mean execution, and accepted under that threat rather than any
conviction of his own. He did not look back afterward. Within weeks the
reluctant colonel forced into revolutionary command was one of the
movement's most valuable assets, and within months, province after province
across China had followed Wuchang's example without needing a threat of
execution to persuade them.
