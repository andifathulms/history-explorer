Ármin Vámbéry crossed the Turkmen desert in 1863 dressed as a Sunni dervish coming
back from Mecca, because that was the only way a European could see Bukhara and
survive it. What he found was a city organised around two things: learning, and
watching people.

## The medreses

Bukhara was the religious authority of Central Asia — the place a scholar from
Kazan or Kashgar or Kabul came to be trained, and the place that certified what
Islam meant for a quarter of a continent.

Vámbéry counted its colleges, described their endowments, and then delivered a
judgement that a man in his position was in some danger for holding: that they had
been founded as seminaries not of learning but of fanaticism. He is a
nineteenth-century European writing for London and his contempt is easy to
discount, but he had spent months with the students, in their language, and the
observation he grounds it on is specific — that the questions the ulema of these
cities wanted answered concerned the correct method of washing the hands, feet,
face and back of the head, and how a man should sit, walk, lie and sleep.

One of the colleges, he notes drily, was founded by the Empress Catherine.

## The police

> The system of police in Bokhara is more severe than in any other city of known
> parts of Asia.

By day the *reis* walked the bazaars and public places himself, with his
dependants and his spies out around him. About two hours after sunset nobody
dared show himself in the streets: a neighbour could not visit a neighbour, and a
sick man ran the risk of dying for want of a doctor. The emir had announced that
the night watchmen might arrest *him* if they met him abroad during the forbidden
hours.

## Who lived there

Two and a half million people, by Vámbéry's estimate, with frontiers that could
not be exactly drawn: Kokand and the Badakhshan mountains east, the Oxus and the
districts beyond it south, the great desert west and north.

The composition is the most revealing part of his survey. Uzbeks, the ruling race,
who had lived so closely with the Tajiks that they had lost much of their
distinctness. Tajiks — the old Persian population of the towns. Kirghiz. Sixty
thousand Arabs, descendants of the armies of the conquest, of whom he found very
few who could still speak Arabic. Forty thousand Merv Persians, deported wholesale
to Bukhara when the emir took that city around 1810. Hindus. Jews, confined to
their own quarter.

And Persians — partly slaves, partly men who had bought their own freedom and
stayed, because living was cheap and the profits better than at home. Vámbéry's
note on them is the sharpest institutional observation in the book: a Persian in
Bukhara commonly rose from slave to the highest offices in the state. Hardly a
governor in the provinces did not employ in some post a Persian who had been his
own slave and stayed loyal. They swarmed round the emir himself, the first
dignitaries of the khanate belonged to that nation, and so did the commanders of
the army.

A Sunni state whose chief officers were Shia captives from the country it raided.
Vámbéry points out the consequence: if Persia ever invaded, the emir would bitterly
rue it, because his generals were Persians.
