Abdul Aziz held the throne his own rebellion had won him for twenty-three
years, and then walked away from it exactly as his uncle and father had
- on pilgrimage, blessing his own successor rather than fighting him for
the privilege of dying on it.

## The fattest man of his age

Abdul Aziz is remembered by his own chroniclers less for statecraft than
for scale: reputedly the stoutest man of his time, a patron who kept a
calligrapher on retainer for seven years to produce a single copy of
Hafiz at the rate of one couplet a day, and a khan who answered a poet's
satire about his own girth not with punishment but with ten thousand
dinars and a robe of honour - a generosity the poet found more
humiliating than a blade would have been, and fled to India rather than
live with. He fought off Abu'l-Ghazi of Khiva's invasions through the
1660s, and in 1680, exhausted by feuding with his own brothers as much
as any external enemy, set out for Mecca with three thousand pilgrims in
his train, fought off a Bedouin ransom demand in the desert at
seventy-something years old, and died at his journey's end.

## Subhan Quli's Balkh problem

His brother Subhan Quli, who inherited Bukhara the way the family now
always did - by outliving whoever went on pilgrimage - spent his own
thirty-one-year reign fighting a version of the same war their father
and uncle had: control of Balkh, the wealthy southern province every
Janid prince treated as a launching pad for the throne itself. Subhan
Quli's own sons murdered each other over its governorship one after
another - a poisoning, an assassination during a four-month
usurpation, a flaying-alive in revenge for it - while Anusha Khan of
Khiva raided to the very outskirts of Bukhara before Subhan Quli's
general broke him at Ghijduvan and drove him home. Aurangzeb, ruling an
empire that had once occupied Balkh outright, wrote seeking an alliance
against Persia instead of another invasion; Subhan Quli, no soldier
himself but a genuine scholar, spent the peace it bought him building
one of Bukhara's most celebrated colleges and writing what Vambery
believed to be the first medical treatise composed in Turkish. He died
in 1702, eighty years old, having reigned at Bukhara for thirty-one of
them after twenty-three already spent governing Balkh.

## The nephew who would not wait

The succession broke along exactly the line Balkh's governors always
threatened it would. The Bukharan grandees crowned Subhan Quli's young
son Ubaydullah without consulting his own grandson Muqim, who had
expected to inherit Balkh's governorship outright and took five months
even to send condolences, then wrote back as an equal rather than a
subject. War between uncle and great-nephew followed within the year,
decided less by battle than by which side the province's real power -
the atalik Mahmud - chose to back.

It was the same appanage machinery that had run the Bukharan throne
since Baki Muhammad's own generation, still turning a century later:
brothers, sons and grandsons treating Balkh as a rival capital, and a
khanate whose military strength lay increasingly with the atalik class
that arbitrated between them. Within another four decades that same
Manghit atalik line would stop arbitrating for the Janids and start
ruling in their place.
