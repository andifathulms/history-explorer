The Janids lost the state in 1740 without losing the throne, and the men who took
it from them waited forty-five years to say so.

## Nader Shah

The Persian conqueror crossed the Oxus in 1740 and reduced Bukhara to submission,
and the khanate never recovered its independence of action under that dynasty. The
Janids stayed on the throne. Everything that made a throne worth having went to
the officers who had been running the state for them — the Manghit ataliqs, chiefs
of one of the Uzbek tribes, who had held the effective administration for a
generation.

It is the oldest arrangement in the Turco-Mongol world: a family that cannot
legitimately hold the title governs in the name of one that can. Edigü did it to the Golden Horde for twenty years. Timur did it to
the western Chagatai khans. The Manghits did it to the Janids for the better part
of half a century.

## Shah Murad

In 1785 Shah Murad ended it, set the last Janid aside and took the sovereignty
himself.

And then solved, in one word, the problem that had defeated everyone else who tried
this. He did not call himself khan. Khan required descent from Chinggis and he had
none; so he called himself *amir* — commander — which required only that he command,
and shifted the whole basis of the state's legitimacy from genealogy to religion.

He was well placed to do it. Shah Murad is remembered as an ascetic who wore coarse
clothes, refused the revenues of his office, and lived on what he earned copying
manuscripts. In the most orthodox city in Central Asia that was worth more than a
pedigree.

The Manghits ruled from 1785 to 1920, longer than any dynasty in the region's
modern history, and every one of them was amir and not khan.

## What the title cost

It also fixed what the state could be.

An emir of Bukhara held his position as the protector of the faith and the patron
of its colleges, which meant the ulema had a permanent veto they did not have
elsewhere, and which meant that piety and severity were the same currency. This is
the context for the police system Vámbéry described, and for the reputation the
city had among its own neighbours.

A khan can be arbitrary and remain a khan. An amir whose whole warrant is religious
zeal has to keep proving it.
