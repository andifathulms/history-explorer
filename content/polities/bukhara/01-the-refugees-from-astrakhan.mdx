A few years after Ivan IV took Astrakhan, an elderly prince of its royal house
left the Volga for Transoxiana and put himself under the protection of the Uzbek
khan at Bukhara. Thirty years after that his grandson was on the Bukharan throne.

## The inheritance

Yar Muhammad — called Qari, the grey, on account of his age — descended from
Kuchuk Muhammad of the Golden Horde through a younger son, which made him a
cousin of the khans the Russians had just dispossessed. He arrived in Bukhara
around 1567, and Iskandar Khan of the Shaybanid line gave his daughter Zuhra
Khanum to Yar Muhammad's son Jan Sultan.

That marriage is the whole legal basis of what followed. When the Shaybanid line
of Abu'l-Khayr failed after Abdullah Khan II, the Uzbeks needed a Chinggisid and
had one to hand — a family with an impeccable Jochid descent, no local power base
and a mother who was Abdullah's own sister.

## The road to the throne

It was not a peaceful transfer. Jan's son Din Muhammad was set up at Herat by his
own grandson's manoeuvring, held only Herat and its district, and was a tool in
other men's hands; he and his father probably died in or after the battle of Pul-i
Salar, where the Persians broke the Uzbeks badly. Din Muhammad himself fell into
the hands of the Qara Turkmen and was put to death.

His wife escaped by the courage of a servant called Khaki Yasaul, who put her and
her two small sons into bags on either side of his saddle and rode. A bullet
struck one of the boys — Nadir Muhammad — in the foot and lamed him for life.

The two surviving brothers, Baqi Muhammad and Wali Muhammad, got away into
Transoxiana, and Baqi Muhammad took the throne. Howorth's verdict is flat: he was
in reality the first khan of Bukhara of the new dynasty.

## What the dynasty was

The Janids, or Ashtarkhanids — the name means *the Astrakhanis*, and that is
exactly what they were.

The change of ruling house was less of a break than it looks. The new line held
its title through marriage into the old one, governed the same Uzbek tribal
confederation, kept the same officers, and faced the same two problems: Persia
across the Oxus, and the tribal amirs at home.

What was genuinely new was that the axis of the Uzbek world had swung. The
Shaybanids had come south out of the Kipchak steppe as conquerors within living
memory of Muhammad Shaybani. The Janids came as exiles from a khanate that a
European empire had already annexed. Between 1552 and 1599 the Jochid world lost
Kazan, Astrakhan and the lower Volga; what was left of its royal families
collected in Transoxiana.

Bukhara for the next three centuries was where that world ended up.
