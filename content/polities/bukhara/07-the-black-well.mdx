Between 1838 and 1842 two British officers were held in Bukhara and killed there,
and the record of it is the most detailed portrait there is of how Nasrullah's
court actually worked.

## Stoddart

Colonel Charles Stoddart was sent to Bukhara as an envoy, and gave immediate
offence — over how he arrived, how he sat, and what he would not say. He was put in
confinement. One day the vizier came into his room and he was taken out to the
Rigistan, the great square, and told to do what everybody but the amir did there;
he objected. He would not make the supplication of a slave, and he refused to be
moved on the point.

Then his papers were seized and burnt, his effects and horses sold, and he was
lowered by a rope into the Siah Chah — the black well — a dungeon pit twenty-one feet
deep where the worst criminals were kept. He found two thieves and a murderer at
the bottom of it. He stayed there two months, covered with vermin and surrounded by
reptiles, his food let down on a rope, smoking most of the time.

Then he was taken out and offered his life if he would become a Muslim. Worn down
by what he had endured, he consented, repeated the confession of faith, and was
circumcised in the public square in front of an enormous crowd drawn by the novelty
of it.

Russian officials worked for his release and got it, whereupon his nerve came back
and he renounced the conversion he had made under force. The amir was so struck by
this that he took off his own fur cloak, gave it to him, and had him led in triumph
through the streets.

## The offers

What follows is stranger than the cruelty.

Nasrullah began courting him. He hoped, one account says, to attach him as Ranjit
Singh had attached other Englishmen. *Those Feringhis*, the amir said, *so powerful
and clever in the arts and diplomacy, so talented in organising an army, bring
everywhere success in their train.* He sent Stoddart tobacco and presents and asked
him in return to replace the quicksilver on his mirrors, to make him a thermometer,
and to make candles that would burn without smoke.

He offered to send him with an embassy to St Petersburg, from which he could simply
have gone home. Stoddart refused, on the ground that he had not received orders
from his own government to withdraw from Bukhara — a devotion that was not
understood and only deepened the suspicion that he was a spy. The amir ordered him
poisoned; he survived by exciting the greed of a courtier who hoped for a ransom.

The letters he got out in this period, sewn into the hems of the robes of
Khorasanis, Kurds, Persians and Jews, breathe the spirit of Cromwell's Puritans;
the soldier and the diplomat in them stand far below the religious conviction that
governed him.

## The answer to Khiva

The sultan of Turkey, the tsar and the khan of Khiva all asked for his release. To
the first two Nasrullah returned ambiguous answers. To Khiva he wrote something
that explains the entire relationship of the three khanates to the two empires
circling them:

> You have an Englishman and so have I. Why do you wish to take mine?

The Englishman at Khiva was Captain Arthur Conolly, who had been sent to Kokand to
explore it and to check the Russian advance. Since Bukhara regarded Kokand as in
some sense dependent on it, and since Conolly's visit coincided with the Kokandi
khan's defiance, Nasrullah decided Conolly was the cause of it. A letter came from
Stoddart inviting him to Bukhara; his Khivan and Kokandi friends told him not to go;
he applied for a safe-conduct and got one at once, because the amir wanted him.

He went to persuade Nasrullah to join the other Uzbek princes in a league against
Russia. Both men were executed in 1842.

## What it was really about

Not two officers. Two empires, and three khanates trying to work out what to do
about them.

Nasrullah's sentence is not a joke. He had understood exactly what a European was
worth on that market — a bargaining counter, a source of thermometers and army
drill, a hostage, a proof of standing — and he was not going to hand his over to a
rival khan who already had one of his own.

Within thirty years one of the two empires had taken all three khanates, and the
khans who had bargained over Englishmen were signing whatever was put in front of
them.
