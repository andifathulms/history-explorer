The emirate lost its independence over five years and never lost a dynasty, a
capital or a khan. That is what makes it the clearest case of how the Russian
conquest of Central Asia actually worked.

## The mechanism

Russia came down the Syr Darya from the north through the 1850s and 1860s, taking
the Kokandi forts one at a time — Ak Masjid in 1853, Tashkent in 1865 — and then
turned on Bukhara. In 1868 a Russian force took Samarkand and the upper Zarafshan.

<Figure id="bk-triumphant" />

The emir kept his throne. He kept his army, his courts, his officials, his taxes,
his title and his capital. What he signed away was his foreign policy, his tariff —
Russian goods entering at two and a half per cent — and the right to make war.

And the water. Samarkand sits on the upper Zarafshan, which is the river the whole
Bukharan oasis lives on. Whoever holds the headwaters holds the harvest of every
field downstream, and the fact did not need to be written into any treaty.

## What Schuyler found

Eugene Schuyler, the American consul at St Petersburg, travelled through in 1873
and was received at the emir's court. He is a useful witness because he had no
stake in Russian self-congratulation and was rude about the Khivan campaign in the
same book.

What he describes is an emirate to all appearances exactly as it had been. The
same bazaars, the same colleges, the same police, the same court ceremonial, the
same emir — Muzaffar al-Din, whom Vámbéry had seen ten years earlier. Nothing
visible had changed and nothing important was any longer being decided there.

## Why it went this way

Because it was cheaper. A protectorate cost Russia one resident and no
administration, produced the same cotton and the same trade access as annexation,
and left the emir to collect the taxes and suppress the discontent himself.

The contrast with Kokand is the argument. Kokand's khan fled his own rebels in 1875
and the khanate was annexed outright the following year and renamed Ferghana.
Bukhara's emir did what he was told, and his family sat in the citadel for another
forty-seven years.

## What ended

A state that had been the religious authority for a quarter of Asia, that trained
the scholars of Kazan and Kashgar, that had taken Kokand and executed its royal
family, and whose emirs held their throne on the strength of having defended the
faith, became — in five years and without a siege of its own capital — the internal
affairs department of a European empire.

Ten years separate the two eyewitnesses. Vámbéry had to cross the
Turkmen desert disguised as a mendicant, because a European who was recognised in
Bukhara became a slave or a corpse. Schuyler arrived as a consul, was received at
court, and went home and published the road distances.
