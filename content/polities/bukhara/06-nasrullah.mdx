Vámbéry, who arrived three years after his death, heard him described as one of
the greatest tyrants the world had been freed of. He also conquered Kokand, which
no emir of Bukhara had managed, and held it for three months.

## The quarrel

The emirate and the khanate of Kokand had been fighting over the Syr Darya towns —
Ura-Tepe, Khojend — for a generation, and the war ran on a loop that Vámbéry
records with some relish.

Nasrullah marched, in the greatest haste and still greater anger. Muhammad Ali of
Kokand retreated, taking much of the population of Ura-Tepe with him because they
feared the emir more. This time escape was impossible: Nasrullah followed him step
by step until he could go no further, beat him at Khojend and took the city, chased
him to his own capital and forced a peace at Kohna Badam by which Kokand ceded
Khojend and much else.

Then he overreached. To insult the beaten khan further he appointed as governor of
the ceded province the khan's own brother and rival, who had fled to Bukhara. The
two brothers' mother reconciled them, and before the emir had heard anything about
it Khojend and the other towns had rejoined Kokand and he had two enemies instead
of one.

## 1842

His fury, Vámbéry writes, knew no bounds. He added ten thousand Tekke and Salor
Turkmen to the thirty thousand horse and thousand *sarbaz* of his standing army,
marched so fast that Muhammad Ali had to fly from his own capital, caught him near
Marghilan, and ten days later executed him in Kokand with his brother and his two
sons. Most of his supporters followed them to the executioners and their property
was confiscated.

To justify it, Nasrullah put out that Muhammad Ali had married his own mother and
had accordingly been punished by death.

He went home loaded with booty, leaving a Merv-born governor and two thousand men
in the city.

## Three months

Three months later the Kipchaks, who had stayed neutral throughout, grew tired of
the Bukharans, took the city, made the garrison prisoner, and put Muhammad Ali's
son on the throne.

Then they used the prisoners. To avoid being surprised again they decided to wall
off the quarter containing the khan's residence, and built it with the forced labour
of the emir's captured garrison.

Nasrullah sent fifteen thousand men back under a Kokandi pretender he had kept at
his court. On the march the pretender reconciled with his countrymen; the gates were
opened to him; and his first act was to turn on the Bukharans who had escorted him
there and put them to flight.

Four times overreached, the emir sent a fifth army. It got as far as Ura-Tepe and
stopped, because news came that Nasrullah had fallen ill at Samarkand and gone home
to Bukhara, where he shortly died.

## What the story shows

Bukhara at its most powerful could beat Kokand's army, take its capital, kill its
dynasty and install a governor — and could not hold the place for a season.

An emirate of two and a half million people, with the richest irrigated land between
Persia and China and the religious authority of a continent, could not project force
two hundred miles up the Syr Darya against a smaller and poorer neighbour. That is
the measure of what the three khanates were by the 1840s, and of what happened when
a power arrived that could.
