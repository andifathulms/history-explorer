In 57 BC the chiefs of six Chin-han clans — Yŭn-jun-yang-san, Tol-san-go-hö,
Cha-sa-jin-ji, Mu-san-dă-su, Kŭm-san-ga-ri and Myŭng-whal-san-go-ya —
met in council at Yun-chŭn-yang and agreed to merge their separate
fiefs into a single kingdom, naming their new capital Sŭ-ya-bŭl, on the
site of present-day Kyŭng-ju. Unlike the founding of its two neighbors,
this was a state built by negotiation among equals rather than by one
man's conquest or exile.

## A king found in an egg, a queen found in an egg

Tradition supplied the new kingdom's first ruler with an origin equal
to its founding: a company of revelers saw a ball of light on a
mountainside, approached it, and watched a horse rise into the air and
vanish, leaving behind a luminous egg that opened of itself to reveal a
boy. He was placed on the throne at thirteen under the name Hyŭk-kŭ-se.
A companion wonder soon followed at a nearby spring, where a hen gave
birth to a girl with a bird's bill for a mouth, which fell away when
she was washed in the spring's water. In the fifth year of his reign
Hyŭk-kŭ-se married her, and the pair became, in the source's words, the
model Koreans hold up as the perfect marriage.

## Four centuries to fill in the map

The kingdom that six clans founded held only their own six districts at
first, and it took more than four hundred years of both peaceful
absorption and armed conquest to reach the boundaries later associated
with Silla — bounded by the sea on the east and south, extending north
toward the Han River and west to Chi-ri San. The island of Tsushima
became a dependency early on, sustained by Silla's own government
against the poverty of its soil, and remained so until Japan took
direct control of it around 500 AD. It was not until the twenty-second
generation of rulers that the kingdom itself took the name Silla — the
name arrived centuries after the state it named.

## Surnames and official grades

In 32 BC the king renamed the six founding clans after the families
that led them — Yi, Ch'oé, Son, Chöng, Pă and Sŭl — names that remain
among the most common in Korea today, and established seventeen
official grades to organize the growing state's administration. Even as
these institutions took shape, large stretches of land between Silla,
Baekje and Goguryeo remained unoccupied by any of the three, held only
by tribes none of them yet controlled — a reminder that "kingdom" in
this period described a center of authority more than a bounded
territory.
