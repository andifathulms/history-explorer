Silla spent the sixth century turning itself from a confederacy with a
Korean name into a kingdom with a Chinese vocabulary, and the process
opened with a scandal too strange for the annalists to have invented.

<Figure id="sl-crown" />

## A raven, a letter, and a zither case

In 502 a raven dropped a sealed letter at the king's feet in a garden
pavilion and flew off. Its outer message warned that opening it would
cost two lives, leaving it closed would cost only one; an attendant
argued the one life meant was the king's own, so the king broke the
seal. Inside were instructions to take his best bow, hurry back to the
palace by a private gate, and shoot an arrow through the zither case
standing in the queen's own chamber. He did exactly that. The arrow
went through the case and killed the royal chaplain hidden behind it,
caught in the act of using the king's absence to violate the queen's
honor; both the priest and the queen were put to death for it.

## A king who ended a five-hundred-year-old cruelty

King Chi-jeung, twenty-second of his line, used the authority that
scandal left him to push through reforms no predecessor had attempted.
In 503 he abolished the custom of burying five boys and five girls
alive whenever a king's own body was interred - a practice the
annalists themselves call barbarous, ended by simple royal decree after
who knows how many centuries of continuance. The same reign introduced
plowing with oxen, a change with far deeper consequences for ordinary
agricultural life than any single battle the kingdom fought. In 504
Chi-jeung adopted the Chinese title Wang for the kingship in place of
the older Korean titles Isageum and Maripgan, and renamed the kingdom
itself Silla, replacing the older names Saro, Seoyabeol and Gyerim it
had gone by until then. The Confucian mourning code followed within a
year: three years of mourning garb for a parent's death, the same
custom China's own gentry observed.

## An island taken by wooden lions

Silla's first real conquest beyond its own borders came in 512, when
General Yi Sa-bu added the small island of Usan - modern Dagelet - to
the crown. Rather than risk a costly landing, Yi had his carpenters
carve wooden lions with gaping, fanged mouths and mounted one in the
prow of each ship in his small flotilla; when the fleet reached the
island's shore, he warned the terrified inhabitants that the lions
would be loosed among them unless they surrendered outright. They did,
and Silla won what its own chroniclers remembered proudly as a
bloodless victory - a small, almost theatrical conquest, but the first
sign that the kingdom which had spent centuries absorbing Chinese
titles and Chinese customs was also beginning to look outward.
