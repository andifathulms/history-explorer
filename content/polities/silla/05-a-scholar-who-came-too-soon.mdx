Ch'oé Ch'i-wŭn left for China at twelve, took a high literary degree at
the Chinese court at eighteen, and returned to Silla laden with a
reputation his own homeland could not make use of. The court that
received him back was too consumed by jealousy and factional hatred to
tolerate his rise, and he withdrew to a mountain retreat to write rather
than fight for a place among men the source suggests were unworthy of
him — remembered afterward, in Korean tradition, as the brightest
literary mind Silla ever produced and a man born a century or two
before his own country was ready for him.

## A queen, a throne, and a court in freefall

Tradition marked the years that followed with omens: a strange
apparition met a royal procession returning by sea and sang that wise
men would die and the capital would change hands. The king who heard it
died within the year, was succeeded briefly by his brother, and then by
his sister, a queen remembered simply as Man. Under her the court's
morals collapsed outright — one lover after another raised to high
office with no attention paid to the state's welfare — until the people
of the capital hung insulting placards in the streets naming the depths
the throne had sunk to.

## Two bandits the government could no longer stop

Corruption at the center meant collection broke down at the edges: by
889 official tax gatherers could no longer reach much of the
countryside, and the men who filled the resulting vacuum did not mean
to hand power back. Kyŭn-whŭn, a soldier who had risen through Silla's
own army before renouncing his allegiance, built a following among
outlaws and the disaffected and proclaimed himself king of a southern
rival state. Kung-ye — a king's own discarded son, thrown from a palace
window at birth and raised in hiding by the nurse who saved him — rose
through a series of bandit camps in the north until he commanded a
force large enough to carve out a kingdom of his own.

## A kingdom that no longer commanded its own map

Within a generation, both men held territory and titles that answered
to no one in Silla's capital, and the peninsula Silla had once
administered as nine unified provinces had split again into three
contending centers of power — a fracture the sources would come to call
the Later Three Kingdoms, and one Silla's own court, absorbed in its own
decline, did nothing effective to prevent.
