In 927 Kyŭn-whŭn, fighting Wang-gön's rising Goryeo on one front and
Silla on the other, turned his full attention south. He burned and
pillaged his way to Silla's own capital and slipped inside the gates
with a picked band of men while the reigning king, his son, the queen
and the court were feasting at the Po-sŭk summer house. Relief — ten
thousand Goryeo troops — was already marching, but arrived too late.
The king and queen fled without attendants; the king was run down and
forced to commit suicide, the palace women were handed to the soldiery,
and the palace itself was stripped of its treasures. Before he left,
Kyŭn-whŭn installed a relative of the murdered king on the throne he
had just emptied: Kyŭng-sun, Silla's last king.

## A rescuer too weak to rescue

Wang-gön's pursuing army caught Kyŭn-whŭn's forces at O-dong forest but
was beaten back, a defeat that cost Goryeo nothing strategically but
cost Silla everything symbolically — the one power capable of punishing
the sack of her own capital had failed to do it. Four years later
Wang-gön visited Kyŭng-sun in person with an escort of only fifty men.
The king of Silla wept openly over the smallness and weakness of his
own kingdom, and even Wang-gön, by the source's account, could barely
hold back tears of his own. When Wang-gön left the capital after twenty
days as a guest, the source records that the people of Silla vied with
each other to honor him — "poor old Silla had gone out of fashion and
the minds of all men were turned Goryeo-ward."

## An old wolf tamed, a kingdom left alone

Kyŭn-whŭn's own house devoured itself before Silla's did: passed over
for a favored younger son, his eldest imprisoned him in a monastery,
and the old warlord escaped his own guards to surrender to Wang-gön
himself, ending his days as a pensioned guest of the enemy he had spent
decades fighting. With Kyŭn-whŭn neutralized and Goryeo's power
unmistakable, nothing stood between Silla and the choice its own king
had been circling for years.

## A scepter laid down, a crown prince who starved himself

Kyŭng-sun determined to abdicate outright and hand what remained of his
kingdom to Wang-gön. When he raised the question with his officials, no
one argued — they knew resistance was pointless. Only the Crown Prince
objected, urging his father to put the decision before the people
first; when the king refused and sent his letter of submission to
Song-do anyway, the prince retired to a mountain in grief, ate coarse
food as a penance, and died there rather than watch his family's throne
end. The royal procession that carried Kyŭng-sun out of his own capital
stretched ten miles, and Wang-gön met him in person at Song-do's gate,
gave him a royal princess in marriage, made him prime minister, and set
aside an entire district's revenue for his upkeep. Silla, the source
notes, had lasted 992 years and fifty-six kings; from that day its old
capital was known only by its ordinary name, Kyŭng-ju.
