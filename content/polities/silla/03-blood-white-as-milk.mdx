King Pŏp-hŭng, who took the throne in 514, spent his reign doing for
Silla's institutions and its religion what Chi-jeung had done for its
name and its laws - and both changes came with the same theatrical,
almost legendary flourish that marks Silla's whole early record.

## A court sorted by color

In 520 Pŏp-hŭng reorganized the official ranks of the Silla court and
assigned each grade its own color of dress: the highest ranks in
lavender, the ranks below them in red, carrying the ivory memo tablets
still associated with court dress today, the ranks below that in blue,
and the lowest orders in silk hats shaped like the broad-brimmed hats
still worn by chair-bearers in Hulbert's own time. It was a system
built to be read at a glance across a crowded court - and a small
piece of the ceremonial color the writer notes had not disappeared
from Korean royal processions thirteen centuries later.

## A priest executed for a miracle he predicted

Buddhism had reached Silla generations earlier, introduced under King
Nulji by a monk named Muk Hoja and briefly boosted when he taught the
court to burn incense and pray for a sick princess's recovery - a
prayer the annalists say was answered on the spot. It survived a
scandal of its own, the chaplain shot through the zither case, and by
524 had won such influence over the king that his own courtiers,
alarmed at the monks' growing power, pressed him to check it. Pŏp-hŭng
reluctantly consented to the execution of the high priest Yi Cha-don.
Tradition records his final words before the blade fell: that his own
blood would run not red but white as milk, proof that Buddhism was
true. When his head was struck off, the blood that flowed was white.
No one could argue with the evidence, and from that day Buddhism
advanced unopposed - the following year the king outlawed the killing
of animals outright.

## A confederacy of four hundred and eighty-two years folded in without a war

In 527 the neighboring confederacy of Ka-rak, which had existed beside
Silla on peaceful terms for four hundred and eighty-two years by the
source's own count, ended its independent existence when its king,
Kim Ku-hyŏng, voluntarily surrendered his sovereignty and merged his
territory into Silla - retained afterward as the confederacy's own
head, but now by Silla's appointment rather than by right. It was the
quietest possible end for a state that had lasted longer than most
kingdoms manage: no siege, no battle, a king simply concluding that
five centuries of independence had run its course, and handing over
what remained of it in a single transaction.
