In 642, flush from a campaign that had seized forty of Silla's frontier
posts, Baekje tried to cut off Silla's road to China, reasoning that an
isolated rival would lose Chinese favor. The plan reached the Emperor's
ears anyway, and rather than isolating Silla it convinced the Chinese
court that Baekje was the peninsula's troublemaker — the diplomatic
opening Silla spent the next two decades turning into a military
alliance that broke both of its rivals in turn.

## Two kingdoms fall, one alliance sours

Combined Silla and Tang forces took Baekje's capital in 660 and
Goguryeo's in 668, and Silla supplied troops, generals and captured
forts to both campaigns. China, having done the fighting alongside her,
had no intention of handing the peninsula to her ally: Chinese
governors were installed over the conquered territories, and when a
Silla envoy's conduct at court suggested Silla wanted the whole
peninsula for herself, the Emperor had him thrown in prison. He then
ordered Silla to manufacture longbows capable of shooting a thousand
paces — bows that, however carefully made, would only ever shoot sixty,
an impossible standard Silla's own craftsmen blamed on wood damaged in
transit. It was the beginning of open estrangement between ally and
ally.

## A war fought against yesterday's own partner

What followed was less a single campaign than a decade of raids,
betrayals and reconciliations. Silla seized Chinese rice meant for the
garrison at P'yongyang and drowned the boat crews; China answered by
having a captive Silla prince, Kim In-mun, proclaimed rival king of
Silla and sent home with an army to enforce the claim; Silla's own king
sent abject letters of apology whenever a battle went badly, then
resumed absorbing Baekje and Goguryeo territory the moment the crisis
passed. Through it all Gen. Kim Yu-sin, already veteran of the wars
against both fallen kingdoms, moved constantly from one front to
another, holding the peninsula's new frontiers together more by
relentless presence than by any single decisive victory.

## Nine provinces out of two conquests and one homeland

By the 680s Chinese interest in the peninsula had visibly cooled — a
Chinese proxy state raised from Goguryeo's own royal line on the border
revolted and was stamped out rather than reinforced, and the border
above the Taedong River was left, in the source's words, "without
special government." In 685 Silla redivided the entire territory she
now controlled — her own homeland and the conquered remnants of Baekje
and Goguryeo alike — into nine provinces, three from each, turning a
decade of contested spoils into a single administered kingdom that
held, for the first time, effectively the whole peninsula south of the
Taedong.
