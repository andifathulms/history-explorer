Sempach settled nothing. Within six days fifty nobles - among them the
Burgrave of Nuremberg, the Archbishop of Mainz and the Bishop of
Bamberg - sent fresh declarations of war to the victorious Swiss, and
Leopold's own second son, barely fifteen, took charge of preparations
to avenge his father. The fighting that followed went no better for
Austria than the battle that started it: Bern joined the confederacy,
the strategic post of Weesen fell to Swiss soldiers, and Austrian
subjects around Gaster and Sargans switched their allegiance to Glarus
outright. Exhausted and stripped of much of their own nobility,
Leopold's young sons agreed to an eighteen-month armistice rather than
keep losing ground.

## Näfels

The truce barely outlasted its own term. Glarus had used the interval
to copy Swiss-style self-government at the expense of Austrian
oversight, and the moment hostilities resumed in spring 1388, Austrian
troops surprised and massacred the garrison at Weesen before an army
eight thousand strong pushed on to Näfels, burning as it went. Only
four hundred defenders - most of them from Glarus, fifty more who had
crossed the mountains from Schwyz by night - waited on the high ground
above the town. Confident of an easy victory, part of the Austrian
force scattered to plunder and torch Näfels while the rest advanced on
the waiting Swiss, who answered exactly as their forebears had at
Morgarten: boulders rolled down onto the advancing column, followed by
a charge from the heights that threw the Austrians into panicked
retreat. A second band of Swiss descending unseen from the upper
valley sealed the rout. Men drowned by the hundreds when the bridge at
Weesen collapsed under the weight of fleeing soldiers; by the end, a
hundred and eighty knights and roughly two thousand common soldiers
were dead, and eleven captured banners went home as trophies.

## A truce that finally held

Zurich besieged Rapperswil, Bern seized Austrian towns as far as the
Rhine valley, and one allied count made a separate peace rather than
keep fighting - and with the Aargau itself now under threat, the
Austrian princes gave up the war for good in 1388, agreeing to a
seven-year truce that let the Swiss keep every conquest but Weesen and
barred Austria from imposing new tolls. When the truce came up for
renewal in 1394, Leopold's own son and heir - now ruling his father's
Swiss and Alsatian territories in his own right - chose not to test the
confederates again: he renounced any remaining claim to their
conquests, promised never to refortify Weesen, and extended the peace
for twenty years. A war that had cost the House of Austria a duke, two
army-shattering defeats, and most of its remaining Swiss possessions
closed, at last, not with victory for either side but with Austria
formally accepting a border it could no longer move.
