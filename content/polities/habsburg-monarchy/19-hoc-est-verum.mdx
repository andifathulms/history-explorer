Rudolf's ambition found its strangest outlet in heraldry and antiquarian
record-hunting. He combed through the archives of his own family and
the "musty records" of Austria's earlier dukes, and set about reviving
every privilege and title any predecessor had ever held or even merely
claimed - commissioning seals stamped with fanciful devices, invented
hieroglyphs and occult characters (one showed a man's head with five
faces), and an alphabet mixing Greek, Runic, Roman and Arabic letters
for the mottoes he had cut into them. He rarely signed his letters with
his own name, closing them instead with his age, his regnal year, and
the phrase "Hoc est verum" - "this is true." Title followed title:
margrave of Baden and Drosendorf, vicar of Upper Bavaria, lord of
several Swabian territories he simply declared independent of the
empire, arch-huntsman of the empire, and a diet of his own vassals held
at Zofingen with all the pomp of a sovereign court.

## A title too far

What finally provoked the emperor was wounded pride rather than any of
this: the Golden Bull, Charles IV's own recent codification of
imperial law, ranked the seven electors above every other prince in
the empire, and Rudolf - denied a vote he had no real claim to in the
first place - answered by assuming the title Archduke Palatine, citing
an old grant supposedly made by Emperor Frederick I to the earlier
Babenberg dukes of Austria. Charles at first dismissed the whole affair
as antiquarian eccentricity, but Rudolf's growing ostentation eventually
forced his hand: he summoned his own son-in-law before the diet
repeatedly and ordered him to drop the invented arms and titles and
return to those his father had actually borne. Rudolf gave up the
fanciful seals and the "Palatine" in public use, but never fully let go
of "Archduke" itself - and the title outlived the rebuke that tried to
kill it, confirmed for the House of Austria in earnest by a later
emperor, Frederick III, decades after Rudolf's own death.

## A fever in Italy

A separate dispute with the Patriarch of Aquileia over contested fiefs
in Styria, Carinthia and Carniola ended more conventionally: Rudolf
won an imperial ban against the patriarch, took several of his
fortresses by force, and extracted a peace that surrendered the
disputed territories and installed an Austrian judge and garrison in
the city of Friuli. It was one of his last successes. Traveling to
Italy in 1365 to attend his brother Leopold's own wedding to a
Visconti bride - part of a broader push to extend Habsburg influence
south of the Alps - Rudolf was struck down by a fever brought on by
the journey's fatigue and the Italian heat, and died on 27 July 1365,
twenty-six years old and without an heir. He left behind a university
at Vienna, founded to rival Prague's own and already, within a few
years, the second such institution in Germany - a more lasting
monument, in the end, than any of the invented titles he had spent his
short reign chasing.
