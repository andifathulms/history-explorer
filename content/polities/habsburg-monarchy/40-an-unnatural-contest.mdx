Poděbrady's arbitration lasted no longer than any earlier settlement
between the brothers. Albert soon tried to claim Lower Austria
outright, took the citizens of Vienna's own oath of allegiance, and
prepared for a fresh round of hostilities; Frederick answered by
securing both the imperial ban and a papal excommunication against his
own brother. Mediators from every direction - the pope, the princes of
the empire, mutual friends of both men - tried to stop what Coxe calls
an "unnatural contest" between siblings, and Albert accepted nothing
better than a temporary armistice. His own governance in the interval
did more to undo him than Frederick's diplomacy ever managed: arbitrary
rule and constant exactions alienated the very Viennese who had once
backed him, and even Wolfgang Holzer, the demagogue who had led the
earlier revolt against Frederick, switched sides and began working
for the emperor's restoration.

## The end of the feud

Albert discovered the plot before it could succeed, had Holzer
executed, and confiscated the property of everyone connected to the
scheme - a final, vindictive act of a rivalry that never got the
chance to escalate further. Albert died suddenly on 4 December 1463,
in the middle of preparing to renew the war against his own brother
one more time, and with his death Frederick lost an "active and
turbulent rival," in Coxe's own phrase, without having to defeat him
outright. The fraternal war that had consumed years of both men's
reigns, cost Vienna two sieges, and dragged in a Bohemian king as
arbitrator ended not with a settlement either brother had actually
wanted, but simply because one of them stopped living long enough to
keep fighting it.
