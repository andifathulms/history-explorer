Every European power had put its name to the Pragmatic Sanction while
Charles VI lived. Weeks after his death, Frederick II of Prussia
tested exactly what that guarantee was worth: he marched twenty
thousand men into Silesia in December 1740, offering Maria Theresa an
alliance and his own support for her husband's imperial election in
exchange for the province outright, and warning through his envoys
that he would have Silesia one way or another. Maria Theresa's own
reply, delivered without hesitation, set the tone for the whole reign
that followed: sooner lose her last man and cede her last town than
surrender what her own signed guarantees had promised her. France,
Bavaria and Spain read Prussia's opening move as an invitation, and by
1741 what had started as a single land grab had become a general War
of the Austrian Succession, with a Bavarian elector crowned Holy Roman
Emperor in Frankfurt while Austrian troops were still fighting to hold
Vienna itself.

## Silesia lost, a throne held

Maria Theresa never recovered Silesia - the province stayed Prussian
through the war's formal end at Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748 and through
the Seven Years' War that reopened the same quarrel between 1756 and
1763, despite an alliance with France and Russia that briefly seemed
capable of dismembering Prussia outright before Russia's own withdrawal
from the coalition saved Frederick's kingdom at its lowest point. What
Maria Theresa did hold, against every expectation the Prussian gambit
had rested on, was everything else: the Pragmatic Sanction's larger
promise survived intact, her husband Francis was elected emperor in
1745, and the Bohemian and Hungarian estates whose loyalty had been
anything but assured rallied to a young queen most of Europe had
expected to be a pushover.

## Centralizing a patchwork state

The wars that defined her reign's first two decades pushed Maria
Theresa toward reforms no purely peaceful Habsburg monarch would have
needed: a standing army rebuilt and professionalized, a unified system
of taxation that for the first time reached the nobility and clergy
rather than falling on the peasantry alone, a centralized bureaucracy
replacing the patchwork of provincial estates that had slowed
Ferdinand I's own great-great-grandchildren for two centuries, and a
public school system extended, by decree, into the countryside. Much
of the practical work fell to ministers like Prince Kaunitz and
Haugwitz rather than to Maria Theresa personally, but the direction -
a state that could tax, conscript and educate more of its own
population directly, rather than through the intermediary privileges
of nobles and towns - was hers.

## Forty-one years

She died on 29 November 1780, sixty-three years old, after a decline
Coxe describes at length and with evident admiration: dictating state
business between attacks of illness almost to the end, and answering
her own son's plea that she rest with the reply that she would soon
stand before the judgment of God and saw no reason to spend her last
hours asleep. Coxe's own summary balances real affection against real
criticism - a ruler who combined public generosity with private
economy and genuine popular devotion, but whose own religious
intolerance, most visibly toward Bohemia's Protestants and Austria's
Jews, "cast a shade on her memory" even by his own admiring account.
Of the sixteen children she bore Francis, her own son Joseph, already
her co-regent for the final fifteen years of her reign, succeeded her
outright - inheriting a monarchy larger, more centralized and more
fiscally capable than the one she had nearly lost to Prussia in the
first weeks of her reign.
