John of Bohemia's growing power in Italy alarmed Louis enough to drive
him back toward his old Austrian rivals, and an alliance between the
emperor and the Habsburg brothers hardened further when Henry, Duke
of Carinthia, died in 1335 without a son - leaving his territories to
an only daughter, Margaret, nicknamed Maultasch for the size of her
mouth. John had already secured her hand for his own eight-year-old
son by paying forty thousand marks of silver, expecting Carinthia and
Tyrol to pass into Luxembourg hands along with her - but Louis, newly
committed to Austria, simply revoked his own earlier decree allowing
female inheritance and granted both duchies to Albert and Otho
instead as fiefs reverted to the empire. Austria took Carinthia
without much resistance; Tyrol's own nobles refused to accept the
transfer, and John, furious at losing what he had already paid for,
built a coalition with Hungary, Poland, Saxony and his own
son-in-law in Bavaria that ravaged Austrian territory from every
direction at once. The armies met at Landau, but a falling-out between
Louis and his Austrian allies let the emperor withdraw before the
fighting turned decisive, leaving Albert and Otho to negotiate their
own terms: they paid war reparations, gave up a Moravian town, and
renounced Tyrol to Margaret outright - keeping, in exchange, permanent
possession of Carinthia, which never again left Habsburg hands.

## A divorce by decree

Otho died in February 1339, leaving Albert sole guardian of his young
nephews and sole administrator of the whole Austrian inheritance -
and the Tyrol dispute was not finished with him. Margaret's marriage
to John of Bohemia's son had curdled into open hatred within a few
years; she had him imprisoned, escaped confinement herself, and threw
herself on Louis's protection instead. Louis, eager for Tyrol on his
own family's behalf now, accepted her, arranged her betrothal to his
own son, and - defying the Pope's objections entirely - dissolved her
first marriage by imperial decree on the pretext of impotence. The new
marriage went ahead at Innsbruck regardless, and Louis's son received
the investiture of Tyrol in February 1342. King John, already blind
and aging, never accepted the loss of the province or the insult to
his own son, and spent his remaining years traveling through France
and the empire building the coalition that would, within a few years,
put his own eldest son on the imperial throne itself - and end his own
life on a battlefield in France he had no real reason to be standing
on.
