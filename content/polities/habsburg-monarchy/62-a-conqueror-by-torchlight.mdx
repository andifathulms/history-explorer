The event that pulled Maximilian away from Guelderland was a conquest
so fast it left the whole of Europe stunned: Charles VIII of France,
pursuing his own house's old dormant claim to Naples through the line
of Anjou, secured his passage through the Alps with the help of Savoy
and Ludovico Sforza, crossed in July with twenty-two thousand men, and
- after a months-long delay from illness in Piedmont - swept through
Milanese territory into Tuscany, driving the Medici into exile and
freeing Florence from their rule. He entered Rome itself on the night
of 31 December, marching in by torchlight in full armor like a
conqueror, and forced the Pope, who had taken refuge in the Castel
Sant'Angelo, to surrender his own fortresses, promise Charles
investiture over Naples, and hand over his own son as a hostage. By
May, Charles was riding into Naples in triumph as its king, mounted on
a white horse and dressed in the trappings of imperial authority,
while the reigning Neapolitan king fled to the island of Ischia rather
than fight.

## Alarm across Italy

The speed of the conquest terrified every power with a stake in Italy,
Maximilian not least - he took Charles's adoption of imperial-style
regalia as a direct affront to his own office, whatever the disputed
truth behind rumors that a claimant to the extinct Byzantine throne
had formally ceded his own imperial title to the French king. Even
Ludovico Sforza, who had helped clear Charles's own path into Italy in
the first place and had just inherited Milan outright on his young
nephew's death, was the first Italian ruler to move against him,
helping assemble a secret league joining the emperor, the Pope, Milan,
Florence and Aragon in a twenty-two-year alliance meant to restore the
deposed Neapolitan king and drive the French out of Italy entirely.
Venice, for its own part, promised the emperor free passage through
its territory - ostensibly so he could travel to Rome for his own
coronation, but really to give Germany a pretext for moving troops
south into the peninsula.
