Bohemia's own religious wars became Albert's proving ground. Sigismund,
who inherited Bohemia's throne and its Hussite rebellion together,
found in his son-in-law nearly the only reliable military partner
among all the German princes he called on for help: during
Sigismund's third campaign against the Hussites in 1431, the main
German army under a papal legate broke and fled the moment the
Hussites appeared, while Albert turned his own Austrian troops into
Moravia instead, drove out the raiding Taborites, and held the
province securely for the rest of the war. Coxe's own verdict is blunt
- while every other German contingent panicked at the mere name of the
Hussites, Albert "at the head of his brave Austrians, was alone
successful." Grateful, Sigismund tried to reward him with the
investiture of Lower Bavaria after its ruling line died out, though
Albert withdrew the claim for a cash settlement once the neighboring
Bavarian branches and the empire's own princes objected.

## A mother-in-law's intrigue

Sigismund's gratitude went further than land grants: he set out to
secure Albert the succession to Hungary and Bohemia outright, over the
opposition of his own wife. Empress Barbara - nicknamed "the German
Messalina" for her scandalous conduct, and described by one Austrian
historian as believing in "neither God, angel, nor devil; neither hell
nor heaven" - wanted her daughter's inheritance to go instead to the
young King of Poland, whom she hoped to marry herself despite a
forty-year age gap between them. She courted the Hussites secretly,
portraying Albert as their enemy and the Polish king as their natural
ally, and pushed her scheme hard enough that Sigismund, sensing the
danger to his own succession plans, left Prague on the pretext of
needing a change of air, had Barbara arrested at Znojmo instead, and
summoned Albert and his own daughter to his side to settle the
succession before it was too late.
