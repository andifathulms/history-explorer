Matthias Corvinus had every reason to hate Frederick by the mid-1470s:
after being egged into war against Poděbrady and then abandoned mid-
campaign, refused the Bohemian crown he had been promised, watched
Frederick formally invest a rival with it instead, had his own
marriage proposal for the emperor's daughter dismissed outright, and
saw Frederick shelter a rebellious Hungarian archbishop - any one of
which, Coxe notes, would have justified a hostile prince's outrage.
Matthias first had to survive a war on two fronts of his own, fighting
off a Polish-backed rival and putting down internal insurrection while
Ottoman raiders pressed his southern frontier; he crushed both threats
through his own skill, defeating the Polish-Bohemian coalition near
Wrocław in 1474 and turning immediately afterward to smash a massive
Ottoman army supporting Wallachia's own breakaway prince. Freed from
both dangers, he declared formal war on Frederick and invaded Austria
outright.

## An emperor humiliated, then humiliated again

The first invasion was swift and total: within a month Matthias had
overrun most of Lower Austria, besieged Vienna itself, and taken every
fortress along the Danube as far as Krems. Frederick fled to Linz and
bought back his own capital by promising a hundred thousand ducats -
a debt he then simply refused to pay once Matthias got tied up in
fresh wars elsewhere, justifying the default with accusations that
Matthias had broken his own side of the bargain. Matthias, furious,
made peace with Bohemia instead and invaded Austria a second time; this
campaign took four hard years of siege warfare rather than one swift
month, but it ended even more completely, with Vienna's own capture
and the fall of Wiener Neustadt, Frederick's favorite residence.

## An emperor without a home

Driven from his own hereditary lands, Frederick wandered from city to
city and convent to convent for years, met everywhere with sympathy
and nowhere with real help. He bore the humiliation with a strange,
almost theatrical calm, repeating the maxim that "to forget what
cannot be recovered is supreme felicity," and took his revenge on
Vienna's own citizens in words rather than arms - comparing them, for
their long history of turning against him, to the frogs in the fable
who rejected a harmless log as king and got a stork that devoured them
instead. Years of personal appeals to individual princes and formal
pleas before the diet at Nuremberg finally produced only a token force
and modest funds, placed under Duke Albert of Saxony's own able
command; Albert managed to slow Matthias's advance through skillful
maneuvering, but without real reinforcement from either the empire or
Frederick himself, he was forced to an armistice. Austria was formally
ceded to Matthias's occupation in November 1487, to be returned only
once Frederick paid off his old debts in full - or reverted outright,
the treaty specified, should Matthias die first.
