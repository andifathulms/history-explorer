Frederick's own reign had tried and failed at nearly every earlier
approach to imperial peace-keeping - a supreme imperial court kept
stalling on unresolved questions of jurisdiction and funding, and
Frederick's own early instinct, shared by most of his predecessors,
had been to suppress the regional leagues that towns and nobles
formed for mutual defense rather than encourage them, on the theory
that any organized association not answerable to the emperor was a
threat to his own authority. He had personally worked to dissolve the
Swiss confederacy on exactly this reasoning. But decades of failure -
his own inability to enforce peace through direct authority alone -
eventually pushed him toward the opposite strategy: build a
confederacy loyal to the crown instead of suppressing every league
that formed without it.

## Built on an old shield

Swabia offered the ideal testing ground - dense with imperial towns,
fragmented into countless small lordships with no single regional
lord to answer to besides the emperor himself. Frederick proclaimed a
ten-year public peace and convened the Swabian states around an old
noble league called the Shield of St. George, but widened its
membership far beyond nobility alone to include princes, towns and
vassals together. It began modestly - four prelates, three counts,
sixteen knights, six towns - and grew fast, reaching twenty-two towns,
thirteen prelates, twelve counts and three hundred fifty knights
within a year, helped along by the threat of fines and the imperial
ban for anyone who refused to join. Powerful princes followed,
including Sigismund of Tyrol and the electors of Mainz and Trier, and
the league's own combined weight finally gave Frederick something his
own personal authority had never managed alone: the strongholds of
robber-nobles came down by the hundred - a hundred and forty in total
- and even the powerful House of Bavaria, whose feuding branches
Frederick accused before the diet of having "repeatedly insulted the
majesty of the empire," was forced into line.
