Peace with France in 1801 stripped the House of Habsburg-Lorraine of
every remaining German possession beyond the archduchy of Austria
itself, leaving Francis II's own imperial title over Germany real in
theory alone. What was left of his actual power lived entirely in his
own hereditary dominions - the hereditary Austrian provinces, Bohemia,
Hungary, Galicia and Venetia, the last two held by conquest rather
than by the older claims of inheritance or compact that secured the
other three. No single title captured that patchwork, and Francis
judged the old styles - archduke of Austria, king of Bohemia and of
Hungary - no longer adequate to a ruler of a monarchy this size, nor
impressive enough to stand beside the courts of Europe.

## An answer to Napoleon's own coronation

Napoleon's own assumption of the imperial title in France gave Francis
the occasion he needed. He announced his decision in a public letter
whose language Lingelbach preserves directly: bound already, "by the
grace of God and the choice of the electors," to the Romano-German
imperial dignity, Francis declared his wish "to strive for the
maintenance of complete equality between our imperial title and
hereditary dignity and those of the other sovereigns," and, following
the precedent of Russia's own imperial title and now Napoleon's,
resolved to take for himself and his successors "the hereditary title
of emperor of Austria, after the name of our house" - while promising
that every one of his kingdoms and provinces would keep its own
separate title, constitution and privileges undisturbed.

## A title that changed nothing and everything

The new style was recognized by every foreign court without objection,
and Lingelbach is careful to note what it did not do: it altered no
province's actual government, Francis and his successors continued to
be separately crowned kings of Bohemia and Hungary exactly as before,
and no Austrian sovereign has ever undergone a coronation specifically
as Emperor of Austria. What it did do was state outright, for the
first time, the historical unity the Pragmatic Sanction had only
implied - a step Lingelbach judges Charles VI himself would never have
dared take explicitly in his own century. At thirty-six, already aged
by eight years of continuous war into what Lingelbach calls a timid
and suspicious man with little interest beyond natural science and
music, Francis had spent his reign losing the Netherlands, ceding
ground to Napoleon, and watching his own uncle's reforms rolled back
one by one - and closed its opening chapter not with a victory, but
with a new name for the same scattered inheritance seven generations
of his family had spent nearly three centuries assembling piece by
piece from a dead king's crowns at Mohács.
