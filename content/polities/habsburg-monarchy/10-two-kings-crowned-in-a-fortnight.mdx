Henry of Luxembourg died in Italy in 1313, reportedly of a poisoned
communion host though Coxe treats the story as unproven rumor, and the
empire's tangled electoral system - several votes split between rival
branches of the same family, with no settled rule for resolving a tie
- produced exactly the deadlock it was built to invite. Frederick spent
the year lining up written pledges from Bavaria, Saxony and Cologne,
married his sister to a Neapolitan prince to secure papal and French
goodwill, and won over Henry of Carinthia, the old rival Bohemia had
displaced from its own throne. John of Bohemia, for his part, understood
that an Austrian emperor would only encourage his own restless new
subjects, and worked with the Archbishops of Mainz and Trier to peel
away enough electors - including a contested Brandenburg vote - to
offer the crown to Louis of Bavaria instead, despite Louis's own
earlier promise not to oppose Frederick.

## Both crowned, neither undisputed

The two factions met separately on 9 October 1314: the Austrian
electors chose Frederick at Sachsenhausen, and within days the
Luxembourg party, having failed to win over the Austrians first,
elected Louis at Frankfurt's own traditional election site instead.
Frankfurt opened its gates to Louis, who was crowned at Aachen by the
Archbishop of Mainz with full solemnity; Frederick, refused entry to
Frankfurt, was crowned at Bonn by the Archbishop of Cologne instead -
two kings, each with a legitimate-looking coronation, and most of the
empire's cities backing Louis's larger and less contested list of
electors. Both sides appealed to the German states and to whichever
pope eventually succeeded the recently deceased Clement V, and both
prepared for war: Leopold marched a Habsburg army to Speyer and
ravaged the country around Louis's own camp, forcing Louis back into
Bavaria and compelling his own brother to formally recognize him as
emperor - a family dispute settled by the same civil war now spreading
across the whole of Germany.

## Disaster in the mountains

Looking for a quicker way to weaken the Bavarian coalition, Leopold
turned against three Swiss cantons that had sided with Louis, seizing
on a minor dispute between an abbey he oversaw and the people of
Schwyz as his pretext. Confident to the point of boasting that he
would trample the "audacious rustics" underfoot, he raised twenty
thousand men in three columns and aimed the largest, fifteen thousand
strong under his own command, straight through the narrow pass of
Morgarten. Fourteen hundred Swiss met him there on 16 November 1315,
having spent the previous day fasting and praying rather than
drilling. Fifty exiles, denied a place in the regular ranks but
determined to fight anyway, took a height above the defile and opened
the battle themselves, rolling boulders and logs down onto the packed
Austrian column below; in the chaos that followed, the confederates
charged down from the heights with clubs and halberds into cavalry too
cramped by the narrow ground to maneuver and infantry too tightly
packed to defend itself. More than fifteen hundred Austrians, most of
them knights and nobles, died in the rout - trampled, drowned in the
lake beside the pass, or cut down where they stood - and Leopold
himself escaped only with a peasant's help, arriving at Winterthur that
evening, in Coxe's words, "gloomy, exhausted, and dismayed."
