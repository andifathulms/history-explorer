Winning Austria and Styria on the battlefield was not the same as
keeping them in his own family, and Rudolf spent the years after
Marchfeld methodically clearing away rival claimants - the surviving
heirs of the old Babenberg dukes, competing Bavarian princes, and
Meinhard of Tyrol, whose support in the war he repaid by handing him
Carinthia instead once his own sons no longer wanted it. Only once
these claims were settled, purchased off or referred to the judgment
of the German diet did Rudolf move to make the duchies permanently
Habsburg: at a diet in Augsburg in December 1282, he formally invested
his sons Albert and Rudolph II jointly with Austria, Styria, Carinthia
and Carniola, and the following year, at the request of the Austrian
estates themselves, declared Austria and Styria an indivisible and
inalienable domain held on the same terms as the old ducal line -
vesting sole administration in Albert alone, with a fixed income set
aside for his brother. It was the moment territory Rudolf had won in
his capacity as King of the Romans became, instead, his own family's
hereditary property - the House of Austria's actual founding, distinct
from and later than his own election nine years before.

## A father's last request refused

Rudolf spent his final years trying to secure one more thing: the
imperial crown itself for his surviving son Albert, so that the
throne he had won through the electors' calculation might pass, for
once, within a single family. Summoning the diet at Frankfurt in May
1291, he pressed the electors directly, appealing to the services he
had rendered the empire and hoping they would break from their
insistence on a fresh election each time. They declined, on the
grounds that the empire could not maintain two kings at once, and put
off the question to a future diet - a delay that, to a seventy-three-
year-old man visibly failing, amounted to a refusal. Mortified, Rudolf
made a final journey through his own hereditary lands, visiting Basel
and Strasbourg among family and the surviving companions of his early
wars, and set out to see his son Albert in Austria before his
strength gave out. Advised by his physicians to rest, he is said to
have replied, as if he already knew what was coming, "Let me go to
Spire, and see the kings my predecessors." He made it only as far as
Germersheim, and died there on 15 July 1291, in the nineteenth year of
what Coxe calls his "glorious reign." His body was carried on to
Speyer and buried among the earlier emperors, as he had asked - though
the imperial crown he had wanted to leave his son went, in the end, to
someone else, and the House of Austria he had built was left holding
only the duchies he had already settled on his own family while he
still could.
