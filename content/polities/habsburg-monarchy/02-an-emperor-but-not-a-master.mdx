The imperial throne had sat vacant through decades of interregnum by
1273, and when the seven electors met at Frankfurt that September, the
two candidates most expected to succeed were King Ottokar of Bohemia
and King Alfonso of Castile. Instead the nomination fell, against
every expectation, on Rudolf, Count of Habsburg - a choice engineered
above all by Werner of Eppenstein, the Archbishop-Elector of Mainz,
who had once been personally escorted across the Alps by Rudolf on a
dangerous road and had long wished to repay the favour. Werner secured
two more clerical votes quietly and won over the secular electors
through a simpler mechanism: Rudolf had six unmarried daughters, and
the promise of a royal marriage alliance proved persuasive to more
than one prince weighing his own succession. Bohemia's ambassadors
protested, but the election was declared unanimous, and Rudolf was
crowned at Aachen with Charlemagne's own ancient crown.

## Why the electors chose him

The reasoning behind the choice, as Coxe lays it out, was almost
entirely calculating rather than complimentary: Rudolf's civil and
military talents suited him to govern, but the modest scale of his own
territories meant he lacked the means to claim back the fiefs the
electors had quietly appropriated for themselves during the long
vacancy, or to rule with the heavy hand the great imperial houses of
Franconia and Swabia once had. The electors, in the source's own
phrase, "desired an emperor, but dreaded a master." Rudolf, fifty-five
years old and untested on this scale, proved them only half right: the
change of station neither altered his character nor corrupted it, but
it opened, at last, a field large enough for the abilities that thirty
years of provincial feuding had never fully tested. His first and most
urgent task was securing the new Pope's recognition against the rival
claims of both Ottokar and the deposed anti-king Alfonso - a
diplomatic contest that would, within two years, turn into an open
war with Bohemia itself.
