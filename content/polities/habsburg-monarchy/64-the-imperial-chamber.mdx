The German states at Worms had their own price for Maximilian's
Italian war, and it had nothing to do with France. What they actually
wanted, after generations of failed attempts by earlier emperors, was
a permanent end to private warfare and a real tribunal to enforce it -
and they used the leverage of Maximilian's own urgent need for troops
to finally extract concessions no predecessor had managed to win from
them. They acknowledged both the Ottoman threat and the danger of
French dominance in Italy, but flatly refused any grant of men or
money until Germany's own internal peace was secured by more than
another unenforceable proclamation. Maximilian, eager enough for their
support to accept the trade, agreed - reserving only his own imperial
judicial prerogatives in public.

## A court that could not be dismissed

Agreement came with unusual speed for German politics: Maximilian
gave his personal consent in early August, and by the 7th the
ordinances establishing a perpetual public peace and a new supreme
tribunal, the Imperial Chamber Court, had his signature and the
diet's own approval. Private warfare was formally abolished, with a
heavy fine and the imperial ban threatened against any noble or town
that dared declare it again. The new court itself broke real ground
in its own design: a president drawn from the empire's princes or
nobility, sixteen assessors split evenly between knights and trained
jurists, chosen by the emperor from candidates the states themselves
nominated - and, for the first time in any German institution of this
kind, members who could not be removed from office once appointed, a
guarantee of independence meant to keep the court answerable to law
rather than to whoever held power at the moment. Its authority was
limited at first, confined mainly to appeals and constrained by the
emperor's own reserved jurisdiction over disputes involving electors,
and the court struggled in its early years for members, funding and
any real means of enforcing its own rulings. But the foundation held:
over time, moved successively through half a dozen German cities
before finally settling at Wetzlar in 1698, it grew into one of the
empire's most durable institutions - a genuine, lasting achievement
born from a diet Maximilian had convened for an entirely different
purpose.
