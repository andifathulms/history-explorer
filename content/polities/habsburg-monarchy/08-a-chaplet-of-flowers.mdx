While Albert planned his return to Bohemia, a rising he had not
anticipated broke out much closer to his own family's original lands:
on 13 January 1308 the mountain cantons of Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden
rose as one against Austrian rule, expelled their governors and seized
their castles under the leadership of three local patriots. Albert,
confident in his own strength, welcomed the revolt as a pretext for
finally subduing territory his family had long coveted, and set aside
his plans against Bohemia and Thuringia to prepare a Swiss campaign
instead - a campaign he would never lead.

## A refused inheritance

His own nephew John, orphaned son of the brother who had died before
inheriting any share of the family's Swiss and Alsatian lands, had for
years pressed Albert for the portion he believed was owed him, and had
been refused each time. Encouraged by disaffected nobles of his own
who resented Albert's rigid governance, John gathered four confederates
of high birth and plotted his uncle's murder. The breaking point came
at Baden, where the travelling court had stopped to rest: John renewed
his demand once more, and Albert, offering him a garland of flowers
instead with the remark that it suited his years better than the cares
of government, humiliated him past bearing. John threw the chaplet to
the ground in tears and confirmed his resolve.

## The ferry at the Reuss

Days later, crossing the river opposite the old family castle of
Habsburg itself, the conspirators went ahead of the main party and
waited on the far bank; Albert followed with a single attendant,
leaving his son Leopold and the rest of his household still on the
other side. As he rode through the fields at the foot of the hills
below his own ancestral seat, chatting easily with his companion, the
conspirators surrounded him. "Will you now restore my inheritance?"
John demanded, and cut him in the neck; one confederate ran him through
the body, another split his skull with a sabre, and Albert fell from
his horse and bled out in the arms of a peasant woman who rushed to
help, dead before his own son could reach him. The killers scattered -
some fled to the very cantons Albert had been preparing to invade,
whose people refused all the same to shelter men who had murdered even
so hated an enemy - and were hunted down or left to die in exile, all
but John himself, who wandered disguised as a friar until conscience
drove him to confess to the Pope, who passed him on to the new emperor
for confinement in an Italian convent, where he died in 1313. Coxe
judges Albert's own reputation for cruelty overstated, the casualty of
too close a comparison with his father's more brilliant character - a
devoted husband and father, by his own account, whose real fault was
a rigidity that made him easier to hate up close than his father's
larger, more forgivable ambitions ever had been.
