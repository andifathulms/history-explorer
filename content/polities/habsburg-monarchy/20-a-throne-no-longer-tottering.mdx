Leopold II inherited, in his own brother's final weeks, a monarchy in
open crisis on every front: the Austrian Netherlands functioning as an
independent republic after driving out Joseph's own garrisons, Bohemia
and Lower Austria's estates in open remonstrance against a new land
tax, and a faction in Hungary arguing that Joseph's own refusal to be
crowned had voided the Habsburg succession outright and returned the
crown to the nation's own choice - "Hungary," as Coxe records the
claim, "has no need of an Austrian king." Prussia backed every one of
these fractures deliberately, sheltering Hungarian dissidents at
Berlin and allying with the Ottomans in the hope of stripping Galicia
from Austria while the monarchy was still reeling.

## A year that undid a decade of crisis

Leopold answered with negotiation rather than force on every front at
once. He met Prussia at the Convention of Reichenbach in 1790, trading
territorial concessions for an end to Prussian meddling in the
Netherlands and Hungary; he restored the privileges Joseph had stripped
from Hungary and Bohemia's own estates, defusing the succession
argument by having himself properly crowned in both kingdoms; and he
recovered the Netherlands from its short-lived republic through
negotiation rather than reconquest. Within a single year, Coxe judges,
Leopold had relieved his country of foreign war and internal revolt
alike and left a throne that had been tottering at his accession
standing on firmer ground than his own brother had managed to build in
a decade of continuous reform.

## A death that opened a different war

Leopold turned his own attention last to France, where his sister
Marie Antoinette's own safety and the French National Assembly's
increasingly radical dismantling of the monarchy demanded some
response. A declaration warning against foreign interference in
France's internal affairs, issued jointly with Prussia, only
inflamed the revolutionary factions in Paris further - Leopold's own
name was met with insult in the Assembly, and calls for war against
Austria spread through the city before he could develop any policy
beyond the declaration itself. He never lived to see where that
confrontation led: seized by dysentery on 27 February 1792, he died
within three days, forty-four years old, leaving a throne he had
spent barely eighteen months securing to his own son.

<Figure id="hm-pillnitz" />

Francis succeeded to the hereditary dominions immediately and gained
the imperial crown that July - inheriting, in the same year as his own
accession, a war with revolutionary France that would run, in one
form or another, for the next generation, reshaping the empire his
family had spent five centuries building into a form none of his
predecessors would have recognized.
