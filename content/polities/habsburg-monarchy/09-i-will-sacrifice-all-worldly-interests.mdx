Maximilian II grew up in Philip II's own bigoted Spanish court, but
absorbed from a secretly Lutheran tutor a religious conviction his
family never managed to dislodge. As a young man he kept a Lutheran
priest in his own household under the title of secretary, received
communion in both kinds from him, corresponded with the leading
Protestant princes of Germany, and answered his own father's threats
of disinheritance with open defiance: "I will sacrifice all worldly
interests for the sake of salvation." His accession in 1564 was
dreaded by Catholics and celebrated by Protestants in equal measure -
until political calculation did what paternal pressure could not.
Maximilian saw the Protestant states divided by their own doctrinal
quarrels while the Catholics stood united behind Philip II and the
papacy, weighed the succession to the Spanish crown his own family
still stood to inherit, and concluded that abandoning the Catholic
church outright would cost him more than it gained. He kept his
father's own confessor, made a public profession of Catholic faith,
and stayed within the church - while never once abandoning the
tolerant instincts his own upbringing had given him.

## A mediator, not a partisan

That balance defined his whole reign. At his first diet, held at
Augsburg in March 1566, he sidestepped an opening religious quarrel by
persuading Catholics and Protestants alike to vote emergency funds
against the Turks before taking up their grievances - securing far
larger subsidies, in men and money, than any diet had previously
granted, precisely because both sides trusted him enough to set their
dispute aside first. When the religious debate reopened, he refused to
take either side directly, simply relaying each faction's complaints
to the other until both recognized their own grievances mirrored in
their rival's - a studied neutrality that won him, from the pope
himself, a subsidy of fifty thousand ducats against the Ottomans,
proof that even Rome preferred an impartial emperor to a doctrinally
committed rival.

## The elector who split the Protestants

Maximilian's even hand was tested again when Frederick III, Elector
Palatine, broke from Lutheran orthodoxy toward Calvinism, publishing
the Heidelberg Catechism and drawing furious demands from rigid
Lutherans - including the Elector of Brandenburg - that the emperor
enforce Lutheran worship against him. Catholics saw an opening to
split their opponents and pressed for a public reckoning; Maximilian,
though personally unable to approve Calvinist doctrine, refused to let
Catholics dictate an internal Protestant dispute and left the
Protestants to settle the question among themselves. The diet
eventually restricted formal toleration to Catholics and adherents of
the Augsburg Confession alone - but Maximilian let the Elector
Palatine shelter under that same confession's nominal cover rather
than expel him outright, extending the Calvinists a toleration that
was tacit rather than legal, and holding the empire together without
ever fully satisfying anyone inclined to press him for more.
