Bohemia's own crown came to Ferdinand as an elective one, hedged by a
constitution that left him, in Coxe's own phrase, "reduced to a mere
cipher" in public affairs: no tax, troop levy, war, peace, coinage or
law without the consent of a diet of barons, knights and town deputies
that he could not even summon without their own cooperation. When
Charles moved in 1546 to crush the Protestant League of Schmalkalden
and its leader, Ferdinand's own brother-in-arms the Elector of Saxony,
Ferdinand needed Bohemian troops for the campaign - but Bohemia's own
Calixtin majority sympathized openly with Saxony's Lutheran cause, and
an old hereditary compact bound the kingdom to the elector's own
family besides. Ferdinand maneuvered around both obstacles: he talked
a diet into voting troops for vaguely worded "defence," then marched
them against Saxony directly.

## A confederacy against the king's own mandate

The troops mutinied the moment their real orders became clear, and
when Ferdinand followed with an outright mandate in January 1547
demanding fresh levies on his own authority alone, Prague's own
citizens and a mass of nobles and knights answered by forming a
confederacy to defend the constitution rather than obey him -
collecting taxes, raising their own army, and renewing Bohemia's old
compact with Saxony over the king's express objection. Ferdinand's
own exchanges with their deputies, as Coxe records them, ran to open
threats: "We are determined to prosecute this warfare, whether you
will go with us or not... If you persist in your disobedience, you
are alone accountable for all the misfortunes which may overwhelm
you." The confederates held their ground regardless, mustering troops
and warning Ferdinand's own ally the emperor against "the effusion of
Christian blood" - until news reached Prague that Charles had crushed
the Elector of Saxony outright at Mühlberg.

## Six hundred men behind a locked gate

The defeat of their own patron broke the confederacy's resolve
overnight; the same assembly that had defied Ferdinand weeks earlier
now begged to disband its army and congratulated him on his victory.
Ferdinand answered submission with punishment rather than mercy. He
marched into Prague at the head of an army, summoned more than six
hundred of the city's magistrates, councillors and leading citizens
to the palace, had the gates locked behind them the moment they had
entered, and from a throne flanked by his own son and the magnates of
Moravia and Silesia, read out the charges against them himself. The
assembled citizens fell to their knees and begged for royal mercy; he
kept them under guard for hours before granting a pardon conditioned
on surrendering the city's own arms, tolls, privileges and property
outright, and forcing an immediate ratification before anyone had the
chance to consult the wider citizenry a confederacy of this size
would otherwise have organized behind.

## The order that formed the resistance

Similar summonses went to every town in the kingdom except the three
that had stayed loyal throughout. Forty of the most prominent
prisoners were held back for public punishment: four were executed to
open the diet Coxe calls, from that act alone, the Bloody Diet,
including a seventy-year-old high judge of the kingdom; eight more in
each of the three Prague towns were publicly whipped under a
proclamation naming their crime as inciting the people "against their
hereditary master" - a title, Coxe notes pointedly, Ferdinand had not
previously claimed to hold. A contemporary Bohemian historian's own
verdict on the result, which Coxe quotes directly, reads as an
epitaph for the towns' political power: "the order of the burghers...
disarmed, weakened, chastised, and so much reduced that they can
scarcely now be said to form one of the three estates of the
kingdom."

## An elective crown made hereditary

Ferdinand followed the punishment with structural change: a royal
court judge in every town to watch over royal authority, the Jesuit
order brought into Bohemia in 1556 to take over public education, and
a standing censorship committee over every publication printed or
imported. He left the Calixtins' own religious privileges formally
untouched, even securing a fresh papal confirmation of their right to
receive communion in both kinds, and created a single court of final
appeal at Prague to spare his subjects the expense of carrying cases
to German tribunals. But the change that outlasted every other was
constitutional: in 1562 he obtained the diet's consent to crown his
own son Maximilian as heir apparent, converting Bohemia's crown from
an elective one into a hereditary one in fact if not yet fully in
name. Coxe's own verdict on the whole episode is divided rather than
triumphant - praise for ending "the mischiefs of unbridled liberty"
paired with regret for the severity of the method, and a note that
Bohemia's own once-famous commerce and military reputation began
their visible decline under the very reign that finally tamed them.
