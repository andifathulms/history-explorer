Sigismund, his health failing and his own end plainly in sight,
summoned the assembled nobles of Hungary and Bohemia and delivered
what Coxe records as a direct plea rather than a formal decree: "I
give him my kingdom, or rather I give him to my kingdoms, to whom I
can give or wish nothing better." He praised Albert's proven record -
restoring order to a ruined Austria, defeating the Turks, earning
Bohemia's own respect on the battlefield - and reminded the Hungarians
and Bohemians that they were, through Albert's marriage to his
daughter, already bound to accept him. Overcome by the effort, he
could barely finish speaking; those present seized his hand in tears
and swore allegiance to Albert on the spot, proclaiming him and his
wife Elizabeth king and queen before the small Bohemian delegation
present could even properly weigh in. Sigismund died the next day, in
his sixty-third year, having sent his own chancellor ahead to carry
his written testament to the wider Bohemian estates.

## A crown refused twice

Hungary accepted Albert without real difficulty, electing him king
after he swore - as Sigismund himself once had - not to accept the
imperial crown alongside it. Bohemia proved harder: the Hussites
rejected the succession outright in a formal protest, arguing that an
elective Bohemian crown could not simply be willed to an heir, that
Albert had already invaded their country in arms, and that they
preferred a Polish candidate they could "converse with... without an
interpreter" to another German ruler. They offered their own crown to
a Polish prince instead and brought in Polish cavalry to back the
claim, but Albert moved faster than his rivals: crowned at Prague amid
genuine popular celebration, reinforced by allied German princes, he
marched thirty thousand men against the Hussite stronghold at Tábor
and very nearly starved it into submission before a young commander
named George of Poděbrady - in his first notable action, and later a
king of Bohemia in his own right - broke the siege with a daring
sortie. A subsequent Habsburg victory in Silesia forced the Polish
army to retreat on foot after eating their own horses, and a truce at
Breslau finally closed the contest for good.

## King of the Romans

In the midst of this fighting, the German electors turned to Albert
as well, judging his own extensive territories and proven talents the
best available answer to Germany's internal disorder and the rising
Ottoman threat together. Albert, bound by his earlier oath to the
Hungarians, at first declined - but once the Hungarian estates
released him from that promise and the Council of Basel granted its
own absolution, he accepted the German crown too. He was never
formally crowned emperor, but Coxe judges he "obtained and deserved a
place among the emperors" all the same: for the first time since
Rudolf I founded the House of Austria on the imperial throne a
century and a half earlier, a Habsburg duke held Austria, Hungary,
Bohemia and the German crown all at once.
