Leopold III, who took Styria, Carinthia, Tyrol and the family's
Swabian and Alsatian holdings in a division that broke the
indivisibility every earlier Habsburg generation had preserved, spent
the 1370s and early 1380s absorbing territory faster than any duke
before him - and spent it just as fast on two humiliations that left
him desperate enough, by 1386, to gamble everything on a single
battle. An invading army of unemployed mercenaries under his own
cousin, the famous soldier Enguerrand de Coucy, was broken not by
Leopold's own diplomacy but by Swiss peasants fighting on their own
account - the very cantons he considered his family's oldest enemies
rescuing him almost by accident. And a marriage arranged to bring his
son William the Polish crown collapsed when the Polish nobles,
dazzled by a rival suitor's promise to convert an entire pagan
duchy to Christianity, overruled their own queen's declared wish to
marry no one but the Austrian match and shut William out of Kraków
for good. The loss struck Leopold harder than any battlefield reverse
- he sank into a depression that let his own bailiffs' extortions run
unchecked, and the resulting unrest drew his oldest enemies, the
Swabian towns and the Swiss cantons together, into exactly the
alliance his own governance should have prevented.

## A mock breakfast invitation

War broke out in earnest once Austrian troops retook and burned the
town of Richensee, killing every inhabitant regardless of age or sex.
Leopold massed his army at Baden and sent a diversionary force toward
Zurich to mask his real target, Lucerne, but the Forest Cantons saw
through the feint and marched to Sempach instead. Leopold reached the
town at dawn on 9 July 1386 with four thousand horse and fourteen
hundred foot, confident enough of an easy victory that his own troops
rode up to the walls to taunt the defenders with a mock breakfast
invitation for the "reapers" in the fields outside. The burgomaster
answered simply: "My masters of Lucern, and their allies, will bring
it."

Surprised to find the confederates already drawn up in the woods
above the lake, Leopold called a council to weigh waiting for
reinforcements - and was shouted down by his own nobles, who declared
it shameful to delay against what they dismissed as an "ill-armed and
almost naked rabble." One veteran urging caution was mocked as a
coward by a younger knight who bragged the peasants would be
delivered up "this very noon." The knights dismounted in ground too
rough for cavalry, formed a dense phalanx bristling with spears, and
advanced; the Swiss knelt first in prayer, prompting some Austrians to
mistake the gesture for a plea for mercy, before the confederates
surged down out of the trees in a wedge that broke against the
Austrian line without effect - sixty of the attackers' best fighters
dead before a single Austrian fell.

## Winkelried

At that moment a knight of Unterwalden named Arnold von Winkelried
broke from the Swiss ranks. "I will open a passage into the line," he
is said to have called out. "Protect, dear countrymen and
confederates, my wife and children!" He threw himself onto the
Austrian spears, gathered as many as he could reach in his own arms,
and bore them down with his weight as he died - and his companions
poured through the gap his body opened. Weighed down by their own
armor and unable to recover formation in the crush, the Austrian
knights broke; their own servants, seeing the battle turning, mounted
their masters' horses and fled, cutting off any hope of escape for
the men they had served. Roughly two thousand Austrians died, nearly a
third of them titled nobility, against perhaps two hundred confederate
dead.

Leopold fought to the end rather than flee, seizing the family
standard himself after two bearers fell in succession and rallying
his remaining knights for one final charge before he was cut down by
an unknown hand. He was thirty-six. His body and those of
twenty-seven of his noblest followers were carried to the family's own
abbey at Königsfelden; the Swiss buried their two hundred dead with
full honors at Lucerne and established an annual memorial for the
victory to stand alongside the one already kept for Morgarten, seventy
years before.

## The war that finally closed

Sempach settled nothing on its own. Fresh declarations of war arrived
within a week, and the fighting that followed went no better: Bern
joined the confederacy, and Austrian subjects around Gaster and
Sargans switched their allegiance outright. When the truce that
followed expired in 1388, Austrian troops massacred the garrison at
Weesen and pushed on to Näfels, where the same mechanism that had
broken Leopold at Morgarten broke his army a second time - boulders
rolled down onto an advancing column, followed by a charge from the
heights that drowned hundreds of fleeing soldiers when the bridge at
Weesen collapsed under their own weight. A hundred and eighty knights
and roughly two thousand common soldiers died, and eleven captured
banners went home as trophies. With the Aargau itself now threatened,
the Austrian princes gave up the war for good that year, and when the
resulting truce came up for renewal, Leopold's own son chose not to
test the confederates again: he renounced any remaining claim to
their conquests and extended the peace for twenty years. A war that
had cost the House of Austria a duke, two army-shattering defeats, and
most of its remaining Swiss possessions closed at last not with
victory for either side, but with Austria formally accepting a border
it could no longer move.
