Charles the Bold died in battle at Nancy on 5 January 1477, and his
death threatened to unravel everything Frederick had spent years
maneuvering toward. Louis XI moved immediately, sending an ambassador
to claim Mary's hand for his own son while simultaneously invading her
territory and seizing Burgundy itself along with parts of Artois and
Flanders. Mary's own counselors, some bought by Louis and others
simply terrified of him, urged her to accept the French match as the
only way to save what remained of her inheritance; meanwhile the
people of Ghent rose in revolt, executed two of her ministers outright,
and effectively held their own sovereign hostage to extract fresh
political concessions. Flanders's own estates pushed a homegrown
candidate, the Prince of Cleves, while an English earl pursued her
hand with the backing of his own royal brother-in-law. By every
practical measure, Maximilian's cause had no real support anywhere in
Mary's own court or country.

## A princess makes up her own mind

What saved the match was the impression Maximilian himself had made
at Trier: Charles had returned from that failed ceremony full of
praise for his prospective son-in-law's talents, military bearing and
personal charm, calling him the most promising prince in Europe - words
that had lodged deeply enough in Mary's own memory that when she
became free to choose for herself, they mattered more than any
councilor's advice. Frederick sent his most distinguished envoys to
press Maximilian's suit at Ghent, and though the Flemish estates and
the Duke of Cleves urged her to refuse, Mary stunned the entire court
by receiving the imperial ambassadors warmly: when the Elector of
Mainz presented the letter and ring she had once sent Maximilian at
her own father's direction, she recognized them as tokens of a promise
she intended to keep, and no amount of protest from her own advisors
could move her. The marriage was solemnized by proxy in April 1477.

## Guinegatte

Maximilian arrived in person that August, and his looks, manners and
bearing more than justified the choice - but it was what he did next
that secured his new position for good. Only eighteen, with no army of
his own and facing the most cunning monarch in Europe, he rallied the
Burgundian Netherlands' own defense, held off the French advance, and
used every lull in the fighting to win his new subjects' loyalty by
confirming their traditional privileges rather than ruling by force.
Pressure from the German diet, roused by Frederick's own exhortations,
forced Louis to restore several imperial territories he had seized,
freeing Maximilian to go on the offensive: he besieged Thérouanne in
Artois and won a bloody, decisive victory at Guinegate on 7 August
1479 that stabilized Burgundian rule over the Low Countries for good -
proof, delivered on a battlefield rather than in a marriage contract,
that the Habsburg claim to Burgundy could actually be defended.
