Charles's own abdication in 1556 handed Ferdinand the imperial title
he had spent thirty years serving as King of the Romans to secure, but
even the formal transfer took two years to settle - the electors
insisting on a fresh capitulation to fold in the religious peace
agreed since Ferdinand's own election, before proclaiming him emperor
in 1558. What followed did more to reshape the office itself than
anything in the transfer's own paperwork. Custom held that no emperor
was fully emperor without a coronation at Rome, and Pope Paul IV -
already offended by the religious concessions Ferdinand had granted
Protestants in the Peace of Passau - refused his ambassador an
audience, dismissed Charles's own abdication as the act of a madman,
and declared Ferdinand's election void because electors he judged
heretical had taken part in it.

## A crown that stopped needing Rome

Ferdinand answered with a deadline: three days for an audience, or his
ambassador would leave Rome outright. Paul backed down just far enough
to avoid open rupture without withdrawing his claim, and died that
same year; his successor Pius IV opened talks instead, and accepted
Ferdinand's own compliment of devotion once an aide, working the
diplomatic language rather than the substance, slipped the word
"obedience" into it. Ferdinand let the phrasing stand without either
endorsing or disowning it - he had what he actually wanted, an
acknowledged imperial title without the journey to Rome a personal
coronation required - and when his own son Maximilian was elected
King of the Romans soon after, his own compliment to the pope
substituted obsequium, mere deference, for obedientiam outright. Every
emperor after Ferdinand followed the same practice: the centuries-old
dependence of the imperial title on a Roman coronation ended, in
practice, with his own quiet refusal to make the trip.

## A referee between two faiths

As emperor Ferdinand kept the same balancing act he had run as king,
enforcing the Peace of Passau against encroachment from either side
and working, without real success, to reunite the churches outright -
pressing Rome, in one memorial the pope himself published to his own
embarrassment, to let priests marry and the laity receive communion
in both kinds, "the last only transgress a law which is capable of
being changed, whereas the first sin against a divine law." Protestant
princes meeting at Naumburg in 1560 answered a papal invitation to the
reconvened Council of Trent by returning the briefs unopened, refusing
even the title of "sons" from a bishop they no longer recognized as a
father - and splitting, in the same meeting, between Lutheran
loyalists and the Calvinist-leaning faction around the Elector
Palatine, a division Coxe notes Ferdinand watched with quiet
satisfaction. The reconvened council at Trent closed in 1563 having
granted the laity communion in both kinds but refused clerical
marriage outright; Ferdinand kept pressing the second point until his
own death the following year.

## Three sons, three shares

Away from religion, Ferdinand's reign gave Germany a reformed coinage
edict that bound every state to strike money bearing its own ruler's
name and likeness, a purged and reordered Aulic Council, and faster
deputation-diets in place of the empire's unwieldy general assemblies.
Confident enough in his own standing that no rival contested his
son's succession, he had Maximilian crowned hereditary king of Bohemia
and later of Hungary in his own lifetime, securing all three crowns
for the next generation before he died. His own testament, signed in
1555, divided the family's hereditary lands rather than keeping them
whole: the Archduchy of Austria itself to Maximilian and his own
line alone, Tyrol and the outlying provinces to his second son
Ferdinand, and Carinthia, Styria and Carniola to his third son
Charles - a partition his own three sons signed themselves, and the
seed of a family split future reigns would spend decades unwinding.

Ferdinand died on 25 July 1564, sixty-one years old, of a fever Coxe
attributes to the chagrin of watching his own efforts at religious
reunion fail. Educated on Erasmus's own plan and fluent enough in
Spanish, Italian, German and French to correspond directly with
scholars across the continent, he left his children a written
exhortation to hold to the Catholic faith regardless of what came
next - paternal advice that outlived him by less than a generation
before his own grandson would turn Bohemia's throne into the opening
battlefield of a war that outlasted every Habsburg who started it.
