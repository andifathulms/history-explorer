Frederick's own reluctance to surrender his ward hardened once his
own fortunes rose independently: he traveled to Rome to receive the
imperial crown from the Pope, taking Ladislaus along in his retinue
rather than leaving him behind with any of the three kingdoms
clamoring for his return. The young king made a real impression there,
delivering a Latin oration before the papal consistory and receiving
a knighthood on the bridge of the Tiber for his precocious learning -
but when Frederick continued on to Naples, Ladislaus was left in
papal custody, and an attempt by deputies from all three nations to
help him escape was discovered and blocked before it could succeed.

## A ransom by siege

Frustrated nobles from Hungary, Bohemia and Austria alike finally gave
up on negotiation entirely. When a last direct appeal to Frederick, on
his own return from Italy, was refused once again, an Austrian noble
named Ulrich Eyczing took matters into his own hands: he raised an
army of sixteen thousand men and laid siege to Wiener Neustadt itself.
Frederick, unable to resist, had no real choice but to give up his
ward, and in 1452 Ladislaus passed into the custody of his own
maternal uncle, the Count of Cilli, pending a formal settlement.
Ladislaus entered Vienna to genuine public celebration, and deputies
from all three kingdoms worked out a lasting arrangement: the young
king would remain under Cilli's guardianship while Hunyadi continued
governing Hungary, Poděbrady continued governing Bohemia, and Cilli
himself took Austria - a three-way division that finally gave each
kingdom the local authority it had been demanding for over a decade,
even without their sovereign's full attention.

## A scholar's education, a courtier's influence

Freed from Frederick at just thirteen, Ladislaus received an education
from Chancellor Kaspar Schlick and the future Pope Pius II better
suited, in Coxe's own judgment, to a scholar than to a king who would
have to govern Hungarians and Bohemians as turbulent as any in Europe.
Cilli used his own privileged access to purge Austrian nobles from
office and seed the king's mind with suspicion of both Hunyadi and
Poděbrady - until Eyczing, the very man who had freed Ladislaus from
Frederick in the first place, organized his own confederacy against
Cilli's growing dominance. Confronting Cilli directly in the young
king's own presence, Eyczing announced that Ladislaus wished him gone
from court; Ladislaus, standing "silent and confounded," gave only a
sullen word of confirmation, and Cilli left Vienna under threat from
an angry crowd, saved only by an escort to the city gates. Ladislaus
was crowned king of Bohemia at Prague that October, guided entirely by
Poděbrady's own counsel - though his religious sympathies, shaped by
years among Catholic tutors, already ran openly against the Calixtin
majority he was now expected to rule.
