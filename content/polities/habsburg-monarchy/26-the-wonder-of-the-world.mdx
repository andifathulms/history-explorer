Albert IV, sixteen when his father died, had already spent years
yielding ground rather than fighting for it - content to let his
cousin Leopold's branch of the family absorb every Habsburg territory
but Austria itself in the interest of peace. He resumed sole
administration on Leopold III's death, only to have Leopold's own now-
grown sons demand and receive their father's former lands back in
turn: William took Styria, Carinthia and Carniola, and Leopold the
younger took Tyrol with the family's Swabian and Alsatian holdings,
while two still-underage brothers waited their own turn. When Albert
III's own death left the Austrian succession disputed between young
Albert IV and his cousin William, the two settled on joint rule for
life, with the survivor to govern alone afterward - a fragile peace
that let Albert leave Austria altogether for a very different kind of
journey.

## A pilgrim's reputation

Albert made a pilgrimage to Jerusalem, leaving William to govern in
his absence, and the journey did more for his reputation than anything
he accomplished on the Austrian throne: it inspired romantic verse and
earned him the epithet "the Wonder of the World," even though Coxe
notes dryly that his actual reign offered "few remarkable events in
the sober pages of history." He married a Dutch duke's daughter on his
return and spent much of his later rule as a trusted intermediary in
the tangled politics of the House of Luxembourg - so trusted that when
King Sigismund of Hungary arrested his own brother, Wenceslas of
Bohemia, Albert was given custody of the royal prisoner, treated him
with real kindness, and appears to have quietly allowed his escape
without losing Sigismund's own confidence in the process. In gratitude
for his help in Hungary's wars, Sigismund promised Albert the
reversion of the Hungarian crown itself should his own line fail.

## Poison at Znaim

That alliance drew Albert into one last campaign. He joined Sigismund
against a Moravian warlord who had seized Austrian and Hungarian
territory, and the two princes besieged him at Znaim until the
attacker, facing defeat, proposed a surrender feast instead - and
poisoned both rulers at the table. Sigismund survived only with great
difficulty; Albert did not recover, dying at Klosterneuburg on 14
September 1404, twenty-seven years old. A contemporary Carthusian
described him as living out his final years in genuine religious
devotion at a monastery near Vienna, attending every office alongside
the monks and calling himself simply "brother Albert" - a pious,
withdrawn reign that left the Austrian succession, once again, to a
child too young to rule in his own name.
