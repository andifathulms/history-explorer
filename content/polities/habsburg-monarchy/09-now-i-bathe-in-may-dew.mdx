Albert's children first feared his murder was the opening move of a
general revolt, and when it proved instead the work of a few
discontented men, they turned that relief into a campaign of
extermination against anyone connected to the killers. Frederick and
Leopold, the two eldest sons, razed the conspirators' fortresses,
slaughtered their garrisons and households indiscriminately, and
confiscated every estate they could reach; sixty-three retainers of
one conspirator's lord were beheaded in a single day before Leopold
and his sister Agnes, protesting their innocence to the end. Agnes is
said to have repeated, through the slaughter, a line from a legend of
her own name-saint meant to express the keenest pleasure - "Now I
bathe in May-dew!" - and was barely persuaded to spare one infant a
soldier snatched from her hands as she prepared to strangle it. More
than a thousand people are reckoned to have died avenging one. Albert's
widow and Agnes together built and endowed a convent on the spot where
he was killed, where both women lived out their lives in austere
penance - though an old hermit, offered a place there himself,
answered that God was served by compassion and forgiveness, not by
convents built from the plunder of ruined families.

## An Austrian passed over again

The five sons inherited jointly, with Frederick - twenty-three, tall,
called "the Handsome," amiable and brave to the point of rashness -
administering Austria, and the younger Leopold, sixteen and already
a formidable soldier despite a twisted, undersized frame, governing the
family's Swabian and Swiss territories. Frederick stood for the vacant
imperial throne, but the electors, still wary of the family after
Albert's own rigid rule, passed him over again: on 29 November 1308
they chose Henry, Count of Luxembourg, instead. The choice cost the
Habsburgs more than the crown itself. Bohemia's own throne had fallen
vacant, and Henry moved quickly to install his own son John there,
marrying him to a Bohemian princess and driving out a rival duke;
within four years John held the whole kingdom. When the new emperor
then tried to strip Austria and Styria from Frederick and his brothers
on an old, barely-relevant legal pretext, Frederick answered with a
threat of his own: "Tell him that within the space of fifty years
Austria has been the grave of five sovereign princes, and Henry of
Luxembourg may be the sixth, if he dares to trouble us in our
legitimate possessions." The threat never had to be tested - both
sides had more urgent business elsewhere, and a compromise in 1309
confirmed the brothers in their father's dominions in exchange for
money, military support, and a formal renunciation of any further
claim on Bohemia.
