Leopold refused to accept his brother's captivity as final, gathering
support from the Pope - locked in his own bitter quarrel with Louis -
and from John of Bohemia, whom Louis had alienated by giving the
Brandenburg margraviate to his own son instead. Leopold ravaged
Bavaria, defeated an army Louis led against him in person, and began
arranging with French and papal envoys to have Louis formally deposed
in favor of the King of France - pressure serious enough that Louis,
after nearly three years of holding Frederick prisoner, chose
negotiation over further war. Frederick, worn down by his confinement,
agreed on 13 March 1325 to renounce the imperial throne, restore what
territory he still held, and support Louis even against the Pope - on
condition that he return to captivity if these terms went unfulfilled.
He was freed, worked earnestly to deliver on his side of the bargain,
and urged his own brothers to submit to Louis as their rightful
sovereign. They refused. Leopold, still the family's fiercest fighter,
rejected every clause that bound him personally, and the Pope himself
voided the whole agreement as extracted under duress.

## A word kept anyway

Unable to make his own family honor terms he alone had signed,
Frederick did something almost without precedent: he went back to
prison of his own accord, exactly as he had promised. Louis, moved by
what Coxe calls "this rare instance of probity," treated his returned
captive less like a defeated enemy than a guest - according to one
contemporary chronicle, the two men ate at the same table and slept in
the same bed, and when Louis had to leave to put down a revolt in
Brandenburg, he left Frederick to govern Bavaria in his place. Worn
down himself by Leopold's continuing attacks and the Pope's implacable
hostility, Louis eventually offered something better than friendship:
a treaty making the two of them joint emperors with equal rights, a
shared seal, and alternating precedence - an arrangement without real
precedent in the empire's history, which the electors and the Pope
both rejected as soon as it became public. The two rivals-turned-
partners settled instead on a division of labor, Louis departing for
Italy with Leopold at his side as vicar-general while Frederick held
the reins in Germany alone, still bearing the title king of the
Romans in fact if not in universal recognition.

## Three deaths in four years

The arrangement never had time to prove itself. Leopold had never
forgiven himself for Mühldorf, blaming his own late arrival for his
brother's defeat, and the strain of years spent fighting to reverse
it wore him down; he died at Strasbourg on 28 February 1326, not yet
thirty-five, "in a fit of frenzy," as Coxe puts it, brought on by grief
and exhaustion together. His brother Henry, broken in spirit by harsh
treatment during his own six months as John of Bohemia's prisoner
after Mühldorf, survived barely a year longer, dying in February 1327
still wasting from a melancholy his release had never lifted. Frederick,
already frail, outlived them both but never recovered his former
vigor; stripped by Louis of the joint authority he had once been
promised and drawn into one final dispute with his youngest surviving
brother over the family inheritance, he settled for the lesser share
and died at Gutenstein castle on 13 January 1330 - a king twice
crowned, one battle short of the throne he had spent his whole adult
life reaching for.
