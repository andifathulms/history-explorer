---
title: Curbing the Swiss

Facing constant French pressure over Burgundy, Naples and Milan alike,
Maximilian recognized how much the Swiss cantons' own military
reputation and strategic position could matter as allies - and worked
to pry them away from their existing alignment with France. He had
some success with the more municipal cantons, but the democratic
cantons, swayed by French subsidies and their own long-standing
distrust of the House of Austria, refused to budge. Frustrated,
Maximilian tried a different approach: demanding, as head of the
empire rather than as an Austrian rival, exactly the support the
Swiss had already refused him as a neighboring prince - contingents
for the Burgundian war and his own Italian expedition, formal
submission to the authority of the new Imperial Chamber Court, and
contributions to the taxes that funded it. He even pushed them to join
the Swabian League, calculating that a Swiss refusal would deepen an
existing rivalry between the League and the cantons over influence in
the region.

## Excommunication and the ban

The Swiss refused every demand and declined to acknowledge themselves
subjects of the empire at all, and Maximilian escalated in response:
he had the Pope excommunicate them as French collaborators, and let
the Imperial Chamber turn its full coercive weight against them -
forcing contributions, summoning the abbot and town of St. Gallen to
answer before the diet, and placing two Rhaetian nobles under the
imperial ban. Rather than intimidating the Swiss into submission,
these measures drove the whole confederacy together: seven of the old
cantons allied with two of the Rhaetian leagues, themselves already
alienated by separate disputes with Tyrol's own government, leaving
only Bern still working to avoid an open break with Austria.

## A conquest of Milan, and a demand too far

Into this already volatile standoff, Louis XII assumed the title Duke
of Milan and began preparing to conquer the duchy outright. Maximilian
warned the Swiss against aiding France's move on a fellow imperial
fief and demanded six thousand men to back his own son's claims in
Burgundy - a request the Swiss refused outright until the Imperial
Chamber's own pressure on them was lifted, though they promised strict
neutrality in the meantime. Both sides stood in a state of mutual
irritation that needed only a spark, and it was, in the end, a small
and otherwise unremarkable local incident that finally set the whole
confrontation alight.
