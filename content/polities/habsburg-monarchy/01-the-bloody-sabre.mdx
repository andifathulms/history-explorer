Süleyman the Magnificent inherited his father's own unfinished design
on Hungary and pursued it with a skill and ambition the Ottoman throne
had never before combined. He took Belgrade - the fortress that had
once defied Mehmed II under Hunyadi himself - through simple treachery
rather than the desperate defense Hungary's own defenders had
mustered two generations earlier, added Rhodes to his conquests soon
after, and by the winter of 1525 was massing his forces on Hungary's
own frontier. He found the kingdom badly placed to resist him: a
young king absorbed in pleasure, a nobility fractured by faction, and
Christian Europe too consumed by its own religious quarrels and wars
to send help.

## "To arms!"

When Süleyman finally crossed the Danube with two hundred thousand
men, Hungary's own king revived an old custom - a blood-stained sabre
carried from village to village as a call to arms - but even that
could not unite a nobility split into rival factions. Louis's own
council urged patience: hold a defensive position until Bohemian,
Austrian and Transylvanian reinforcements arrived. A bishop commanding
the vanguard, flushed with a few minor skirmish victories and
convinced God had already promised him the field, overruled that
caution, and the nobles drowned their own young king's hesitation with
shouts of "To arms!" - branding anyone who counseled otherwise a
traitor. Louis yielded. On 29 August 1526, thirty thousand Hungarians
met Süleyman's army at Mohács, fighting with real religious fervor
that could not make up for the disparity in numbers or match the
sultan's own tactical skill: twenty-two thousand of the thirty
thousand who fought fell on the field, seven bishops and hundreds of
nobles among them.

## A king drowned in a marsh

Louis himself tried to escape the collapse, but at a marshy crossing
near the village of Csele his own heavily armored horse, struggling to
climb the far bank, fell backward and crushed him beneath it. A
Silesian nobleman who had crossed safely turned back to help, pulled
him from the mud and unlaced his helmet, but the king had already been
mortally injured and died within moments. Ottoman troops were too
close for any proper search, and his body was not recovered for two
months, eventually buried at Székesfehérvár. Buda and Pest surrendered
without a fight in the immediate aftermath, and only Süleyman's own
need to turn back and suppress a rebellion in distant Karaman - sparked
by a false rumor of his own death - spared Hungary from being
subjugated outright in that same campaign. Louis died childless, the
last of his line, and left both the Bohemian and Hungarian crowns open
to whichever claimant could actually make good on them.
