While Albert expanded the family's fortunes toward Bohemia and
Carinthia, the Habsburg patrimony in Switzerland itself was steadily
slipping away. The family's own possessions - the Aargau, the
Thurgau, the towns of Zug and Lucerne, an advocacy over abbeys that
gave them authority as far as Glarus - almost surrounded the three
mountain cantons that had already humiliated Leopold at Morgarten, but
the spirit of that victory had spread rather than been contained.
Lucerne went first: worn down by endless border warfare, heavy war
levies, and factional strife between Austrian loyalists and everyone
else, its citizens secured imperial protection from Louis of Bavaria
and a twenty-year truce with the Swiss cantons, then beat back an
Austrian attempt to retake the town by surprise before formally
joining the confederacy - carefully, and without repudiating any of
the House of Austria's existing rights, but joined all the same.

## Zurich and Glarus

Zurich followed through a stranger route: a popular revolution under
a perpetual burgomaster named Rudolf Brun overthrew the city's old
noble government, and when the exiled nobles conspired with a Habsburg
kinsman to retake it by force in 1351, Brun crushed the plot,
destroyed the conspirators' own base at Rapperswil, and - fearing
Albert's revenge - secured Swiss protection by bringing Zurich into
the confederacy that same year. Albert, freed at last from his
entanglements in Germany and Bohemia, marched on Zurich with sixteen
thousand men to force its submission, and an arbitration eventually
favored him on paper; but when he tried to enforce the harshest terms
against the smaller cantons, Schwyz refused outright, and its
soldiers marched under the same banner that had triumphed at Morgarten
to drive out his commissioners. Glarus and Zug went the same way soon
after - the peasants of Glarus welcoming a Swiss relief force that
expelled their Austrian governor in the dead of winter and routed an
Austrian detachment at Rütli the following spring, after which Glarus
too joined the confederacy it had just helped defend.

## "God forbid"

Albert, aging and increasingly crippled by the paralysis that had
afflicted him since his thirties, finally gave up the contest in
disgust, retreating to Vienna in such despondency that his household
learned never to mention the Swiss in his presence again; his son
negotiated an eleven-year armistice that quietly closed a war Austria
had spent two decades losing piece by piece. Albert died on 16 August
1358, twenty-eight years into a reign that had earned him, despite a
twisted body that once won him the nickname "the Lame," the very
different epithet "the Wise" - deserved, in Coxe's judgment, in
everything except his handling of the Swiss. He is remembered too for
a kindness that outlived the wars: when an earthquake and fire nearly
levelled Basel and a counselor urged him to seize the ruined city while
it lay open, Albert refused outright - "God forbid that I should smite
those who are visited by the hand of the Almighty!" - and sent his own
men to help clear the rubble and rebuild it instead. Widowed young
sons who might easily have ended the Habsburg line outright - nineteen
years of childless marriage before four sons finally arrived - carried
the family forward instead, among them a nineteen-year-old who would
soon claim, without anyone's permission, a title no Habsburg had ever
held before.
