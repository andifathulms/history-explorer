Charles VI came to the Austrian throne through a family compact that
had never actually settled who should inherit if a Habsburg emperor
died without a son - only that Joseph's own daughters would take
precedence over Charles's if both brothers left none. Charles reversed
that order almost as soon as he was crowned, and on 2 August 1718
issued the decree known as the Pragmatic Sanction, entailing the
succession first on any daughters of his own, ahead of his late
brother's. The reversal mattered because it was never merely
theoretical: his only son died in infancy, and three daughters -
Maria Theresa, Maria Anne and Maria Amelia - survived him instead.
Charles forced his own nieces to renounce their competing claims as
the price of their marriages to the Electors of Saxony and Bavaria,
but distrusted paper renunciations enough to make something larger
his life's central project: securing every major European power's own
guarantee that his daughter would inherit intact.

## The price of a guarantee

That pursuit shaped two decades of Habsburg foreign policy, and Coxe's
own verdict is blunt - Charles sacrificed nearly every other interest
of his reign to it. He bought Spain's own acceptance of the
succession by conceding Habsburg claims to territories the War of the
Spanish Succession had only just secured; he bought Britain's support
with commercial concessions that crippled his own newly founded
Ostend trading company; he bought France's eventual guarantee, in
1738, at the cost of the whole War of the Polish Succession, ceding
Lorraine outright to the deposed Polish king and, through him, to
France on his death. One by one, the great powers of Europe put their
own names to a guarantee of an inheritance none of them had any real
stake in honoring once Charles was no longer alive to enforce it.

<Figure id="hm-pragmatic" />

## A daughter who inherited a paper promise

Charles never took the one step his own ministers, his wife and the
British court all pressed him toward: securing his son-in-law Francis
of Lorraine election as King of the Romans while he still lived, which
would have kept the imperial title itself inside the family regardless
of what happened to the female succession elsewhere. He judged his own
wife's death more likely than his own and held out hope for a second
marriage and a male heir that never came. He died in 1740, and Maria
Theresa, not yet twenty-four, inherited the full Habsburg patrimony
under a Pragmatic Sanction every neighboring power had formally
guaranteed - a guarantee that, within weeks of her father's death,
proved worth exactly as much as the armies Prussia's own new king was
already marching to test it against.
