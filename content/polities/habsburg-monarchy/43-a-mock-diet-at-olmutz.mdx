Unable to muster real support from the empire's own princes against
Poděbrady's invasion, Frederick turned to a more dangerous solution:
he offered the Bohemian crown itself to Matthias Corvinus, King of
Hungary, in exchange for turning against the man who had once ransomed
him from Bohemian captivity and made him king in the first place.
Matthias, dazzled by the prospect despite everything he owed
Poděbrady, joined German crusaders already operating under papal
sanction and invaded Bohemia while papal intrigue simultaneously
stirred Bohemia's own Catholic minority into open revolt - plunging
the kingdom back into exactly the kind of civil and religious war it
had only recently escaped. Poděbrady, still capable at defending his
own throne even as an old man, crushed the internal uprising and threw
back the Hungarian invasion outright, forcing an armistice and then a
formal peace treaty at a conference in Moravia in April 1469.

## No compact with heretics

The peace lasted barely any time at all. Persuaded by a papal legate
that no agreement made with a "heretic" king carried any real
obligation, Matthias resumed the war, overran Moravia and Silesia, and
staged what Coxe pointedly calls a "mock diet" at Olomouc with a
hand-picked assembly of Catholic loyalists - who duly elected him King
of Bohemia and let the papal legate crown him on the spot. He went on
to Wrocław to collect Silesia's own homage and install a governor,
acting for all purposes as Bohemia's rightful sovereign even while
Poděbrady still held Prague and the loyalty of most of the kingdom.

## An offer withdrawn

Frederick, who had handed Matthias the pretext for this whole
campaign, now got cold feet at the thought of a single ambitious rival
ruling both Hungary and Bohemia together. He quietly withheld the
military support he had promised Matthias and left the theater of war
entirely, disguising his retreat as a pilgrimage to Rome he claimed to
have vowed during the Vienna siege - then stretched the trip out
further by convening an imperial diet at Regensburg to plan a crusade
against the Ottomans he had no real intention of launching, offering
himself as its leader precisely because he knew the project would
never actually happen. He still hoped Hungary and Bohemia's crowns
might eventually fall to him regardless, encouraged by papal promises
- hopes Poděbrady was about to outmaneuver entirely.
