Süleyman crossed into Hungary in the spring of 1529 with an army put at
three hundred thousand men, and found almost nothing standing in his
way. John Zápolya, driven into Poland after Ferdinand's own troops had
broken his cause in the field, met the sultan's camp on the plain of
Mohács and was received with regal honours - a client king restored by
the same power that had killed his rival's predecessor on that same
ground three years before. Süleyman advanced through Pest, took Buda
by the treachery of its own garrison and handed the castle to Zápolya,
then pushed on almost unopposed - Wissegrad, Gran, Komorn, Raab and
Altenburg fell in quick succession - until his army stood beneath the
walls of Vienna itself. Coxe's own explanation for how little
resistance so warlike a kingdom offered is unsparing: Bohemia was too
divided by its own internal feuds to send help, Charles was occupied
with his Italian wars, and the German states were too consumed by
religious antipathy toward one another to unite against a distant
enemy. Ferdinand did what he could regardless, throwing some twenty
thousand reinforcements into the city and pressing the German diet for
troops; a relief corps under Frederick, Count Palatine, reached Krems
in September, too late to enter Vienna itself but near enough to keep
the Turkish camp in continual alarm.

## A dilapidated wall and a sunken flotilla

The city Süleyman found was badly placed to withstand him: open,
extensive suburbs and walls built in an old style never meant to
answer artillery. What actually held it was a combination the sultan
had not fully weighed - the skill of the commandant, Nicholas, Count
of Salm, the courage of his garrison, and the loyalty of Vienna's own
burghers, reinforced by a stroke of luck rather than design. The
flotilla carrying Süleyman's heavy artillery up the Danube never
reached the siege at all; the garrison at Pressburg dispersed or sank
it on its passage upriver, leaving the sultan to invest a European
capital without the guns that would ordinarily have opened its walls.
Reduced to mining, he spent thirty days attempting to breach the
defences from beneath rather than above, and every assault that
followed a breach was thrown back by Salm's own garrison. A last,
desperate effort in the closing days of the siege fared no better than
those before it, and with winter closing in and the fear of Frederick's
approaching relief force weighing on his own council, Süleyman broke
off the siege and turned back toward Buda.

## A crown delivered on the retreat

He did not leave empty-handed. At Buda, before a large assembly of
Turkish and Hungarian nobles, Süleyman proclaimed John Zápolya king in
earnest, received his homage, and handed over the sacred crown of St.
Stephen and the rest of the regalia he had held since capturing the
city. Zápolya kept a Turkish garrison in Buda as the price of his own
restoration, and Süleyman withdrew from Hungary the way he had come -
devastating the country as he went, carrying off captives in numbers
large enough, Coxe notes, to draw tears even from the king who owed
his own throne to the army doing the devastating. Ferdinand, watching
the Turkish army retreat, judged the moment right to move against his
rival directly, retaking Altenburg, Gran and other posts before the
season closed against him - the opening exchange in a contest over
Hungary that Süleyman had left unsettled on purpose, and would return
to finish himself within three years.
