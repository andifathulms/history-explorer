Frederick's death handed the family to a genuinely different kind of
ruler: active, liberal, still in the prime of his life, and heir to
territories that had grown far beyond anything a previous Habsburg
duke had commanded. His own name was itself a novelty - no earlier
member of the family had ever been called Maximilian, and Coxe
credits the choice to Frederick's own fondness for astrology,
supposedly built by combining the names of the Roman generals Fabius
Maximus and Paulus Aemilius rather than any inherited family
tradition or saint's day.

## A rivalry with no natural cause

Before 1477, Coxe observes, France and the House of Austria had no
real quarrel at all - their territories lay too far apart, and their
interests never crossed. Maximilian's own marriage to Mary of
Burgundy changed that permanently: it planted the two powers directly
against each other along a long shared frontier, and the personal
insult of the broken match with Anne of Brittany and the public
humiliation of the returned Archduchess Margaret turned a matter of
territorial friction into something felt as a private grievance by
the rulers themselves. The result, in Coxe's own reckoning, was a
hostility between the houses of Habsburg and Valois that would go on
to soak Europe in blood for more than three centuries - flaring up
again and again over the smallest provocations even when formal
treaties tried to paper over it.

## A new rival in the south

At the same moment, a second power was consolidating itself on
Europe's own southwestern edge. Castile and Aragon, united since 1469
by the marriage of Ferdinand and Isabella, had spent their reign
curbing an overmighty nobility, expanding royal revenue by folding
Spain's own military orders directly into the crown, and building the
disciplined army and navy that decades of war against Granada's Moors
had hardened into genuine professional forces. Their conquest of
Granada finally freed them from that centuries-old internal threat, and
in 1492 Ferdinand added Sicily to his own crown as well - transforming
Spain, within the span of Maximilian's own accession, into a power
capable of shaping the rest of Europe rather than merely defending its
own peninsula. It was a transformation the House of Austria would, in
time, find far more useful as a marriage partner than as a rival.
