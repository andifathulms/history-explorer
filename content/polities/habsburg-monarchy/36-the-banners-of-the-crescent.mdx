Ladislaus's own dependence on his regents met its sternest test at
Belgrade, the fortress guarding Hungary's southern approach, where
Sultan Mehmed II laid siege with an army and a river flotilla in 1456.
John Hunyadi, still regent in all but title, broke the Ottoman ships
blocking the Danube and Sava and reached the garrison in time to
repair its breached walls, evacuate the wounded, and resupply the
defenders before the sultan's main assault came. It nearly succeeded
anyway: Ottoman troops burst into the town and planted their own
banners on the ramparts before Hunyadi's own presence and skill turned
a collapsing defense into a counterattack, driving the Turks out with
heavy losses, turning captured cannon on their own former camp, and
wounding Mehmed himself. The siege broke after a day and a night of
fighting, and the Ottoman army withdrew under cover of darkness having
lost some thirty thousand men.

## A regent's death, and a king's ingratitude

Hunyadi did not long survive his own greatest victory. A fever brought
on by the exertions of the siege killed him at Zemun that August,
leaving a widow and two sons, Ladislaus and Matthias, whom Coxe
credits with promise to match their father's own fame. Ladislaus
Posthumus, whose own throne owed more to Hunyadi than to any other
single subject, felt the loss chiefly as relief: freed from a
regent more powerful than the king himself, he transferred his
patronage instead to Count Cilli, his own maternal uncle, and
nominated him governor of Hungary in a move that inflamed the very
nobles who had idolized Hunyadi's memory. The king made a show of
affection toward Hunyadi's own elder son when the two met, praising
his father's victory and promising a personal visit to Belgrade - but
the elder Corvinus, suspicious of Cilli's real intentions, would admit
only the king and his immediate court into the fortress itself,
excluding Cilli's own escort and setting the stage for a confrontation
neither man would survive.
