Margaret Maultasch's second marriage had never received papal
approval, leaving her son Meinhard branded illegitimate and her own
union with Louis of Bavaria's son treated as little better than an
open scandal. Rudolf saw both a problem to solve and an opportunity:
he won the pope's approval to legitimize Meinhard, then married him to
his own sister - and Margaret, grateful for the favor, granted Austria
the right to inherit Tyrol outright should her husband and son both
die without heirs of their own. They did, in quick succession: her
husband in 1361, and Meinhard himself, still a child, in 1363. Fearing
the Bavarian princes would move on Tyrol first under an older marriage
contract of their own, Rudolf crossed the Alps in the depths of winter
to secure Margaret's agreement in person, and - well aware how
changeable her temper could be - pressed her for immediate possession
rather than risk a second reconsideration. He got it, and persuaded
her to leave her mountain duchy behind entirely and settle at his own
court in Vienna, dazzled, in Coxe's telling, by the splendor Rudolf
made sure to put on display.

## A reconciliation at Brünn

Securing the emperor's own approval proved harder than securing
Margaret's. Despite his marriage to Charles IV's daughter, Rudolf and
his three brothers were, at that very moment, allied with Hungary and
Poland against Charles in his capacity as King of Bohemia - old
Habsburg-Luxembourg rivalry outliving the family tie meant to end it.
It was Rudolf's own wife who arranged the peace: she brought about a
meeting between her husband and her father at Brünn in 1364, where
Charles confirmed Margaret's grant, formally invested Rudolf with
Tyrol, and sealed a broader family compact promising each house the
right to inherit the other's territories should either line die out.
The house of Lower Bavaria, cut out of an inheritance it had once
expected, contested the transfer by force, and Rudolf spent months
trading sieges with Duke Stephen's army until the Pope - more
interested in German troops for his own war against the Visconti in
Italy than in a quarrel over one Alpine province - brokered a
suspension of hostilities that left Tyrol in Habsburg hands for good.
It was, with Carinthia a generation earlier, the second permanent
territorial gain in the family's history, and it came from a
twenty-four-year-old duke who had been ruling in his own right for
barely six years.
