Rudolf died in 1291 still trying to get the electors to break their
own habit and confirm his son Albert while he lived; they refused, on
the reasoning that the empire could not maintain two kings at once,
and let the question lapse until he was gone. It was a lesson the
family would keep learning for the next hundred years: the same
calculation that had put a minor Swabian count on the throne kept
reproducing itself every time a Habsburg looked strong enough to make
the crown hereditary. Albert, confident enough of his own claim to
seize the imperial regalia before a single vote had been cast, watched
the Archbishop of Mainz maneuver the college toward a weaker cousin,
Adolf of Nassau, instead - the electors "desired an emperor, but
dreaded a master," and Albert's own arrogance had done nothing to
change their minds.

## Yield your life and your crown

Adolf's reign gave Albert his opening. Arbitrary and grasping enough
to alienate the very archbishop who had made him king, Adolf was
deposed for contumacy by a diet Albert had spent a year quietly
assembling, and the two rival kings met in battle at Göllheim on 2
July 1298. Albert had armed picked infantry with poniards made
specifically to strike at horses, and when Adolf cut his way through
the melee toward him shouting "Yield your life and your crown! The
event is in the hand of Providence!" Albert drove a lance into his
face and had him cut down where he fell. Wary of holding a throne
people might call a battlefield prize, Albert had the electors
formally renounce his own earlier deposition of Adolf and re-elect him
clean - a legal fiction that gave him the crown his father had wanted
for him, at the cost of a rival's life rather than a peaceful
succession.

Albert's own ambitions reached further than his father's had: a brief,
disastrous bid to put his eldest son Rudolph on Bohemia's throne ended
in Rudolph's death within a year, and a second attempt to install
another son was shouted down in the Prague assembly itself - "We will
have no Austrian king!" - the crowd killing three Austrian partisans
rather than accept the nomination. Passed over for Bohemia twice,
Albert was still planning a larger campaign to force the issue when an
entirely different grievance ended his reign for good. His own
nephew John, denied the share of the family's Swiss and Alsatian
lands he believed was owed him, had been humiliated once too often -
offered a garland of flowers instead of his inheritance, with the
remark that it suited his years better than the cares of government.
Days later, crossing the river opposite the family's own ancestral
castle, John and four confederates ambushed Albert with a single
attendant at his side, cut him down within sight of the walls his
family took its name from, and left him to bleed out in a peasant
woman's arms before his own son could reach him.

## Two crowns in a fortnight

Passed over yet again for the German throne after Albert's murder -
the electors, still wary of the family, chose Henry of Luxembourg
instead, who moved fast enough to install his own son John on
Bohemia's throne within four years - the Habsburgs spent the next
decade watching a Luxembourg foothold harden next door into exactly
the rival dynasty their own repeated exclusions from Bohemia had been
meant to prevent. When Henry died in 1313, the empire's tangled
electoral system produced the deadlock it seemed built to invite: on 9
October 1314 the Austrian electors chose Frederick the Handsome at
Sachsenhausen, and within days the Luxembourg party crowned Louis of
Bavaria at Frankfurt instead. Two kings, each with a legitimate-
looking coronation, prepared for war.

Looking for a quicker way to weaken the Bavarian coalition, Frederick's
brother Leopold marched against three Swiss cantons that had sided
with Louis, boasting he would trample the "audacious rustics"
underfoot. Fourteen hundred Swiss met his fifteen-thousand-strong
column in the narrow pass of Morgarten on 16 November 1315, rolling
logs and boulders down onto cavalry too cramped to maneuver before
charging into infantry too tightly packed to defend itself. More than
fifteen hundred Austrians died, most of them titled nobility, and
Leopold escaped only with a peasant's help - a defeat that cost the
family more than the war it was meant to help win, and the first of
several the Swiss mountains would deal the House of Austria over the
following seventy years.

## A brother's late arrival

The wider war ground on for seven more years before Frederick, waiting
at Mühldorf in 1322 for Leopold's own army to arrive, abandoned a
sound defensive position to give battle alone - and lost, when a
disguised flanking force broke his exhausted line in the day's final
hour and took him prisoner. Louis, unexpectedly gracious in victory,
confined rather than executed his rival, and when Leopold's continued
resistance and the Pope's own hostility wore Louis down, Frederick did
something almost without precedent in the family's history: bound by a
parole he alone had sworn, he returned to captivity of his own accord
once his brothers refused to honor the terms he had agreed to on their
behalf. Louis, moved by "this rare instance of probity," treated his
returned prisoner as a guest rather than an enemy - the two men, by
one contemporary account, ate at the same table and slept in the same
bed - and eventually offered him joint rule of the empire outright, an
arrangement without precedent that the electors and the Pope both
rejected the moment it became public.

The partnership never had the chance to prove itself. Leopold, who had
never forgiven himself for arriving too late at Mühldorf, died at
Strasbourg in 1326; their brother Henry, broken by six months as a
prisoner of his own, followed within the year; and Frederick, stripped
of the joint authority he had briefly held, died in 1330 - a king
twice crowned, one battle short of the throne he had spent his adult
life reaching for. It was, paradoxically, his death and Leopold's that
finally steadied the family: with no more Habsburg pretender left to
contest the crown, the surviving brothers Albert and Otho made peace
with Louis at Hagenau that same year, ending sixteen years of a war
that had cost the dynasty a king's freedom, three princes' lives, and
its last real claim on the German throne for more than a century.
