Albert's own guardians, uncles Leopold and Ernest, had actively
encouraged him toward hunting and idle amusement rather than
government - a young ward kept distracted was a young ward who did
not ask why his own inheritance was being plundered. His tutors
thought otherwise: they gave him a genuine education in the sciences,
statecraft and religion despite his guardians' preferences, and when
Leopold died in 1411, Emperor Sigismund declared the fourteen-year-old
of age early rather than let Ernest's own misrule continue unchecked.
Vienna greeted him as a deliverer, and Albert - who had watched his
uncles' feuding wreck the country for a decade - answered that welcome
by abandoning the hunting and amusements that had defined his
childhood and applying himself seriously to government, choosing
experienced, honest advisors and genuinely following their counsel
rather than merely tolerating it.

## Austria restored

Austrian chroniclers, Coxe notes, wrote about the young duke's
administration with real enthusiasm: sound laws, close attention to
justice, a sustained campaign against the banditry that had run
unchecked through his uncles' chaotic regency, and active patronage of
scholars and letters. Austria, by their own proud account, became an
island of order and prosperity while its neighbors tore themselves
apart in civil war and foreign conflict - a striking reversal for a
duchy that only a few years earlier had been, in Coxe's own words,
in a "deplorable state." Much of that transformation rested on one
relationship above all others: Albert's bond with his father-in-law
and patron, Sigismund, King of Hungary and Bohemia and Holy Roman
Emperor, a son of Charles IV whose own turbulent path to three
crowns - contested regencies, an assassinated rival, a captive
brother, a schism within his own empire - had taught him exactly how
much a stable, capable ally like Albert was worth.
