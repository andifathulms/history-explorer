Maximilian was at Innsbruck when news of his father's death reached
him, and he opened his reign in a manner that immediately set him
apart from Frederick's own decades of caution: rather than leave
frontier defense to his generals, as his father almost always had, he
personally raised fifteen thousand men on short notice and marched
against an Ottoman raiding party that had pushed as far as Ljubljana
into Styria and Carniola. The Turks retreated before he even reached
them, and Maximilian arrived in Vienna to genuine acclaim as the
deliverer of his own country - a striking contrast to the humiliations
Vienna had inflicted on his father within living memory.

## An unequal match

After burying Frederick and formally taking charge of the family's
hereditary lands, Maximilian married Bianca Maria Sforza, sister of
Milan's own reigning duke and niece of its actual regent, Ludovico
Sforza, on 16 March 1494. His motives were frankly practical: a
substantial dowry from a family desperate for the prestige of an
imperial connection, and an alliance that promised him real leverage
in Italian affairs. Germany's own nobility took the marriage as an
insult to the family's dignity, denouncing the Sforzas as a low-born,
illegitimate house unworthy of an emperor's alliance, and some went so
far as to argue that any children from the marriage should be barred
from the succession under German law. Stung by the criticism,
Maximilian quietly delayed the investiture he had promised Ludovico
over Milan itself, waiting until the reigning duke's own convenient
death - possibly by poison at his own uncle's hand, though Coxe treats
the accusation as suspicion rather than proven fact - cleared the way
for Ludovico to take the duchy outright.

## A prisoner escapes

With his marriage settled, Maximilian handed the government of the
Low Countries to his own sixteen-year-old son Philip and turned to
suppressing a revolt in Guelderland, led by a young pretender named
Charles who had been raised at Maximilian's own court, watched
constantly as a virtual hostage, and had finally escaped to raise a
following in his ancestral homeland. Maximilian moved to crush the
rebellion in person, but events in Italy soon pulled him away
entirely, and he left the campaign and the government of the
Netherlands to Duke Albert of Saxony, who put down the revolt and
drove the young pretender out of the country in his absence.
