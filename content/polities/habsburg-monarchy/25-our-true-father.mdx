While his brother fought and died against the Swiss, Albert governed
the Austrian heartland in a wholly different register: suppressing
banditti, restraining nobles who mistook his mildness for weakness,
and pouring his real energy into the university his own brother Rudolf
had founded, endowing new chairs in theology and mathematics and
deepening its rivalry with Prague. He married a daughter of Emperor
Charles IV, cementing the Luxembourg tie that had bound the two houses
together since the Tyrol settlement, and showed almost none of his
predecessors' appetite for war - a single expedition to Prussia in
1377, fighting alongside the Teutonic Knights and earning a knighthood
for it, stands out precisely because it was so unlike him.

## A fatal expedition to Bohemia

Age did not mellow that pattern until, in 1395, Bohemia's own
disintegrating politics pulled him in regardless. King Wenceslas IV's
erratic and tyrannical rule had driven a faction of Bohemian nobles to
seize him outright and imprison him in Prague Castle, handing power to
a cousin as regent; Wenceslas's own brother John raised an army,
forced his release, and marched him to an Austrian castle for
safekeeping before civil war broke out in earnest once he returned
home. Albert took the nobles' side and led an army into Bohemia
himself - his first real campaign in nearly two decades of peaceful
rule - but fell mortally ill in camp before the fighting resolved
anything, and died at Laxenburg on 29 August 1395, forty-six years
old.

## "Our true father"

Vienna mourned him as it had mourned no Habsburg duke before: crowds
gathered around his body crying "We have lost our friend, our true
father!" - a tribute Coxe judges genuinely earned rather than merely
customary. A contemporary chronicler remembered him as "a lover of
peace, a pious and meek prince," who kept his main residence at
Laxenburg specifically to escape the pomp of court life, gardened with
his own hands, and studied classical treatises on rural economy for
pleasure. He earned two lasting nicknames from his own subjects:
"the Astrologer," for a genuine fascination with judicial astrology
that matched his scholarly turn of mind, and "Albert with the Tress,"
for a lock of hair - given to him, by different accounts, either by
his wife or by another lady of rank - that he wore braided into his
own and used to found a chivalric society modeled on the gesture, an
oddly gentle emblem for a prince remembered above all for governing
without needing to be feared.
