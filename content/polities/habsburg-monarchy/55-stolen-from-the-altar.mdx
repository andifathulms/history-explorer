title: Stolen from the altar

Charles VIII, having just turned nineteen and taken the reins of
government himself, moved against Brittany with a duplicity Coxe
traces straight back to his own tutelage under Anne of Beaujeu. He
recognized that conquering the duchy outright, against a duchess
backed by both Maximilian's empire and English support, would be
brutally difficult - so he pivoted to a subtler plan: marrying Anne
himself, despite his own existing marriage contract with Maximilian's
daughter Margaret, who had lived at the French court as queen-in-
waiting for seven years already. He kept faith scrupulously with the
Peace of Frankfurt's terms regarding Flanders to lull Maximilian's
suspicions, while stalling on the Brittany provisions with excuses
about English troops still in Breton service - and when the marriage
between Maximilian and Anne was formally announced, Charles used the
shock of it to peel away Anne's own remaining allies, buying off Alain
d'Albret with the surrender of Nantes and pressing French troops
forward against Rennes, the last stronghold left to the duchy.

## A mock treaty

Maximilian scrambled for help - imperial troops, an English
contingent - but moved far too slowly against a French court working
with speed and total secrecy. Charles freed the imprisoned Duke of
Orléans specifically to use his personal influence over Anne, who
still detested the French for the suffering they had brought her
country and found Charles himself personally repellent; she held out
against every pressure until her own generals and counselors, bought
or intimidated, left her without hope of relief and her own capital
under blockade. She finally agreed to what looked like generous
terms - French withdrawal from Brittany, arbitration of Charles's
claims, safe passage for herself into Germany - terms the French
honored to the letter, right up until Maximilian's own ambassadors,
sent to escort their new queen home, learned instead that she had gone
to Langeais and married Charles VIII there. Brittany, the object of
the whole scheme, was absorbed into the French crown in the same
stroke.

## An indemnity, not a victory

Maximilian's fury was total - his own daughter publicly humiliated
after seven years as France's presumptive queen, and his own wife
taken from him after their marriage had already been solemnized. He
built a coalition with England and Aragon, appealed to the Swiss and
the imperial diet, and found exactly the kind of half-hearted support
that had failed him at Bruges: the Swiss offered only mediation, German
troops arrived too slowly to matter, and England's Henry VII, more
interested in money than in war, sold his own neutrality to France for
cash and a pension rather than fight. Aragon's Ferdinand, tied down by
his own war against Granada, accepted a territorial bribe to abandon
the coalition as well, leaving Maximilian to negotiate alone. The
resulting Treaty of Senlis returned his daughter Margaret and
compensated him with Franche-Comté, Artois and several lesser
territories in place of Brittany - honorable terms, in Coxe's own
judgment, but a settlement that could not undo the personal insult of
losing a wife to the very kingdom he had spent a decade fighting.
