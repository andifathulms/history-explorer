Victory at White Mountain emboldened Ferdinand II past the point his
own coalition could sustain. Flush with conquered Bohemia and a war
that seemed decided, he issued the Edict of Restitution on 6 March
1629, ordering every piece of church property secularized since the
Peace of Passau in 1552 - two archbishoprics, a dozen bishoprics,
hundreds of monasteries - returned to Catholic hands outright.
The edict alarmed Protestant and Catholic princes alike, who saw in
it an emperor no longer bound by the empire's own settled compromises,
and it gave Sweden's King Gustavus Adolphus the pretext he needed to
carry his own army into Germany the following year, landing on the
Pomeranian coast to defend a Protestant cause his own conquests would
soon eclipse in scope.

## A general dismissed, then recalled

Ferdinand had already been forced to dismiss the general most
responsible for his run of victories, Albrecht von Wallenstein, whose
private army and outsized ambition had alarmed German princes on both
sides of the religious divide. Gustavus's own advance - a rout of the
Catholic League's forces at Breitenfeld in 1631, a march that carried
Swedish troops as far as the Rhine and into Bavaria itself - left
Ferdinand no real alternative but to recall the one commander capable
of matching him. Wallenstein returned to raise a fresh imperial army
from nothing within months, and the two greatest commanders of the
war finally met in open battle outside the small Saxon town of Lützen
on 6 November 1632.

## Lützen

Fog delayed the fighting past midday, and when the lines finally
engaged, Gustavus led a cavalry charge into a gap in his own line
without adequate escort and was shot from his horse in a confused
melee his own army barely noticed until his riderless mount reappeared.
The Swedes fought on without knowing their king had already died, and
carried the field by nightfall regardless - Wallenstein's army broke
and retreated, leaving Sweden the tactical victor of a battle that
cost it the one commander no other general in Europe could yet match.
Coxe's own eulogy calls him "one of the greatest monarchs who ever
adorned a throne," a soldier who told those urging him to spare
himself in battle that "my hour is written in heaven, and cannot be
reversed on earth." His body, stripped and left under a heap of the
slain, was carried first to Naumburg and eventually home to Stockholm.

## A general too powerful to keep

Gustavus's death did not end the war Wallenstein's own victories had
briefly seemed to be winning back for Ferdinand. Wallenstein's own
conduct after Lützen - opening independent negotiations with Sweden
and Saxony, holding his army apart from the emperor's own strategic
direction - convinced Ferdinand's court that a general who had once
raised and paid an entire army on his own credit could no longer be
trusted with one. Officers loyal to the emperor assassinated
Wallenstein at Eger in February 1634, ending, by violence rather than
dismissal, the career of the one commander whose private resources had
kept Ferdinand's war effort solvent through its hardest years - and
opening a command structure the emperor's own son, the future
Ferdinand III, would take over in the war's final, exhausting phase.
