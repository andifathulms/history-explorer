Maximilian was at Innsbruck when news of his father's death reached
him, and he opened his reign in a manner that immediately set him
apart from Frederick's own decades of caution: rather than leave
frontier defense to his generals, as his father almost always had, he
personally raised fifteen thousand men on short notice and marched
against an Ottoman raiding party that had pushed into Styria and
Carniola. The Turks retreated before he even reached them, and
Maximilian arrived in Vienna to genuine acclaim as the deliverer of
his own country - a striking contrast to the humiliations Vienna had
inflicted on his father within living memory. His own name was itself
a novelty: no earlier Habsburg had ever been called Maximilian, and
Coxe credits the choice to Frederick's own fondness for astrology,
supposedly built by combining the names of the Roman generals Fabius
Maximus and Paulus Aemilius rather than any inherited family
tradition.

After formally taking charge of the family's hereditary lands,
Maximilian married Bianca Maria Sforza, sister of Milan's own reigning
duke, on 16 March 1494 - a match driven by a substantial dowry and the
promise of leverage in Italian affairs, though Germany's nobility took
it as an insult to the family's dignity, denouncing the Sforzas as a
low-born house unworthy of an emperor's alliance. With his marriage
settled, Maximilian handed government of the Low Countries to his own
sixteen-year-old son Philip and turned to suppressing a revolt in
Guelderland led by a young pretender who had escaped his own court
after years spent as a virtual hostage there - a campaign soon left to
Duke Albert of Saxony once events in Italy pulled Maximilian away
entirely.

## A new rival in the south

At the same moment, a new power was consolidating itself on Europe's
southwestern edge. Castile and Aragon, united since 1469 by the
marriage of Ferdinand and Isabella, had spent their reign curbing an
overmighty nobility and building the disciplined army and navy that
decades of war against Granada's Moors had hardened into genuine
professional forces; their conquest of Granada finally freed them from
that centuries-old threat, and in 1492 Ferdinand added Sicily to his
own crown as well, transforming Spain, within the span of Maximilian's
own accession, into a power capable of shaping the rest of Europe
rather than merely defending its own peninsula - one the House of
Austria would, in time, find far more useful as a marriage partner
than as a rival.
