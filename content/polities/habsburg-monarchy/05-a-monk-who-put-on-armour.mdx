Years of indecisive raiding followed Süleyman's retreat from Güns,
broken by occasional truces and treaties that changed little while
John Zápolya kept possession of most of the kingdom. Both rivals came
to see, eventually, that the sultan profited from their quarrel far
more than either of them did, and in 1538 they concluded the treaty of
Várad under Charles V's own mediation: Zápolya would keep the royal
title, Transylvania, and whatever Hungarian territory he already held
for life, but on his death his own male heirs would inherit only his
family's private lands and the voivodeship of Transylvania, while the
crown itself reverted to Ferdinand. The arrangement favoured
Ferdinand precisely because Zápolya was old and unmarried - until,
almost as soon as the ink dried, he married his young cousin Isabella
of Poland. She bore him a son, John Sigismund, and a fortnight later,
on 21 July 1540, Zápolya died of a stroke.

## The bishop who would not yield an inheritance

Ferdinand's claim under the Várad treaty was now, on paper, simply due
- but Isabella refused to surrender a kingdom she had just become
regent of, backed by a churchman whose ambition matched her own
resolve. George Martinuzzi, bishop of Várad, had risen from a poor
Croatian family through a monastery, a friendship with Zápolya formed
in his own youth, and a talent for both administration and command
that let him move, in Coxe's description, as easily in armour as in
vestments. Under his direction a party of Hungarian nobles crowned the
infant John Sigismund in place of a proper election, carried mother
and child to Buda, and sent to Constantinople for Süleyman's own
protection - a maneuver Coxe judges a breach of both patriotism and
good faith, and the opening of a civil conflict that would keep
Hungary divided under Ottoman influence for more than a century.

Ferdinand answered first with diplomacy, offering to hold Hungary
himself as an Ottoman fief on the same terms Zápolya had once accepted.
Süleyman refused outright - an infant he could crown or depose at will
suited his own purposes better than a grown king from a powerful
family who might one day outgrow his tribute - and demanded, as the
price of peace, that Ferdinand give up not only Hungary but
acknowledge Austria itself as an Ottoman fief. Ferdinand sent an army
instead, which reached Buda in 1541 and besieged Isabella and her
infant son in the citadel; Martinuzzi held the defence with what Coxe
calls singular skill and resolve until the pasha of Belgrade arrived
and broke the siege outright.

## Ten thousand janissaries in the citadel

Süleyman followed in person with a still larger force, took the gates
of Buda by stratagem, and secured the queen and the infant prince
under a garrison of ten thousand janissaries - occupying the very
capital he had handed Zápolya as an ally a decade before, this time
for himself. Buda's fall gave the sultan command of the Danube and a
base from which to extend his own territory on both banks, and every
attempt Ferdinand made to recover the lost ground failed: his brother
Charles was absorbed in his own ambitions elsewhere, imperial subsidies
arrived scant and irregular, and his own hereditary lands were
exhausted by more than a decade of intermittent war. By August 1545 he
had no real choice left but to ask Süleyman for a truce, granted for
five years only at the French king's own intercession, and only on
humiliating terms: an annual tribute of thirty thousand ducats, and
formal status as a feudatory of the Ottoman Porte.

Isabella and Martinuzzi were made, in the same settlement, to renounce
the crown outright in the infant's name; in exchange John Sigismund
kept Transylvania and the district east of the Tisza as a tributary
principality Süleyman could resume at pleasure - not the kingdom his
father had died holding, but a fragment of it, held now on Ottoman
sufferance rather than Hungarian election, and the durable shape
Hungary's own three-way division between Habsburg, Ottoman and
Transylvanian rule would keep for the rest of the century.
