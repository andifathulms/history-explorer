Ludovico Sforza's own alarm at a threatened second French invasion of
Milan was enough to draw Maximilian back into Italy himself, though
with a force of only five hundred horse and eight companies of foot -
a fraction of what his own sanguine hopes for German reinforcement and
Italian subsidy led him to expect. He crossed the Alps only to
discover Charles VIII had already postponed the expedition that
supposedly justified the whole trip; no German troops joined him, and
his own allies, no longer needing his presence, began hinting broadly
that he should simply go home. Unwilling to leave without some visible
achievement, Maximilian pushed his allies toward retaking Asti, but
Milanese-Venetian rivalry blocked every plan he proposed, and he was
eventually permitted only a lesser consolation: a siege of Livorno,
meant to pry Florence loose from its own French alliance.

## A blockade that never worked

Embarking a fleet from Genoa, Maximilian blockaded the port by sea,
but found he could not actually stop supplies and reinforcements
reaching the defenders, and when a storm scattered his own ships he
was forced to land his troops and try a land siege instead. His allies
proved no more reliable on the ground than they had at sea - the
Venetian generals in particular slowed and undermined the siege at
every turn - and Maximilian, finally recognizing that his own
coalition partners had no real interest in his success, vented his
fury on their ambassadors and abandoned the whole enterprise in
December 1496, retreating from Italy in disgust. Coxe judges the
episode harshly: a "precipitate and romantic" venture that cost
Maximilian real standing as both head of the empire and a prince of
genuine ability - the second time in two years his own Italian
ambitions had collapsed for want of the resources needed to match
them.
