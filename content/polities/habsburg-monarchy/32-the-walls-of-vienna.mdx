Albert governed the empire itself with the same competence he had
already shown in Austria: reforming corrupt courts, curbing the
notorious secret tribunals of Westphalia, and proposing a public peace
that would outlaw the private wars German princes had waged against
each other for generations - an early sketch of the administrative
"circles" system Maximilian I would later complete. He also steered
carefully between the Pope and the rival Council of Basel, whose
quarrel over Church reform had split Christendom into competing
factions and popes yet again, without letting either side drag Austria
into the dispute directly. But the crisis that actually ended his
reign came from the east, where Sultan Murad II's Ottoman armies -
already masters of Smyrna, Thessalonica and much of Greece, and
pressing hard against Serbia and Hungary's own southern frontier -
saw Sigismund's death as their opening to strike before Albert could
consolidate his new inheritance.

## A last campaign

When the Ottomans besieged the Serbian fortress of Smederevo, its
defenders appealed to Albert directly. He hurried to Buda and raised
what forces he could, though the Hungarian nobles supported him only
half-heartedly, and was still in the field when Smederevo fell and its
garrison was massacred - a disaster that spread panic through Hungary
as the Ottoman army pushed toward the Danube. Stung by Albert's own
public reproaches, the Hungarians finally rallied to his standard in
real numbers, but dysentery tore through both camps before the armies
could properly engage, forcing Murad to withdraw - and striking Albert
himself just as hard. Advised to rest, he pressed on toward Vienna
anyway, repeating, "I shall recover if I can only once more behold the
walls of Vienna," and worsened his condition further by eating melons
during the journey. Sensing the end was near, he took the sacrament
and made his will, but insisted on continuing in a litter rather than
die away from his pregnant wife - and died short of Vienna, in a
village near Esztergom, on 17 October 1439, forty-three years old and
not even two full years into the reign that had, for one brief
moment, reunited Austria with the imperial crown his family had first
worn under Rudolf I.

## The Grave, and the Magnanimous

Tall, dignified and famously well-proportioned, Albert earned two
epithets from his own contemporaries - "the Grave," for his dark
complexion and serious bearing, and "the Magnanimous," for the
genuine scale of his ambitions and achievements in barely two years of
combined rule. His death left no adult heir behind him: only a
pregnant widow, and a Habsburg inheritance about to be pulled apart
once again between rival claimants before his own child was even born.
