Matthias, childless and aging, spent his own last years maneuvering to
secure the succession for his cousin Ferdinand of Styria - a devout
Catholic already notorious for suppressing Protestant worship in his
own duchies - and had him crowned King of Bohemia in 1617 over
Protestant objections that his own Letter of Majesty guarantees would
not survive such a successor. Those fears proved founded almost
immediately: when Bohemia's Protestant estates complained that two
churches built on royal land had been shut down in violation of the
Letter, the council of regents governing in Matthias's name - guided,
in the delegates' own eyes, chiefly by two hardline Catholic
councillors, William Slavata and Jaroslav Martinic - sent back an
answer they read as an outright denial of their rights.

## Out the window

A delegation of Protestant nobles led by Count Thurn confronted the
council directly at Prague Castle on 23 May 1618, demanding to know
who had actually drafted the offending reply. When persuasion failed
to satisfy them, one delegate invoked what Coxe records as "the
ancient custom of Bohemia" and called for the offending councillors to
be thrown from the window; Martinic went first, into the castle ditch,
followed - after the delegation's own momentary horror nearly broke
their resolve, and Thurn rallied them back to the act - by Slavata and
his secretary. All three survived an eighty-foot fall, saved by the
moat's own soft mud and a broken landing rather than any mercy from
the crowd still firing on them from above. Thurn moved immediately to
make the act irreversible, riding through Prague to declare to the
gathered crowds that "the die is thrown, it is too late to recall what
is past," and within days the estates had revived their old
confederacy, elected thirty directors, expelled the Jesuits and the
archbishop, and appealed to every Protestant power in reach for
support.

## A king who stayed at dinner

Matthias's death in 1619 left Ferdinand to inherit a revolt already
in open war: Bohemia's estates deposed him outright and offered the
crown instead to Frederick V, the young Calvinist Elector Palatine,
whose acceptance turned a regional rebellion into a contest for the
whole religious balance of the empire. Ferdinand, elected Holy Roman
Emperor in the middle of the crisis, spent a year assembling the
coalition that would decide it - the Catholic League under Bavaria's
Duke Maximilian, the Elector of Saxony detached from the Protestant
cause with the promise of Lusatia, and support from Spain and the
pope - while Frederick's own popularity in Bohemia curdled as quickly
as it had bloomed. When the two armies finally met outside Prague at
the White Mountain on 8 November 1620, Frederick was at the palace
hosting a dinner for the English ambassador rather than with his own
troops; by the time repeated couriers convinced him to ride out, his
army had already broken, and he watched its final collapse from the
city ramparts before ordering the gates opened for the fleeing
survivors. Offered a chance to hold Prague itself behind seventeen
intact battalions and reinforcements still in the field, Frederick
chose flight instead, leaving the Bohemian crown and regalia
abandoned in the marketplace and slipping out of the city by night -
earning, from the war he had accepted a crown to fight, the lasting
nickname "the Winter King."

## The reckoning

Prague surrendered the next morning, and for three months Ferdinand's
own restraint let former rebels believe the worst might be over.
It was not. Forty of the revolt's leaders were arrested in a single
night on 21 January 1621; after four months' imprisonment,
twenty-three were publicly executed in Prague's own old town square,
their property confiscated outright, and the rest banished or
imprisoned for life. A mandate of what Coxe calls "more than
inquisitorial severity" then ordered every landholder who had taken
part in the rising to confess their own guilt under threat of still
harsher punishment if convicted later without having done so - and
in the panic that followed, more than seven hundred nobles and
knights, guilty and innocent alike, added their own names to the
list rather than risk the alternative. The rebellion that had begun
with two councillors thrown from a castle window ended with the
confiscation of most of the Bohemian nobility's own land, the
Letter of Majesty it had been fought to defend torn up by Ferdinand's
own hand, and a religious and political settlement imposed on Bohemia
that would hold, in one form or another, for the better part of two
centuries.
