As his reign drew toward its close, Frederick's record as emperor
came in for a harder-edged assessment than his own passivity in most
crises might suggest. Coxe judges him unfairly blamed for every civil
war that afflicted Germany during his decades on the throne - languid
and inactive by temperament, certainly, but the same reign also
produced real, lasting reforms to how the empire actually governed
itself. Frederick's own government laid out, for the first time, a
genuine system for raising military contingents from the empire's
princes and states, rather than leaving the matter to whatever
power and self-interest happened to apply in a given crisis: a
"greater contingent" of thirty-four thousand men and a "lesser"
contingent of eight thousand, with the lesser force obligated to march
immediately on alarm and the greater the following year, funded by
fixed assessments collected through imperial commissaries. It was, by
Coxe's account, about the most workable system the empire's own
patchwork of jealous, semi-independent territories could realistically
support.

## The right to declare war on anyone

A second problem proved far harder to solve: the right of private
warfare, under which any noble, town or even guild could formally
declare feud against any other party over a grievance real or
imagined, with no higher authority required to approve it. The abuse
reached genuinely absurd extremes - associations of tradesmen and
household servants exchanging formal declarations of war with each
other "on the most ridiculous pretences," in Coxe's own phrase, in a
manner he judges scarcely credible to a later age. One surviving
challenge came from a private individual, Henry Mayenberg, addressed
directly to Frederick himself: "I will no longer obey your grace, but
will be the enemy of your country, and your subjects, and will do them
as much harm as possible." Another came from a nobleman against the
entire city of Frankfurt, provoked by nothing more than a young lady's
refusal to dance with him at a ball. Frederick's own reforms chipped
at the edges of this culture without ever fully suppressing it - a
problem that would remain unsolved when his own long, turbulent reign
finally passed to his son.
