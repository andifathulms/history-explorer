Albert inherited Austria, Styria, Carinthia and Carniola outright on
his father's death, having already administered them from Vienna
since 1283 with a firmness that curdled, in Coxe's telling, into
outright unpopularity - he favoured his own Swabian followers with the
richest offices and heiresses in the country, and when the Austrian
nobility demanded their dismissal and Albert refused, the barons
plotted a general insurrection with outside help, offering pieces of
his own duchies to the kings of Bohemia and Hungary and to the
Archbishop of Salzburg in exchange for their support. Vienna itself
rose against him after his father's death; Albert, retreating to high
ground outside the city, cut off its supply routes until hunger forced
a surrender, then made the city's own magistrates walk to his camp
barefoot and bareheaded to watch him tear up their charters in front
of them. Styria rose in turn, backed by Bavaria, Salzburg and Hungary
together, and Albert broke the siege of Bruck by force-marching an
army through winter snow to catch the besiegers by surprise.

## Passed over

With Austria still smouldering, Albert set his sights on the vacant
imperial crown his own father had failed to secure for him, and was so
confident of success that he took possession of the fortress holding
the imperial regalia before the electors had even voted. He was
wrong: the Archbishop of Mainz maneuvered the college instead toward
his own cousin, Adolf of Nassau, a capable soldier from a much smaller
house that posed no threat to the electors' own independence - the
same calculation, played out a second time, that had put Albert's own
father on the throne twenty years before. Furious, Albert threatened
open resistance, but a Swiss league forming against his own Helvetian
territories, backed by the Count of Savoy and the cities of Zurich,
Bern and Basel, left him too exposed to risk a war in Germany as well.
He campaigned hard enough in Switzerland to force a general amnesty
from the new king, then swallowed his pride: he acknowledged Adolf,
surrendered the regalia, and received his own fiefs back from his
rival's hands. It was a prudent retreat rather than a real
reconciliation, and within five years he would have his revenge.
