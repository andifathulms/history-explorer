Neighboring provinces petitioned for Maximilian's release, and a
loyalist noble raided the rebels' own territory to pressure them, but
it was Frederick who finally forced the issue - abandoning, for once,
the indolence that had defined most of his reign to fight for his
son with real urgency. He appealed personally to Germany's princes,
secured troops from Lorraine and the still-loyal Low Countries, and
within weeks had assembled fifteen thousand men at Cologne, pushing
into Brabant as far as Mechelen while persuading the Pope to
excommunicate the rebels for good measure. Terrified by the advancing
German army, the Flemings released Maximilian on 16 May 1488 - but
only after extracting a renewed promise to honor the Treaty of Arras
in full, surrender every fortress under his control, and withdraw all
foreign troops from the country, backed by hostages and his own
pledge to secure his father's ratification.

## No trace of resentment

Maximilian showed no anger at any of it. He went straight to church to
give thanks, telling the crowd outside, "We are now at peace," then
visited the very shop where his captivity had begun and read an
insulting inscription scrawled on its walls without any visible
reaction, forgiving the people who now knelt before him begging
pardon. He read the terms of his own release aloud from a public
scaffold and swore to them on the consecrated host, then dined openly
in the town before riding out under an honor escort his father had
sent to meet him. Back with Frederick, he quietly worked to talk his
own father out of further war against the rebels - and when Frederick
pressed on regardless, besieging Ghent for three months before handing
the campaign to Duke Albert of Saxony and returning home, it was
diplomacy rather than the siege that finally closed the conflict. King
Charles VIII of France, eager to pursue his own designs on a newly
vulnerable Brittany rather than keep fighting Maximilian, concluded
the Peace of Frankfurt on 22 July 1489 - restoring Breton conquests,
confirming the Arras settlement, and using French influence to press
the Flemish rebels into finally accepting Maximilian's guardianship of
his own son along with a substantial fine.

## A door to Austria reopens

With the Netherlands finally quiet, Frederick and Maximilian turned at
last to recovering Austria itself from Matthias Corvinus - though the
German diet, willing enough to rescue Maximilian from captivity,
refused to treat what it considered a private Habsburg quarrel as the
empire's own business. Frederick, trusting astrologers who promised
Matthias would die within the year, refused even to pay the ransom
Duke Albert had already negotiated; Maximilian, with none of his
father's patience, opened his own separate talks with Matthias instead
and secured a much cheaper settlement, twelve thousand ducats rather
than seventy thousand. Frederick, irritated at his son's interference
but unwilling to pass up the better terms, accepted anyway, and a
peace was proclaimed in September 1489 with a meeting planned between
the two rival sovereigns at Buda. Matthias's own worsening illness
kept delaying that meeting - until his death in April 1490 made it
unnecessary, and reopened, after more than a decade of exile,
Habsburg Austria's own path home.
