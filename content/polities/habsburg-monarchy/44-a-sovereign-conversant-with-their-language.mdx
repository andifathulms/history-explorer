Poděbrady answered both rivals with a single stroke. He summoned a
diet at Prague and proposed as his own successor Vladislaus, eldest
son of King Casimir of Poland and a granddaughter of Emperor Albert
V's own line through his mother - a candidate who satisfied nearly
everyone at once. Bohemia's Catholics welcomed a prince of their own
faith; the Calixtins saw in him the surest way to keep both Frederick
and Matthias, each hostile to their own doctrines, off the throne for
good; and the wider nation liked the idea of a king who actually spoke
their own language and descended, through the female line, from their
beloved Charles IV. The estates approved the nomination without real
opposition, and Vladislaus was declared heir to the Bohemian crown
while Poděbrady still lived.

## An armistice, then a death

Matthias, furious at being passed over so completely, kept fighting
with undiminished intensity, and the two armies - each led personally
by a capable and experienced king - fought each other to exhaustion
before agreeing to a truce on 22 July 1470 that finally quieted the
devastated countryside both sides had spent years ravaging. Poděbrady
did not live to see it hold for long: he died the following year, and
Frederick, sensing one more opportunity, put himself forward as a
candidate for the vacant throne a second time - only to find even less
support than before. Matthias's own faction proved larger this time,
but the majority of the Bohemian estates held to Poděbrady's own
choice, and Vladislaus was duly elected and proclaimed king. Frederick,
unable to win the crown himself, settled for backing Vladislaus over
Matthias instead - a policy that would keep stoking unrest in Hungary
for years to come, even as it settled nothing of Frederick's own,
much larger ambitions.
