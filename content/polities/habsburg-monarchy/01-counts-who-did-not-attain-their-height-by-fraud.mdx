The family that would rule Austria for more than six centuries began
as minor counts in Alsace and Switzerland, holding a castle - built,
by the family's own foundation records, by a bishop of Strasbourg
named Werner in 1027 - on a hill above the village of Windisch that
gave the line its name: Habsburg. Werner's descendants added to their
modest inheritance through marriage, imperial grants, and appointment
as advocates over neighbouring abbeys and towns, an office that
carried real revenue and authority and, over generations, tended to
become both permanent and hereditary. Rudolf, born in 1218 and
presented at the baptismal font by the Emperor Frederick II himself,
grew up at Frederick's own court and camp, trained from boyhood in
wrestling, horsemanship and the javelin, and inherited on his father's
death in 1240 only a modest landgraviate, a burgraviate, and a share
of the family's original county - territories confined enough that, in
Coxe's own telling, Rudolf spent much of his youth doing what German
princes of his rank did: fighting his neighbours.

## A count who kept his word

His early wars were unglamorous and local - a fortress stormed from a
resentful young baron in 1242, a feud with his own guardian uncle over
an inheritance, an extortionate dispute with another uncle's family
over the county of Kyburg - the ordinary business of a minor lord
building his position through opportunism and force. What set him
apart, in the source's telling, was less restraint than reliability:
when a delegation of would-be assassins offered to kill his rival
Ottokar of Bohemia for him decades later, Rudolf refused and warned
Ottokar instead, a decision consistent with the values his own father
had left him at the family's ancestral convent of Muri before departing
on crusade - to act with intrepidity once committed to a cause, but
never to seek advantage through fraud or treachery. It was this
reputation, combined with the modesty of his actual territory, that
would make him acceptable to the very electors most anxious not to
crown a master.
