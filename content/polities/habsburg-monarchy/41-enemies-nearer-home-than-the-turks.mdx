While Frederick fought his own brother for Austria, a separate crisis
in Germany threatened his hold on the imperial crown itself. Frederick
the Victorious, regent of the Palatinate for his infant nephew, had
assumed full electoral authority with the estates' own approval and
the pope's confirmation - approval the emperor alone withheld,
earning himself a formidable and permanent enemy. The elector spent
years building a coalition against Frederick, drawing in the
archbishop-electors of Mainz and Trier, the landgrave of Hesse, and -
most damaging of all - Frederick's own brother Albert and cousin
Sigismund, and won George of Poděbrady's support outright with the
outright offer of the imperial crown itself. In February and March
1461 the confederates convened rival diets at Eger and Nuremberg
without the emperor's consent, summoned a further diet at Frankfurt as
a prelude to formal deposition, and sent Frederick a scathing letter
blaming Germany's disorder squarely on his own weakness and fifteen
years of absence from the diets he was supposed to preside over.

## A crown saved by diplomacy

Threatened with the loss of everything his family had spent nearly
two centuries reclaiming, Frederick abandoned his usual indolence and
fought back with intrigue rather than arms - working every ambassador
and circular letter he had to split his enemies apart. He held
Brandenburg's own elector, who distrusted the Palatinate as much as
Bohemia; secured Saxony despite its ties to Bavaria; and even peeled
away George of Poděbrady himself by offering to mediate the Bohemian
king's own worsening quarrel with Rome. His strongest ally proved to
be Pope Pius II, who had every personal reason to keep his own
benefactor on the throne and threw the church's full weight behind
him. A related war over a disputed archbishopric of Mainz spilled into
open battle - the Elector Palatine crushed an anti-Wittelsbach
coalition at Seckingen in 1462, taking a bishop and a margrave
prisoner, while an allied Bavarian duke won a second victory at Gingen
and even captured the imperial standard itself - but battlefield
losses proved almost irrelevant next to the diplomatic collapse of the
league that had assembled them. One by one the confederates made
separate peace, and by the time the dust settled, the Elector Palatine
had abandoned the deposition scheme entirely in exchange for his own,
narrower territorial gains.

## An empire too divided to fight the Turks

Frederick kept his crown, but Coxe's own verdict on the whole affair is
withering: the constant infighting among German princes left the
empire unable to answer a genuine external threat, as Ottoman armies
- already masters of Constantinople - made their first incursions into
German territory during Frederick's own reign. The Elector Palatine
spoke for most of his peers when he said he had "enemies nearer home
than the Turks," and Frederick himself, Coxe notes with open
disapproval, often treated Ottoman pressure on Hungary as a convenient
check on his own rival there rather than a danger worth uniting
against. Even Pius II's own zeal for a crusade collapsed into
factionalism: when George of Poděbrady offered to lead an army against
the Turks himself, the papal legate argued it would serve Christendom
better to turn German arms against the "heretic" Bohemian king
instead - a preference Pius's successor pursued even harder, trading
a war against the Ottomans for one against Bohemia.
