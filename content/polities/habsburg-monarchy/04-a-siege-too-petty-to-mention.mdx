Stung by his failure before Vienna, Süleyman spent two years preparing
a second invasion meant to settle Christendom's fate outright rather
than merely punish Ferdinand's rival claim - not just the Austrian
dominions this time, but Germany itself. He marched with a larger army
than he had ever led into Hungary before, and after a march of
fifty-six days reached the country around Belgrade, spreading what
Coxe calls a "direful alarm" through provinces that still carried the
fresh memory of the last invasion's devastated fields and captured
families. Ferdinand tried negotiation first, sending ambassadors to
Süleyman with rich presents and proposals of peace; the sultan
answered by ordering the ambassadors to follow his camp and await his
pleasure, then embarked his artillery in a flotilla of three thousand
vessels and led his army through the western provinces as though
making for Styria over the mountains.

## Eight hundred men at Güns

His route brought him to a small, badly fortified town called Güns,
garrisoned by only eight hundred men under Nicholas Jurišić - and there
his advance stopped for nearly a month. The Turkish army planted
artillery on the surrounding hills and even on earthworks raised above
the town's own rooftops, breached the walls repeatedly, and assaulted
the breaches again and again; Jurišić refused every bribe, promise and
threat alike, and after twenty-eight days Süleyman accepted a token
submission rather than press a siege that a garrison of eight hundred
had already cost him a month to fail to finish.

## Christendom together for once

The delay before Güns gave Ferdinand and Charles exactly the time they
needed. Charles had just pacified Germany's own religious disputes
with the peace concluded at Ratisbon on 23 July 1532, and turned that
settlement immediately to military account, drawing Catholic and
Protestant princes alike into a common muster with a unanimity Coxe
calls almost unexampled. Charles brought veterans from the Low
Countries and Italy, Ferdinand raised troops from Bohemia and the
neighbouring provinces, the pope sent subsidies and officers, and even
King Sigismund of Poland let his own subjects join the field. The
combined force that gathered under Vienna's walls ran past ninety
thousand foot and thirty thousand horse - the flower of Germany, in
Coxe's phrase, from the Vistula to the Rhine.

Süleyman, discouraged by how long so small a place as Güns had held
him and alarmed by the size and unanimity of the army now waiting for
him at Vienna, gave up his design on the capital outright. Separated
from his own artillery flotilla, which dared not run the guns of
Pressburg a second time, he turned his irregular cavalry loose to
raid the countryside and crossed the mountains to Graz instead,
avoiding the very engagement his own massive preparations had seemed
built for. Charles, judging correctly that winter would finish what
he chose not to risk in battle, kept his army at Vienna and let the
season do the work; a diversion by Andrew Doria's fleet against
Ottoman positions in the Aegean, including the capture of Coron,
added pressure from the opposite end of Süleyman's own empire.

## An army too useful to keep together

Süleyman withdrew in September 1532 by the same route he had advanced
on, and his retreating irregulars, cut off from the main army's
protection, were cut to pieces or captured almost to the last man. The
retreat opened a genuine opportunity to recover Hungary outright, but
the army capable of doing it evaporated almost as quickly as it had
assembled: winter approached, provisions ran short, disease spread
through the camp, and Charles himself cared more about convening a
general council to settle Germany's religious quarrel than about
finishing a Hungarian campaign. The great combined host dispersed, and
Ferdinand was left, within a season, with barely more than his own
forces - to face a rival king still very much in the field, and a
sultan who had lost a battle of nerve rather than a war.
