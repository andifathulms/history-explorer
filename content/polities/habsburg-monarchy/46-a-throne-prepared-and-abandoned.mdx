While Frederick wandered Germany as a landless exile, he pursued a
diplomatic project that would ultimately matter more to his family's
future than anything he lost to Matthias: a marriage between his son
Maximilian and Mary, heiress of Burgundy - by then the single richest
territorial bloc in Western Europe, stretching from the borders of
Provence to the shores of the North Sea through generations of French-
descended dukes who had accumulated Flanders, Artois, Holland,
Brabant and a dozen other provinces through purchase, inheritance and
outright extortion. Duke Charles the Bold, ambitious enough to have
already schemed unsuccessfully for the German crown itself, offered
Frederick a different bargain: he would give his only daughter and
heiress to Maximilian if Frederick would elevate the Duchy of Burgundy
into a kingdom in its own right.

## A ceremony that never happened

Frederick, dazzled by the prospect of the match, traveled to Trier
with the fourteen-year-old Maximilian, formally invested Charles with
several imperial territories, and watched Charles prepare every
regalia for a royal coronation - a throne built in the cathedral, the
young princess already affianced to Maximilian. Then, in the middle of
this apparent harmony, Frederick abruptly left Trier under the flimsy
excuse of a dispute in Cologne that needed his attention. The real
cause, Coxe explains, was French interference: King Louis XI had
worked on Frederick's own naturally suspicious temperament, warning
that a crowned Charles would never be satisfied with a mere royal
title and would soon revive his old ambitions for the imperial crown
itself. Neither party fully trusted the other to begin with - Charles
was reluctant to commit his daughter, courted as she was by half the
princes of Europe, and Frederick was equally unwilling to grant the
crown before the marriage was actually solemnized - and the standoff
collapsed the whole arrangement without a wedding or a coronation.

## An uneasy alliance, quietly bought off

Charles, humiliated by the failure, found an outlet for his anger in
German politics instead, marching sixty thousand men into the
Archbishopric of Cologne to support an ousted elector and besieging
the town of Neuss. Frederick, roused to unusual energy, assembled an
imperial army, took the field in person, and joined a coalition
against Burgundy alongside France, Tyrol, the Swiss and Lorraine - only
to abandon his own allies almost immediately once Charles offered him
two hundred thousand crowns and revived the prospect of the Maximilian
match. The two men met privately, agreed to submit their disputes to
papal arbitration, and left the larger coalition against Burgundy to
fight on without its most powerful member.
