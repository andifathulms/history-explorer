Joseph II had spent fifteen years as his mother's co-regent chafing
against a share of power that left him touring Europe's arsenals,
courts and charitable institutions while real authority stayed in
Maria Theresa's own hands. When he inherited the monarchy outright in
1780, he moved to remake it faster than any Habsburg before him: an
Edict of Toleration extending legal protection to Protestants and
Orthodox Christians in Catholic-only hereditary lands, and full civic
rights to Jewish subjects previously barred from most trades and
professions; the abolition of serfdom across the Bohemian and Austrian
lands, converting peasants bound to their lords' own soil into free
subjects who owed rent rather than labor and person; the dissolution
of contemplative monasteries whose revenue he redirected to
parish priests and charitable institutions; German imposed as the
single administrative language across a monarchy that had never had
one; and a codified, more humane criminal law that abolished torture
and sharply limited capital punishment.

## Reform issued faster than it could be enforced

Joseph's own method undid much of what his reforms might have
achieved. Frederick the Great's own verdict, which Coxe quotes with
evident agreement, called his rival's mind "a confused magazine of
despatches, decrees, and projects" - laws issued before the machinery
to enforce them existed, then revised or reversed by the next
courier before the first had taken effect. Hungary's own nobility,
whose privileges and Latin administrative language Joseph overrode
without ever having himself crowned with St. Stephen's crown, resisted
almost every one of his edicts outright; the Austrian Netherlands,
whose own constitutional liberties he dismantled with the same
impatience he brought to Vienna's bureaucracy, rose in open revolt in
1789 and drove his own garrisons out of Brussels within months.

## A revolt he could not put down

The Netherlands rebellion broke Joseph as much as any military defeat
could have. Already weakened by tuberculosis and by a costly, largely
fruitless war against the Ottomans undertaken more from personal
rivalry with Catherine of Russia than from any Austrian interest, he
wept openly on hearing Brussels was lost, and spent his final months
watching every power he might have appealed to for help - Prussia,
England, the Dutch Republic - decline to lift a hand for a ruler each
had reason to see humbled. He died on 20 February 1790, not yet fifty,
reportedly composing his own epitaph in the final weeks: "Here lies a
sovereign, who with the best intentions never carried a single project
into execution." Twice widowed and without a surviving child, he was
succeeded by his younger brother Leopold, who inherited a monarchy
whose Netherlands provinces were in open revolt, whose Hungarian
nobility was in a state of organized refusal, and whose Turkish war
was still unfinished - the practical wreckage of a decade spent trying
to remake, by decree, what Maria Theresa's own reign had built more
slowly and more durably.
