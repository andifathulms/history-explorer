Even as Maximilian fought France abroad, his own authority in the
Netherlands kept eroding at home. Foreign garrisons, the misconduct of
German troops he had brought in, and fresh demands for money on top of
what he already refused to account for turned discontent into open
insurrection, nowhere more so than in Ghent. When the people of Bruges
summoned him, he went in person, trusting a governor's promise that
thousands of loyal burghers stood ready to back him. Instead, the
morning after his ceremonial entry on 9 February 1488, word arrived
that Ghent's own rebels had taken Ypres and Courtrai; as Maximilian
tried to leave the city, the population rose as one, seized the gates
and market square, and demanded he dismiss his foreign troops and hand
over the counselors they accused of corruption.

## A city in revolt against its own king

A false rumor that an avenging army was already marching on Bruges
tipped the crowd into open fury: they rang the alarm bell, raised the
banner of Brabant, and very nearly stormed the palace to kill
Maximilian outright before their own leaders talked them down. The
next day, further messengers from Ghent and a bungled escape attempt
by Maximilian's own loyalists reignited the crisis, and when Maximilian
addressed the crowd directly - "Here I am, behold me ready to live and
die with you" - the majority dragged him instead to an apothecary's
shop and held him there for days while torturing, beheading, or
exiling his own ministers in the market square outside. Ghent executed
thirteen more of his supporters and, together with Bruges, formally
stripped him of his son's guardianship, setting up a new government in
Philip's name with the King of France recognized as its overlord.
Moved afterward to a nobleman's house and confined like a common
criminal, Maximilian was at least spared delivery into French hands by
the people of Bruges themselves, who resisted Ghent's own demands to
hand him over - and throughout the ordeal, Coxe records, he bore
himself with a courage and dignity that did more than any army to
cool his captors' own rage.
