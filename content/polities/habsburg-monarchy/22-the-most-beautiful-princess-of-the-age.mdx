Trieste's gain was soon overshadowed by a much larger disappointment.
Louis the Great, King of Hungary and Poland, had no sons, and planned
to marry his two daughters into a settlement that would keep both
crowns close to his own family: Maria to a Bohemian prince, and
Hedwig, raised partly at Leopold's own court in Vienna, to Leopold's
son William. The young couple were formally betrothed at Buda, and
by every account genuinely devoted to each other - William, elegant
enough to earn the nickname "the Delightful," and Hedwig no less eager
to bring him the Polish crown as her dowry. Louis died before the
marriage could take place, and Hedwig was crowned queen of Poland in
her own right; almost immediately, Jogaila, Grand Duke of Lithuania,
offered to convert to Christianity, unite his own vast duchy with
Poland, and marry her instead. Hedwig refused outright, declaring
publicly that she would marry no one but the Duke of Austria - but the
Polish nobles and clergy, dazzled by the prospect of annexing Lithuania
and converting an entire pagan nation at a stroke, overruled her and
accepted Jogaila's offer regardless of what their own queen wanted.

## A door closed in Kraków

William hurried to Kraków with a splendid retinue and a fortune in
treasure, only to be refused any audience with Hedwig by the court
faction that favored Jogaila; every attempt the two of them made to
meet and marry in secret was blocked, and William eventually gave up
and left Poland for good in 1386, just before Jogaila arrived, was
baptized under the name Władysław, married Hedwig in his place, and
took the Polish throne beside her. The loss struck Leopold harder than
the failed Italian war had - already drained financially and worn down
by both disappointments, he sank into a depression that left him
unable to govern, and his own bailiffs and feudal officers, freed from
his oversight, turned to open extortion across his Swabian territories.

## An old grievance revives

The resulting unrest gave Leopold's oldest enemies their opening.
Forty Swabian towns renewed an old defensive league against princely
oppression, drew in Strasbourg, Mainz and the other Rhine cities, and
courted the Swiss confederacy as natural allies against Austria. New
tolls at Rapperswil and Rothenburg, and Leopold's own meddling in a
private dispute between Bern and a collateral Habsburg cousin,
confirmed the Swiss cantons' old suspicions; Bern, Zurich, Zug and the
town of Solothurn joined the Swabian league outright. Leopold, roused
from his despondency by the threat, managed to talk several cantons
back out of the alliance and calm the Swabian cities through a mix of
concession and threat - but the peace was only ever partial. When his
own pledged lords resumed their old oppressions in the territories he
had mortgaged to them, the people of Entlibuch appealed to Lucerne
instead of their own sovereign, and Lucerne's citizens, already
aggrieved by Austrian tolls, tore down an Austrian castle and admitted
the rebellious district - along with the towns of Sempach and
Richensee - into their own citizenship. Leopold, watching his own
subjects defect town by town, prepared at last for the war he had
spent a decade trying to avoid.
