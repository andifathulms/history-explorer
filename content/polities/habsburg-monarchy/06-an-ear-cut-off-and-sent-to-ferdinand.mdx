The 1545 truce bought time rather than peace. Within Martinuzzi's own
regency government, rivalry between the bishop and his fellow guardian
Petrovics, compounded by Ottoman exactions the country could no longer
absorb, pushed Martinuzzi himself toward the enemy he had spent a
decade resisting on Isabella's behalf. In 1550, backed by his own
partisans and an Austrian corps under General Castaldo, he pressed
Isabella - partly by persuasion and partly by force - into ceding
Transylvania and John Sigismund's Hungarian possessions to Ferdinand
outright, taking the Silesian duchies of Oppeln and Ratibor and a cash
payment of 100,000 ducats in exchange, and surrendering the crown and
regalia the Zápolya family had kept since Süleyman handed them over at
Buda. Ferdinand rewarded Martinuzzi with a cardinal's hat, the
archbishopric of Gran, and the governorship of Transylvania itself.

## A colleague turned accuser

The reward carried its own poison. Ferdinand split military authority
between Martinuzzi and Castaldo, a Spanish general whose own haughty
temper matched neither the cardinal's influence nor his methods, and
the two men's rivalry curdled quickly: Martinuzzi used his own
standing to obstruct Castaldo's command, while Castaldo fed Ferdinand
a steady account of a minister he suspected - or claimed to suspect -
of secretly angling to hand Transylvania back to the Turks. Ferdinand,
already wary of a subject whose ability he could not match with trust,
concluded there was no safer remedy than to have him killed. Castaldo
had the cardinal's own secretary carry out the killing, visiting
Martinuzzi's residence at Vinţu under the cover of ordinary business
on 19 December 1551; when the first knife-blow to the neck failed to
kill him, Martinuzzi fought back and wrestled his attacker to the
ground before the rest of the party burst in, cut him down with a
sword, and mutilated the body further - and, in a detail Coxe records
without comment, cut off an ear notable for a distinctive tuft of hair
and sent it to Ferdinand as confirmation the deed was done.

## The province the murder was supposed to secure

The killing achieved the opposite of its purpose. No manifesto
Ferdinand issued to justify it could reconcile Transylvania's nobles
to the loss of the one governor capable of holding the province
together, and only the presence of the Austrian army kept outright
rebellion from breaking out immediately. Isabella and her son returned
within years, backed by a Turkish army under the pasha of Belgrade,
and by 1556 the Zápolya family was restored to power in Transylvania
under Ottoman protection - Ferdinand keeping only the fortress of
Erlau and a strip of territory east of the Tisza for his own trouble.
Isabella herself, no happier under Ottoman tutelage than she had been
under Ferdinand's pressure, opened talks to cede the province a second
time, but died in 1559 before any agreement was reached; her son John
Sigismund chose Ottoman protection over a Habsburg settlement instead,
demanding the royal title, the disputed district between the Tisza and
Transylvania, and the Silesian duchies his mother had once accepted in
exchange for all of it.

## An old man's truce

Three more years of raiding followed before Ferdinand, wanting a
settled peace above all to secure his own eldest son Maximilian's
succession, obtained a fresh eight-year truce from Süleyman in 1562 -
resuming the annual tribute, promising not to disturb John Sigismund's
own hold on Transylvania, and keeping only the ground his own troops
already occupied. John Sigismund refused to be bound by an agreement
he had no part in making, and kept raiding Habsburg Hungary regardless;
Ferdinand, unwilling to risk reopening the wider war over what
remained a marginal frontier dispute, absorbed the losses and left the
frontier's real settlement to the son whose own accession the truce
had been built to protect.
