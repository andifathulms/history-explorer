Spain's own throne fell vacant in 1700 when Charles II died without
an heir, and Leopold I answered the resulting Bourbon claim by
pressing his own family's rival right through his second son, the
Archduke Charles - a dispute that widened almost immediately into a
European war, since neither England nor the Dutch Republic would
accept a single Bourbon crown spanning both France and Spain. Charles
himself, not yet eighteen, was proclaimed King of Spain at Vienna in
1703 and shipped off through England on his way to the Iberian
peninsula; Queen Anne received him at Windsor for three days, and a
contemporary account Coxe quotes admired a gravity "beyond his age,
tempered with much modesty" in a young man who managed to seem
pleased with everything at court without smiling once. The war that
followed his own claim ground on across the Netherlands, Germany,
Italy and Spain itself for a decade, carried in the north chiefly by
the Duke of Marlborough's own generalship and in the empire's own name
by Prince Eugene of Savoy, without ever fully settling the Spanish
succession Charles had gone to Madrid to claim.

## An emperor gained, a Spanish crown lost

Leopold's own death in 1705 passed the imperial title and the
Austrian hereditary lands to his elder son Joseph I, leaving Charles
to fight on as the Austrian claimant to Spain alone. Joseph's reign
lasted barely six years, cut short by smallpox in 1711, and left no
son to inherit - only two daughters, and a brother still contesting a
Spanish throne an ocean away. The war's own logic collapsed the moment
Charles became the only plausible heir to the whole Habsburg
inheritance: the same coalition that had gone to war to stop one
family uniting France and Spain now balked at uniting the empire and
Spain under Charles instead, and English support for his Spanish claim
evaporated almost overnight. Charles inherited Austria, Bohemia and
Hungary as Charles VI, and the Peace of Utrecht in 1713 settled Spain
itself on the Bourbon claimant his own war had been fought to prevent
- leaving Charles with the empire his brother's death had handed him,
and the Southern Netherlands and Habsburg Italy as compensation for a
Spanish throne he had spent a decade trying to reach in person and
never sat on for more than a few contested years.
