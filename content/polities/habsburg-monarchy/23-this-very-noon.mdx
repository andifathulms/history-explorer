War broke out in earnest after the Austrians retook and burned
Richensee, killing every inhabitant without regard for age or sex,
and both sides mobilized quickly - twenty separate declarations of war
arrived against the confederates in a single day. Leopold massed his
army at Baden and sent a diversionary force toward Zurich to mask his
real target, Lucerne, but the Forest Cantons saw through the feint and
marched thirteen hundred men to Sempach instead, gathering
reinforcements from Glarus and the surrounding villages along the way.
Leopold reached Sursee the evening before, and at dawn on 9 July 1386
advanced on Sempach with four thousand horse and fourteen hundred foot,
confident enough of an easy victory that his own troops rode up to the
town walls to taunt the defenders with a mock breakfast invitation for
the "reapers" working the fields outside. The burgomaster answered
simply: "My masters of Lucern, and their allies, will bring it."

## "This very noon"

Surprised to find the confederates already drawn up in the woods above
the lake, Leopold called a council to weigh waiting for reinforcements
- and was shouted down by his own nobles, who declared it shameful to
delay against what they dismissed as an "ill-armed and almost naked
rabble." One veteran who urged caution was mocked as a coward by a
younger knight who bragged the peasants would be delivered up "this
very noon." The knights dismounted in ground too rough for cavalry,
formed a dense phalanx bristling with spears, and advanced; the Swiss,
by their custom, knelt first in prayer, prompting some Austrians to
mistake the gesture for a plea for mercy before the confederates
surged down out of the trees in a wedge formation that broke against
the Austrian line without effect - the Lucerne banner nearly lost, and
sixty of the attackers' best fighters dead before a single Austrian
fell.

## Arnold von Winkelried

At that critical moment, a knight of Unterwalden named Arnold von
Winkelried broke from the Swiss ranks. "I will open a passage into the
line," he is said to have called out. "Protect, dear countrymen and
confederates, my wife and children!" He threw himself onto the
Austrian spears, gathered as many as he could reach in his own arms,
and bore them down with his weight as he died - and his companions
poured through the gap his body opened. Weighed down by their own
armor and unable to recover formation in the crush, the Austrian
knights broke; their own servants, seeing the battle turning, mounted
their masters' horses and fled, cutting off any hope of escape for the
men they had served. Roughly two thousand Austrians died, nearly a
third of them titled nobility, against perhaps two hundred confederate
dead.

## "I will share your fate"

Leopold fought to the end rather than flee. Urged repeatedly to save
himself, he answered, "God forbid that I should endeavour to save my
own life, and leave you to die! I will share your fate, and in this my
country, and with my own people, I will either conquer or perish!" -
and later, pressed again, "I would rather die honourably than live
with dishonour!" When the knight carrying the Austrian standard fell
and his replacement went down moments later crying "Help! Austria!
help!", Leopold himself seized the blood-soaked banner and raised it
again, rallying his remaining knights for one final charge before he
was cut down by an unknown hand and found afterward beneath a heap of
the slain. He was thirty-six, and had reigned, in his own turbulent
fashion, for twenty years - a prince Coxe judges better suited to
knight-errantry than to the sober duties of government, whose
restless ambition to reclaim everything his ancestors had ever held
finally cost him everything he actually possessed. His body and those
of twenty-seven of his noblest followers were carried to the family's
own abbey at Königsfelden; the Swiss buried their two hundred dead with
full honors at Lucerne, and established an annual memorial for the
victory to stand alongside the one already kept for Morgarten seventy
years before.
