The Guelderland question, seemingly settled when Duke Albert of Saxony
crushed the young pretender Charles of Egmont's own revolt, reopened
once the four Rhenish electors formally rejected Egmont's claim and
declared the duchy a fief of the empire, restored in 1495 to
Maximilian's son Philip. Egmont accepted the ruling in appearance
only; when the new tax established at Worms provoked real discontent
among his own former subjects, he took up arms again, and with local
support and quiet French backing recovered most of the duchy outright.
Friesland's own people, encouraged by their neighbors' success,
mounted a parallel resistance - arguing their territory had never
actually been a fief of the empire in the first place, refusing to
accept Albert of Saxony as governor, and placing themselves instead
under Guelderland's own protection.

## Two revolts, one exhausted campaign

Albert struggled to suppress both uprisings at once. He took Roermond
and Venlo, but was forced to abandon the siege of Nijmegen and settled
for a six-month armistice in December 1498. Once his own dispute with
France was finally resolved, Maximilian marched into Guelderland
himself to press the war further, but found Egmont's French backing
too strong to overcome, and abandoned the campaign in hopes of better
results in Switzerland instead. The dukes of Cleves and Jülich, left
in charge once Maximilian departed, negotiated their own peace under
Louis XII's mediation that let Egmont keep most of the duchy he had
already reclaimed - another regional conflict that ended not in
victory, but in a settlement France had quietly shaped from the start.
