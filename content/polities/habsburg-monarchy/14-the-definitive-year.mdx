Wallenstein's assassination and Gustavus's death did not shorten a war
that had already outgrown its own original religious quarrel. Ferdinand
III, who succeeded his father in 1637 with a reputation for mildness
and prudence Coxe judges better suited to peace than to war, spent his
own reign managing a contest that had become a straightforward
struggle between the Bourbon and Habsburg dynasties, fought increasingly
by Swedish and French armies on German soil for stakes that had little
left to do with the Bohemian succession that started it. Sweden's own
armies reached the outskirts of Vienna and besieged Prague in the
war's final years; France, allied with Sweden since Richelieu's own
intervention, fought as much to weaken the Habsburgs generally as to
defend German Protestants in particular. By the 1640s exhaustion had
outpaced ambition on every side, and negotiations opened at two
neighboring Westphalian towns, Osnabrück for the empire's own religious
and internal settlement, Münster for the wider peace with France - a
division that let Catholic and Protestant power in Germany settle its
own account separately from the international one.

## What changed hands

The peace signed in 1648 redrew both the map and the rulebook.
France gained sovereignty over Alsace and confirmed its hold on Metz,
Toul and Verdun; Sweden took Western Pomerania and a cash indemnity,
along with three votes in the imperial diet; Brandenburg, Bavaria,
Mecklenburg and Hesse-Kassel each received territorial compensation
for their own losses or services; and the Palatinate was split, its
restored branch given a newly created eighth electorate rather than
displacing Bavaria from the one it had held since 1623. Every prince
and state stripped of rights since the Bohemian revolt of 1619 was
formally restored to its prewar standing, and the Swiss Confederation
was released from imperial jurisdiction outright, closing three
centuries of nominal subjection that had been a fiction since Basel's
own day.

## A settlement built on a fixed date

The religious terms mattered more than any territorial line. Calvinists
won the same legal standing Lutherans had held since 1555, ending the
narrower peace that had recognized only two confessions; the disputed
question of secularized church property was settled not by principle
but by a fixed calendar date, 1 January 1624, chosen as the "definitive
year" whose ownership pattern would simply be restored and then frozen
- a compromise that asked neither side to concede the argument, only
to stop refighting it. Ferdinand's own Edict of Restitution, the act
that had reignited the war in 1629, was effectively undone by the same
stroke.

Ferdinand III lived nine more years after the peace he had spent a
decade negotiating toward, working to rebuild imperial authority within
the settlement's own new limits before his death in 1657. His own
successor, Leopold, inherited a Habsburg Monarchy that had held Bohemia,
Hungary and the Austrian lands together through Europe's longest and
costliest religious war, at the price of a Germany now formally,
permanently divided among rival faiths and a France newly established,
by treaty, on the Rhine's own left bank.
