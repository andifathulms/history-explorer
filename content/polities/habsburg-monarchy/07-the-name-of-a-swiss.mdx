Every Habsburg loss to the Swiss cantons over the following seventy
years ran on the same mechanism Morgarten had already shown once:
overconfident nobles, certain that a peasant militia could not stand
against armored cavalry, marched into terrain that neutralized the
cavalry entirely and met a smaller force using the high ground against
them. The pattern repeated at Näfels, it repeated in the countryside
around Sempach, and by the time it had run its course the family's
own Swiss patrimony - towns, tolls, advocacies over abbeys, and a
military reputation two centuries in the making - was gone.

Lucerne went first, worn down by border warfare and factional strife
between Austrian loyalists and everyone else; its citizens secured
imperial protection and a truce with the Swiss cantons, beat back an
Austrian attempt to retake the town by surprise, and joined the
confederacy without repudiating any of Austria's existing rights -
carefully, but joined all the same. Zurich followed through its own
revolution: a popular government under the burgomaster Rudolf Brun
crushed an attempted noble counter-coup backed by a Habsburg kinsman,
and, fearing Duke Albert's revenge, brought the city into the
confederacy itself in 1351. Albert marched sixteen thousand men
against it and won an arbitration on paper, but when he tried to
enforce the harshest terms against the smaller cantons, Schwyz
answered under the same banner that had broken his brother Leopold at
Morgarten a generation before, and Glarus went the same way soon
after - a relief force expelling its Austrian governor in the dead of
winter, then joining the confederacy it had just helped defend.

## An old man's despair, a young man's truce

Albert, aging and crippled by the paralysis that had afflicted him
since his thirties, finally gave up the contest in such disgust that
his own household learned never to mention the Swiss in his presence
again. He died in 1358 having earned, for everything except this one
failure, the name "the Wise." His son Rudolf, by his father's own
measure the most gifted of all the Austrian princes so far, inherited
a war he judged unwinnable the moment he actually looked at the
ground it was being fought on - the mountain terrain gave the
confederates an advantage no cavalry charge could answer - and did
what his father never could: he negotiated a truce instead of
reaching for one more defeat, and held to it faithfully for the rest
of his short reign. It bought the family twenty years of quiet on its
Swiss frontier, and it was the last time for a generation that a
Habsburg duke chose to cut Austria's losses there rather than reopen
a fight the family had already spent decades losing, piece by piece,
to a people its own dukes had once dismissed as rustics.
