Rudolf II inherited an empire Maximilian's own tolerance had left
calm, and spent his reign undoing that calm one concession at a time.
Raised from age twelve at Philip II's own Spanish court and educated
by Jesuits trained to fix rather than open a young mind, he emerged
devoted to his mother's rigid Catholicism, absorbed in alchemy and
judicial astrology rather than government, and unwilling to extend the
informal toleration his own father had allowed Vienna's Protestant
nobility. Where Maximilian had quietly let burghers attend Lutheran
services held for the nobility, Rudolf enforced the letter of the old
law, confined Protestant worship among the nobles and knights to a
room too small for any other audience, and stripped Vienna's most
popular Protestant preacher of his office - lighting, in Austria,
Hungary and above all Bohemia, the same resentment his father had
spent a reign defusing.

## A brother made king in Hungary

Rudolf's own incapacity gave his ambitious younger brother Matthias
the opening his father-in-Habsburg-terms had never had. When Hungary's
own nobility, exhausted by Rudolf's mismanagement of the Ottoman
frontier and his refusal to confirm their privileges, turned openly to
Matthias instead, he accepted their allegiance, negotiated a favorable
peace with the Turks on his own authority, and in 1608 forced Rudolf
to cede him Hungary, Austria and Moravia outright, keeping only
Bohemia and the imperial title for himself. Matthias returned to
Vienna intending to crush the Protestant unrest his own rise had
depended on, but found himself needing Moravian mediation to hold his
new territories together, and on 19 March 1609 confirmed the same
religious privileges for Austria's Protestants that Maximilian had
once granted and Rudolf had revoked.

## The Letter of Majesty

Bohemia's own Protestants, watching their neighbors win by pressure
what petitions had never obtained, organized their own defiance:
a diet convened without royal permission in May 1609, an armed
militia under Count Thurn raised within days, and an alliance with
Silesia's own Protestant estates that put several thousand troops
in the field before Rudolf could respond. Isolated, humiliated, and
unable to summon help from a brother he had already lost most of his
territory to, Rudolf capitulated on 5 July 1609 with the document
history remembers as the Letter of Majesty - full freedom of worship
for anyone receiving communion in both kinds, the right to build
churches and schools, an elected Protestant consistory, and a clause
voiding any future royal act that contradicted it. Bohemia's
Protestants held their arms until Silesia secured the same terms, then
stood down; for a brief interval, Coxe notes, Catholic and Protestant
villages returned to worshipping side by side in something like the
tolerance Maximilian's reign had once made ordinary.

## An abdication torn under his own feet

The concession bought Rudolf no real peace. Matthias, backed by an
army and by Bohemia's own restless nobility, forced the issue a second
time in 1611: Rudolf, on hearing his envoys had failed to hold Prague
for him, was said to have flung open his own window and cried,
"Prague, unthankful Prague, who hast been so highly elevated by me;
now thou spurnest at thy benefactor." He refused for as long as he
could to sign the instrument releasing his own subjects from their
oath of allegiance, and when finally compelled, blotted the document
and snapped the pen underfoot rather than complete it cleanly. Matthias
was crowned King of Bohemia on 23 May 1611 under guard, in a city
whose gates stayed shut against any last attempt on Rudolf's own
behalf. Rudolf lived barely a year longer, dying in Prague in January
1612 in the sixtieth year of his life, reportedly welcoming death as a
release from "the calamities of human nature" after a reign that had
converted his father's hard-won religious balance into the standing
grievance the whole House of Austria would spend the next three
decades fighting over.
