Leopold arrived in Vienna expecting to take sole charge of Austria and
the young Albert V's guardianship, only to find his own brothers
Ernest and Frederick contesting the arrangement; forced to compromise,
he accepted Ernest - by Coxe's account the most violent and ambitious
of the three - as a co-regent he had never wanted. The result was
exactly what a shared, contested guardianship over a distant child-heir
usually produces: two rival factions formed around the two regents,
power swung back and forth between them as each gained temporary
advantage, and both used every swing to enrich themselves and punish
the other's supporters through what Coxe calls "a species of legal
tyranny" - confiscations, exactions, and the settling of scores
dressed up as governance.

## A country left to banditry

With no strong hand actually running the country, the nobility
reverted to the lawlessness earlier Habsburg dukes had spent
generations suppressing. Robbers and bandits again infested the roads,
towns and villages were plundered with impunity, and Austria, in
Coxe's judgment, fell into a state of disorder worse than at any point
since the house of Habsburg had first taken possession of it more than
a century earlier - the direct, predictable cost of a family that had
spent thirty years dividing and subdividing its own inheritance until
no single ruler held enough authority to keep the peace. Leopold, "the
Big" for his imposing height and "the Courtly" for his polished
manners, was remembered as a genuine patron of scholars even amid the
chaos of his own regency - but the chaos itself never really lifted
during his lifetime. He died in 1411, forty years old and childless,
leaving Austria's disorder to whichever of his brothers, or his own
young ward Albert V, could finally impose order on a family that had
spent a decade proving it could not govern itself.
