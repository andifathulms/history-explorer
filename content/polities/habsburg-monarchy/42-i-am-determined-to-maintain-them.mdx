The gratitude Frederick owed George of Poděbrady for breaking the
siege of Vienna lasted only a few years before papal politics and
Frederick's own instinct for playing rivals against each other pulled
him into an alliance against his own rescuer. Pope Pius II, who as a
cardinal had helped negotiate the compacts protecting Bohemia's
Calixtin majority, reversed course completely once he held the papal
tiara himself, reviving the church's old claim to total authority over
Bohemian religious practice. Poděbrady, pressed by his own kingdom's
Catholic minority, banished the more radical Hussite sects and
required outward conformity to Catholic doctrine - but when the
Catholics demanded he go further and annul the compacts entirely, and
Pius refused even to reconfirm an agreement his own predecessors and
the Council of Basel had already approved, Poděbrady sent an embassy
to Rome that returned empty-handed.

## A king defies a legate

Summoning a diet to address the failed embassy, Poděbrady spoke
plainly: the compacts were the only thing that had ended Bohemia's
own religious civil war, they contained no heresy, they had been
approved by the most learned council in Europe and confirmed by an
earlier pope, and "I and my wife have followed them from our
childhood, and I am determined to maintain them till my death." When
the papal legate Fantino della Valle answered with a bitter tirade,
Poděbrady had him thrown out of the assembly and imprisoned on bread
and water. Pius responded by annulling the compacts outright in 1463
and threatening excommunication unless the king traveled to Rome to
answer for himself - a crisis defused only when Poděbrady released the
legate and apologized, and Frederick, still mindful of his own debt
from the Vienna siege, personally interceded with the pope to suspend
the sentence.

## A debt forgotten

Frederick's gratitude did not survive Pius's own death in 1464. His
successor, Paul II, pursued Poděbrady with even greater hostility -
inciting Catholic unrest in Silesia, trying and failing to bribe the
King of Poland into claiming the Bohemian crown himself, and finally
wearing down Frederick's own resistance through a mix of threats and
promises. By 1467 imperial ambassadors were openly backing a papal
call for a crusade against Bohemia at the diet of Nuremberg; though
the diet itself refused the proposal, the pope formally deposed
Poděbrady anyway and let crusade preachers operate freely across
German and Austrian territory. Poděbrady, understandably furious at
being betrayed by the very ally his own troops had once saved from
starvation, denounced Frederick's ingratitude in 1468 and declared war
outright, opening a fresh invasion of Austria that reached as far as
the Danube itself.
