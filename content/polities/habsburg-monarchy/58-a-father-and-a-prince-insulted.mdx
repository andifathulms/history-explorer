The Swabian League's newfound authority let Frederick settle a
personal grievance he could never have pressed alone. In 1485 Albert
of Munich seized Regensburg, a city his own ancestors had once held
before Frederick Barbarossa raised it to imperial status centuries
earlier, and - without so much as informing Frederick, let alone
asking his consent - married the emperor's own daughter Cunegunda,
securing through her a promised claim on the reversion of Tyrol as
her dowry. Frederick, insulted twice over as both a slighted father
and a defied sovereign, threatened Albert and his Tyrolean accomplice
with the imperial ban - but the threat carried no real weight, since
Frederick was at that very moment an exile driven from his own
territory by the King of Hungary, and Albert's own contempt for his
"unwarlike and indolent" reputation was, in Coxe's telling, entirely
justified by events.

## Mediated, broken, and settled again

Maximilian, genuinely fond of his sister and openly admiring of his
new brother-in-law's own spirit, brokered a first reconciliation:
Albert agreed to give up his claim on Tyrol, promised to return
Regensburg to the empire, and even fought loyally for Frederick in the
Netherlands campaign that followed. The peace held only until Albert
simply refused to honor his own promise to restore the city, at which
point Frederick, now backed by the full strength of the empire and
the Swabian League, published the ban against both Albert and
Regensburg outright. An army of twenty thousand infantry and twenty-
six hundred cavalry, more than a third of it supplied by the league
itself, assembled with unusual speed under Brandenburg's own command
and advanced to the Lech - and once again it was Maximilian's own
mediation, not the army's presence, that actually settled the
dispute. Albert restored Regensburg, reaffirmed his renunciation of
Tyrol, and received a cash settlement and a minor lordship in
compensation; he then brought his wife and three young daughters to
visit Frederick at Linz, where the aging emperor, his own health
failing, found real comfort in a reconciliation that finally brought
both family peace and public order to his final years.
