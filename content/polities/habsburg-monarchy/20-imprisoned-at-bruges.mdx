---
title: Imprisoned at Bruges
drafted_from: coxe-austria-1847

Even as Maximilian fought France abroad, his own authority in the
Netherlands kept eroding at home. Foreign garrisons, the misconduct of
German troops he had brought in, and fresh demands for money on top of
what he already refused to account for turned discontent into open
insurrection, nowhere more so than in Ghent. When the people of Bruges
summoned him, he went in person, trusting a governor's promise that
thousands of loyal burghers stood ready to back him. Instead, the
morning after his ceremonial entry on 9 February 1488, word arrived
that Ghent's own rebels had taken Ypres and Courtrai; as Maximilian
tried to leave the city, the population rose as one, seized the gates
and market square, and demanded he dismiss his foreign troops and hand
over the counselors they accused of corruption.

A false rumor that an avenging army was already marching on Bruges
tipped the crowd into open fury: they rang the alarm bell, raised the
banner of Brabant, and very nearly stormed the palace to kill
Maximilian outright before their own leaders talked them down. When
Maximilian addressed the crowd directly the next day - "Here I am,
behold me ready to live and die with you" - the majority dragged him
instead to an apothecary's shop and held him there for days while
torturing, beheading or exiling his own ministers in the market square
outside. Ghent executed thirteen more of his supporters and, together
with Bruges, formally stripped him of his son's guardianship, setting
up a new government in Philip's name with the King of France
recognized as its overlord. Moved afterward to a nobleman's house and
confined like a common criminal, Maximilian was at least spared
delivery into French hands by the people of Bruges themselves, who
resisted Ghent's own demands to hand him over - and throughout the
ordeal, Coxe records, he bore himself with a courage and dignity that
did more than any army to cool his captors' rage.

## No trace of resentment

Neighboring provinces petitioned for his release, but it was Frederick
who finally forced the issue - abandoning, for once, the indolence
that had defined most of his own reign to fight for his son with real
urgency. He assembled fifteen thousand men at Cologne within weeks and
pushed into Brabant, terrifying the Flemings into releasing Maximilian
on 16 May 1488, though only after extracting a renewed promise to
honor the Treaty of Arras in full and withdraw every foreign soldier
from the country.

Maximilian showed no anger at any of it. He went straight to church to
give thanks, telling the crowd outside, "We are now at peace," then
visited the very shop where his captivity had begun and read an
insulting inscription scrawled on its walls without any visible
reaction, forgiving the people who now knelt before him begging
pardon. Back with his father, he quietly talked Frederick out of
further war against the rebels; when Frederick pressed on regardless,
besieging Ghent for three months before handing the campaign off and
returning home, it was diplomacy rather than the siege that finally
closed the conflict. King Charles VIII, eager to pursue his own
designs on a newly vulnerable Brittany, concluded the Peace of
Frankfurt in July 1489 - restoring Breton conquests, confirming the
Arras settlement, and pressing the Flemish rebels into finally
accepting Maximilian's guardianship of his own son.
