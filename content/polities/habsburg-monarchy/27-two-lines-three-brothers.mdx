Albert IV left a seven-year-old son, Albert V, and William - his
father's own co-regent and cousin - took over as guardian and governed
Austria with real competence until his own death in 1406, two years
later, childless. It was that second death, not the first, that broke
the family apart. Charles IV's old prediction that the Habsburgs had
humbled themselves by dividing their own inheritance now played out in
full: the house split permanently into an Albertine line, holding
Austria itself for the young Albert V, and a Leopoldine line descended
from Leopold III, which further split three ways among his surviving
sons - Leopold the elder keeping Switzerland, Alsace and Swabia,
Ernest taking Styria, Carinthia and Carniola, and Frederick receiving
Tyrol. Four separate Habsburg administrations now governed what had
once been, however imperfectly, a single family patrimony.

## A prisoner in Italy

Leopold the elder had already had his own costly adventure abroad
before any of this. Where his cousins William and Albert had backed
the House of Luxembourg in imperial politics, Leopold sided with a
rival claimant to the German throne, was rewarded with a free passage
through Tyrol and joined him on an Italian campaign meant to avenge
Leopold's own father-in-law, deposed and driven into exile by the Duke
of Milan. The campaign ended in disaster: Milanese troops routed the
whole army at Lake Garda in October 1401, and Leopold himself was
captured, buying his freedom only by promising to abandon the fight
and go home. He returned to ordinary administration of his own
territories without further incident - until William's sudden death
in 1406 called him back to Vienna to take up the guardianship of young
Albert V, and a share of Austria, in his cousin's place.
