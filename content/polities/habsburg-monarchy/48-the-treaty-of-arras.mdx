Mary of Burgundy's own death on 26 March 1482, still young and
leaving two infant children, Philip and Margaret, undid much of what
her marriage to Maximilian had secured. Flanders's own turbulent
population, the most powerful of all the Burgundian provinces, had
never fully accepted Maximilian as anything but a foreign consort, and
once Mary was gone they treated his continued war against France as a
purely personal ambition rather than a defense of their own interests.
The Flemish estates seized custody of the young Philip, appointed his
guardians themselves, and shut Maximilian out of his own son's
upbringing entirely - a dispute Louis XI exploited immediately by
negotiating directly with the Flemish states over Maximilian's head.

## A daughter as dowry

The resulting Treaty of Arras, concluded on 23 December 1482, forced
terms on Maximilian he had no real power to refuse: his infant
daughter Margaret was betrothed to the French dauphin and sent to
France as his intended bride, carrying Franche-Comté, Artois and
several lesser lordships as her dowry, with the territories reverting
to Philip only if the marriage failed to take place or Margaret died
without heirs. Louis promised in return to restore captured territory
in Luxembourg and stop backing Maximilian's other enemies, while
Margaret's own education passed to the French court and Philip's
guardianship remained with the Flemish estates alone. Louis died soon
after, and his young son ascended as Charles VIII under his sister's
own regency - leaving Maximilian's own daughter being raised as a
future queen of France, and Maximilian himself formally excluded from
governing the province his own marriage had won for his family.
