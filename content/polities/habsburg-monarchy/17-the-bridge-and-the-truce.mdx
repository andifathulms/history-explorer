Rudolf, born in 1339 and raised under his father's own close
supervision in both arms and learning, was Coxe's pick for the most
gifted of all the Austrian princes so far - "first in virtues and in
talents, as he was first in birth." He received the Austrian estates'
homage as his father's co-administrator at sixteen, married the
Emperor Charles IV's own daughter in 1359, and took charge of the
family's Swabian and Alsatian territories from a residence near
Schaffhausen, where he governed with a maturity well beyond his years
- keeping the peace, suppressing banditti, protecting trade, and
building a wooden bridge across the southern end of Lake Zurich nearly
a mile long, a structure contemporaries considered one of the wonders
of the age.

## Ending a war his father could not win

When Albert finally abandoned the Swiss campaign in disgust and
withdrew to Vienna, broken by illness and despair, Rudolf took sole
charge of the family's affairs at the front - and did what his father
never could: recognizing both the Swiss cantons' courage and the
natural advantages of their mountain terrain, he judged the war
unwinnable and negotiated a truce instead of pursuing yet another
defeat. He held to that truce faithfully for the rest of his short
reign, a rare instance of a Habsburg prince choosing to cut Austria's
losses in Switzerland rather than reopen a fight the family had been
losing for half a century. On his father's death in 1358 he moved the
family seat to Vienna and kept a court, by Coxe's account, splendid
enough to rival any in Europe - the first sign of an ambition that
would, within a few years, reach far beyond what any previous Habsburg
duke had dared to claim.
