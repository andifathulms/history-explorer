---
title: A scattered empire, easy prey
drafted_from: lingelbach-austria-hungary-1910

Francis II inherited, at twenty-four, a monarchy second only to Russia
and France in size, but scattered across six historically distinct
blocs of territory - the Austrian and Bohemian hereditary lands, the
Kingdom of Hungary with Croatia, Slavonia and Transylvania, Galicia,
the Milanese and Mantuan districts cut off from everything else by
Venetian territory, and the Austrian Netherlands standing entirely
alone on the far side of Germany. That scattering, rather than any
single weakness in its own army or treasury, made the monarchy what
Lingelbach calls "an easy prey to an enemy": whichever outlying
province an opponent could reach first would be the one that fell
first, and the Netherlands, most isolated of all and least attached to
Habsburg rule, proved exactly that.

## The Low Countries lost twice over

War with revolutionary France broke out before Francis had even been
elected emperor - the French declared war on him simply as King of
Bohemia and Hungary - and the fighting bore out the monarchy's own
geography almost immediately. A French victory at Jemmappes in
November 1792 carried the Austrian Low Countries as far as Luxembourg
into French hands; an Austrian counterattack the following spring won
them back only long enough for a second French victory at Fleurus in
June 1794 to take them for good. Prussia, nominally Austria's ally,
gave what Lingelbach calls lukewarm support throughout, more absorbed
in partitioning Poland than in holding the coalition together, and
pulled out of the war entirely in 1795 by signing a separate peace at
Paris - leaving Francis unable even to avenge his own aunt Marie
Antoinette's execution, let alone hold the Netherlands he had already
lost.

## Poland instead of Belgium

Denied any real compensation from France, Francis turned to the one
place still open to Habsburg expansion: what remained of a Poland
Russia and Prussia were already dismantling. The third and final
partition, agreed with both powers, gave Austria the palatinates of
Sandomierz and Lublin along with portions of Kraków, Mazovia,
Podlachia and Brest - territory organized as the new province of
Western Galicia, a substitute Lingelbach frames plainly as
compensation for a Belgian loss no diplomacy had been able to reverse.

## A treasury and a press both mortgaged to the war

Eight years of continuous fighting drained a monarchy that had never
been wealthy to begin with. Francis mortgaged his own private estates
to raise money; paper currency multiplied, forced loans were raised
across every province, and Joseph II's own internal free-trade reforms
were undone as customs barriers went back up between Hungary and the
rest of the Austrian lands. Domestic policy moved in the opposite
direction from his uncle's reforming instincts on nearly every other
front too: press censorship passed from university professors to the
police outright, and a tax on printed matter was added on top of it -
a government that met revolutionary ideas from across its own western
border by tightening exactly the freedoms Joseph had spent a decade
extending. Not every reform stopped: serfdom was abolished in Galicia
in 1799, roads were pushed through Carniola, Croatia, Bohemia and
Moravia, and the legal code Maria Theresa had begun continued to grow
- but Francis, in Lingelbach's own phrase, did nothing at all for
public instruction, judging the risk of educated subjects not worth
the reward.
