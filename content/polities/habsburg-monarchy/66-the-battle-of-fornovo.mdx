Germany's own paralysis turned out not to matter as much as
Maximilian feared: Charles VIII abandoned Naples almost as fast as he
had taken it, the moment he learned of the league forming against him
and, worse, Ludovico Sforza's own defection to it. Leaving a viceroy
with six thousand men to hold what he could, Charles marched north
with eight thousand troops on 20 May, passing again through a Rome
now abandoned by the Pope and his cardinals, and on through Siena and
Florence to Pisa - where he waited a fortnight in vain for the Duke of
Orléans, blockaded by Sforza's own forces at Novara, to join him.
Crossing the Apennines unopposed, he pressed on toward Parma, only to
find the league's own combined army waiting for him at Fornovo, on the
Taro river.

## Outnumbered, but through

Despite commanding the smaller force, Charles attacked on 6 July 1495
and, after a hard-fought engagement, forced his own way through the
confederate line, relieved the siege of Novara, and reached the safety
of Asti. Having secured his own retreat, he made peace with Ludovico
Sforza on the spot - restoring Novara and promising not to back
Orléans's own rival claim to Milan - and continued home to France,
his Italian conquest already unraveling behind him. Naples turned
against its French occupiers almost as soon as Charles was gone: the
same population that had welcomed the French with real enthusiasm
grew disgusted with their occupation, and the exiled King Ferdinand,
returning from Ischia with a body of loyal followers and Spanish
reinforcements under the celebrated commander Gonzalo de Córdoba,
drove the French from their remaining strongholds and reclaimed his
kingdom - though he did not live to enjoy it long, dying that October
without a male heir. Charles, undeterred by the collapse of his first
conquest, was already planning a second Italian expedition before the
year was out, this time aimed squarely at Milan itself.
