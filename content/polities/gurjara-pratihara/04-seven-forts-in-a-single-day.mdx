Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni made a career of raiding Northern India,
leaving his capital each October and marching three months into the
richest interior provinces before returning home laden with plunder -
seventeen such expeditions in all, by Smith's count. Early in January
1019 he appeared before Kanauj itself. Rajyapala, whose confederacy
against the Ghaznavids had already been broken once at the Kurram
valley, "made no serious attempt to defend his capital," and the
seven forts guarding the city all fell to Mahmud in a single day.
Mahmud plundered the forts, spared the city itself, and returned
quickly to Ghazni with his booty; Rajyapala made what terms he could,
abandoned Kanauj outright, and retired across the Ganges to Bari.

## A punishment for surrender

Rajyapala's own submission enraged the Hindu allies who had counted
on Kanauj to hold. An army under Vidhyadhara, heir apparent of the
Chandela king Ganda, struck Kanauj that spring or summer, soon after
Mahmud's own departure, and killed Rajyapala for the humiliation of
his surrender - handing his diminished territory to a successor,
Trilochanapala. Mahmud, who regarded Rajyapala as his own vassal, was
furious at the killing and marched back into India that same autumn,
capturing the new Pratihara capital at Bari early in 1020 before
advancing against Ganda's own Chandela territory - where the Chandela
king, exactly as Rajyapala had done, lost his nerve and fled the
field without giving battle, abandoning his camp and elephants to the
Sultan.

## A dynasty that disappeared utterly

Trilochanapala's own reign left little trace beyond an ineffectual
attempt to resist Mahmud's crossing of the Jumna and a single land
grant recorded in 1027; a king named Yasahpala, attested in an
inscription of 1036, may have been his immediate successor. Beyond
that point Smith can name no more Pratihara kings - only "other
obscure chiefs" who continued to be recognized as rajas of Kanauj,
governing in subordination to Muslim overlords, their names surviving
in scattered records from a residence at Zafarabad near Jaunpur. The
old dynasty's own hold on Kanauj itself ended a little before 1090,
when a Gaharwar chief named Chandradeva conquered and occupied the
city and established a new line there. Smith's own verdict on the
transition is unambiguous: the later chiefs "did not belong to the
old Gurjara-Pratihara dynasty, which disappeared utterly" - a line
that had once ruled, under Mihira Bhoja, an empire stretching from the
Punjab to the Arabian Sea, ending not in a final battle but in a slow
fade into obscure, tributary chieftaincy nobody thought worth naming.
