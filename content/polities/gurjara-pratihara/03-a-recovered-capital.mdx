The equilibrium Mihira Bhoja had held broke under his grandson
Mahipala, who took the throne around 910. In 916 the Rashtrakuta king
Indra III's armies captured Kanauj outright - Smith calls it a blow
that "gave a severe blow to the power of the Pratihara dynasty" - and
the loss of Kathiawar and other outlying provinces that Mahipala had
still held two years earlier followed soon after. Indra III's own
forces were not positioned to hold so distant a capital for long,
though, and Mahipala recovered Kanauj with help from the Chandela
king of Jejakabhukti - the same southern neighbour whose own rise the
Pratiharas had watched from a position of strength under Bhoja, now
returning the favour as an ally rather than a subordinate.

## A weakening hand

The recovery did not restore the balance of power the sack had
broken. Mahipala's own successor Devapala, who reigned from around
940 to 955, was forced to surrender a prized image of Vishnu to the
Chandela king Yasovarman, who installed it in one of the finest
temples of his own new capital at Khajuraho - a small transaction
that Smith reads as proof the Chandela kingdom, once a Pratihara
subordinate, had grown stronger than its old overlord. Devapala's
brother and successor Vijayapala, ruling from about 960 to 990, lost
Gwalior itself, the dynasty's ancient possession, to a Kachchhapaghata
chief who founded a local line that would hold the fortress for more
than a century and a half. By the time the Solanki kingdom of
Anhilwara was founded in Gujarat around the same period, Smith notes,
the king of Kanauj "no longer had any concern with Western India" -
territory the empire of Bhoja had once controlled outright.

## A new threat from the northwest

The contest for Kanauj that had defined the dynasty's rise was, by
the last decades of the tenth century, no longer the main danger
facing it. A new power was pressing through the northwestern passes:
the Amir of Ghazni, Sabuktigin, whose raids against the Hindu Shahi
kingdom of the Punjab drew King Rajyapala of Kanauj into a great
confederacy of Hindu princes assembled in 991 to resist him -
a coalition that included the Chandela king Ganda and was, in the
event, disastrously defeated. The frontier that had once separated
Kanauj from any serious outside threat had closed, and within another
three decades the dynasty would face, in Sabuktigin's own son Mahmud,
an enemy more dangerous than the Rashtrakutas and Palas had ever been
combined.
