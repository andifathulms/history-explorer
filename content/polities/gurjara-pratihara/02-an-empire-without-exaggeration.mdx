title: An empire without exaggeration

Nagabhata's grandson Mihira, better known by his title Bhoja, reigned
for nearly half a century, from around 840 to 890, and gave the
Pratihara dynasty the territorial reach Smith judges genuinely
imperial: "beyond question a very powerful monarch, whose dominions
may be called an 'empire' without exaggeration." His kingdom ran from
the Punjab borderlands across most of Rajputana into the greater part
of the present United Provinces, with Gwalior and, by the evidence of
his successors' own control of Kathiawar, likely Gujarat and Malwa as
well. Bhoja liked to present himself as an incarnation of Vishnu,
taking the title Adi Varaha, "the primaeval boar," and struck base
silver coins bearing it in numbers common enough across Northern India
to attest both the length of his reign and the breadth of his rule.

## Three rivals, one city

Bhoja's own borders show exactly how contested that rule was. To the
east his territory abutted the Pala kingdom of Devapala, whom he
fought successfully; to the southwest his "powerful Rashtrakuta rival"
- allied, notably, with the Muslim rulers of Sind against him - kept
his armies constantly alert; to the south the rising Chandela kingdom
of Jejakabhukti, in the region later called Bundelkhand, probably
acknowledged his overlordship without yet challenging it outright.
The three-way contest for paramountcy in the Gangetic plain that had
opened with Nagabhata's own seizure of Kanauj was, under Bhoja, a
genuine equilibrium: Pratihara strength held both the Palas and the
Rashtrakutas at bay without conclusively defeating either.

## An heritage preserved intact

Bhoja's son and successor Mahendrapala, who ruled from about 890 to
908, kept the inheritance whole rather than expanding it further -
Smith describes him ruling "all Northern India, except the Panjab and
Indus valley, from the borders of Bihar to the shore of the Arabian
sea," with inscriptions from Gaya suggesting Magadha itself came under
Pratihara control for a time. His own court hosted the poet
Rajasekhara, author of the Karpuramanjari and a treatise on the art of
poetry, who continued to serve the dynasty into the reign of
Mahendrapala's younger son - a literary patronage that marks the
Pratihara court at its most secure, in the brief interval between
Bhoja's own conquests and the decline that would begin within a
generation of Mahendrapala's death.
