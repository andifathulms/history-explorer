title: Caravans that did what armies had not

The renewed expansion that had resumed after the succession crisis of
1780-1810 carried Luba's own military and political authority further
than it had ever reached - by the first half of the nineteenth
century, the Mulopwe ruling from the capital at Kitenta commanded a
loose but real supremacy over a vast stretch of country between the
Lomami and Lualaba rivers and the western shore of Lake Tanganyika,
built less on direct rule than on the spread of the kingdom's own
political idea, the bulopwe - chieftaincy as the birthright of rulers
descended from the founders Kongolo and Kalala Ilunga. Most of the
chiefdoms that fell under Luba's military reach in this period already
shared that concept of power before any Luba army arrived, which is
part of why the structure could extend so far without the kind of
standing administration a conquest state usually needs to hold new
territory.

## Trade routes that answered to nobody

What that same looseness could not survive was a rival source of
wealth and weapons arriving from outside the kingdom's own political
economy. Through the middle decades of the century, Swahili and Angolan
trading networks - Arab and Nyamwezi caravans working west from the
Indian Ocean coast, Ovimbundu traders working east from Angola -
pushed steadily further into the Luba sphere in search of ivory,
copper and slaves. By the 1870s these had hardened into standing
trading principalities answering to nobody in Kitenta: the most
famous, established by the trader Tippu Tip at Kasongo in 1875, came
to dominate long-distance trade across the Sankuru river and the whole
Luba "empire" in its own right. During the reign of the Mulopwe
Kasongo Kalombo, roughly 1870 to 1880, the wealth and weapons these
networks put directly into the hands of provincial chiefs let them
bypass the throne's own authority rather than compete for it, turning
disputes that might once have been settled within the kingdom's own
political hierarchy into contests between chiefs backed by outside
capital.

## An empire that stopped needing a center

No single battle or treaty marks the point at which Luba's own
authority gave way; Vellut's account describes something closer to a
loosening than a defeat, playing out over the very decades in which
the kingdom's territorial reach was, on paper, at its widest. By the
close of the period, the states and chiefdoms that had once answered
to the Mulopwe's own sacred fire had become almost entirely
autonomous, each increasingly running its own trade, its own alliances
and its own wars - not a kingdom destroyed, but one whose provinces
had quietly stopped needing it.
