title: A kingdom that grew by losing people

Luba's own state, once established on the plains north of the Upemba
Depression, absorbed the Kikondja kingdom to its south and the Kalundwe
kingdom to its west - the same Kalundwe whose own capital at Cifinda had,
generations earlier, been the starting point of the whole founding
sequence. Further expansion, at some point in the same period or shortly
after, brought the lower reaches of the Lovua river under the kingdom's
own control as well.

## A founding that scattered its own subjects

Consolidating this expanded territory produced disturbances serious
enough that groups of the kingdom's own people left rather than stayed to
live under the new order. Emigrants from the lower Lovua founded an
entirely separate state, the Shila, on the western shore of Lake Mweru
and along the lower Luapula river - though it remains unclear whether the
rulers who eventually led the Shila state actually descended from Luba
emigrants at all, or simply claimed a Luba origin because it was a
prestigious one to claim. The Kanyok, to the north-west, were subjected
more directly: Luba immigrants organized, or founded outright, a kingdom
among them that would remain nominally loyal to Luba overrule for a
century before finally breaking free.

## Famine as a permanent migration engine

The most consequential emigration ran north rather than south or west.
Following the disturbances that accompanied the kingdom's own founding,
later compounded by wars of succession and by recurring famine, groups
identified in tradition as the Luba Kasai left Shaba entirely and spread
north along the Lubilash river into better-watered country. Famine
recurs constantly as the stated motive in the oral traditions of the
kinship groups who made this move, and the pattern was not a single
migration but a continuous one: whenever famine struck, more people
moved north, a process that continued for well over a century and was
still adding new arrivals as late as the early 1800s. By the time these
migrations tapered off, some of the Luba Kasai had continued moving
further west still, from the Lubilash toward the Lulua river - far enough,
by around 1750, to bring them into direct contact with an entirely
different kingdom, the Kuba, on the far side of the Kasai river system.

## Two Luba political systems, not one

The country Luba Kasai settlers occupied did not reproduce the political
system their ancestors' kingdom had built in Shaba. Village government,
or offices sold to the highest bidder for a limited term, replaced the
sacred, centralized kingship model of the Shaba heartland - a system the
Luba Kasai shared, in this respect, with some of their Songye neighbors
rather than with the kingdom their own ancestors had left behind. Two
peoples who spoke closely related languages and traced their origin to
the same founding kingdom had, within a few centuries of separation,
built two entirely different kinds of state - one built around a sacred,
inherited throne, the other built around office as a commodity anyone
with sufficient means could buy.
