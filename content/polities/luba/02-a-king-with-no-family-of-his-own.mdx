Luba political thought rested on two principles that Luba's own language
held together in a single word, bulopwe: the sacred, near-mystical
character of kingship on one hand, and rule exercised through a closed
association of initiates on the other. In the kingdom's northernmost
territories, among the Songye, the second principle came to dominate
almost entirely. In the kingdom's own heartland, both principles stayed in
balance - though even there, the sacred character of the king himself
came through as the stronger of the two.

## A pyramid built from other pyramids

Luba's own state was organized, in its own political imagination, as what
might be called a pyramid of pyramids. At the base, households were bound
together by patrilineage; relations between whole villages were understood
the same way, as relations between lineages, and each district answered to
a titled chief whose own authority rested on a personal connection to a
local territorial spirit. Above all of this stood the capital itself, laid
out to reflect the same structure in physical space - separate quarters
for royalty and for titled officials, divided again by military or civil
function - with the bulopwe, the royal office itself, at the exact center.

## A king who belonged to no lineage and every lineage at once

The mulopwe, Luba's king, was formally understood to have no lineage or
clan of his own - an office standing entirely outside the ordinary kinship
system it governed. Yet in practice the throne passed from father to
brother to son, and the Luba resolved the apparent contradiction by
treating this transmission as a rotation between the lineages of
different queen-mothers rather than as simple patrilineal inheritance.
The king was, by this account, above the political struggles of ordinary
kinship and yet bound by kinship to a great many of his own district
governors at the same time - the summit of a structure he stood both
inside and outside of, depending on which relationship was being invoked.

## Rituals that answered to no one visible

The rituals surrounding a new king's investiture, including an act of
royal incest recorded among the sources, existed to underline exactly this
paradox: the uniqueness and supremacy of the office, and the fact that its
holder was merely the latest in a long line of others who had held it
before him, whose spirits now spoke through mediums attached to their own
shrines. Behind the visible structure of district chiefs and court
officials stood a second, less visible one: the Bambudye, a closed
association whose secrets were never fully revealed even to the
missionaries and colonial officials who documented most of the rest of
Luba court life, and which exercised real ritual and secular control over
the country - and, when necessary, over the king himself.
