Long before any single kingdom unified the plains north of the Upemba
Depression, the small chiefdoms scattered through the region's river
valleys were already bound together by exchange - raffia and palm oil
moving south from the forest edge, fish moving out from the Lualaba river
system, copper and salt moving north from the mineral-rich south-east, and
a lubricant called mbafu oil moving out from the central-southern
country. Trade on this scale needed a medium more practical than barter,
and the region supplied one from its own resources: standardized crosses
of smelted copper, in use as currency in the Upemba Depression from
around 1300.

## A currency that quietly lost weight for four centuries

The copper cross evolved steadily over the following centuries in ways
that trace the region's own economic history even where no written record
survives to narrate it directly. By 1500 the crosses had become
standardized in weight, suitable for larger transactions across a wide
trading area. By 1600 they had shrunk considerably, small enough to serve
minor, everyday purchases as well as major ones - a change that may
equally well reflect a currency doing its job better, reaching further
down into ordinary trade, or a currency losing value the way debased coin
does anywhere. After 1700 the copper cross vanished from use altogether,
and by the nineteenth century imported glass beads had taken over its
role as the region's standard medium of exchange.

## An economy that outlasted any single political structure

The trade networks the copper cross once served did not depend on the
Luba kingdom's own political unification to function - they were already
old by the time Kalala Ilunga's line took the plains north of the
Depression, and the goods moving along them (fish, raffia, copper, salt,
palm oil) stayed largely the same goods for centuries regardless of which
chiefdom, or later which kingdom, controlled the routes they traveled. The
kingdom's own tribute system, run through district officials responsible
for collecting food, salt, iron, raffia and baskets on the king's behalf,
sat on top of an exchange economy that long predated any king's own claim
to control it.
