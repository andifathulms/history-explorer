For most of the kingdom's own early history, no source gives enough
detail to narrate its territorial affairs at all - only around 1700, with
the reign of a king named Kadilo, does Luba's history become concrete
enough to follow reign by reign rather than in broad, undated strokes.
That this first clearly narratable reign happened to be built around a
military failure says something about how thin the record is for
everything that came before it, more than it says anything unusual about
Kadilo himself.

## Campaigns that did not go as planned

Kadilo led campaigns north against the neighboring Songye towns, in what
appears to have been an attempt to extend Luba authority over territory
the kingdom had not previously controlled directly. The campaigns ended in
defeat. Rather than press the conflict further, Kadilo's court negotiated
an alliance with some of the same Songye towns his own army had failed to
subdue - a settlement that, whatever it cost Luba's own military
prestige, kept the northern frontier stable enough to avoid renewed war.

## An alliance that outlasted the war it ended

The alliance had consequences its own negotiators could not have fully
anticipated. Songye political and cultural influence, once let into Luba
affairs through the settlement that ended Kadilo's war, did not recede
once the immediate crisis passed. By the close of the same century,
Songye influence was shaping the affairs of the Luba court directly,
visible above all in a succession crisis whose outcome the kingdom's own
Songye allies had a genuine hand in determining. A defeat on the
battlefield, absorbed and converted into a diplomatic settlement, had
opened a door into the center of Luba politics that would not fully close
again for the rest of the century.
