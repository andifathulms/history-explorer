The settlement Æthelstan had imposed at Eamont held for six years
before it was tested. In 934 he marched against Scotland by land and
sea, in response to some breach of the agreement the sources do not
specify, and pushed as far north as Caithness - a demonstration that
the collapse of Norse York had brought an English army within striking
distance of the Scottish kingdom's own heartland. Three years later
the coalition that resulted from that demonstration came for him:
Olaf, king of the Dublin Norse, allied with the kings of Scotland and
Strathclyde, both now openly hostile, and invaded England in force.

## Brunanburh

Æthelstan and his brother Edmund met the invasion at the head of an
army drawn from both Mercia and Wessex together - the alliance
Brunanburh's own memory would do as much as anything to cement - and
broke it after a long struggle, pursuing the survivors until nightfall.
Five kings and seven Irish earls were left dead on the field, along
with a son of the Scottish king; Olaf returned to Dublin with the
wreckage of his fleet. The site itself has never been securely
identified, but the battle's place in the kingdom's own memory was
never in doubt - later writers ranked Æthelstan alongside Alfred and
Edgar as one of three kings whose histories might steady a nation
under threat.

## A peace that needed no more battles

What Æthelstan had won by fighting, his grand-nephew Edgar inherited
already settled. Edgar's sixteen-year reign is recorded as
strikingly free of incident - no barbarian invasion to repel, no rival
English kingdom to absorb - and its one dramatic moment was a
ceremony rather than a battle: a long-deferred coronation at Bath on
Whit Sunday, 973, under a rite reached only after the archbishop and
his colleagues had drafted at least two earlier versions. An
eyewitness account of the day survives, and it makes clear that the
ceremony's essence was not the crown itself but the anointing that set
a king apart from other men - a form of coronation that outlived
Edgar's own dynasty. A contemporary writer, working when England again
faced invasion, placed Edgar last of the three kings he named as a
model for troubled times: not the one who fought the Danes to victory,
like Alfred, nor the one who broke an invading army, like Æthelstan,
but the one whose enemies sought peace from him without a battle at
all.
