For most of the eighth century Wessex answered to Mercia. King
Beorhtric had married a daughter of the Mercian overking Offa, and
the man who might otherwise have succeeded him, Egbert, spent years
in exile on the continent, driven out by Offa and Beorhtric together.
Only when Beorhtric died in 802 was Egbert able to return and take
the West Saxon throne - and for the first twenty years of his reign
he had little influence beyond his own kingdom's borders.

## Ellendun

The change came in 825. Egbert met the Mercian king Beornwulf in
battle at Ellendun and broke his army decisively. The victory did
more than remove a rival: within the year, Kent, Sussex, Surrey and
Essex all transferred their allegiance from Mercia to Wessex, and a
chronicler writing under Egbert's own successors gave him the old
title *Bretwalda* - a word better rendered "Britain-ruler" than any
literal translation of its parts - the eighth king, on that
chronicler's own reckoning, to hold it.

## A supremacy without an institution

What Egbert had not created was any lasting machinery of overlordship.
The title itself belonged to poetry more than to law; it named a
recognized pre-eminence rather than a defined office, and the kingdoms
that submitted to Wessex after Ellendun kept their own separate
identities and, in Mercia's case, before long their own kings again.
Wessex had stopped being anyone's client. It had not yet become
anything more permanent than the strongest kingdom among several -
and within a generation a new threat, arriving from the sea, would
decide whether it survived as even that.
