Renewed Viking raiding returned to England in Æthelred's reign after
a lull of two generations, and the response that set the pattern for
what followed came in 991. After a Danish fleet defeated and killed
Byrhtnoth, ealdorman of Essex, at Maldon - he had refused a demand for
tribute shouted across the tidal causeway at Northey Island, and paid
for the refusal with his life and his militia's defeat - Æthelred's
government chose a different answer: a heavy tax raised to buy the
raiders off. The precedent held for the next twenty-five years,
repeated levy after levy, and later Norman kings inherited the same
device under the name Danegeld.

## St Brice's Day

By 1002 a truce cost 24,000 pounds of tribute-money, and a few weeks
after paying it Æthelred married Emma, sister of Duke Richard II of
Normandy - a marriage whose consequences neither party could have
foreseen that year. Then, that autumn, Æthelred ordered the killing of
Danish men across England on 13 November, St Brice's Day, telling one
monastery afterward that he acted because he had been told the Danes
were plotting to kill him and take his kingdom. The order could never
have been carried out through the Danish-settled north and east - York
and Lincoln were themselves Danish towns - but a massacre did take
place, remembered long afterward, and among its victims, by a
well-recorded tradition, was Gunnhild, sister of the Danish king
Swein.

## What the marriage bought, too late

Swein's invasions that followed would end in the conquest St Brice's
Day had been meant to prevent. Æthelred's marriage to Emma, whatever
its original purpose, turned out to matter for a different reason
entirely: it entitled him to shelter in Normandy once the Danes had
in fact conquered England, and the sons of that marriage were raised
at the Norman court. One of them would return to England as king with
a strong sense of obligation to his mother's homeland - and the tie
his father had made in 1002 would, six decades later, give a Norman
duke his own claim to English soil.
