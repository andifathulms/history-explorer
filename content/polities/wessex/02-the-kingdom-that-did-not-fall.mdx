By the 870s the other three kingdoms that had shared England with
Wessex a generation earlier were gone. Northumbria, East Anglia and
most of Mercia had been fought over and settled by Danish armies, and
in the first days of 878 Wessex itself came within a season of joining
them. A Danish force under Guthrum seized Chippenham in midwinter, an
attack timed against the ordinary rhythm of campaigning, and within
weeks a large part of the West Saxon people had submitted. King
Alfred fell back into the marshes of Somerset around Athelney with
whatever following he had left.

<Figure id="wx-athelney" />

## Athelney to Edington

From that base Alfred spent seven weeks harrying Danish raiding
parties before he judged himself strong enough to risk open battle.
Gathering the levies of Somerset, Wiltshire and the part of Hampshire
west of Southampton Water, he met Guthrum's main army at Edington,
some fifteen miles south of their camp, and broke it. The defeated
Danes held out at Chippenham for a fortnight before agreeing to terms:
Guthrum would leave Wessex, and he would accept baptism. Three weeks
later Alfred stood as his godfather at Aller, near Athelney, giving
the Danish king the English name Athelstan.

<Figure id="wx-jewel" />

## What one battle decided

The scale of what Edington had settled only becomes clear against the
wider collapse of the previous fifteen years. Of the four kingdoms
that had divided England in the middle of the century, Wessex alone
now survived entire; Danish armies had found permanent settlement
across what had been Northumbria, East Anglia and eastern Mercia, and
the imposition of West Saxon rule on their descendants would occupy
the next seventy years. None of that reconquest was yet certain in
878. What Edington had secured was narrower and more essential: that
there would still be a West Saxon kingdom left to attempt it.
