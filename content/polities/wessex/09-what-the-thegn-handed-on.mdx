---
title: What the thegn handed on
drafted_from: stenton-1943
phase: afterlife
---

By 1087 every English landowner of real political weight had been
replaced. Fewer than half a dozen lay lords of any standing were
still Englishmen; no Englishman held an English bishopric; French
sheriffs ran all but a handful of shires. It would never be guessed
from Domesday Book, or from the witness-lists on William's own later
charters, that he had begun his reign hoping to govern with Englishmen
and Frenchmen together on equal terms. The old ruling class did not
survive the conquest that ended this kingdom. Its institutions did.

## What the new lords kept

The chief administrative divisions of the country - shires, hundreds,
and wapentakes - were accepted by William's barons as a matter of
course, not replaced or redrawn. They ran their new estates by Old
English methods and tolerated the same inconvenient, inherited variety
of manorial arrangements King Edward's own landowners had lived with.
The few institutions the Normans did introduce - the honorial court
for a great fief's internal business, the castlery built to support a
single fortress - left the accustomed working of shire and hundred
untouched. Even where William legislated in his own name, the
substance was rarely new: the one enactment that reads as a deliberate
change to English practice replaced the death penalty with mutilation
for certain offences, and even the new machinery built specifically to
protect the incoming French population - a fine on the hundred if a
murdered Frenchman's killer went unfound within five days, a choice
for an accused Englishman between the native ordeal of iron and the
Norman judicial combat - was grafted onto courts that already existed
rather than courts invented to replace them. In most of its detail,
the law an Englishman answered to in 1087 was still the law of King
Edward, and before him of Cnut and Æthelred.

Stenton's own judgement on the continuity is direct: there is a
genuine continuity of function between the thegns of the shire to whom
Edward the Confessor had addressed his own writs and the knights of
the shire whose cooperation, a century later, made the Angevin
kingdom's experiments in centralised government possible at all.

## What it cost the men who carried it

None of this was any comfort to the Englishmen who lived through it.
A thegn who made his peace with the Conqueror kept, at best, enough of
his land to live on in modest circumstances, stripped of the standing
his family had held before 1066 and governed thereafter by a foreign
aristocracy that regarded him with tolerant indifference at the very
best. It was, in Stenton's own phrase, as the depressed survivor of a
beaten people that such a man handed on the tradition of local
government to the men who had overthrown the state that tradition
belonged to.

The Normans who inherited it had produced little in art or scholarship
and nothing in literature that could be set beside the work of the
kingdom they had just destroyed. But they were, as Stenton puts it
plainly, the masters of their world - and the machinery of shires and
hundreds that let them govern it without having to invent one of their
own was the one part of Egbert's kingdom that answered to nobody's
conquest at all.
