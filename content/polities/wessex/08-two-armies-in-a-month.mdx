Edward the Confessor died at Westminster on 5 January 1066, his health
broken since a rebellion against his own brother-in-law Tostig the
previous autumn, too ill even to attend the consecration of his own
new abbey eight days before. He left no son. The leading men of his
council met in London while his death was still expected and settled,
apparently without dissent, on Harold Godwinson - the king's own chief
counsellor, and by every account the one Englishman capable of
organizing the kingdom's defence against the two invasions everyone
expected that year.

## Fulford and Stamford Bridge

Both invasions came within the same month. Tostig, exiled after his
own Northumbrian earldom rose against him, joined Harald Hardrada of
Norway in an invasion of the north; the Norwegian army defeated the
earls Edwin and Morcar at Fulford outside York on 20 September, a
victory costly enough to leave the northern earls unable to help
Harold in the campaign that followed. Harold marched north with
extraordinary speed and destroyed the Norwegian army at Stamford
Bridge on 25 September, ending more than two centuries of
Scandinavian invasion of England in a single battle - and killing both
Hardrada and Tostig.

## Hastings

Three days later, while Harold's army was still in the north, a change
of wind let William of Normandy cross the Channel unopposed and land
at Pevensey. Harold covered roughly 190 miles from York to London and
then marched another fifty to Sussex within thirteen days, assembling
what army he could as he went - the core of it, by every account, his
own household troops and those of his brothers, since there had been
no time for the thegns of distant shires to join him. William forced
the battle on 14 October, catching the English army, by a
contemporary's own account, before it was fully arrayed on the ridge
where Battle Abbey would later stand. Harold was killed in the
fighting, along with his brothers Gyrth and Leofwine.

## Submission at Berkhamstead

There was no second English army to meet William afterward. Edgar
Ætheling, the last male of the old royal house, was proclaimed king by
some of the surviving nobles but never crowned; within weeks the
leading Englishmen still able to negotiate - Edgar himself, the earls
Edwin and Morcar, and the archbishop of Canterbury among them -
submitted to William at Berkhamstead and swore fealty, and William
allowed his army to ravage the country between there and London as a
last demonstration of force before entering the city. The kingdom
Egbert had begun to build at Ellendun two hundred and forty-one years
earlier now answered to a Norman duke.
