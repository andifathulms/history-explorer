Alfred's son Edward the Elder inherited a kingdom that had held its
ground; the reconquest of the Danish-settled midlands and east was his
own achievement, and he did not accomplish it alone. When his
brother-in-law Æthelred of Mercia died in 911, Æthelred's widow -
Edward's own sister Æthelflæd - was recognized as "Lady of the
Mercians," an office with no real precedent. She held it for eight
years, kept a Mercian military household loyal to her personally,
planned campaigns and led them in person, and built a chain of
fortresses whose siting shows a genuine feel for the ground she was
defending.

## Fortress by fortress

The West Saxon advance that followed was less a matter of great
battles than of a fortress built, a submission taken, and the process
repeated. Edward secured the Thames-side lands the Danish army at
Bedford had held, then, methodically, brought the East Anglian and
Essex Danes to submission by 917 - "a crowded year," in the account
that survives, ending with the army of Cambridge giving way along
with the rest. In the new year Æthelflæd took Leicester without a
fight, and most of the Danish force settled there transferred its
allegiance to her directly.

## A death that closed one door

Æthelflæd's reach was already extending toward Northumbria: the
leading men of the country around York had sent her a formal promise
of allegiance, hoping for her support against Norse raiders arriving
from Ireland. She died at Tamworth before she could act on it, on 12
June 918, and no comparable offer was ever made to Edward. What might
have been a peaceful absorption of southern Northumbria did not
happen; a Norse kingdom took shape at York instead, and dealing with
it would fall to the next generation. South of the Humber, though,
Edward's reconquest went on without her. By the time of his own death
in 924, every Danish army between the Thames and the Welland answered
to Wessex.
