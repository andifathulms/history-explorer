Male rule came back to Aceh in 1699, under an Arab dynasty, and again in 1727
under a Bugis one. Neither restored anything. The eighteenth century is a
sequence of succession disputes, coups and counter-coups that brought the
sultanate's political fortunes to their lowest point.

What had happened between 1641 and 1699 turned out to be permanent. The
sultanate survived as a symbol whose status every Acehnese acknowledged and
whose effective authority stopped at the edge of the port. The imams, the
*uleebalang* and the other officers of the state had become hereditary, secular,
territorial chiefs, and they stayed that way.

In Aceh Besar they organised. Three federations of chiefs emerged, each known by
the number of *mukim* — parishes — it contained: twenty-six on the right bank of
the Aceh river, twenty-five on the left, twenty-two in the much larger upland
district. The war-leaders chosen to head them became hereditary in their turn,
and were paid a gratuity by each sultan at his installation, which is a precise
measure of who was confirming whom. By the nineteenth century Acehnese spoke of
"the twelve uleebalangs who appoint and dethrone princes".

No sultan after this period could command obedience. Concerted action required
the agreement of the important chiefs — and yet none of them ever tried to take
the throne. The affairs of the court and the harbour were not their affairs, and
each side could ignore the other except in a crisis.

## The pepper coast

Then, in the space of about fifteen years, two things changed the economics of
the whole country.

The British had been trying since 1762 to establish themselves in Aceh, and were
rebuffed every time — the Acehnese had not lost their distrust of a foreign
military post on their soil. In 1786 the British gave up and settled on Penang
instead, which put a free port and a hungry merchant community a few days' sail
away.

At almost the same moment, about 1787, an Acehnese called Leubé Dapa, chief of
Susoh, contracted to supply pepper to a merchant from the failing British
settlement at Bengkulen. He planted the undeveloped west coast between Singkil
and Meulaboh, drew a stream of immigrants down from the north coast, and
succeeded beyond all proportion. The new colonies were producing five thousand
tons of pepper by 1803. By the 1820s the figure had roughly doubled — something
close to **half the world's supply**.

Almost none of it went to the East India Company. It went to the merchants of
Salem and Boston: twenty-one American ships called on the new pepper coast in
1802, and Americans dominated the trade until the 1850s.

So the wealth of Aceh moved decisively out of the capital and onto coasts the
sultan did not control, into the hands of chiefs dealing directly with foreigners
who had no interest whatever in the old rule that outsiders traded only at Banda
Aceh.

## Jauhar al-Alam

Sultan Jauhar al-Alam Shah, who reigned from 1795 to 1823, understood the problem
exactly and lost anyway.

He had served aboard an East India Company ship in his youth. He spoke good
English, employed Europeans as his chief advisers, and tried to introduce western
notions of government. His offer to Britain was straightforward: help me subdue
my vassals, and I will channel the trade through my capital to Penang.

Penang refused, and not by accident. The merchants there disapproved of his
European advisers and, far more importantly, opposed the whole idea of royal
authority over the commerce of the coasts. Their prosperity depended on the
fragmentation Jauhar was trying to end.

In 1813 a Penang tycoon called Sayyid Husain — a British subject, and a
descendant of a discarded branch of the Acehnese royal house — spent enough money
among the dissatisfied chiefs of Aceh Besar to have his own son proclaimed sultan
as Saif al-Alam Shah. The coup would have been impossible without him.

The naval war between the two sultans then wrecked the trade it had been fought
over, and the British intervened to end it. Raffles, against the settled opinion
of Penang, backed the legitimate claimant, and a treaty was signed with Jauhar at
Pidié on 22 April 1819: arms and money from the East India Company, the removal
of his rival, and in exchange a defensive alliance in which Aceh would admit no
European residents and make no foreign treaty without British consent.

Almost none of it happened. Saif al-Alam was chased out by a British gunboat in
1820; no British agent was ever appointed; the chiefs of the upland federation
went on refusing to acknowledge Jauhar; and he died in December 1823 without ever
having re-entered his own capital.

## The article nobody in Aceh signed

Five months later, on 17 March 1824, Britain and the Netherlands settled their
colonial quarrel in London. Britain handed over its Sumatran settlement at
Bengkulen and took Malacca, and undertook by Article 9 that no British settlement
would be formed on Sumatra and no treaty concluded there with any native prince,
chief or state.

Aceh was not a party to it and was not consulted. But the practical effect was
that the one European power with an interest in Acehnese independence had
contracted itself out of Sumatra — and the one with an interest in ending that
independence had been left the island.

Half a century later the Dutch declared war, and the country they invaded was the
one this century had made: a sultanate that was a symbol, a coastline of
hereditary chiefs with their own revenues and their own arms, and no single
authority anywhere whose surrender could end anything.
