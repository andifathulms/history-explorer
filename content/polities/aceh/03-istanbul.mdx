Some time around 1562 Aceh sent an embassy to the Ottoman court, nine thousand
kilometres away, asking for help against the Portuguese.

The two states had never shared a frontier and had no dealings before or after.
The request went the length of the Indian Ocean, across a subcontinent's worth of
other people's politics, to a court that had barely heard of the petitioner's
enemies in that quarter of the world — and it worked, partially.

## What came back

Cannon arrived, with founders who could cast more and gunners who could work
them. Acehnese artillery is attested in Portuguese accounts for the rest of the
century, which is the practical test, because the people being shot at had no
reason to exaggerate.

One of the guns was still standing at the entrance to the royal enclosure at
Banda Aceh three centuries later. It was called *lada sicupak* — one measure of
pepper — because that, according to the story Acehnese told about it, was all the
tribute the ambassadors had left by the time they finally reached the Caliph.

For a sultanate not yet seventy years old, on the far edge of the Muslim world,
the whole thing is a remarkable piece of diplomacy. Aceh identified the one power
capable of matching Portuguese gunnery, reached it across an ocean it did not
control, and came back with the technology and the men who understood it.

## What Aceh became, and to whom

Whether Aceh also became an Ottoman vassal is a genuinely disputed question, and
the dispute is more interesting than either answer.

The Turkish account — recorded much later — has the Acehnese making a formal act
of submission into the hands of Sinan Pasha, recognising the sultan's authority
as Caliph, raising the Ottoman flag in their ports and on their ships, declaring
themselves vassals and asking for his protection in return. On that telling, the
guns were what an overlord sends a dependant.

Acehnese tradition tells it differently again, and some versions move the whole
episode to the reign of Iskandar Muda, two generations later, where it flatters a
greater king.

Reid's caution is the part worth carrying away. The version that reads the
mission as tribute rather than as alliance is late, and it may owe a good deal to
1873 — the year the Dutch declared war, and the year Aceh had the strongest
possible motive to describe itself as a country under Turkish protection. A
sixteenth-century embassy was being remembered by people who urgently needed it
to have meant something specific.

What is not in dispute is the ordnance, the founders, and the gunners. Whatever
the transaction was called at either end, it left Aceh with artillery and with a
story about the Caliph's protection — and three hundred years later, with the
Dutch fleet off the coast, it was the story the sultanate reached for first.
