The sultan surrendered in 1903 and the fighting did not stop. The Dutch abolished
the sultanate; the ulama who had taken over the war from the chiefs kept it going
for another decade, and the last of the Tiro teungkus — the family of Teungku
Chik di Tiro, who had led the resistance until his death in 1891 — were killed in
battle years after the surrender that supposedly ended things.

That is the pattern for the whole century that follows. Every settlement of the
Aceh question is agreed by somebody, and somebody else keeps fighting.

## The chiefs are destroyed

The uleebalang had run the country since the 1600s and had been the real power
since 1641. The Dutch governed through them, which was sensible and, in the end,
fatal to them.

In 1939 the reformist ulama organised themselves as PUSA under Teungku Muhammad
Daud Beureu'eh. In 1942 a PUSA-inspired revolt broke out just ahead of the
Japanese invasion. When the Japanese surrendered in 1945 they left the uleebalang
establishment in charge, and independence was proclaimed in Jakarta. Within a
year a violent social revolution had ended the authority of the chiefs
permanently, and the ulama held Aceh.

Three hundred years of hereditary territorial lordship, closed inside about
twelve months.

## The bargain, and what happened to it

Aceh went into the Indonesian republic on two understandings: that its
contribution to the independence struggle entitled it to an equal stake, and that
the new state would be founded on Islamic principles.

The contribution was real. PUSA sent soldiers to fight the Dutch around Medan,
bought national bonds in 1946 and two aircraft in 1948, and when Dutch advances
had overrun everything else, Aceh was the only stronghold the Republic had left —
enough that the vice-premier ran his office out of Banda Aceh for five months in
1949, in case the negotiations in the Hague collapsed.

In December 1949 that vice-premier, at the request of Acehnese leaders, decreed a
separate Province of Aceh. After sovereignty was transferred, Jakarta questioned
whether the decree had been constitutional — it had been issued three days after
the administration that made it was dissolved — and in August 1950 Aceh was
folded into North Sumatra in a reorganisation that left the country with ten
provinces.

The sense of betrayal was severe, and it was made worse by what followed: an
influx of non-Acehnese workers and troops, the end of Aceh's special foreign
exchange arrangement, and a budget that increasingly favoured Java over the outer
islands.

In September 1953 Daud Beureu'eh rebelled, joining the Darul Islam revolt that
had begun in West Java and aimed to turn the republic into an Islamic federation.
The most senior religious figure in Aceh had spent the 1940s delivering the
province to Indonesia and spent the 1950s in the mountains fighting it.

Jakarta restored the Province of Aceh in 1957, and on 26 May 1959 granted it
*Daerah Istimewa* — special region status, with autonomy over religion, education
and customary law. It was enough for most of the rebellion. Beureu'eh himself
refused to come down until 1962, and by then had reduced his demand from an
Islamic state of Indonesia to the application of Islamic law in Aceh.

## Gas, and a second war

In 1971 Mobil found natural gas at Arun, near Lhokseumawe. Production began in
1977.

In December 1976 Hasan di Tiro declared the independence of Aceh with something
like two hundred supporters. He was the great-grandson, in the maternal line, of
the Teungku Chik di Tiro who had fought the Dutch, and he had been schooled at a
madrasah run by Daud Beureu'eh. The claim he made was continuity: that Aceh had
been a sovereign state, that the Dutch had taken it by force, and that the
republic in Jakarta was simply the latest occupier — "the Javanese colonial
government", in the movement's standard phrase.

The propaganda worked because it did not have to be invented. When students
filled the streets of Banda Aceh in November 1999 demanding a referendum, they
recited verses from the *Hikayat Prang Sabil*, the holy-war poem that had been
sung against the Dutch a century earlier. Parents were teaching those verses to
children in the displacement camps.

What followed was worse than what it echoed. Counter-insurgency from 1977;
military operations from 1989 to 1998 under the status known as DOM; six thousand
special forces troops in 1990; the fall of Suharto and a brief opening; a
cessation-of-hostilities agreement in 2002 that collapsed within months; and in
May 2003 a declared military emergency, with forty thousand troops still in the
province a year later.

## How it ended

On 26 December 2004 the earthquake and tsunami destroyed much of Banda Aceh and
the west coast, and opened the province to foreign aid workers for the first time
in decades.

Peace talks began in Helsinki in January 2005. At the second round in February
GAM accepted self-government within Indonesia rather than independence, and the
memorandum was signed on 15 August.

So Aceh is not an independent country and did not become one. It is a province of
Indonesia with a degree of self-government that no other province has, including
authority over Islamic law — the thing Daud Beureu'eh went to the mountains for
in 1953, arrived at fifty years later by people who had been fighting for
something else.

The autonomy law Megawati signed in 2001 gave the province a name to go with it.
It is called Nanggroe Aceh Darussalam — the same name the sultanate carried, in
the same words, four hundred years after Ali Mughayat Syah assembled it and a
century after the Dutch abolished it.
