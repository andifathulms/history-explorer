When Albuquerque took Melaka in 1511 he took a customs house rather than a
trade. The commerce that had passed through the city did not stop; it went
somewhere else, and a good deal of it went north.

The northern tip of Sumatra was a cluster of small ports before that. Pasai was
the important one — the sultanate that had converted Melaka's own ruler a
century earlier — and around it lay a string of harbours on both coasts, none
of them individually much. Ali Mughayat Syah had been consolidating them into a
single sultanate since the 1490s, and the Portuguese then handed him a windfall
he had done nothing to earn.

Every Muslim merchant who would not trade under a Portuguese flag needed
somewhere else to unload. Aceh was on the right side of the strait, upwind of
Melaka, and Muslim.

## What arrived, and what did not

What moved north was Melaka's business: its merchant communities, its shipping,
its Malay court culture, its scholars. Within a generation Aceh was doing what
Melaka had done, in the same language, with many of the same families.

What did not move was Melaka's authority. Aceh's sultans traced their line to
their own coast and never claimed to be continuing anybody else's; there was no
inheritance, no assertion of descent from the Melakan house, no attempt to
present the new sultanate as the old one restored. Taking a city's trade and
claiming its throne are different acts, and Aceh only ever did the first.

## The other half

Pasai went the other way — by force, and to the same beneficiary.

The Portuguese took Pasai in 1521. Ali Mughayat Syah drove them out of it three
years later and absorbed the sultanate into the one he was assembling forty
miles up the same coast. So Aceh was built out of two things: the commerce that
fled Melaka, and the coastline of the older sultanate next door.

It is worth noticing what that combination produced. A polity assembled from a
refugee trade and an annexed neighbour has no ancient claim to make and does
not need one — and Aceh spent the next century behaving accordingly, taking
what it could reach and justifying it afterwards.
