Iskandar Muda's son-in-law and successor died in 1641 without a son. The
*orangkaya* — the merchant aristocracy that Iskandar Muda had spent thirty
years suppressing, executing and taxing — put his widow on the throne.

Safiatuddin reigned thirty-four years. She was followed by three more women,
and Aceh was ruled by queens without interruption from 1641 to 1699.

<Figure id="safiatuddin-coin" />

Aceh was not alone in this. Patani, across the peninsula, had four queens of
its own across a hundred and four years, from 1584 to 1688 — beginning
fifty-seven years before Safiatuddin — and for much the same reasons.

It was not a rule and it was not a principle. It was a mechanism. A queen could
not lead an army in person, could not accumulate a personal following among the
war captains, and depended on the merchants for revenue. The orangkaya had
watched what an unrestrained sultan did to them, and chose rulers who
structurally could not do it again, four times running, until the ulama
produced a fatwa against female rule and the practice stopped.

## Not a matriarchy

It is worth being exact about this, because fifty-eight unbroken years of women
on the throne invites a conclusion it will not support.

Descent at Aceh ran through men. Every one of these queens held the throne as
the widow or the daughter of a sultan, and the second, third and fourth were
chosen by a council of merchants. Nothing was inherited through the female
line, and nothing in Acehnese law or custom made women eligible as women.

The distinction matters because genuine matrilineal succession does exist not
far away — in Ghana, where al-Bakri states the rule outright, in Makuria, and
in Asante, where the matriclan defined who could rule at all. Those are
constitutional arrangements. Aceh is a merchant class solving a political
problem four times in a row, and it happens that the solution wore a woman's
crown.
