Melaka's revenue coding on this site is `trade-toll` and nothing else, because
the city produced nothing and taxed everything that passed. Aceh, forty
kilometres of water further north, is coded `trade-toll` and `monopoly`, and the
second value is the whole difference between them.

Aceh grew pepper. The vine had come to Sumatra from India, it took to the
northern coasts, and by the later sixteenth century Aceh was one of the largest
suppliers on earth of a commodity Europe would pay almost anything for. The
sultan claimed a monopoly over the pepper of the coasts he controlled: growers
sold to the crown, the crown sold to the merchants, and the difference was the
state's income.

That is a revenue base of a kind Melaka never had and could never have had. It
is also the reason Aceh's power had a shape Melaka's did not — Melaka needed the
strait to be busy, and Aceh needed the west coast pepper ports to stay
obedient. Its wars were mostly against those ports and against the rival that
might take them, which is why a sultanate whose fortune came from the sea spent
most of its campaigns on land, working down its own coastline.

The arrangement had an obvious failure mode and it eventually failed that way.
Once European companies could buy from a pepper chief directly, upcountry,
without calling at the capital, the monopoly was a claim rather than a
mechanism. The crown's cut depended on being the only buyer, and it stopped
being the only buyer.
