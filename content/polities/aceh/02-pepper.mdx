Melaka had produced nothing and taxed everything that passed. Aceh, forty
kilometres of water further north, did something Melaka never could: it grew
the commodity itself.

The pepper vine had come to Sumatra from India and took to the northern coasts.
By the later sixteenth century Aceh was one of the largest suppliers on earth
of a spice Europe would pay almost anything for. The sultan claimed a monopoly
over the pepper of the coasts he controlled — growers sold to the crown, the
crown sold to the merchants, and the difference was the state's income.

## What that changes about a state

A port that lives on tolls needs the strait to be busy. A port that lives on
its own crop needs the countryside to be obedient, which is a different problem
and produces a different kingdom.

So a sultanate whose fortune came from the sea spent most of its campaigns on
land, working down its own coastline. Its wars were against the pepper ports of
the west coast, against the ones on the east, and against whichever rival might
take them — not against shipping, and rarely for control of the water. The map
of Acehnese aggression is a map of where pepper grew.

It also meant Aceh could be squeezed in a way Melaka could not. Tolls are
collected at a single point that an enemy must either hold or bypass. A
monopoly on a crop has to be enforced across a whole coast, at every landing
where a grower might sell to somebody else.

## How it broke

Exactly there.

Once the European companies could buy from a pepper chief directly, upcountry,
without calling at the capital, the monopoly became a claim rather than a
mechanism. The crown's cut had never depended on owning the pepper; it depended
on being the only buyer, and the moment it stopped being the only buyer the
whole arrangement was a formality.

The chiefs who sold around the capital were the same *uleebalang* who would
later hold the country against the Dutch for thirty years. The thing that
undermined the sultanate's revenue was the thing that gave the country its
staying power.
