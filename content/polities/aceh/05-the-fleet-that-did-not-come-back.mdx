---
title: The fleet that did not come back
drafted_from: reid-1988
phase: peak
---

Aceh had thrown itself at Portuguese Melaka repeatedly since the sixteenth
century — half a dozen sieges and major expeditions across a hundred years,
none of them successful, all of them survivable. Iskandar Muda's assault in
1629 was different only in scale: it was the largest fleet the sultanate ever
put to sea, drawn from a treasury and a conscripted military system that had
just spent two decades conquering the pepper ports on both coasts of the
strait.

## What was actually risked

The army Iskandar Muda sent against Melaka was the same one built to answer
to nobody but him — war captives and levies with no local loyalty, held at
the capital, backed by an artillery train and an elephant corps the
territorial chiefs had no share in. Putting most of it into a single naval
campaign against a fortified Portuguese city was a wager that this new,
centrally held force could finish what six earlier Acehnese generations of
besiegers, working through the old chief-levied armies, had never managed.

## The defeat

The wager failed completely. Melaka's Portuguese garrison, reinforced in
time, destroyed the Acehnese fleet in the roads outside the city. Aceh lost
not just ships and soldiers but the specific instrument Iskandar Muda had
spent his reign building — a force that existed only because it was kept
concentrated and paid for out of the centre, and that could not simply be
replaced the way a chief's own levy could be reconstituted from his own
district after a defeat.

## What stopped afterward

Aceh never again mounted an offensive campaign beyond its own coasts. The
long rivalry with Melaka, later Portuguese and then Dutch, settled into
something closer to a standing hostility than an active war — pepper,
tribute claims and the occasional raid, but no second attempt at the city
itself. Iskandar Muda spent his remaining seven years ruling the empire
1629 had already stopped growing, and it was his son-in-law, not he, who
would preside over its first real contraction.
