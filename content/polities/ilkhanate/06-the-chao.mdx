In September 1294 the Ilkhan Gaykhatu introduced paper money to Iran. It was the
first fiat currency issued outside China, and it lasted about two months.

## The idea

The treasury was empty and the vizier was desperate. A man whom Wassaf refers to
only with indignation suggested a plan that would restore the public prosperity and
be without reproach: put into circulation, in place of metal, a paper currency like
the *chao* in China.

The vizier liked it. Gaykhatu liked it, and took counsel with Bolad Chingsang, the
great khan's own representative at the Ilkhanid court, who knew how the Chinese
system worked. The most intelligent of the Mongol grandees warned against it and
was told he had mercenary motives.

The order to create the chao was issued in May 1294.

## What the note looked like

It is worth describing, because the object is the whole thirteenth century in one
rectangle of paper.

An oblong sheet with several words in Chinese characters along the sides. At the
top, on both faces, the Muslim profession of faith: *there is no God but God, and
Muhammad is his prophet*. Below it, the name the Buddhist lamas had given Gaykhatu
at his accession — Irinjin Dorji, in Tibetan. In a circle in the middle, the
denomination, from half a dirham to ten dinars. And underneath:

> The sovereign of the world has issued, in the year 693, this propitious chao.
> Whoever defaces it will be punished with death, with his wives and children, and
> his goods will be confiscated.

A Chinese financial instrument, carrying the Islamic creed and a Tibetan Buddhist
name, denominated in Persian units, issued by a Mongol in Tabriz.

## The rollout

A mint in every province, with its governor, scribes and cashiers. Metal currency
prohibited throughout the kingdom. Gold and silver forbidden for bowls and for
cloth of gold, except for the Ilkhan's own wardrobe and his chief officers'. The
goldsmiths thereby put out of work were given orders on the chao banks. Old notes
exchangeable for new at ten per cent discount. Only the merchants of the Persian
Gulf trading abroad might exchange notes for gold at the treasury — and they were
to be watched closely.

The first issue was at Tabriz on 12 September 1294, with an edict read by criers in
the streets: death for refusing it, death for buying or selling in any other money,
death for not bringing your coin to the mint to be exchanged.

Gaykhatu had been told that once gold was displaced there would be no more poor in
the land and provisions would be cheap. Poets wrote in praise of the paper money.

## Eight days

Fear of the penalty held for eight days. Then the shops and markets emptied.

Nothing could be bought in Tabriz. People began to leave the city. The hungry went
out to the gardens outside the walls to take fruit. Prices, where anything was
traded at all, went arbitrary: Wassaf records the sellers of a horse not worth more
than seventy gold pieces asking seven hundred and fifty in paper.

Gaykhatu rode through the bazaar one day and noticed the shops were shut, and asked
why. The vizier told him that a great magistrate had died and that it was customary
for the citizens to close the bazaars on such occasions.

On the Friday the congregation broke out into lamentation in the mosque. Open
imprecations were shouted against the author of the scheme; attempts were made on
the vizier's life and his people's; the troops had difficulty holding the crowd. The
vizier's brother had to authorise buying food with coin, and several men were
executed for taking part in the disturbances.

Then coin came back for provisions, and then for everything, and then the chao was
suppressed altogether — *amidst universal joy*. Commercial dealing had effectively
stopped for two months. The mints had cost a fortune to build; the one at Shiraz
alone cost five gold tumans. Wits and poets competed at lampooning the paper money
and the men who made it.

## Why it failed and what it shows

The Chinese chao worked because the Yuan state could enforce it over a closed
economy with a long habit of paper instruments and a tax system that took the notes
back. The Ilkhanate had none of those things. It had an open frontier with the
Mamluks and with India, a merchant class whose business was long-distance trade in
metal, and a government that had been farming its own revenue to the highest bidder
for thirty years and was trusted by nobody.

A currency is a promise. Gaykhatu's government could impose the death penalty and
could not make a promise anyone believed.

But notice what the experiment demonstrates about the thing this state belonged to.
A Mongol prince in Azerbaijan could adopt a Chinese fiscal technology within a
generation of its invention, on the advice of an official sent from Beijing who
happened to be at his court, and implement it simultaneously in every province from
Anatolia to Khurasan.

Nobody in that part of the world had been able to do anything of the kind before,
and nobody would again for six hundred years.
