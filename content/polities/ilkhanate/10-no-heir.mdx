The Ilkhanate ended in 1335 because a khan died at thirty without a son, and
nothing in the state survived the discovery that there was nobody to obey.

## Abu Sa'id

He came to the throne as a boy in 1316 and reigned nineteen years. The reign was
not a disaster: peace was finally made with the Mamluks in 1323, ending sixty
years of war on the Syrian frontier, and the administration went on working.

He died in 1335, on campaign against the Golden Horde, aged about thirty. He had
no son.

That is the whole of it. Within a year there were rival puppet khans in
different provinces, each a Chinggisid of some line, each maintained by a
non-Chinggisid commander who could not take the title himself. Within twenty
years there were no khans at all, and the men running the provinces stopped
bothering to produce one.

## Why a succession problem was fatal here

Every Chinggisid state had disputed successions and most survived them. The
Ilkhanate did not, and the reason is the thing that had made it work.

It was a conquest elite ruling a Persian population through a Persian
bureaucracy. The bureaucracy was competent, indispensable, and had no
independent claim to authority: viziers ran the finances and were periodically
executed for it — Rashid al-Din, the greatest of them, was put to death in 1318
on a charge of poisoning a previous khan, and his quarter of Tabriz was
plundered.

So the state had a ruling caste whose only qualification was descent, and an
administrative class with no qualification at all beyond usefulness. Remove the
descent and there is nothing left that anybody is obliged to obey. The
Mongol commanders — Jalayirid, Chobanid, Injuid — divided the country between
them, and each of them needed a khan for exactly as long as it took his
neighbours to stop pretending.

## What went with it

Iran did not return to anything. The period from 1335 to Timur's arrival in the
1380s is a patchwork of regional dynasties, and it is the least legible half
century in Iranian history precisely because there was no centre producing
records.

The Ilkhanate had also spent seventy years doing something the Mongols did
nowhere else: rebuilding. Ghazan's fiscal reforms after 1295 had fixed tax
rates, curbed the tax farmers, restored the postal system to something the
peasantry could survive, and re-established the coinage on a uniform standard.
Rashid al-Din's charitable foundation outside Tabriz was a small town of
libraries, a hospital and a scriptorium.

Almost none of that outlasted the dynasty by a generation. The Rab'-i Rashidi
was sacked when its founder fell. Tabriz changed hands repeatedly. The
observatory at Maragha, built for Nasir al-Din al-Tusi at Hülegü's expense and
the best-equipped in the world when it was made, was derelict within a century.

## The one thing that did last

The books.

Rashid al-Din's *Compendium of Chronicles*, commissioned by a Mongol khan and
written by a Jewish convert to Islam serving as vizier, is a history of the
world — the Mongols, the Chinese, the Indians, the Franks, the Jews — assembled
from informants of each nation who were at the court because the court was the
centre of an empire that reached all of them.

Nothing like it had been attempted anywhere, and nothing like it was attempted
again for centuries, because the conditions that made it possible lasted about
forty years and then stopped.
