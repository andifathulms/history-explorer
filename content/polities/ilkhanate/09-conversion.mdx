## Forty years of the wrong religion

The Ilkhanate's first four decades were run by and for a Mongol military elite that was
shamanist and Buddhist, with a marked preference for Christians and Jews in the
administration over Muslims, and with the Persian bureaucratic class doing what
it had done for every conqueror since the Arabs. Nomad pasture requirements
displaced agriculture across large parts of the plateau, and the tax system
collapsed repeatedly under short-term farming of revenue.

## Ghazan

In 1295 Ghazan took the throne and took the faith, and the dynasty that had killed
the caliph became a Muslim dynasty within a year.

It was not a private change of heart, whatever it was privately. His accession
depended on the support of the amir Nawruz, who was a Muslim; the conversion was
public and it was followed by policy. Buddhist temples across Iran — built by his
own father and grandfather — were pulled down or converted. The Christian and Jewish
communities lost the privileged position they had held under a shamanist regime and
went back to the status they had had under Muslim rulers, with the jizya and the
restrictions. The coinage carried the profession of faith. He took the name Mahmud
and the title *padishah of Islam*, and had the Friday prayer said in his name.

He also kept the Mongol law, the Mongol army, the Mongol titles and the Chinggisid
claim, and went on fighting the Mamluks — who now had to explain why a Muslim ruler
was attacking them.

## Why it came too late

The state fell apart after Abu Sa'id died without an heir in 1335, and
nobody conquered it. A state whose ruling caste held power by descent and whose
administrators held none of their own has nothing left to obey once the descent
runs out.

And the conversion did not save it. Ghazan died in 1304 aged thirty-two; his brother
Öljeitü converted to Shi'ism and back; Abu Sa'id came to the throne at twelve and
died at thirty without a son. The dynasty ran out of men faster than it ran out of
money, and the reforms had been the personal project of one ruler and one vizier,
both of whom were dead by 1318 — the vizier executed on a charge of having poisoned
his own master.
