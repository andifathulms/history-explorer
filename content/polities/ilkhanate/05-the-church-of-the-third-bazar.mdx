The Ilkhanate's foundation is usually narrated from the outside — a fortnight's
siege, a caliph executed, an office abolished. There is an account of the same
fortnight by a man who lived under the regime it founded, in a community it did
not kill, and it is a different kind of document.

Bar Hebraeus was a Syriac bishop, later primate of his church in the east, who
spent his career in Mongol-ruled Mesopotamia and knew Ilkhanid officials. He
wrote the chronicle in the 1280s. His account of 1258 is circumstantial, cold,
and in places quietly devastating.

## The first day

Baghdad won it.

The Mongol advance guard under Baiju met the Baghdad troops west of the city and
*the company of Baju Nawin was broken, and the Baghdad troops were triumphantly
victorious*. Then Bar Hebraeus gives a conversation.

In the evening an old commander called Bar Kurar said to a young one called
Dawithdar that God had given them the victory this time, that it was right to go
into their houses and rest, and then to sally out and attack again. *The young
man, however, was puffed up, and did not want to go in, and both of them passed
the night outside.*

The Baghdad camp was on low ground. At midnight the Mongols cut the banks of the
great canal from the Tigris, and the water came down on them; they fled from it
with their bows, their arrows and the sheaths of their swords soaked. At daybreak
the Mongols came back and by the ninth hour the army was destroyed. The old man
was killed. The young man got into the city.

That is the entire military history of the fall of Baghdad, and it turns on a
decision not to go indoors.

## The embassy

With Hulegu's camp east of the city and the bombardment falling on the palace
front, the caliph sent out his last offer, and Bar Hebraeus lists the party: the
vizier, a senior official, and Mar Makika, the Catholicus of the Church of the
East. They were to take gold in abundance, royal possessions and Arabian horses;
to bring the Mongol ambassadors out of prison, dress them well and load them with
gifts; and to ask for the lives of the caliph and his children, explaining that
what had happened had been the work of evil counsellors. If the lives were
granted they would become his slaves and pay tribute.

Hulegu kept the envoys and did not let them go back, and *continued the war with
still greater ferocity*.

The Mongols breached a tower and entered, and the citizens drove them out again.
The next day they took the whole circuit of the walls. The people of Baghdad hid
in their houses and in the underground chambers.

## What the Catholicus did

Then the passage the chronicle exists for.

> And the Mongols drew their swords, and slew all the people of Baghdad — tens of
> thousands of men … And the Catholicus collected all the Christians into the
> church of the Third Bazar, and there he protected them, and none of the
> Christians was injured.

Bar Hebraeus does not pause on that, and immediately writes the next sentence:

> And the wealthy Arabs brought out vast amounts of their riches to the
> Catholicus, that peradventure if they were able to save themselves they might
> retain their property, but all of them were killed.

Two sentences. In the first, a Christian bishop gets his own people through the
sack of a city by putting them in one building. In the second, the Muslims of
that city, understanding exactly what protection was available and on what terms,
bring their money to him — and are killed anyway.

He also notes which contingent did the worst of the killing, and it is not the
Mongols: *the Iberians especially effected a great slaughter* — the Georgian
troops in Hulegu's army, Christians fighting for a pagan khan against a Muslim
capital.

## The seven days

The caliph and his two sons came out on the Saturday. Hulegu had iron fetters put
on him and kept him under guard in a tent for seven days, until he had gone in
person to the palace, inspected the treasures *old and new, which were hidden
away and laid up in stores*, and had them all brought out. Then he passed
judgement and condemned him, and had him sewn into a piece of sackcloth.

A week is how long it took to count it.

## Why this witness matters

Because of what the new empire looked like from the position Bar Hebraeus
occupied.

The Ilkhanate would spend forty years as a pagan state ruling a Muslim country,
favouring Christians and Buddhists, corresponding with popes and kings about a
joint attack on the Mamluks, before Ghazan converted and it became a Persian
Muslim monarchy like its neighbours. For the Syriac and Armenian churches those
were the best decades they had had in six centuries, and the price was being
visibly on the side of the people who had done this.

Bar Hebraeus writes about it without triumph and without much comment. He records
that the Christians were saved and that the Arabs who paid for the same were
killed, in consecutive sentences, and moves on to the next year.
