How the Abbasid caliphate actually ended is better documented than almost any other
event of the century, and the documentation is mostly about a quarrel over a poem,
an argument between two astrologers, and a joke about a beard.

## The riot at Karkh

The village of Karkh, outside Baghdad, was almost entirely Shia. In 1256 a
dissension broke out between its people and the Sunnis, and the caliph's troops,
under his *dawatdar*, sacked it: the houses of the descendants of Ali robbed, the
people of the Prophet's own clan taken prisoner, and the women carried through the
public streets on horses' cruppers with their faces and feet bare.

The caliph's vizier, Ibn al-Alqami, was himself a Shia. He wrote to the chief of
Hilla, the great centre of Shia scholarship, complaining that the dishonour done to
Husayn's household had been done again. The reply he received is the hinge of the
whole episode:

> The heretics must be put to death and destroyed, and their race be uprooted. If
> you will not side with us you will be lost. You will be despised in Baghdad, as
> henna, which delights women, is despised by rough men, and as a ring is despised
> by him who has had his hand cut off.

The vizier then wrote to Hülegü, who had just taken Alamut, pointing out the
weakness of Baghdad and inviting him to come.

He had already prepared the ground. He had persuaded the caliph that with so many
powerful vassals he had no need of such a large force, that the money saved could
buy off the invaders, and that the army should be cut from a hundred thousand men
to twenty thousand.

## The astrologers

Hülegü was not confident. The caliph's troops had beaten Mongol forces twice
already, and he consulted the astrologer Husam al-Din, whom the great khan had sent
with him.

Husam al-Din, a Muslim, predicted six consequences of attacking the House of Abbas:
all the horses would die and the soldiers be struck with pestilence; the sun would
not rise; rain would not fall; there would be hurricanes and earthquakes; plants
would cease to grow; and the emperor would die within the year.

Hülegü made him write all of it down.

Against him stood the Mongol shamans, the amirs, and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi — the
astronomer taken out of Alamut, a Shia, and a man with a grievance of his own. He
had sent the caliph a poem; the vizier had written a sneering note on the back of
it about the composer's knack for putting other people's thoughts into his own
writing; and the poem had ended in the Tigris. He advised that the expedition would
succeed.

Hülegü sent his summons from Hamadan. He denounced the caliph for not helping
against the Isma'ilis, reminded him what had happened to the Khwarazmshahs, the
Seljuks, the rulers of Daylam and the atabegs — all of whom had at some point been
masters of Baghdad — and asked why its gates should be shut to him. *Do not strike
with your fist against an iron spike, nor mistake the sun for a taper.*

## Let us see what you can give us

The city fell in February 1258. The Georgians in Hülegü's army, who had their own
long account to settle, breached the walls; the Georgian chronicle claims they
opened the gates for the Mongols.

On the tenth of February the caliph came out with his three sons and three thousand
others — sayyids, imams, judges, grandees. On the fifteenth, Hülegü went into the
city to look at the palace, had the caliph brought to him, and said:

> You are the master of this house and I am your guest. Let us see what you can
> give us.

The caliph broke some locks and offered two thousand sets of robes, ten thousand
gold dinars and a quantity of jewels. Hülegü would not take them. *It is
unnecessary to point out what is patent; disclose your hidden treasures.* The caliph
told them where to dig, and they found a cistern full of gold pieces of a hundred
miskals each.

Wassaf, the Persian historian, records what the soldiers got: gold and silver
vessels from the caliph's kitchens handled as though they were lead, stuffs and
goods of Greece and Egypt and China, Arab horses and mules, Greek and Alan and
Kipchak boys, Turkish and Chinese and Berber slave girls, past counting. Men broke
their swords off at the hilt and filled the scabbards with gold. Enough of it
reached Shiraz that the wretchedly poor there became rich.

The caliph's harem was counted: seven hundred wives and concubines and twelve
hundred servants. He asked for a hundred of the women back, *on whom the sun and
moon had never shone*, and was allowed to choose; he chose his relatives.

## The two basins

Wassaf tells one more story about the treasure, and it is the best thing in the
account.

The caliph al-Nasir had left behind two cisterns filled with gold. His grandson
al-Mustansir was sitting one day with a trusted companion and said he hoped not to
live long enough to have to spend it, and the companion laughed. Asked why, he said
that he had once come into the grandfather's presence in that same room when one of
the basins was not yet full, and the old man had said he wished to live only until
he had filled them both.

Al-Mustansir spent all of it on good works, including the college that carries his
name.

*The point of this story*, Wassaf adds, *is that when Musta'sim came to the throne
he once more filled up these basins by his avarice, and finally emptied them as
well.*

## The beard

Hülegü might well have spared him. The Muslims around him warned that if the
caliph's blood were shed on the ground there would be an earthquake, and Husam
al-Din said so again.

Tusi answered that no such portents had occurred when John the Baptist was killed,
nor the Prophet Muhammad, nor the Imam Husayn, and that they were not likely to
happen now. Others argued that a living caliph would raise every Muslim in the army
and outside it. Asked for his advice, the vizier — who had brought the whole thing
about — replied only, *the vizier has a long beard*, repeating a jibe that had been
used against him, from the Arab proverb: long in beard, short in wit.

The caliph asked leave to make his ablutions and objected to the five Mongols sent
to escort him. He recited a verse:

> In the morning we dwelt in a house like paradise or heaven,
> In the evening we had no longer a dwelling as if we had been homeless.

On the twentieth of February he was put to death at the village of Waqf with his
eldest son and five eunuchs who had stayed with him. Rashid al-Din does not say how,
and his editor suggests the method was deliberately kept secret.

The Georgian chronicle says that when he was brought in and ordered to kneel he
refused, and stood, and said: *I am an independent sovereign, who am dependent on no
one. If you choose to set me free I will submit to you; if not I will die before
becoming any man's slave.*

Husam al-Din the astrologer was executed four years later, because his prophecies
had proved false.
