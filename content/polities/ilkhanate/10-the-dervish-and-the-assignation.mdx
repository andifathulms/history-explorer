The most detailed account anywhere of how a Mongol state actually extracted money
from a conquered country was written by the man who reformed it, and it is a
description of a machine designed to steal from everybody including itself.

## How it worked before Ghazan

Each province had a *hakim*, a governor, responsible for collecting the taxes. The
amount he had to account for was fixed, and so were the charges on it. Everything
else about the arrangement was an opportunity.

He began by setting aside the sum he owed the treasury. Then, every time a
commissary arrived from the court demanding money, he levied a fresh *qubchur* — a
supplementary collection. He made as many as ten of these in a year, and in some
places twenty.

The more commissaries came, Rashid al-Din records, the better pleased the governor
was: he levied a contribution for their lodging, their entertainment, their food
and their presents, kept a large part of it himself, and gave the rest to the
garrison commander and the treasury clerks to make sure none of it appeared in the
official accounts.

## The circle

What makes the account remarkable is that it explains how the system fed itself.

The provincial revenue was mostly consumed by current expenses and by
*assignations* — treasury orders drawn on a province's taxes, issued to soldiers,
officials, pensioners and creditors. In Khurasan four-fifths of them went unpaid.
The holders would come back to the treasury waving their orders. They would be told
the province still owed part of its contribution and that it must be paid at once,
and a severe ordinance would be issued to that effect.

So they went back to the province with it — and the governor, on their report,
immediately made another levy, explaining to the taxpayers that many commissaries
were waiting and their needs must be supplied. And nobody dared point out that he
should be paying them out of the collections he had already made, which greatly
exceeded the proper quota.

Two-thirds of each new contribution went to the governor and his people. The
holders of the unpaid orders got a third, which barely covered the cost of
travelling back and forth.

Meanwhile the officials at court did not enquire what any province actually
received, and granted assignations indiscriminately — orders that were never paid
but that temporarily satisfied whoever was making a nuisance of himself. The vizier
and the provincial governors had an understanding: unless certain secret marks were
put on a treasury order, no money was produced when it was presented.

The public treasury, in consequence, received nothing from the provinces at all,
and the provinces were charged with a fifth of the normal payments.

## What it did to people

Salaries and pensions simply stopped.

In spring, claimants were told the treasury must be satisfied first and promised
payment at harvest; at harvest they were told to stand aside until the crowd of
commissaries had been paid. Men waited for their salaries, *hungry and naked, from
year's end to year's end*. The adroit ones got half of what was owed from the
governor's officers by signing a receipt for the whole, and then had to take payment
in goods valued at twice their worth. Anyone who got a quarter of what was due
thought himself lucky, because so many got nothing.

If one of them made the journey to the court and laid his case before the council,
and the governor was asked why the claim had not been met out of the first receipts
as it should have been, the governor replied that he had not managed to collect the
whole levy — and issued an order of payment against the arrears, which were arrears
of illegal exactions he had already collected several times over.

Villagers who could not meet the repeated demands abandoned their villages and their
families. If the last levy was then remitted, the clerks entered it in the books as
still owing.

Rashid al-Din's summary is worth having exactly:

> All the viziers who have held authority to this time have shared in this guilt.

Then his illustration. A dervish or a sheikh or some other deserving poor man would
be granted an assignation of five hundred dinars. Overjoyed, he would spend a
hundred of it on a mount and the expenses of the journey to go and collect the rest.

## Why it matters

Two reasons, and the second is the larger.

The first is that this — not the massacres, which were over in a generation — is
what ruined Iran under the Ilkhans. Land goes out of cultivation when the man
working it cannot predict what will be taken; a village empties when the collectors
come twenty times a year; and no amount of cavalry can make a depopulated district
pay.

The second is who wrote it down. Rashid al-Din was Ghazan's vizier, the head of the
system he is describing, and he set out its corruption in detail as the
justification for the reform he was about to carry out — fixed rates published on
stone tablets in the provinces, restrictions on the use of the post, standard
weights and coinage, waste land granted out to get it back under the plough.

A government explaining, in its own official history, exactly how thoroughly it had
been robbing the country, in order to argue that it had stopped. Very few states
have ever produced such a document, and the reason this one did has everything to
do with the kind of man Rashid al-Din was.
