In February 1258 the Mongols took Baghdad and killed the Abbasid caliph. In
September 1260 a Mamluk army destroyed a Mongol force at Ain Jalut in Galilee.
Thirty-one months separate the two, and between them they fixed the shape of
the Middle East for the next two and a half centuries.

## The city

Baghdad had been the caliphal capital since 762. By the thirteenth century the
caliph's actual authority ran over Iraq and not much else, but the office was
the constitutional foundation of Sunni Islam: sultans ruled by his investiture,
and the Friday prayer everywhere from Morocco to Bengal named him.

The siege took a fortnight. The caliph al-Musta'sim surrendered, was made to
watch the sack, and was executed — by the usual account rolled in a carpet and
trampled by horses, because Mongol custom forbade shedding royal blood on the
ground.

The numbers killed in the city are given by the sources in the hundreds of
thousands and cannot be relied on. What is not in doubt is that the libraries
were destroyed and that the office was gone.

## What the office had been holding

Every Sunni state in the world had derived its legal standing from an investiture
by a man who no longer existed. The Mamluks in Cairo dealt with the problem
within three years by producing an Abbasid survivor and installing him as a
caliph of their own, which nobody outside Egypt took very seriously and which
lasted until the Ottomans took Cairo in 1517.

The Hafsids in Tunis were briefly acknowledged as caliphs by the sharif of
Mecca. Delhi went on naming the dead caliph in the khutba for years because
there was nobody else to name.

An institution that had been decorative for three hundred years turned out,
when it was removed, to have been holding up more than anyone realised.

## Ain Jalut

Then the expansion stopped, and the reason is partly the Mamluks and partly the
death of a Great Khan.

Möngke died in 1259 and Hülegü withdrew east with the bulk of his army to be
near the succession, leaving a much smaller force under Kitbuqa in Syria. Qutuz
and Baybars came out of Egypt and destroyed it at Ain Jalut.

It is often called the battle that stopped the Mongols, and it should be
qualified in both directions. The force beaten was a detachment, not the army
that had taken Baghdad, and the Ilkhans went on attacking Syria repeatedly for
sixty years — they took Damascus more than once. But they never held it, and the
Mamluk state that beat them was the one power in the region that had been built
specifically to produce heavy cavalry out of the same steppe the Mongols came
from, bought as boys through the Black Sea trade the Golden Horde controlled.

## And the Golden Horde

Which is where the second consequence comes in.

Berke of the Golden Horde had become a Muslim, and his reaction to the killing
of the caliph by his own cousin was to go to war with him. From 1262 the two
Mongol states fought each other in the Caucasus, and the Golden Horde allied
with the Mamluks — supplying, among other things, the slave recruits the Mamluk
army was made of.

So the Ilkhanate's northern frontier was a Mongol enemy, its western frontier
was an Egyptian enemy the northern one was arming, and its eastern frontier was
the Chagatai ulus, which was also hostile. Within four years of Baghdad, a state
created to conquer the west was fighting on three sides and expanding on none.

What it did instead was govern, and it turned out to be unexpectedly good at it.
