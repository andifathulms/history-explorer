The Ilkhanate lasted seventy-nine years and left more behind it than either of
its longer-lived Mongol cousins, most of it visual and most of it produced by
the collision of two artistic traditions that would not otherwise have met.

## Chinese painting in Persian books

The Mongols moved craftsmen the way they moved troops. Chinese painters,
silk-weavers, potters and their pattern books came west with the administration,
and Persian workshops absorbed them.

What came out of that is the Persian miniature. The dragons and phoenixes, the
cloud-bands, the twisted rocks and the treatment of landscape that define
Persian book painting for the next four centuries are Chinese in origin and
arrived in Iran under the Ilkhans. So did the blue-and-white ceramic tradition,
in both directions: Persian cobalt went east to Jingdezhen, and Chinese
porcelain forms came west.

The great illustrated *Shahnama* made at Tabriz in the 1330s — the epic of
Persian kingship, in a manuscript whose battle scenes are painted with Chinese
brushwork — is the whole transaction in one object. A Mongol court paid Persian
painters using Chinese methods to illustrate a poem about the Iranian kings the
Mongols had replaced.

## The observatory

Maragha did not survive as a building and did survive as a method.

Nasir al-Din al-Tusi persuaded Hülegü to fund an observatory on a scale nobody
had attempted, with instruments, a library said to run to hundreds of thousands
of volumes, and a staff drawn from across the Islamic world and from China. The
planetary models developed there — the Tusi couple and the work of Ibn al-Shatir
after him — are the mathematical devices that turn up, two centuries later and
without acknowledgement, in Copernicus.

How they got to Europe is argued and unresolved. That the same constructions
appear in both places, in the same order, is not.

## The idea of a world history

Rashid al-Din's *Compendium* is the Ilkhanate's most characteristic product
because it could only have been made there.

A history of the world requires informants from the world, and the Ilkhanid
court had them: Chinese scholars, Buddhist monks from Kashmir, a Frankish envoy,
Mongol nobles who remembered the traditions, and a vizier with imperial funds and
imperial authority to ask. The result treats the Franks and the Chinese as
subjects of history in their own right rather than as barbarians at the edge of a
Muslim narrative, which is not how history had been written in Arabic or Persian
before.

It is also, for the Mongols themselves, a primary source of the first
importance. Much of what is known about the early Mongol confederation comes
from a Persian vizier writing in Tabriz from Mongol oral tradition, because the
Mongols had not written it down and the people who could remember it were at
his court.

## The frontier that stayed

The last inheritance is a line on a map.

Sixty years of Ilkhanid war with the Mamluks fixed a frontier in northern Syria
that neither side could cross, and the peace of 1323 confirmed it. That division
— an Iranian power east of the Euphrates, an Egyptian-Syrian power west of it —
is the same division the Ottomans and the Safavids fought over two centuries
later, in the same places.

The state that drew it had ceased to exist within twelve years of the treaty,
and the line outlasted it by six hundred.
