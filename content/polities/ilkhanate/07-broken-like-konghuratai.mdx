Abaqa's chosen heir was his own son Arghun. The throne went instead to
his brother Tegüder, by the plain seniority rule the yasa was supposed to
guarantee - and within two years Tegüder was dead, killed by the nephew
he had passed over, in the same manner as one of his own murdered
supporters. It was the shortest reign of a khan in the dynasty's first
century, and the only one that turned on religion rather than territory.

## A baptised prince who converted the other way

Tegüder had been baptised Nicholas as a boy, before growing into a keen
patron of the Muslim viziers who ran the administration at Tebriz and
Baghdad. Elected on the 6th of May 1282 with Arghun's reluctant consent,
he was formally enthroned on the 21st of June, took the name Sultan
Ahmed and the Islamic royal title, and had a written profession of the
faith read out in Baghdad: "We who are seated on the throne of
sovereignty are Mussulmans... let no one transgress the laws of Islam."
It was the first time a ruling Ilkhan had said any such thing, and it won
him the province's religious establishment along with a wave of
conversions among his own court.

It also cost him the army. Ahmed pursued peace with the Mamluk sultanate
his father and uncle had bled against for two decades, a policy his own
Mongol generals read as surrender to a Muslim enemy for the sake of a
shared religion - and it left the frontier commanders, the men who had
fought at Homs, with every reason to prefer the nephew Ahmed had shut out
of the succession.

## The camp turned on him

Arghun rose against his uncle within two years, and when Ahmed's own
brother-in-arms Konghuratai was killed by Arghun's faction, Ahmed's
position collapsed with him. Fleeing toward the Karaunas - Mongol
irregulars nominally still loyal to him - he was instead seized by them,
stripped, and handed over as a prisoner. Arghun's officers greeted the
capture with the shout Mongol archers used for a winning shot: "Morio."

Brought before judges drawn from Konghuratai's own household, Ahmed
confessed to the charge of ingratitude toward the men who had raised him.
Arghun wanted to spare him for his mother's sake; Konghuratai's mother
and six sons wanted blood, and word arrived that two more princes were
already raising troops at Hamadan in his name. Ahmed was executed on the
10th of August 1284 by having his back broken - the method reserved for
Chinggisid royalty, which killed without shedding a prince's blood on the
ground - the same death Konghuratai himself had been given. A chronicler
wrote that the executioners "broke his back, and at the same time the
hearts of the people of Islam."

## What the reign settled

Nothing about religion, in the end. Ghazan would convert the dynasty for
good a decade later and face no comparable revolt, because by then the
Mongol military elite Ahmed had alienated had had a generation to
reconsider what an Islamic Ilkhan could still deliver them. What Ahmed's
fall actually decided was narrower and more immediate: that the yasa's
seniority rule could put a khan on the throne, but could not keep him
there against an army that preferred someone else's son.
