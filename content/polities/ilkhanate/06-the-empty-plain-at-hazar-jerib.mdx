Abaqa inherited an office his father had built by conquest, and spent
seventeen years defending it on two fronts at once - against a Chagatayid
army that nearly took Khorasan, and against a Mamluk sultanate that beat
his own brother in the field. He held both frontiers, though not by
winning every battle on them.

## Borrowed spies

In 1270 Baraq, khan of Chagatai, crossed the Oxus and overran most of
Khorasan, forbidding his own cavalry to be ridden so the horses would
fatten on the province's best pasture while his troops went to and fro on
bullocks and asses. Abaqa marched from Azerbaijan to meet him, and did it
with a trick before a single arrow flew: three of Baraq's spies were
captured and interrogated at his own tent pole, then let go, one deliberately.
The one who escaped carried back exactly what Abaqa wanted him to see - a
camp near Herat abandoned in apparent panic, its baggage and tents left
standing on the empty plain of Hazar Jerib, as though the whole army of
Azerbaijan had fled north in fear of a rumoured Golden Horde raid on
Tebriz.

Baraq's men spent a day looting the deserted camp and feasting on what
they found in it. Abaqa's army was a few miles away, formed for battle.
When the two forces met the next morning, Baraq's line broke at the third
charge; he was unhorsed, begged his own fleeing officers for a mount, and
survived only because one traded him a horse for a handful of arrows.
Khorasan was Abaqa's again, and the Chagatai ulus did not seriously
threaten it for a generation.

## The frontier that did not stay won

The Mamluk frontier never settled the way the eastern one had. Abaqa's
brother Mongke Temur led a Mongol, Georgian and Armenian army of some
tens of thousands into Syria in the autumn of 1280 and met Sultan
Qalawun's forces near Homs on the 30th of October. The Mongol right broke
the Egyptian left and pursued it to the gates of the city; the Mongol
centre, commanded by Mongke Temur himself, panicked when an Egyptian
officer feigning desertion rode in close enough to unhorse him, and the
prince fled the field before his own victorious wing had returned from
its pursuit. Even the Mamluk chronicler Makrizi called the escape
providence rather than generalship: had the Mongols held the ground
another hour, he wrote, "the troops of Islam were not in a position to
resist."

## Death at Hamadan

Abaqa set out for Baghdad in February 1282, fell ill at Hamadan, and by
most accounts died within days after a night of drinking during which he
believed he saw a raven on a branch and ordered his guard to shoot at it
- a bird nobody else in the room could see. Some sources call it apoplexy,
others poison; his brother Mongke Temur, defeated at Homs two years
earlier, died within a fortnight of him under circumstances just as
disputed. What is not disputed is what he left behind: an office intact
on both frontiers, an eastern one considerably safer than he had found
it, and a succession that would fall, for the first and only time in the
dynasty's history, to a convert to Islam.
