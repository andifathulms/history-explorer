The first thing Hülegü was sent to do was destroy a state that had made itself
invulnerable by making itself unnecessary to attack — and that everybody had
nevertheless been afraid of for a hundred and sixty-six years.

## What Alamut was

The Nizari Isma'ilis held a chain of mountain fortresses across northern Iran and
Syria, of which Alamut in the Elburz was the chief. They had no field army, no
cities and no revenue worth the name. What they had was the ability to put a man
next to anybody.

Two Abbasid caliphs, a Seljuk vizier, princes, generals and the ruler of Jerusalem
had died at their hands; the Crusaders, who met the Syrian branch, brought home the
word *assassin* and an etymology — from hashish — which Howorth repeats and which
has never been substantiated. The Khwarazmshah Jalal al-Din had campaigned against
them and got nowhere. Every power in Iran had at some point paid them or negotiated
with them, and none had taken their castles.

They were also, and this is the part usually left out, a place of learning. The
library at Alamut was one of the great collections of the Islamic world, and the
most learned man of the age, Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, was living there — having left
Baghdad in a rage after the caliph's vizier sneered at one of his poems and the
caliph had it thrown into the Tigris.

## Why they were first

Because the great khan had been asked.

Envoys from the Isma'ilis had appeared at the assembly in Mongolia, and the western
campaign that Möngke gave to his brother had two objects listed in order: destroy
the Isma'ilis, then deal with the caliph. Qadis and lawyers from Qazvin had been
petitioning the Mongol court for years about neighbours they could not defend
themselves against.

A sect that survived by being impossible to besiege had spent a century and a half
making itself the one enemy everybody agreed on.

## How it ended

Not in a great siege. Hülegü brought up Chinese engineers and mangonels, took the
outlying castles one at a time, and negotiated.

The Isma'ili ruler, Rukn al-Din Khurshah, surrendered in 1256 on terms and then
ordered his own garrisons to give up their fortresses, which most of them did. He
was kept alive and used, sent from castle to castle to talk the commanders out, and
when there were no more castles left he was sent east to the great khan, who is
said to have asked why he had been brought so far for nothing, and had him killed
on the road home.

At Alamut the library was burned. Tusi was taken into Hülegü's service, where he
advised on the campaign against Baghdad and afterwards built the observatory at
Maragha.

Howorth's historian Von Hammer notes the coincidence that in the same year two of
the most famous libraries in the East were destroyed — the one at Alamut, and the
one at Medina, which burned in a fire and was then plundered by the Arabs.

## What it proved

That the thing which had protected the Isma'ilis was not their castles but the
unwillingness of any Muslim power to spend a decade on them.

The Mongols were willing. They had siege engineers from China, no local politics to
manage, a general with one instruction, and no reason to make terms. It took about
a year to end an institution that had outlasted the Seljuks, and it demonstrated to
everyone west of the Elburz — including the caliph, who had refused to send troops
to help — what was now possible.

Two years later they found out what it meant.
