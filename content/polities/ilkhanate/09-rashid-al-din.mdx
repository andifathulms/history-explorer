Rashid al-Din Hamadani was a physician, a Jewish convert to Islam, and Ghazan's
vizier. At his master's instruction he compiled the *Jami al-Tawarikh*, the
Compendium of Chronicles, and the result is unlike anything written before it.

It contains a history of the Mongols; then histories of the Arabs, the Persians,
the Turks, the Chinese, the Jews, the Franks and the Indians — each attempted
from informants and texts belonging to those traditions, with Chinese and Kashmiri
and European material gathered through people the empire had at its disposal.
The Frankish section works from a Latin chronicle. The Chinese section used
Chinese scholars resident in Iran.

It is the first serious attempt at a history of the world that does not place the
author's own community at the centre of it, and the reason it was possible is
purely political: for about fifty years one administration had access to
informants from China to the Mediterranean, and a ruler with a motive for
commissioning a universal account of an empire that claimed universal authority.

Rashid al-Din was executed in 1318 on a charge of poisoning a previous ruler, and
his endowed suburb at Tabriz was looted. The Ilkhanate outlived him by seventeen
years.

Nothing about the Ilkhanate's size, reach or duration would lead anyone to
expect it. A conquest state of seventy-nine years, on three hostile frontiers,
produced the first attempt anywhere to write the history of the whole world as
one subject — because for about forty years it was the only place on earth where
informants from all of it were in the same room.

## What was actually in it

The *Jami al-Tawarikh* was commissioned as a history of the Mongols and grew into
something nobody had attempted.

The first part is the history of the Turks and Mongols, drawn from an official
chronicle in Mongolian that Rashid al-Din was given access to and which has not
otherwise survived — which makes him, for the early Mongols, a primary source
standing in for a lost one. The second part was to cover everybody else: the
pre-Islamic kings of Iran, the Prophet and the caliphs, the dynasties of Iran and
Anatolia, the Turks, the Chinese, the Jews, the Franks, and India.

He wrote each section with informants to hand. There was a Chinese scholar at the
court, and a Kashmiri Buddhist monk, and Mongol commanders who remembered the
steppe, and European friars and merchants passing through Tabriz. The section on
the Franks is built on a Latin chronicle. The section on China was checked against
Chinese texts. The section on India came from the monk.

That is the thing worth pausing on. A world history with chapters on China, India
and Europe, written in Persian in Azerbaijan around 1310, each chapter informed by
somebody from the place it describes — because they were all in the same building.

## The suburb

He built the profits of office into a quarter of Tabriz named after himself, the
Rab'-i Rashidi: a foundation with a hospital, a library said to hold sixty thousand
volumes, paper-makers, a scriptorium, lodgings for scholars brought in from Egypt,
Syria, China and India, and an endowment to pay for all of it.

Part of the endowment deed survives and specifies the arrangement for the books.
Two complete copies of his own works, one in Arabic and one in Persian, were to be
made every year, on good paper, checked against the master copy for errors, and
sent out to the cities of the empire.

An author with the state's revenues behind him instituting an annual print run, two
centuries before printing.

## The end

He was a Jewish physician's son from Hamadan who converted to Islam around thirty,
served three Ilkhans, ran the finances of Iran for two decades and wrote the first
history of the world that tried to be one.

In 1318 his enemies accused him of having poisoned the Ilkhan Öljeitü. He was
seventy-three. He was executed, along with his son; his property was confiscated;
the Rab'-i Rashidi was plundered by the mob.

His works survived because he had spent twenty years having copies made and sending
them to other cities.
