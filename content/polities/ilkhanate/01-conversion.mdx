Hulegu's army destroyed the Assassins at Alamut in 1256 and the caliphate at
Baghdad in 1258, and the state he founded governed Iran and Iraq as a Mongol
appanage — the *il-khan*, subordinate khan, notionally deputy to the great khan
in China.

Its first four decades were run by and for a Mongol military elite that was
shamanist and Buddhist, with a marked preference for Christians and Jews in the
administration over Muslims, and with the Persian bureaucratic class doing what
it had done for every conqueror since the Arabs. Nomad pasture requirements
displaced agriculture across large parts of the plateau, and the tax system
collapsed repeatedly under short-term farming of revenue.

Ghazan's conversion to Islam in 1295 reversed the confessional order. Within a
generation the dynasty that had killed the caliph was building mosques, endowing
Shi'i and Sunni foundations, and issuing coinage with the Muslim profession of
faith. Rashid al-Din, Ghazan's vizier — a Jewish convert to Islam and a physician
— wrote the *Jami al-Tawarikh*, a history of the world that treats Mongols,
Franks, Chinese and Indians as subjects of one narrative, which is the sort of
book only an empire of that shape produces.

The Ilkhanate fell apart after Abu Sa'id died without an heir in 1335, into a
fragmentation with no conqueror to name. That is the fourth polity on this site
to end that way, and all four ended by inheritance dispute rather than defeat.
