Twenty years of intermittent war followed the Vespers, waged across
Sicily and the wider Mediterranean between the Angevin claimants to the
whole kingdom and the Aragonese dynasty the island had chosen for itself.
By 1302 both sides were exhausted: Charles of Valois, brother of the King
of France and by then leading the Angevin war effort, found his own army
starving and diseased outside Sicily's fortified towns, while King
Frederick of Sicily, though undefeated in the field, governed a kingdom
too poor to sustain the war much longer.

## Terms that ended a war without erasing a defeat

Negotiators for both sides met and, on the 19th of August 1302, concluded
a peace on terms generous enough to satisfy even the losing Angevin
side's own pride. Frederick would remain King of Sicily for his lifetime,
but could not use the royal title of "King of Sicily" itself - only "King
of Trinacria," a deliberately antiquarian name for the island - and the
crown would revert to the Angevin line, specifically to Charles II's own
heirs, upon his death. Prisoners on both sides were freed without ransom,
and Frederick was to marry Charles's own daughter Leonora, sealing the
peace with a dynastic tie between the two royal houses that had just spent
two decades trying to destroy one another.

## A treaty that outlived its own terms

In practice the reversion clause never took effect: Frederick's own line
held Sicily for generations after his death, in defiance of the peace's
letter. What the 1302 settlement did accomplish, and permanently, was
formalize the split the Vespers had already made real twenty years
earlier - Sicily and the mainland now had two separate kings, two separate
courts, and two separate futures, with Naples confirmed as the seat of
the Angevin crown that no longer ruled the island whose name it still, in
diplomatic memory, claimed to hold.
