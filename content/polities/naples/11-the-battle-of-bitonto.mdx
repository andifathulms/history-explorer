Spanish viceregal rule over Naples gave way, after the War of the Spanish
Succession, to an Austrian Habsburg viceroyalty in 1707 - a change of
masters that left the kingdom's own status as somebody else's dependency
unaltered. That changed in 1734, when the War of the Polish Succession
gave Philip V of Spain's son, the Infant Charles, an opening to claim
Naples and Sicily for the House of Bourbon in his own right rather than as
a Spanish province.

## A rout in a single morning

Charles landed in Puglia with a Spanish army and moved to secure the
kingdom's own submission, while the Austrian garrison commander, Prince
Belmonte, withdrew his outnumbered force of some eight thousand German
soldiers to Bitonto, a fortified town whose walls and ditches he judged
easier to defend than Bari. On the morning of the 25th of May, 1734, the
Spanish commander Montemar deployed a larger, more experienced force
against him; a feigned retreat drew no pursuit, but when Montemar
committed his infantry and cavalry to open battle, the Austrian cavalry
broke and fled toward Bari almost at once, and the rest of Belmonte's
army collapsed within the same morning. Bitonto itself surrendered that
same day, and the news alone was enough to bring the rest of Puglia's
fortresses over to Charles without further fighting.

## A prince proclaimed king of a reunited crown

Weeks later, on the 15th of June, Charles published his father's own
decree ceding all Spanish claims on the Sicilies to him outright, and had
himself proclaimed King of the Two Sicilies - not as a Spanish viceroy or
an Austrian dependency, but as sovereign of a kingdom independent of both
crowns for the first time since Gonzalo de Córdoba had taken Naples for
Ferdinand of Aragon more than two centuries earlier.
The city's own
victory festivities were marred within weeks by a collapsed viewing
platform that killed and wounded hundreds of spectators, a grim footnote
to a reign that would otherwise open a fresh chapter of independent
Neapolitan kingship.
