The 1816 decree that fused Naples and Sicily into a single crown
settled the throne's paperwork without settling the kingdom's politics.
Both halves of the new state rose against the dynasty within four
years - Naples in 1820, put down by an Austrian army; Sicily and then
Naples again in 1848, put down by the king's own artillery, which
earned Ferdinand II the nickname "King Bomba" for turning it on his own
capital. The Kingdom of the Two Sicilies survived both revolts, the second
put down with an artillery bombardment thorough enough to leave
Ferdinand II remembered afterward as "Bomba." It did not survive a
third.

## A dynasty nobody would defend

In the summer of 1860 Giuseppe Garibaldi, at the head of an irregular
volunteer force that had crossed from Sicily with barely a thousand
men in May, marched up the Calabrian coast toward Naples with almost
no resistance. The army meant to stop him would not fight for a king
who had granted a constitution under pressure and was widely expected
to revoke it the moment the pressure lifted; the citizens of Naples,
remembering exactly what had followed the constitutions of 1820 and
1848, refused even to put their own names on the electoral rolls for
fear the list would be used against them later. King Francis II fled
the city by sea on 6 September, and the following morning a count
drove along the main street standing up in his carriage, shouting to
the citizens to gather at the station: the Dictator would arrive at
midday.

## The entry

Garibaldi came into Naples on 7 September with no army behind him at
all. He arrived by train, escorted by a handful of old campaigners
who had fought beside him in South America and Sicily, and the crowd
that met him at the station swept aside the National Guard's own
lines before the official address of welcome could even be finished.
The carriage that finally carried him away from the crush went the
wrong way by the official plan, passing directly under the loaded
cannon of the Carmine fortress, still garrisoned by the king's own
troops. Garibaldi stood up in the open carriage, folded his arms, and
looked the gunners in the face. Some of them saluted. Nobody fired.

The mile-long quay beyond was packed with as many of the city's half
million people as could find standing room, every window of the
commercial buildings along it full of waving arms, every ship in the
harbour crowded with sailors singing. Garibaldi, who had sat through
the ride from the station largely still, stood up as the carriage
reached the water and stayed standing, bareheaded, for the rest of the
journey past two more royal garrisons that could have destroyed him
and did not. "A son of the people," he wrote afterward of his own
entrance, "accompanied by a few friends who called themselves his
aides-de-camp, enter the proud capital acclaimed by its five hundred
thousand inhabitants, whose fierce and irresistible will paralysed an
entire army."

## Two entrances, two months apart

What followed was a siege rather than a revolution: Francis II held
out at Gaeta for months longer, and Garibaldi's own volunteers fought
the last battles of the campaign on the Volturno before regular
Piedmontese forces arrived to finish the war in the kingdom's name
rather than the Dictator's own. On 7 November 1860, Victor Emmanuel of
Piedmont made his own entry into the city that had already crowned
Garibaldi with its acclaim two months earlier - riding in through
pouring rain, with Garibaldi seated beside him in the same carriage,
both men out of temper with each other over a slighted army review the
day before. The rain ruined the triumphal arches built to greet them.
The crowd's enthusiasm was unaffected.

The 1816 decree that had created the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies had
closed five and a half centuries of the mainland's separate history
with no new dynasty, no war, and nothing beyond the stroke of a pen.
Its own end, forty-four years later, was the opposite of that in every
particular - fought over, celebrated in the streets, and sealed not by
a decree withheld from publication but by two rival entrances into the
same capital, a Dictator's and a king's, within two months of each
other.
