Independent Bourbon rule lasted seven decades before Napoleon's own wars
reached the kingdom. In February 1806 a French army under Masséna crossed
the Garigliano, meeting resistance only at the fortresses of Gaeta and
Capua, and reached Naples within a week; the Bourbon court had already
fled to Sicily under British protection. Napoleon's brother Joseph
Bonaparte made his formal entry into the city on the 15th of February,
styling himself Prince of France and Lieutenant of the Emperor, and two
years later, when Joseph moved on to the throne of Spain, Napoleon's own
brother-in-law Joachim Murat took his place. For a decade the mainland
kingdom and the Bourbon court exiled on its own island answered to two
separate crowns for the first time since 1734.

## A restoration on someone else's terms

Murat's own fall in 1815, after he gambled his throne on backing Napoleon
during the Hundred Days, let King Ferdinand return to a Naples the
Congress of Vienna's diplomacy, rather than any Neapolitan choice, had
arranged for him. Austria's own price for restoring him was explicit:
no political persecution of Murat's former officials, recognition of the
sales of state property and honors Joseph and Murat had granted, and the
continued payment of the debts and pensions the French decade had created.
Much of the Napoleonic kingdom's own legal and administrative
architecture - a unified civil and penal code chief among it - survived
the restoration essentially intact, whatever the returning court's own
instincts toward the old order might otherwise have preferred.

## A decree published a year after it was decided

The kingdom's own final act was administrative rather than
military. Sicily had kept its own separate constitution since 1812, and
Ferdinand's own ministers, resentful of the island's continued autonomy,
spent the years after 1815 quietly persuading Britain that Sicily itself
wanted new arrangements, using selectively edited petitions to make their
case. The statute merging the two crowns had been substantially agreed
at the Congress of Vienna itself, but its publication was withheld for
more than a year, appearing only on the 1st of December, 1816: Ferdinand
IV of Naples and Ferdinand III of Sicily became, in a single decree, King
Ferdinand I of a single Kingdom of the Two Sicilies - no new dynasty, no
war, and no ceremony beyond the stroke of a pen closing five and a half
centuries of a mainland kingdom the Vespers had first split from the
island in 1282.
