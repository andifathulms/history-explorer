Alfonso the Magnanimous's own united crown of Naples, Sicily and Aragon
did not survive his death intact: he left Naples to an illegitimate son,
Ferrante, while Sicily and Aragon passed instead to his brother. Six
decades later, in 1500, King Louis XII of France and King Ferdinand of
Aragon - a cousin of Naples's own reigning King Frederick, Ferrante's
successor - concluded a secret treaty at Granada to invade and divide
Frederick's kingdom between them, while both continued to profess
friendship to Frederick himself.

## A bull dividing a kingdom that did not yet know it

Pope Alexander VI, no friend to Frederick after an earlier marriage
proposal had been refused, issued a bull on the 25th of June, 1501,
formally depriving Frederick of his throne and splitting the kingdom
between Louis, who took Naples itself and the title of king, and
Ferdinand, who took Calabria and Apulia under the lesser style of Duke.
Frederick learned of the partition only as French troops under Aubigny
were already marching on his own territory, and belatedly appealed to
Ferdinand for the help Ferdinand had secretly agreed to deny him. Capua
fell to a brutal French sack in July; Naples itself surrendered on terms
days later, and Frederick, choosing exile in France over any further
resistance, gave up his crown for a French duchy and a pension.

## Allies who could not share the spoils

The victors soon fell to fighting each other over the partition's own
unclear boundaries, since the treaty had never precisely defined which
provinces belonged to which crown. War between France and Spain over the
disputed territory dragged on for two more years, until Gonzalo de
Córdoba - the Spanish commander already known across Europe simply as
"the Great Captain" - broke a French army in the field despite a peace
already agreed upon by the two kings' own representatives, choosing
military advantage over a truce he judged not yet binding. He marched
directly on Naples, and on the 14th of May, 1503, entered the city to
wide rejoicing, receiving its oath of allegiance to King Ferdinand of
Aragon the following day. Naples would not have a king who actually
resided within it again for more than two centuries, its Aragonese
throne folded permanently into the wider Spanish crown Ferdinand ruled
alongside Castile.
