By 1647 a century and a half of Spanish viceregal rule had loaded Naples
with taxes on nearly every article of daily life, and a long-standing
gabelle on fruit had become a particular grievance among the city's own
fruit-sellers and their customers. On the 7th of July that year, a
twenty-four-year-old fisherman and fish-seller from the Piazza del
Mercato named Tomaso Aniello - known to everyone as Masaniello - turned a
routine shopkeepers' protest against the fruit tax into something the
viceroy's own government could not contain.

## A crowd taught its own slogans

Masaniello had spent days coaching the boys of the market quarter to
chant fixed prices for oil, bread, cheese and meat alongside a carefully
balanced cry of loyalty and defiance: long life to God, the Virgin, the
Pope and the King of Spain, and death to bad government. When a scuffle
broke out between fruit-sellers refusing to pay the gabelle and the
official sent to enforce it, Masaniello threw himself into the crowd,
and what had been a market dispute became, within hours, a citywide
uprising against the tax collectors and the households of the wealthy who
had profited from farming the gabelle.

## An unelected captain-general

For the next several days Masaniello, backed by a self-organized militia
of armed boys and market people, effectively ran the city in the
viceroy's own name, forcing the abolition of the fruit gabelle and
negotiating directly with the Spanish viceroy and the city's own
archbishop from a position no elected office had ever given a fisherman
before. But the strain told quickly: contemporaries described him within
days making wild, erratic public displays - throwing himself fully
clothed into the sea, brandishing his sword at imagined enemies - that
cost him the confidence of the same people who had followed him.

## Shot dead at his own altar

On the tenth day, with armed bands already forming in the viceroy's own
favor, Masaniello took refuge in the Church of Carmine to hear Mass on
its feast day, telling the archbishop he sensed the people were about to
abandon him and asking only for a solemn procession before he died. Not
long after, a group of armed men found him nearly alone in the church
cloister and shot him four times. His last words, by the account of a
witness who set the whole episode down within months, were a cry against
"ungrateful traitors." His head was cut off and paraded on a lance
through streets that, ten days earlier, had cried his name as their
liberator.
