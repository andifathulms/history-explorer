Sixteen years of Angevin rule left Sicily heavily taxed and administered
by French officials who treated the island's own nobility and common
people alike with open contempt. Palermo, the ancient capital, had
suffered worst of all: its Justiciary and his subordinates had recently
grown bolder still, dragging citizens from a church during Holy Week
itself to collect unpaid taxes while the congregation looked on. By the
spring of 1282 the kingdom's barons and townspeople alike needed only a
spark.

## A search that went too far

That spark came on Easter Tuesday, the 31st of March, at the festival of
Santo Spirito outside Palermo's walls, where crowds of citizens had
gathered among the spring flowers to picnic, dance and prepare for
evening prayers. French soldiers mingling in the crowd, "to keep the
peace" as they put it, began searching young men for hidden weapons and
making free with the women present. When a soldier named Drouet seized a
young married woman and searched her under the pretext of a weapons
check, her husband cried "Death to the French!" and a young bystander -
his name never recorded - drew a knife and killed Drouet on the spot.

## A city, then an island, in open revolt

The cry spread through the crowd and then through the whole city within
hours: French soldiers and settlers were hunted down and killed wherever
they could be found, reportedly including some Sicilian women who had
married Frenchmen. Within weeks nearly every town in Sicily had followed
Palermo's example, and Charles's viceroy and his officials were driven
from the island entirely. Charles retained the mainland - Naples,
Apulia, Calabria, the Abruzzi - but never recovered Sicily itself, whose
own rebels turned instead to Peter III of Aragon, married to Manfred's
daughter, to defend the independence the Vespers had won them. What had
been one Kingdom of Sicily was, in practice if not yet in treaty, now
two.
