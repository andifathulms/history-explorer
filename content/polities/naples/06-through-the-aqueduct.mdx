The decades after Joanna I's murder saw the Angevin succession contested
by rival branches of her own family, and by the 1420s the childless queen
Joanna II had adopted first one foreign prince and then another as her
heir, finally settling on René of Anjou. René's own claim was contested
from the start by Alfonso V of Aragon, already king of Sicily, and the war
between them dragged on for years - until Alfonso's patient diplomacy
began peeling away René's own commanders one by one, most importantly the
condottiere Antonio Caldora, whose desertion in 1441 left René's own
position in the kingdom badly weakened.

## A priest, an island, and eighty thousand crowns

Fortune then handed Alfonso a stroke of luck disguised as a trifle: in
1442 a priest on the small island of Capri offered to surrender it to
him without a fight. The gain seemed minor until a French supply galley,
mistaking Capri for still being in René's hands, put in there and was
captured along with eighty thousand crowns meant to keep René's own war
effort funded - a loss René could not make good.

## A city taken from beneath its own streets

Alfonso pressed his advantage, besieging Naples itself and reducing the
outlying towns of Pozzuoli and Torre del Greco one by one. The city held
out under a garrison too thin to properly man its own walls, until
Alfonso's own soldiers found a way in through the city's Roman-era
aqueduct and opened its gates from within on the 2nd of June, 1442.
Alfonso's own troops began to sack the city in the heat of the moment,
but the king himself rode through the streets at the head of a mounted
guard and threatened death to any soldier who harmed a citizen, cutting
the pillage short after only four hours. René fled by ship to Florence,
then to the papal court, and finally home to France, ending a century and
a half of native Angevin rule and uniting Naples with Sicily and Aragon
under a single Iberian crown for the first time since the Vespers had
split them.
