The Kingdom of Naples begins, in one sense, as an ending: the collapse of
the last Hohenstaufen ruler of the old Norman Kingdom of Sicily. Manfred,
an illegitimate son of Emperor Frederick II, had held the crown against a
hostile papacy for years, but the papal court, unable to defeat him
directly, offered the kingdom instead to Charles of Anjou, a younger
brother of the King of France, in exchange for an annual tribute and a
free hand to unseat Manfred by force.

## An army that could not afford to wait

Charles crossed the Alps in the autumn of 1265 with an army of French and
Italian troops and was crowned at the Vatican on the 6th of January 1266,
but winter and short supplies left him no real choice but to force a
decision quickly or disband his own army. He advanced south through
Ceperano and San Germano, meeting little resistance, until he reached
Benevento, where Manfred had gathered Germans, Apulians and the Muslim
soldiers of Lucera - men who, hated everywhere else in the kingdom, had
remained loyal to him alone.

## A body flung to the dogs

The battle was hard-fought, but the French carried the field, and Manfred,
rather than flee, rode into the thick of the fighting and was killed among
the slain. His own soldiers raised a pile of stones over his body on the
battlefield, but even that rough burial was denied him: the papal legate
travelling with Charles's army refused Manfred a Christian grave as an
excommunicate, and his remains were, according to the poet who later
placed him in Purgatory rather than Hell, left exposed by the River Verde
outside the kingdom's own border. Naples itself welcomed the victor
without a fight, and within weeks the whole of Sicily, Apulia and
Calabria had submitted to Angevin rule - all but the Muslim garrison of
Lucera, which held out alone.
