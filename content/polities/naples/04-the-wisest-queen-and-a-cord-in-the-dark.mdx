Joanna I inherited the throne of Naples as a girl of sixteen in 1343,
raised at her grandfather King Robert's own court alongside a prudent and
respected queen, Sancia. Her own long reign, spanning nearly four decades,
would eventually earn her the praise of the two most eminent lawyers of
her age, who called her the wisest queen ever to sit on a throne - a
verdict grounded in the laws she herself left behind, all aimed at
restoring the discipline of courts and magistracies that had slipped
during her grandfather's final years.

## A husband strangled in his own palace

That reputation survived a scandal that would have ended a lesser
monarch's rule. Joanna's first husband, Andrew of Hungary, was young,
resented by the Neapolitan court, and - by his own Hungarian retinue's
account - poorly treated by it in turn. On the night of 18 September
1345, while the royal couple lodged at Aversa, a gentleman summoned
Andrew from the queen's own bedchamber with word of urgent business; in
the hall beyond, a cord was thrown around his neck, and he was strangled
and thrown from a window while his own Hungarian guard slept off the
night's wine. No one in the city dared ask who was responsible. Popular
suspicion fell on Joanna herself for years afterward, and even generations
later some historians still repeated the charge - but Giannone himself,
weighing the praise later legal scholars gave her without qualification,
judged that the best-informed men of her own age believed her innocent of
her husband's murder.

## A reign that outlasted the scandal

Whatever the truth of Andrew's death, it did not end Joanna's own rule.
She married twice more, navigated the interests of Hungary, Provence,
Avignon and her own restless Neapolitan barons for another thirty-seven
years, and administered a kingdom that, by the account of the jurists who
served under her, was as well and justly governed as any in Europe of her
age - the high-water mark of Angevin Naples before the crisis that would
end her own life.
