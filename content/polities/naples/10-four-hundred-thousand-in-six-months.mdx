Naples had survived wars, banditry, Turkish raids, drought and
earthquakes in the decade after Masaniello's revolt, but 1656 brought a
catastrophe that made all of it look minor by comparison: a plague
that killed roughly 400,000 people in the capital alone within six
months, a toll the chronicler Giannone contrasted directly with an
earlier sixteenth-century epidemic that had taken almost two years to
carry off a sixth as many.

## A cover-up that let the plague spread

The contagion arrived, by most accounts, aboard a ship of soldiers from
plague-stricken Sardinia that was given clean bills of health despite
standing quarantine orders - whether through the harbor guards'
negligence or the Viceroy's own order to avoid delaying the troops is
disputed. The first deaths were misattributed to fevers and apoplexy
by physicians reluctant to name the real cause; one doctor who insisted
publicly that the disease was pestilential was thrown into a dungeon
on the Viceroy's orders and released only, as a mercy, to go home and
die. The Archbishop, Cardinal Filomarino, eventually felt compelled to
warn the Viceroy directly that more caution was needed, but the Count
of Castrillo, anxious not to interrupt reinforcements he was sending to
the ongoing war in Milan, tried to suppress the reports rather than
act on them until the disease was already spreading through the
city's poorest quarters.

## A hermitage built by a dying city

As the epidemic worsened, panicked Neapolitans turned to public
penance and construction: acting on a dead nun's prophecy that a
hermitage built on the slope of San Martino's hill would end the
plague, nobles and commoners alike stripped themselves of jewelry and
turned out in crowds to dig, carry stone, and donate gold, silver and
copper by the barrel-load - a mass mobilization that, by bringing
thousands of infected and uninfected citizens into close contact,
almost certainly spread the contagion faster than it slowed it. Rumors
spread that the plague was a deliberate Spanish reprisal for the
Masaniello revolt, since the wealthier quarters where the Spanish
garrison lived remained largely untouched while the poorest wards were
devastated; mobs hunted for supposed poison-spreaders in the streets,
adding lynchings to the epidemic's own death toll before the disease
finally ran its course and a new viceroy was sent to govern what was
left of the city.

## A weakened kingdom passed to a new crown

The plague permanently altered Naples's demographic weight within the
Spanish system at exactly the moment Spain's own global power was
entering its long decline. When the Spanish Habsburg line finally died
out with Charles II in 1700, the War of the Spanish Succession that
followed transferred the Kingdom of Naples not to independence but to
a new set of foreign masters, the Austrian Habsburgs, in 1707 - one
viceroyalty succeeding another without the throne ever returning to a
resident king, a condition that would not end until a Spanish Bourbon
prince finally won it back by force at Bitonto in 1734.
