Don Pedro de Toledo governed Naples as viceroy for more than twenty
years from 1532, reforming its courts and building the long avenue
that still carries his name, but the achievement he is remembered for
in the city's own popular memory is different: his attempt, at the
Emperor Charles V's direction, to introduce the Spanish Inquisition,
and the revolt that stopped him.

## An edict torn from the cathedral door

Rumors of the Inquisition's coming had circulated for years before an
edict actually appeared, posted on the doors of Naples Cathedral one
day in Lent 1547. A crowd tore it down within hours. Toledo, summoning
the city's own elected officials, downplayed the matter and blamed the
unrest on mob disorder rather than address the Inquisition directly -
a evasion the Neapolitans read correctly. When a second, far more
explicit edict went up on 11 May, the city rose in earnest. Crowds ran
through the streets shouting "To arms, to arms," tore down the notice,
and forced out the city's own sitting officials as suspected
collaborators with the viceroy, replacing them with men the mob
trusted. Toledo, enraged, returned to Naples and ordered the ringleaders
prosecuted - among them Tommaso Anello, a prominent citizen of the
Mercato district who had personally torn the first edict from the
cathedral door, and whose given name a very different Neapolitan
revolutionary would echo exactly a century later.

## Cannon fired on the city that bore his name

The confrontation escalated further when Spanish garrison troops,
brought into the city under Toledo's orders, opened fire on a crowd
near Castel Nuovo and pursued them through the streets, killing
indiscriminately; the enraged population rang the alarm bell of San
Lorenzo, and Naples's own royal forts answered by cannonading the city
they were meant to defend. Toledo accused the city of open rebellion;
the city's own deputies accused him of turning Spanish arms against
his sovereign's subjects as if Naples belonged to a hostile power
rather than the Emperor's own crown. The city's leading jurists ruled
that armed resistance to an "incensed minister" was not rebellion
against the King himself, and the standoff ended without the
Inquisition ever being formally established in Naples - one of the few
outright popular victories the viceroyalty's subjects won against
Madrid's authority in two centuries of Spanish rule, and a memory of
resistance to arbitrary viceregal power that the city would draw on
again when Masaniello's own revolt broke out a hundred years later.
