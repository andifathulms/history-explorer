Joanna I had no surviving son, and in her final years she settled the
succession on Charles of Durazzo, a cousin she and her third husband had
raised at their own court and married to her niece. It was a fatal
misjudgment. When Joanna, in a late dispute over the wider politics of
the Papal Schism, tried to adopt a rival heir instead, Charles moved
first: he marched on Naples, and the city and its barons swore their
allegiance to him in the archbishop's own palace before Joanna could
organize any resistance.

## A letter asking how she should die

Charles placed guards around the queen and, within days, sent her under
guard to the remote castle of Muro in the Basilicata, while her adopted
heir Otho was confined separately. He then wrote to the King of Hungary -
whose own brother, Andrew, Joanna had been suspected of having murdered
thirty-seven years earlier - to ask how she should be dealt with. The
answer that came back was that she should be treated exactly as Andrew
himself had been.

## A body displayed for seven days

In 1382, Charles had Joanna smothered with a bolster in her cell at Muro.
Her body was carried to Naples and left unburied and on public display
for seven days in the Church of Santa Chiara, a deliberate message to
whatever support her own partisans still had, before she was finally
buried near her grandfather's tomb without ceremony. Giannone, writing
nearly three and a half centuries later, still felt the need to defend
her reputation once more: whatever charge had shadowed her over Andrew's
death, he wrote, nothing in the rest of her life gave any honest witness
cause to think her anything but blameless. Her murder opened four decades
of rival Angevin claims to the throne she left behind - a succession
crisis that would end only when a foreign king took the kingdom by force.
