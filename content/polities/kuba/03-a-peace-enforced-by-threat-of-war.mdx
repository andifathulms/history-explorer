title: A peace enforced by threat of war

Before ascending the throne, Shamba Bolongongo left his own mother's court
and traveled for several years among neighboring peoples to the west - the
Bapende, the Ambono and the Babinji - against his mother's own wishes,
telling her that a future king ought to know the virtues and vices of
other nations rather than only his own. When he returned and took power,
he brought back more than stories: the technique of weaving raffia cloth,
in place of the bark cloth his own people had used until then; cassava, a
crop locusts could not easily destroy, to plant alongside the traditional
maize and millet; the game of mancala, said to have curbed his subjects'
appetite for gambling; and the art of embroidery, which the Bushong went
on to develop further than the people who had first taught it to them.

## A warrior nation that gave up its own weapon

Shamba's most consequential act as king was to renounce war outright,
restricting his own kingdom's forces to police duty and abolishing the use
of bows, arrows and the shongo - a throwing-knife so central to Bushong
military identity that neighboring peoples called them Bakuba, "the
people of the lightning," after it, and the kingdom's own name, Bushongo,
may itself derive from the same weapon's name. The shongo's own design
traced back to peoples of the Ubangi-Chad region far to the north,
evidence that the Bushong themselves had migrated a long distance to
reach the land Shyaam later united - a detail Torday only pieced together
when the reigning king, generations later, drew the weapon's outline in
sand for him and Torday recognized a throwing-knife style unknown among
Bantu-speaking peoples generally.

## Peace kept by the threat of overwhelming retaliation

Renouncing war did not mean tolerating attacks on Bushong subjects. When
a trader was killed in some distant village, Shamba's own response was
swift: the war-horn sounded, and Bushong forces descended on the
offending village in overwhelming numbers, burning it, destroying its
crops and cutting down its plantain trees - but under strict orders that
no man, woman or child was to be killed in the process, "for are they not
the children of God, and have they not a right to live?" Only those who
actively resisted were harmed; the rest were compelled by weight of
numbers to submit, pay a fine and hand over hostages. It was, in effect,
a policy of guaranteed and total economic retaliation substituted for the
lethal warfare the kingdom had formally renounced - severe enough to deter
attacks on Bushong traders, who came to travel armed with nothing but the
ceremonial leaf-shaped knife of state, relying on the mere fact of being
recognized as a subject of the Nyimi to keep them safe among strangers.

## Reforming the council that would one day check the throne

Shamba's reforms reached into the kingdom's own government as well as its
military posture. He reorganized the Great Council into essentially the
form it still held centuries later, opening it to a representative from
every craft guild in the kingdom - a decision that both honored Bushong
artisans directly and built the same council that later generations of
kings would have to negotiate with, rather than simply command. Under his
own rule, by every account Torday collected from elders in both the
capital and the outlying provinces alike, Bushong arts and crafts reached
a level of development the surrounding region never matched.
