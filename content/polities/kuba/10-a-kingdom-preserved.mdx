Jan Vansina, the scholar most responsible for reconstructing Kuba history
in detail, titled a chapter of his own study of the colonial period "A
Kingdom Preserved" - and called the Kuba kingdom the only precolonial
African state of its kind and scale to survive nearly intact,
"encapsulated in the colony's administrative grid like a fly in amber."
The description is a striking one precisely because it was so rarely true
anywhere else on the continent: colonial administrations elsewhere
routinely dismantled, restructured or hollowed out the kingdoms they
absorbed, even where they kept a chief's title in place for
convenience. Kuba's own nine provinces, its courts, its councils of
kolm, kept functioning as recognizably the same institutions Shamba
Bolongongo's reforms had shaped, all the way through to Congolese
independence in 1960.

## A secession that failed before the kingdom did

Independence did not bring stability to the wider region the kingdom sat
within. Between 1960 and 1962, the Kasai region attempted an ill-fated
secession from the newly independent Congo, an effort that collapsed
within two years amid the broader chaos of the Congo's own post-
independence crisis. Whatever the secession's own aims had been, it left
the kingdom's institutions standing much as it had found them - shaken by
the surrounding turmoil, but not yet dismantled by it.

## What survived the kingdom, and what did not

Sustained institutional decline came later, and from a different source
than colonial rule or the failed secession combined: the kleptocratic
regime of Joseph Mobutu, in power from 1965 to 1997, oversaw a severe and
prolonged neglect of infrastructure and public services across the whole
Kasai region, Kuba territory included. Even so, the continuity researchers
have documented in Kuba institutions and culture across the colonial
period, and into the post-colonial one, stands in genuine contrast to
what happened to comparable kingdoms elsewhere on the continent, where
colonial conquest or restructuring had typically broken the institutional
thread outright. A kingdom founded by an outsider who preferred
negotiation to war left behind, three and a half centuries later, one of
the few precolonial African political structures a visitor could still
recognize as substantially the same one its own founder had built.
