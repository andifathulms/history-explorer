The Congo passed from the personal rule of Belgium's King Leopold II to
the direct control of the Belgian state in 1908, and two years later, in
1910, a Belgian colonial decree established a system of indirect rule
across the new colony, formally subordinating African chiefs and
traditional institutions everywhere as the lowest tier of colonial
government. The decree marked, in every legal sense that mattered, the
end of Kuba independence: the kingdom's own king and councils were no
longer a sovereign government answering to nobody, but the bottom rung of
a Belgian administrative hierarchy, obliged to enforce forced crop
quotas, head taxes and compulsory labor drafts on their own subjects like
every other traditional authority in the colony.

## Ordinary colonial rule, extraordinary institutional survival

In the ordinary sense of colonial administration, Kuba's own experience
under the 1910 decree had, in the words of the historian Jan Vansina,
"a great deal in common with colonial rule elsewhere in Congo" - the same
impositions, the same extraction, the same subordination to a distant
colonial capital. What did not follow the
ordinary pattern was what happened to the kingdom's own governing
apparatus underneath that subordination. Rather than being swept aside
and replaced with an entirely new colonial administrative structure, as
happened to most of the large precolonial kingdoms elsewhere on the
continent, Kuba's nine provinces, its councils of kolm, its courts and its
king continued to function largely as they had before 1910 - now
administering Belgian impositions rather than the kingdom's own
sovereign policy, but administering them through the same institutions
Shamba Bolongongo's own reforms had shaped nearly three centuries
earlier.

## A distance that worked in the kingdom's own favor

Part of the explanation lay simply in geography and colonial priorities.
Belgian rule in the Congo operated with a very small number of colonial
officials spread across an enormous territory, and the Kasai region sat
well away from the main hubs of Belgian administrative attention.
Historians studying the period generally agree that Kasai as a whole was
less affected by direct colonial intervention than other parts of the
colony - a circumstance of distance and neglect that happened to work in
favor of institutions the colonial state had no strong incentive to
dismantle so long as they kept functioning well enough to deliver the
taxes and labor the decree required.
