title: A ruse and an eclipse

Shamba's own successors inherited a kingdom already remade by his
reforms, and the two centuries that followed - anchored at one end by
a king's cannibal war and at the other by a solar eclipse nobody
realized they were witnessing history through - were, by the oral
record's own honest admission, mostly quiet ones.

## A mother's killer, and an empire founded elsewhere

Shamba was succeeded by Bongo Lenge, and decline began almost at once:
the Isambo people broke away from the kingdom in revolt. More
consequential still was an act of private violence at the edges of the
court. One of Bongo Lenge's own sons, serving as viceroy among the
Pianga, killed his mother in a fit of rage and fled the country with
his own followers rather than face judgment. Bushong tradition, as
Torday's informants told it, identified this exile as the same figure
remembered elsewhere as the hunter who founded the Lunda empire far to
the south - a claim the Lunda court's own traditions tell differently,
tracing their founding hunter-husband to the Luba court instead rather
than to Bushong. Both stories cannot be literally true at once, but
both agree on the shape of the story: a foreign hunter-prince who
married into a ruling lineage and built a kingdom on the strength of
it.

## A war fought by trick rather than by force

Bongo Lenge's successor, Bom Bosh, faced a more direct threat: bands of
Bankutu raiders, described in Bushong memory as forest cannibals, who
avoided open battle by attacking villages at night and vanishing before
dawn. Unable to catch a night-raiding enemy in the field, Bom Bosh
arranged a ruse with one of the Bashoba chiefs already settled within
his kingdom - the chief would pretend to join the Bankutu and lure them
into attacking the capital in daylight, then switch sides mid-battle
and catch the raiders between two forces. The plan worked exactly as
designed. When the surviving Bankutu were brought before him, Bom Bosh
asked why they had killed so many people; told that his young warriors
had simply wanted glory, he had every prisoner executed on the spot.

## An eclipse, and the honest gaps around it

Bom Bosh's own successor was Bo Kama Bomanchala, the ninety-eighth
chief in the Bushong king list and the ruler in whose reign, by the
elders' own account, "nothing remarkable happened" beyond a brief
darkening of the noon sun - the total solar eclipse of 30 March 1680
that let Torday anchor the entire oral chronology to a real calendar
date. The reigns that followed are, by the same oral record's own
honest admission, mostly a blank: "seven other kings" are named in
sequence with no events attached to them at all, until a ruler called
Koto Che answered an appeal from subjects under attack by a Luba army.
A warrior named Masakana asked Koto Che's permission to fight the
entire Luba force single-handed, and by the court's own telling did
exactly that - killing enemies with each hand in turn until the
survivors surrendered and Koto Che's kingdom absorbed them as subjects.
After Koto Che, the record goes quiet again: "three uneventful reigns"
pass before Mikope Mbula took the throne and, falling in love with an
enslaved woman, abolished the kingdom's own prohibition on marriage
between free people and slaves so that he could marry her himself - a
reign remembered as both happy and unusually long, and the last
before the ruler who would still be on the throne when the first
European reached the kingdom's own frontier.
