Kuba kingship left behind a physical record most oral traditions cannot
match: a carved wooden statue commissioned for each reign, each one
showing the king seated with a personal object that defined how he wanted
to be remembered. Shamba Bolongongo's own statue, the finest of the set
Torday examined at Mushenge in 1907, shows him seated peacefully in front
of a lela board, the game he had brought back from his travels in the
west - a king remembered, in the very object commissioned to represent
him, as a man of games and diplomacy rather than of war. Other statues in
the same royal line showed a king celebrated as a great smith with his
own anvil, and another, known as a dancer, with a drum. Torday could tell
at a glance that the statues were not the work of a single artist working
across generations - each one carried its own distinct style, evidence of
different sculptors working, presumably, close to the time of the reign
each statue commemorated.

## An object that outranked its own maker's caution

Torday resolved, on seeing Shamba's own statue, that it should reach the
British Museum whatever the cost of arranging it - a decision made easier
by the trust he had built with the reigning king, who had told Torday
repeatedly that Shamba represented the ideal he himself wanted to
emulate, and who chafed openly against his own elders' resistance to
following that same example. The statue's departure from Mushenge, in the
years following Torday's expedition, was itself an act requiring the
king's own direct cooperation - a work of state regalia, representing the
kingdom's own most celebrated ruler, agreed to leave the country that had
kept and venerated it for nearly three centuries.

## A tradition that recorded change as it happened

The statues, taken as a set, function as something between portraiture and
constitution: each one fixes, in wood, what a particular reign was
remembered for at the moment its own successor commissioned the work, in
a way spoken tradition alone could shift and reshape with each retelling.
Long before any outside visitor arrived to ask what the kingdom's own
history had been, Kuba's kings had already been answering that question
for themselves, one carved figure at a time.
