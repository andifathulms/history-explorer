By the late nineteenth century, the kingdom Shyaam had founded two and a
half centuries earlier had grown into one of the most thoroughly
bureaucratized states anywhere in precolonial sub-Saharan Africa. Its
territory was divided into nine provinces, themselves subdivided into
counties each headed by its own chief, and its central administration at
the capital, Mushenge, employed more than a hundred full-time officials
by the time colonization began.

## A hundred and twenty titles, and a king who could not simply command

Beneath the king stood a system of title-holders called kolm - a hundred
and twenty distinct titles by the late nineteenth century. Some were
reserved for members of eighteen aristocratic clans, but most were awarded
on merit, with status earned rather than inherited. The king's own
authority was checked directly by the most powerful of four courtly
councils, the ishyaaml, which excluded the king himself, ran under fixed
quorum rules, and had the standing power to veto royal orders and edicts
outright. A vetoed matter went next to the mbok ilaam, a council that
included every kolm and met in a dedicated square deep inside the palace
to work out a compromise between king and council. Two further councils,
the ibaanc and the iyoot, handled other specific circumstances - the
iyoot, for instance, was where the king informed the kolm of military
developments and decisions. As one scholar who spent decades studying the
kingdom's own institutions put it, the power of the kolm was balanced
against that of the king through overlapping council memberships and
through the representation, in the ishyaaml, of every faction in the
kingdom except the king's own potential successors.

## Courts, juries and a right of appeal

Justice ran through an equally layered system. Minor disputes went to
informal moots, gatherings of relatives and kin who heard evidence and
arbitrated in public. More serious cases went to courts, where a panel of
judges chosen for their expertise in the matter at hand heard the case
after the complainant deposited seven hundred cowrie shells to bring the
action. From a basic court, appeal ran upward to a higher court presided
over by a kolm called a baang, and from there to a court presided over by
the kikaam, the kingdom's most senior bureaucrat. A final tier of appeal,
described by one researcher as the kingdom's own "supreme court," brought
together the senior members of the eighteen aristocratic clans, with the
king present only as a spectator empowered to grant a last-minute stay of
execution. Every case of murder in the kingdom went directly to this
highest court rather than working its way up through the lower tiers.

## Taxes, censuses and forced labor, kept in one ledger

The state that ran this judicial system also ran an annual census
recording births and deaths across its territory, levied yearly taxes on
every village, and required corvée labor for building and maintaining
roads, bridges, and the capital itself. By the time European visitors
began describing it directly, in the last years of the nineteenth
century, a formally organized police force - forty men in Bushong
territory alone, commanded by the king's own son, with two policemen
posted in every village - enforced a body of law and administration that
had been two and a half centuries in the building.
