title: An eclipse fixes a date

Emil Torday, an ethnographer traveling among the Bushong in 1907 and 1908,
ran into the ordinary difficulty of working with a long oral king list:
nobody among his informants could say with any confidence how long ago a
given reign had actually happened. Elders could recite the sequence of
named chiefs - ninety-eight of them, by the point Torday reached in his
own inquiry - but pressed for a date, the answer was always some
variation of "long, long ago."

## A scorpion sting that wasn't

Then, while elders were recounting the notable events of each reign in
turn, they reached the ninety-eighth chief, a man named Bo Kama
Bomanchala, and mentioned that nothing remarkable had happened during his
reign at all - except that one day, at noon, the sun had gone dark for a
short time. Torday's own reaction was immediate and, to the elders
listening, alarming: he leapt up in excitement, certain he had just been
handed exactly the kind of fixed astronomical event that could anchor an
entire oral chronology to a real calendar date. The elders, watching a
foreign visitor suddenly lose his composure over an old story about a
darkened sun, assumed he had been stung by a scorpion.

## A single afternoon, and no other candidate

Months later, working with an astronomer, Torday had his answer: a total
solar eclipse crossed exactly over the Bushong region on 30 March 1680,
with totality at 11:58 local time - two minutes before noon, close enough
to the oral account's own "at noon" to leave no real doubt. Crucially, it
was the only solar eclipse visible in that specific region during the
whole of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, which meant there was
no other event the story could plausibly be describing.

## Working backward to a founder

From Bo Kama Bomanchala's own securely dated reign, Torday could count
backward. Three reigns separated the ninety-eighth chief from Shamba
Bolongongo - the same founding figure recorded elsewhere as Shyaam - and
since both Shamba's own reign and the two that followed it were
remembered as unusually long ones, Torday placed the start of Shamba's own
rule at approximately 1600, a generation or so earlier than the
independent estimate modern scholarship has since reached by other means.
Two entirely different methods, a genuinely dated eclipse and a
reconstruction from documented outside contact, converge on the same
few decades - about as much confirmation as an oral chronology this old
is ever likely to receive.
