title: What the restoration cost

In July 1261 a Nicaean commander with a small force found that the Latin
garrison of Constantinople had gone off on a raid, and walked into the city.
The Latin emperor took ship. Michael VIII Palaiologos — who had made himself
co-emperor by pushing aside the child heir of the Laskarids, and later had him
blinded — entered in August and was crowned in Hagia Sophia.

The restoration is where the Byzantine page picks the story up, and it is
usually told as an ending: the empire returns, and the last two centuries are
decline. The more useful reading is that the restoration cost the empire the
thing that had made the restoration possible.

Constantinople was a ruin with a fraction of its former population, and it had
to be repopulated, rebuilt, garrisoned and defended, and its walls and fleet
paid for. The threat now came from the west — from the Latin powers who wanted
the city back, and from Charles of Anjou in southern Italy who spent two
decades preparing to take it — so the army and the money went west. Anatolia,
which had funded the whole enterprise, was left with a frontier nobody was
watching.

Within forty years that frontier had dissolved. The Turkmen bands pressing on
it moved in, the Byzantine hold on western Anatolia broke, and by about 1300
the beyliks this site's Anatolia region now records were established across it
— including, on the north-western corner facing the Marmara, the one this
site's Ottoman page begins with.

That is the argument for giving this fifty-seven-year state a page of its own.
The empire of Nicaea is the last period in which the Roman state governed
Anatolia effectively, and the decision that ended it — to spend everything on
recovering the capital — is the decision that gave Anatolia to the Turks. The
Byzantine record on this site ends in 1453 in Constantinople. The part that
actually decided it happened here, two hundred years earlier, and was
technically a success.
