Three Greek states claimed to be the Roman empire after 1204.

**Trebizond**, on the Black Sea coast, was founded weeks before the sack by two
grandsons of a previous emperor with Georgian help, which means it is not
strictly a successor state at all — it was already in existence when
Constantinople fell. It outlasted all of them and never seriously contended,
because it was eight hundred kilometres
from anywhere that mattered and its route to the west ran through everybody
else's territory.

**Epirus**, in western Greece, was the most dangerous. Theodore Komnenos Doukas
took Thessalonica from the Latins in 1224, had himself crowned emperor there, and
was closer to Constantinople than Nicaea was. He was then destroyed by the
Bulgarians at Klokotnitsa in 1230 and blinded, and Epirus never recovered the
position.

**Nicaea** won, for reasons that had little to do with luck.

## Anatolia

The decisive advantage was the oldest one in Roman history: western Anatolia was
where the empire's manpower and grain had always been.

<Figure id="nicaea-walls-2" />

Theodore Laskaris established himself in the well-watered valleys behind
Smyrna and Nicaea — country with cities, a taxable peasantry and a functioning
agriculture, which Epirus's mountains and Trebizond's coastal strip did not have.
He then did the necessary unglamorous thing: he fought the Latins, the Seljuks of
Rum and his Greek rivals to a standstill and stopped, rather than marching on
Constantinople with an army he could not replace.

The Seljuk frontier settled after he killed the sultan Kaykhusraw I at Antioch on
the Maeander in 1211, and stayed quiet for a generation. That quiet is the whole
basis of what follows: a state that does not have to defend its eastern frontier
can spend everything on its western one.

## The Latin empire

It is worth saying how weak the thing at the centre was.

The Latin emperors held Constantinople, a strip of Thrace and not much else. The
crusade's own settlement had given three-eighths of the empire to Venice, which
took the islands and the ports and had no interest in the land. The Latin regime
had no revenue base, no fleet of its own, and a hinterland held by Greek and
Bulgarian enemies; its emperors spent their reigns travelling in western Europe
asking for money, and one of them pawned the Crown of Thorns to Venetian
merchants, from whom Louis IX of France redeemed it and built the
Sainte-Chapelle to house it.

By the 1240s Nicaea had taken Thrace and most of Macedonia and the Latin empire
was Constantinople and its suburbs. The recovery of the city, when it came, was
not a campaign. The next two chapters are about what Nicaea did with the
twenty years in between.
