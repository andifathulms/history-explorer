This record's four institution fields are all coded, which is unusual for a
fifty-seven-year state and is possible because the Nicaean administration is
unusually well described — partly by its own officials, and partly because
Byzantine writers afterwards used it as a stick to beat the restored empire with.

## Pronoia

`military_basis` reads `land-grant, mercenary`. The *pronoia* was a grant of the
revenues of an estate to a soldier in return for service, revocable in principle,
not heritable in principle, and both of those in practice increasingly less true.

This site's Byzantine record describes the theme system arriving at the same
arrangement six hundred years earlier and for the same reason: a state that must
field cavalry and cannot pay them in coin. What is striking is that the pronoia
is not a continuation of the themes — those had dissolved before 1204 — but an
independent arrival at the same solution by a state facing the same problem
again.

It is also the commonest coded value on this site. Thirty-one records carry
`land-grant` in their `military_basis` field, from the Third Dynasty of Ur in the
twenty-second century BC to the Ottomans: the iqta, the timar, the pronoia, the
theme, the Mamluk system, the Frankish benefice, the ilku of Middle Assyria, the
Old Babylonian ilkum that Hammurabi's code legislates in sixteen consecutive
paragraphs.

Some of those are genealogically related — the timar descends from the iqta and
the Mamluk system from both — and a good many are not related to anything. The
Ur III version predates the next one on the list by six hundred years. The
Byzantine themes and the Frankish benefice arrived at it independently in the
same century at opposite ends of a broken Mediterranean, and the pronoia arrived
at it again in Greek six hundred years after the themes had dissolved.

That is the clearest single thing this site's coded fields have shown that no
individual page would have. A state that must put cavalry in the field and
cannot pay them in coin has one solution, and it has been found independently in
Sumerian, Akkadian, Greek, Latin, Arabic, Persian and Turkish.

Beside it, mercenaries: Latin knights, Cuman horse archers settled on land in
Anatolia and in Thrace, and Turkish troops.

## Vatatzes

John III Vatatzes reigned thirty-two years and the accounts of his government
read like a treatise on how to run a state that intends to be bigger later.

He put the imperial estates to grain and livestock production and sold the
surplus. He restricted the import of Italian and eastern luxuries so that silver
stayed in the country, and the chroniclers report him telling his son that a
crown paid for out of the sale of eggs from the palace farms was worth more than
one bought with foreign gold. He kept taxes at a level people paid. He founded
hospitals and endowed monasteries and libraries. He built up a treasury and an
army out of a territory a fraction of the size of the one he intended to recover.

Some of that is a court's admiring story about its own ruler. The measurable part
is not: by the 1250s a government in exile in western Anatolia was richer, better
armed and better organised than the regime sitting in its capital, and had taken
most of the European territory that regime had claimed.

## What it did not do

It did not go for Constantinople.

Vatatzes took Thrace and Macedonia and left the city, which was defended by walls
his army could not have taken and by a Venetian fleet he could not have fought.
The strategy was to isolate it and wait, and it was correct: the city fell in
1261 to a scouting force that found the garrison away, without a siege.

The final chapter is about what the empire then spent the resulting position on.
