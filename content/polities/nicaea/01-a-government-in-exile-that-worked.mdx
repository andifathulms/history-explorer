In April 1204 a crusading army recruited to attack Egypt took Constantinople
instead, sacked it for three days, and installed a Latin emperor in it. The
Roman empire ceased to have a
capital for fifty-seven years.

What it had instead were three governments claiming to be it. Epirus in western
Greece, Trebizond on the Black Sea, and Nicaea, in the well-watered country of western Anatolia a hundred miles from
the lost city.

<Figure id="nicaea-walls" />

Nicaea won because it was the only one that behaved like a state rather than a
claim.

Theodore Laskaris spent his reign fighting the Latins, the Seljuks of Rum and
his Greek rivals into a stable frontier. John Vatatzes, who followed him and
reigned for thirty-two years, did the part that mattered. He assumed that the
empire would be restored, and governed as though the revenue had to last: he
promoted grain and livestock production on the imperial estates, restricted
imports of Italian luxuries to keep silver in the country, kept taxes payable,
and built up a treasury and an army out of a territory much smaller than the one
he intended to recover. The chroniclers report him refusing to buy foreign silk
and having his crown paid for from the sale of eggs off the palace farms, which
is the kind of story a court tells about a ruler it is proud of and is
consistent with everything else recorded about the administration.

The result was that by the 1250s the government in exile was substantially
richer and better organised than the Latin regime sitting in its capital, and
had taken Thrace and most of Macedonia besides. The empire was being rebuilt
from Anatolia, which is where its manpower and grain had always been.
