Nicaea lasted fifty-seven years and is better documented than most states that
lasted five hundred — partly because its own officials wrote about it, and partly
because Byzantine writers afterwards used it as a stick to beat the restored
empire with.

## Pronoia

The *pronoia* was a grant of the revenues of an estate to a soldier in return for
service: revocable in principle, not heritable in principle, and both of those
increasingly less true in practice.

The eastern Roman empire had arrived at the same arrangement six hundred years
earlier with the themes, for the same reason — a state that must field cavalry
and cannot pay them in coin. The striking thing is that the pronoia is not a
continuation of that. The themes had dissolved well before 1204. This is an
independent arrival at the same solution by a state that found itself facing the
same problem again.

It keeps happening. Land granted for military service is the single most widely
repeated institution in this whole period: the iqta, the timar, the pronoia, the
theme, the Mamluk system, the Frankish benefice, the ilku of Middle Assyria, and
the Old Babylonian ilkum that Hammurabi's code legislates in sixteen consecutive
paragraphs. Some are genealogically related — the timar descends from the iqta,
and the Mamluk system from both. A good many are not related to anything at all.
The Ur III version predates the next one by six hundred years; the Byzantine
themes and the Frankish benefice appeared independently in the same century at
opposite ends of a broken Mediterranean.

A state that must put cavalry in the field and cannot pay them in coin has
essentially one solution, and it has been found over again in Sumerian, Akkadian,
Greek, Latin, Arabic, Persian and Turkish.

Beside the pronoia soldiers were mercenaries: Latin knights, Cuman horse archers
settled on land in Anatolia and Thrace, and Turkish troops.

## Vatatzes

John III Vatatzes reigned thirty-two years, and the accounts of his government
read like a treatise on how to run a state that intends to be bigger later.

He put the imperial estates to grain and livestock production and sold the
surplus. He restricted the import of Italian and eastern luxuries so that silver
stayed in the country, and the chroniclers report him telling his son that a
crown paid for out of the sale of eggs from the palace farms was worth more than
one bought with foreign gold. He kept taxes at a level people actually paid. He
founded hospitals and endowed monasteries and libraries. He built a treasury and
an army out of a territory a fraction of the size of the one he intended to
recover.

Some of that is a court's admiring story about its own ruler. The measurable part
is not: by the 1250s a government in exile in western Anatolia was richer, better
armed and better organised than the regime sitting in its capital, and had taken
most of the European territory that regime claimed.

## What it did not do

It did not go for Constantinople.

Vatatzes took Thrace and Macedonia and left the city alone. It was defended by
walls his army could not have taken and by a Venetian fleet he could not have
fought, so the strategy was to isolate it and wait. The strategy was correct: the
city fell in 1261 to a scouting force that found the garrison away, without a
siege at all.
