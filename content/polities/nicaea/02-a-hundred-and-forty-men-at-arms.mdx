---
title: A hundred and forty men-at-arms
drafted_from: villehardouin-smith

The Nicaean state nearly did not get a first winter. The only contemporary
narrative of its earliest months was written by the marshal of the army trying to
strangle it, and it is worth reading because of what it takes for granted.

In the autumn of 1204 a Latin force crossed to Asia under two knights, fortified a
castle near the ruins of Cyzicus, garrisoned it, and pushed inland to take more.
Theodore Laskaris collected everything he had and met them near Poimaninon on
6 December.

Villehardouin's account of the battle is four sentences and an excuse. Our people
suffered greatly, he writes, and no wonder, considering the great number of the
Greeks and that we had only seven score men-at-arms, not counting sergeants. But
since Our Lord gives victory according to His grace and pleasure, the Greeks were
beaten and scattered in spite of their numbers, and so badly that within the week
much of the country came back in. Poimaninon, a strong castle; Lopadion, one of
the finest cities of the country; and Polychna, a fort by a freshwater lake, were
all given up, and the neighbouring provinces submitted to the pilgrims.

A hundred and forty knights took the Asian shore off the man who was building the
Byzantine successor state, in his first year, and had the country surrendering
inside a week.

That is where Nicaea started: not as the natural heir with the provinces behind
it, but as one of three claimants with a defeated army, whose most defensible
asset was that the Latins had too much else to do. Within four months of
Poimaninon the emperor of Constantinople was a prisoner in Bulgaria and every
knight who could be spared had been recalled to Europe, and the Asian campaign
stopped. Laskaris used the interval to take the cities back, absorb the other two
claimants' followings, and get himself crowned by a patriarch he had appointed.

The Latin account is also useful for what it never says. Villehardouin lists
castles and men-at-arms and God's pleasure, and shows no awareness that the man
he had just beaten was assembling a government — a patriarchate, a treasury, a
land settlement for refugee soldiers — out of the wreck. To the marshal of
Champagne this was a province being pacified at a satisfactory rate. It was the
last quarter in which anyone could have thought so.
