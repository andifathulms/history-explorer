A Latin patriarch sat in Hagia Sophia from 1204. The Orthodox patriarch of
Constantinople sat at Nicaea, elected by the bishops who had fled there and
recognised by everyone in the Orthodox world who was not under Latin rule.

That is the second reason Nicaea won, and it is not a coded field on this record
because no field on this site could hold it.

## What a patriarch is worth

The Bulgarian and Serbian churches, and the Rus church — all of which this site's
Byzantine record's missions chapter is about creating — were canonically under
Constantinople. With Constantinople in Latin hands, the question was who they
were under now, and the answer everybody gave was Nicaea. Epirus, which had a
rival emperor, could not produce a rival patriarch that anybody recognised; when
Theodore of Epirus had himself crowned in 1224 he had to use the archbishop of
Ohrid, and the canonical objection to it was immediate and general.

So Nicaea held the one asset that could not be taken by force and could not be
manufactured. The Latin empire had the city, the walls and the relics. Nicaea had
the church.

## The price

Which is also why the union negotiations that ran throughout this record's life
were so difficult, and why they mattered.

Every Nicaean emperor negotiated with the papacy about reuniting the churches,
because papal recognition would have removed the Latin empire's justification and
possibly the crusading support that kept it alive. Every one of them failed,
because what Rome wanted — acknowledgement of papal primacy, and the western
position on the procession of the Holy Spirit — was the thing that would have
cost them the Orthodox world that was the point of the exercise.

Vatatzes came closest, in 1250, and died before anything was signed.

The same trade was made and lost twice more afterwards, and both are on this
site's Byzantine record: Michael VIII accepted union at Lyon in 1274 to stop
Charles of Anjou, and was buried without a funeral by his own church; the emperor
and church accepted it again at Florence in 1439 in exchange for help against the
Ottomans, and the population of Constantinople rejected it.

## Why this belongs on the record

Because this site's `legitimation` field for this polity reads `titulature,
descent` — the assertion that this was the Roman empire, temporarily housed
elsewhere, and that the crown was the same crown — and that assertion had to be
recognised by somebody to be worth anything.

The recogniser was the Orthodox church, which Nicaea held and its rivals did not,
and there is no field on this site for *held the institution that certified
everybody's claims*. Coding rule 2 counts a religion carried and this record
carries none; the church here was not propagated outward but held, and holding is
not carrying.

It is the third page in the last few records to end on the same observation. The
Carolingian record's real achievement is a script reform the rules cannot count.
The Nabataean record scores nothing anywhere except an alphabet. This one won a
fifty-seven-year contest by controlling a patriarchate, and the coded fields
record a land tax and some pronoia grants.
