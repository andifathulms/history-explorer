The Tran had a practice, adopted deliberately at the start of the dynasty and
applied for most of its length: the reigning emperor abdicated in favour of his
designated heir while still alive and in good health, took the title of senior
emperor — *thượng hoàng* — and governed alongside the man he had just installed.

The heir was on the throne, publicly, with the previous holder standing next to
him, years before there was anything to fight over.

## Why that is worth stopping on

Succession is where most of the polities on these pages come apart, and the
failures are monotonous. Singhasari's chronicle is four generations of revenge
killings. Ayutthaya changed dynasty five times in four hundred years, most of the
transitions violent. In Aceh the merchants installed four queens in succession
precisely to keep the throne weak. At Angkor the throne was repeatedly taken by
force, and the taking is most of what is recorded.

A designated heir is normal; every one of those places had the idea. A designated
heir who is *already reigning*, with the man who designated him alive and in the
room to enforce it, is not.

## And what happened when they stopped

The Tran abandoned the practice late in the dynasty. The throne was usurped
within two reigns, and the dynasty ended.

That is the closest thing to a controlled experiment these centuries offer. The
rule was in force and the successions held; the rule lapsed and the succession
failed in the ordinary way. What it does not tell you is why the practice was
abandoned by rulers who could see, as clearly as anyone reading now, what it had
been doing for them.
