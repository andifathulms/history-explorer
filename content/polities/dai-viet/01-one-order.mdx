title: One order, three and a half centuries

Ly Thanh Tong proclaimed the name Dai Viet in 1054. It was still the state's
name in 1804, when Gia Long changed it.

Those seven hundred and fifty years are not one thing, and treating them as one
is the commonest mistake made about Vietnamese history. Between 1400 and 1804
the Red River delta had a usurping dynasty, a twenty-year Chinese occupation,
the Le, a century of contest with the Mac, a two-century partition between two
families holding opposite ends of the country while both nominally served a Le
emperor with no power, and finally the Nguyen. The name was continuous. Almost
nothing else was.

The Ly and the Tran are one order. A capital at Thang Long, a Buddhist court, an
examination system growing alongside an aristocracy, a defined relationship with
the empire to the north, and an expansion southward against Champa: it starts,
it runs three and a half centuries, and it is ended by a usurpation with a date.

That is one polity, and what follows it in 1400 is a different one wearing the
same name — which is why the histories that run straight from Ly Thanh Tong to
Gia Long have to keep changing the subject without saying so.
