The dynasty that beat the Mongols three times was finished a little over a
century later by a combination of a Cham king, an estate system and a family
that had stopped producing adults.

## The estates

The Tran had won the Mongol wars partly through a peculiar arrangement: princes
of the imperial clan held large personal estates in the delta, with their own
dependants, their own retainers and their own military followings, and the wars
were fought substantially by those private forces under family command.

It worked because the family was united and the enemy was existential. Neither
condition is permanent.

Afterwards the estates remained, and they did what such things do. Land and the
labour on it accumulated in the hands of princes and monasteries; the registered
peasantry that the state taxed and conscripted shrank; and the court's own
revenue and manpower declined while the private establishments grew.

The Tran also married exclusively within the clan — cousin marriages, generation
after generation, as a deliberate policy to keep the throne out of any other
family's reach. It kept the throne secure and it produced, by the fourteenth
century, a run of emperors who were children, invalids or both.

## Che Bong Nga

Into that came the one Cham ruler who could have reversed four centuries of
Vietnamese advance.

Che Bong Nga sacked Thang Long — the capital — in 1371, and again in 1377, when
the Vietnamese emperor was killed attacking Vijaya, and again in 1383. Three
sackings of a capital in twelve years.

The court fled each time. The chronicles record the imperial archives burned,
the palaces looted, and the emperor of the day taking to the river.

He was killed in 1390 by cannon fire from a Vietnamese ship after a defector
pointed out which vessel he was aboard, and Champa never threatened the delta
again. But the dynasty did not recover its standing. A ruling house that cannot
keep an enemy out of its own capital three times in a generation has demonstrated
something to everybody who was watching.

## The man who was watching

Ho Quy Ly was a court official related to the imperial family by marriage who
made himself indispensable during the Cham wars and then made himself
unavoidable.

Through the 1390s he removed the people who might have stopped him, in the
usual order. In 1400 he took the throne outright.

What is unusual is what he did with it, and it is the reason he is argued about
rather than dismissed. He capped the amount of land any individual could hold
and the number of dependants they could keep, which was aimed directly at the
princely estates. He issued paper money and called in the bronze coin. He
reformed the examinations, added mathematics, and had Chinese classics
translated into Vietnamese written in *chu nom*. He built a new capital of
enormous dressed stone blocks in Thanh Hoa, and its gates are still standing.

Every one of those is an attempt to fix what had gone wrong: too much land in
private hands, too little revenue, an administration recruited from too narrow a
group.

He had seven years. In 1407 a Ming army arrived on the pretext of restoring the
Tran, took the country, and held it for twenty.
