The Red River delta had been administered from China, on and off, for something
over a thousand years, ending in the tenth century. What it built afterwards was
a state that described itself in the northern empire's own imperial vocabulary.

The ruler was an emperor at home, in the same characters the court in the north
used for itself, with the same mandate, the same ritual, the same calendar, and
his own claim to be the centre of a civilised world with barbarians around its
edges — among whom he counted the Chams and the Khmer. The bureaucracy sat
examinations on the Confucian classics. The records were kept in literary
Chinese.

And abroad, to the northern court, the same ruler was the King of Annam, holding
that title by investiture from the emperor, sending tribute missions on schedule.

## The value, and the discomfort

This record's `legitimation` carries `investiture`.

That value was added to this site's vocabulary for a group of records — mostly
Islamic — where a ruler held authority granted by an external sovereign and
publicly said so, and where the older `caliphal-investiture` named the wrong
grantor. This corpus's Melaka record then showed it was right to be general:
Parameswara sailed to Nanjing in 1411 and came back with a seal from a Ming
emperor, an identical transaction with a grantor no caliph-specific value could
have held.

Dai Viet is the closest fit and the least comfortable one. Melaka wanted the
recognition and had nothing to lose by it. Here the same investiture was
accepted continuously for three centuries by rulers who called themselves
emperor at home, by a state that had fought its way out of the grantor's empire,
during periods when the two were at war.

Coding rule 8's test is a public assertion of authority derived from an external
sovereign, and this is that — asserted in one direction and contradicted in the
other, by the same court, deliberately, as policy. The rulebook asks for the
rule to be applied uniformly including where the answer is inconvenient. The
value is on the record and this chapter is the inconvenience.

## Chu Nom

`descendant_scripts` reads zero, and the near miss is chữ Nôm: a script for
Vietnamese assembled from Chinese characters, some taken for their sound, some
for their sense, and many invented by combining two of them.

Coding rule 3 puts an adaptation on the record of the script that was adapted.
The parent here is Chinese, and its owner is on this site — the Han and Tang
records. It is the same reasoning that sent the Hittite claim to Old Babylonian
and the Urartian to Neo-Assyrian.

That is the second script claim in this region to be redirected to a parent that
has a page here. The other is the Thai script, which counts on Angkor — and
which, unlike this one, is a count the region actually gains rather than one it
hands to a record five thousand kilometres and a thousand years away.
