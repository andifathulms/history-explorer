The Ly dynasty's power had always rested on the upper Red River plain, the
agricultural heartland of the delta. The Tran were a different kind of family
entirely: their base was the coast, where the port of Van Don had grown, over
the twelfth century, into a gathering point for merchants trading with southern
China under the Southern Song — wealth accumulating in the hands of local
producers, traders, and the family positioned to profit from all three. By the
early thirteenth century that family was strong enough to fight its way into
the capital.

## A kingdom in pieces

The takeover was neither quick nor bloodless. Two local strongmen, Nguyen Non
and Doan Thuong, held out against the Tran's advance up the delta for years,
partitioning what had been the Ly heartland between them. The Tran won control
of the reigning king, Ly Hue Tong, largely by protecting his sister-in-law and
her newborn daughter and building royal favour from that position rather than
by any single decisive battle — a slow consolidation carried out while Nguyen
Non and Doan Thuong remained undefeated in their own districts, kept apart
from each other more by Tran manoeuvring than by military defeat.

## The brother who did not need the throne himself

Tran Tu Khanh, who had led the family's military campaigns, died in 1223. His
younger brother, Tran Thu Do, stepped into his position and remained the
effective ruler of the country for the next quarter century without ever
taking the crown himself. The nominal head of the family, the elder brother
Tran Thua, held formal precedence and a real role in government — but Tran Thu
Do had the last word in every decision that mattered.

## Two daughters, and which one the throne would need

Ly Hue Tong had no son, only two young daughters by his queen, herself a
Tran. The elder, Thuan Thien, had already been married as a child to Tran
Thua's eldest son and so was set aside; Tran Thu Do judged that son unfit to
be king in any case. The younger daughter, Chieu Thanh, not yet ten years old,
was the one Tran Thu Do used instead. He had her father abdicate in her
favour, then arranged her marriage to Tran Thua's second son, Tran Canh — a
child bridegroom the same age as his child bride. At the end of 1225 Tran Canh
was crowned king, his father took the newly invented title of senior emperor,
and the Tran dynasty existed in fact, sealed by a marriage between two
children neither of whom had chosen it.

## What happened to the king who stepped aside

Ly Hue Tong and his mother were sent to live out their retirement in a temple
in the capital. It did not last. Within a year, worried that a living former
king might become a focus for lingering Ly loyalty, Tran Thu Do drove the
retired king to suicide — and then, with the old dynasty's last male claimant
gone, took the deposed king's own former queen, his own sister, as his own
wife. The Tran had not simply replaced the Ly on the throne. They had married
into it, waited for the family they married into to run out of options, and
then removed what remained of it by hand.
