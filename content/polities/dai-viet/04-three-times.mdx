The Mongols came into the Red River delta in 1258, in 1285 and in 1287–88. Each
time they took Thang Long, which each time had been evacuated. Each time they
left.

The third ended in the Bach Dang estuary, where Tran Hung Dao's forces planted
iron-tipped stakes in the riverbed at low water, drew the Yuan fleet in on the
flood tide, and destroyed it as the tide fell. It was the third time in three
hundred and fifty years that a Vietnamese commander had won a battle at that
estuary by that method, which the Yuan admiral might reasonably have looked up.

The Mongols built the largest contiguous land empire in history, and the list of
places that stopped them outright is short. The Mamluks turned a Mongol army
back at Ain Jalut in 1260 and held Syria afterward. Java met the fleet when it
arrived in 1293, used it against a domestic rival, and then turned on it in
turn. And Dai Viet did it three times, on its own ground, against forces
marching overland from a province the empire already held.

Japan is the other name usually given, and it had a strait and a typhoon
working for it. Dai Viet shared a land border with the empire that invaded it.

## How

Both dynasties kept household registers of adult men and called them up by
quota. The Tran added something more unusual: the princes of the ruling clan
held their own estates and raised their own forces from them, which meant that
when the capital fell — and it fell three times — there was no single army whose
destruction would end the war. The court withdrew, the countryside was stripped,
the invaders held a city with nothing in it, and the levies came back at them
from several directions at once.

An empire that won by destroying field armies and taking capitals had nothing to
take.
