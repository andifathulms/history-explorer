Ho Quy Ly had married into the Tran, held the regency, and controlled the last
two emperors. In 1400 he deposed the child on the throne — his own grandson —
and took it.

<Figure id="dv-hocitadel" />

Nobody conquered anything. The senior official of a dynasty that could no longer
defend its own capital removed the last child in the line and took the throne,
and his own dynasty lasted seven years.

What happened next is the part worth the chapter. In 1407 the Ming invaded,
announcing that they had come to restore the dynasty Ho Quy Ly had displaced.
Having taken the country they did not restore it. They annexed it, administered
it as a province for twenty years, shipped its records and its scholars north,
and were expelled in 1427 by a rebellion out of Thanh Hoa that founded the Le.

So the state whose court had accepted investiture from the northern emperor for
three centuries, as the price of being left alone, was reconquered by that
emperor's successor using a dynastic technicality as the pretext. The
arrangement described two chapters back had exactly one failure mode and this
was it.

## What came out of it

Ho Quy Ly's state lasted seven years and the occupation that followed lasted
twenty, and what emerged from both was the Le — a dynasty founded by a
provincial landowner from Thanh Hoa who spent ten years running an insurgency
and drove the Ming out in 1427.

The Le ruled, in one form or another, for three hundred and sixty years, and
under Le Thanh Tong in the second half of the fifteenth century the Vietnamese
state took the shape it kept until the French: six ministries, a censorate, a
cadastral survey, a law code that lasted three centuries, and an army that
finished Champa in 1471.

So the reforms Ho Quy Ly was executed for attempting were substantially carried
out, by the dynasty that replaced the dynasty that replaced him, within two
generations.
