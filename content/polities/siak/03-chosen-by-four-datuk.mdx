Siak's own government, once Raja Kecil's founding claim had become a
working state rather than a contested inheritance, did not settle the
throne by any single fixed rule the way Johor's own bendahara line
had tried to. Authority at Buatan and later Siak Sri Indrapura rested
on a council of four hereditary datuk - Datuk Tanah Datar, Datuk
Limapuluh, Datuk Pesisir and Datuk Kampar - a structure a modern
account of the sultanate traces to the influence of Pagaruyung, the
Minangkabau kingdom the dynasty's own founder had drawn early support
from.
When a datuk died, the sultan's
own decree confirmed whichever successor the lineage itself put
forward; when the throne itself needed filling, it was these same four
men, meeting in council, who settled the matter by consensus rather
than by a stated order of birth or seniority.

## A ruler chosen, not simply inherited

The arrangement gave Siak's kingship a character distinct from most of
its neighboring Malay sultanates: legitimacy still ran through royal
descent, as it had for Raja Kecil himself, but which descendant
actually took the throne in any given generation was a decision the
four datuk made together rather than an automatic right of the eldest
son.
No fixed term of office bound the sultan once chosen, and no
schedule governed how often the council needed to meet - only the
recurring fact of a vacant throne, whenever it came, requiring the
same four lineages to reach for consensus once again.
