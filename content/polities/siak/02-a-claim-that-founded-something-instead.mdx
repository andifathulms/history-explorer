By around 1723 to 1725, Raja Kecil had settled on the Siak river,
built a fortified capital, and stopped contesting Johor's own throne
directly. It was a striking turn for a man whose entire public
identity had been built on a specific inheritance claim: rather than
keep fighting to make good on being Mahmud Shah's own posthumous son,
he founded a kingdom that owed nothing to Johor's own territory or
institutions at all.

## The same argument, a different throne

Raja Kecil's own legitimacy at Siak still rested on descent - the same
royal bloodline he had claimed at Johor - but now it authorized a new
sultanate rather than a disputed one. In that sense Siak was less a
retreat than a conversion: a claim that could not be enforced against
an established rival court became, instead, the founding charter of
an entirely separate state, on ground nobody else had a prior claim
to contest.

## A kingdom outlasting the man who founded it on a story

What began as one man's assertion about his own birth went on to
become a real, lasting Sumatran sultanate, continuing long after Raja
Kecil's own lifetime and into the era of Dutch colonial rule, well
beyond what any account of its founding actually covers. The
posthumous-son story that had briefly won and then lost a throne at
Johor turned out to be durable enough, once redirected toward founding
rather than claiming, to establish something that held.
