Mahmud Shah II, the last sultan of the old Melaka royal line, was
murdered by his own nobles in 1699 on his way to the mosque, and his
chief minister's family took the throne in his place - ruling under
the same name, in the same capital, with the same regalia, but
without the one thing three centuries of Johor kingship had actually
rested on: a bloodline nobody could dispute. That vulnerability sat
untouched for almost twenty years before someone found a way to
exploit it directly.

## A claim built for a specific weakness

Raja Kecil arrived in the strait claiming to be Mahmud Shah's own
posthumous son - a child born after his father's murder, raised in
secret, now come to reclaim an inheritance. Whether the story was
true or invented, it was aimed with real precision: it did not merely
assert a grievance against the bendahara family that had taken power,
it asserted the one thing that family could never manufacture for
itself, a direct line of royal blood. In 1718 he pressed the claim
with an army rather than with genealogy, and took Johor outright.

## A throne that could not hold

The seizure did not last. The bendahara line recovered Johor within a
few years, and Raja Kecil found himself back where he had started -
a claimant with a strong story and no capital to rule from. What he
did next mattered more, in the long run, than the brief seizure
itself: rather than keep fighting for a throne that had slipped from
his hands twice, he crossed to the Sumatran side of the strait and
built an entirely new kingdom of his own.
