Xiao Daocheng deposed the child emperor Shundi in 479 and took the
throne as Kaoti, founding the Southern Qi in the place of the Song
dynasty he had served. Contemporaries thought well enough of him
beforehand - Boulger quotes one court official's speech declaring
that no one could match Xiao Daocheng "both in merit and for services
rendered," and warning that anyone who thought otherwise should
expect to feel "the weight of my arm and the keenness of my sabre."

As emperor, Kaoti was remembered less for military achievement than
for his patronage of learning, and for one striking, unfulfilled
boast: that if he ruled the country for ten years, he would make gold
as common as earth. History, Boulger notes dryly, preserved no record
that he came close. A prolonged war with the northern Wei kingdom
ended in nothing better than a draw - Kaoti's generals defended the
towns of Chouyang and Juchang successfully, but Kaoti himself never
dared follow the retreating Wei army back into its own territory. He
died in 482 having reigned three years, leaving the throne to his son.
