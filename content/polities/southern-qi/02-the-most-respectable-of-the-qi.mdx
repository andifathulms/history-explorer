Kaoti's son Xiao Ze, who took the throne as Wuti on his father's
death in 482, presided over what Boulger calls, with evident
qualification, the most respectable reign of the whole dynasty - a
period when it was claimed, with some exaggeration, that China's
division among rival princes had produced rulers who thought only of
their subjects' welfare.

The war with Wei flared again under Wuti and the Qi held their own
without ever clearly prevailing, a state of affairs Boulger attributes
less to Qi merit than to circumstance - underscored by a public
proclamation, made during this very reign, that the dynasty held its
throne "not by merit, but by force," and that a rule built on that
foundation could not last.

<Figure id="sq-ewer" />

Wuti's own most substantial act of domestic legislation was reviving
an old prohibition on marriage between families sharing a surname,
meant to address the same falling population in districts still
following older kinship customs. It was a modest, careful reign - and,
as the proclamation itself predicted, the last one the dynasty would
get. Wuti died in 493 after eleven years on the throne.
