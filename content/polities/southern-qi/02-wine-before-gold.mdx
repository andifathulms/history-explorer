Wuti's death in 493 opened the same violent pattern that had already
doomed the Song. His infant grandson Xiao Zhao held the throne only
briefly before Xiao Luan, brother of the dynasty's founder, murdered
him and seized power outright, taking the name Mingti. Mingti's own
reign brought, in Boulger's judgment, no real ability to match the
brutality of his rise - the Qi still could not match their northern
rival Wei in either power or policy, and Mingti's last years saw a
revival of the same violent methods that had carried him to the
throne in the first place. His single most consequential act was the
assassination of the general Xiao Yi, the man who had done more than
anyone to defend the state against Wei - an act Boulger identifies
directly as the one that "brought about eventually the fall and
extirpation" of the entire ruling family, since it left Xiao Yi's own
brother, Xiao Yan, with a grievance and the military standing to act
on it.

## Two youths, one revenge

Mingti's death in 498 left the throne to a pair of teenage sons in
quick succession, both entirely unequal to the position. Paokwen
ruled two years before his own deposition and murder; his brother
Hoti held the title for only a few months more, "mere youths," in
Boulger's phrase, who could offer no real resistance to Xiao Yan's own
campaign of revenge. Xiao Yan's rebellion drew soldiers and popular
support in numbers that made loyalty to the Qi itself dangerous, and
by 502 the dynasty that had begun with Xiao Daocheng's proclaimed
virtue closed with the killing of a boy who had barely begun to rule.

## What need have I of gold after my death

Hoti's own end became, in Boulger's telling, a small, bitter irony
worth preserving on its own terms. His captors sent him a quantity of
gold to mark his final days; Hoti asked instead for wine - "what need
have I of gold after my death? A few glasses of wine would be more
valuable" - and, once he had drunk himself into a stupor, was
strangled with the silken cord of his own robe. Boulger's own
judgment on the dynasty closes in the same key as its own founding
verdict: a family that had "shut its eyes to the necessities of the
people" and had always sought the shortest path to power, ending, in
Hoti, exactly as unceremoniously as it had begun with Shundi
twenty-three years before.
