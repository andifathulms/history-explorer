The Chola grip on the Pandya country did not last forever. By the
close of the twelfth century, Chola power itself had begun to
weaken, and a Pandya line that had spent a generation as real,
working feudatories of the Chola king Kulottunga III broke that
relationship outright with the accession, in 1190, of Jatavarman
Kulasekhara. What followed is the period later historians call the
Second Empire, and no reign within it did more to earn that name than
the one that began sixty years later.

## A small force, a large ambition

Jatavarman Sundara Pandya I came to the throne in 1251, and the record
of his opening years reads less like the start of a reign than a
single unbroken campaign. He marched against the Chera king with a
small force and destroyed him in battle; he compelled the Chola king —
by this point little more than a Hoysala protectorate — to pay him
tribute; and he turned next on the Hoysalas themselves, besieging their
forces in the Kaveri country and inflicting heavy losses, killing their
commander Singana in the fighting. Rather than press a beaten enemy
further, Sundara let the Hoysala king retreat from the field, then
turned on a different target: Kannanur-Koppam, a stronghold no other
power had dared to approach, which he stormed and occupied outright.

## Tribute from every direction

The victories kept compounding. Sundara imposed a tribute of elephants
on the Karnata king, compelled the ruler of Ceylon to surrender pearls
and elephants of his own, and marched against the fortress city of
Sendamangalam, terrifying its Pallava ruler into submission — before
restoring him to his own rulership rather than annexing the territory
outright. From Nellore in the north to Ceylon in the south, every
neighboring power that mattered had been made to answer to Madurai.

## A ceremony that announced what the campaigns had won

Sundara marked the achievement with religious spectacle rather than
another battle. He traveled to Chidambaram to worship at the temple of
Nataraja, then on to Srirangam, where he performed the tulabharam
ceremony — weighing himself against gold and precious offerings for
distribution to the temple and its poets — wearing a golden crown and
sitting on a jeweled throne beside his queen in a shrine whose roof he
had gilded for the occasion. It was theater as much as religion, and
it announced, to anyone who had missed the campaigns themselves, that
the Pandya kingdom had become the paramount power of the Tamil
country.

## The old rival finally broken

The Cholas Sundara had reduced to tribute did not recover. His
successor Maravarman Kulasekhara finished what Sundara had begun,
defeating the Chola king Rajendra III and his ally Vira Ramanatha in
battle in 1279 — after which no further Chola inscription is known
anywhere. A dynasty that had dominated the Pandya country for two
centuries after Parantaka I's own conquest had, in the end, been
undone by the kingdom it once conquered.
