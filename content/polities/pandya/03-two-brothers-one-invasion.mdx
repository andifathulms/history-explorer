Maravarman Kulasekhara reigned long and, in his final years, badly. As
he aged, two of his sons — Vira Pandya and Sundara Pandya — fell into a
dispute over the succession that hardened into open civil war. Amir
Khusru, writing from the Delhi side, describes the conflict in his own
account of the period as a struggle between "the two Rais of Ma'bar,"
independent testimony that the war was real and not a later
invention. What makes the moment harder to reconstruct is that it
coincided almost exactly with an invasion from the north.

## An invasion tangled up with a civil war

Malik Kafur, a general of the Delhi Sultan Ala-ud-din Khalji, led an
army into the Pandya country at the same time the two brothers were
fighting each other for the throne. The traditional account, repeated
in later chronicles, holds that Sundara Pandya himself invited the
invaders in to settle the war in his favor — but the source treats
that story as unreliable, resting on a Persian chronicler's account
that does not withstand scrutiny. What can be said with more
confidence is only that the two conflicts were genuinely simultaneous,
each shaping the other in ways the surviving records do not fully
untangle.

## A capital lost, not a kingdom

Vira Pandya outlasted the invasion by decades, and later records of
his reign around 1340 show a country recovering from the devastation
Malik Kafur's army had left behind. But the recovery could not undo
what had already happened at Madurai itself. Around 1330 a rebellious
governor of the Delhi Sultanate declared the city the seat of an
independent state, the Sultanate of Madurai — and the Pandyas never
recovered their own capital from it. The loss did not mean the
immediate end of Pandya power: inscriptions continue to place Pandya
rulers in parts of Madura, Ramnad and Tanjore for decades afterward,
and a separate line lingered in the Tinnevelly district toward the
close of the sixteenth century or the start of the seventeenth. But
the kingdom that had once made the Cholas and Hoysalas pay tribute at
Srirangam never again ruled from the city where it had worn its golden
crown.
