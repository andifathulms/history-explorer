Somewhere around the turn of the seventh century, a Pandya prince named
Kadungon put an end to an occupation of his own country by a people
called the Kalabhras. Almost nothing about the occupation itself
survives: a single grant calls their king "a Kali king" who "took
possession of the extensive earth, driving away numberless great
kings," and names the invaders only in the plural, as though they were
a people rather than a dynasty. How long they held the Pandya country,
or how Kadungon finally drove them out, the sources that mention them
at all do not say.

<Figure id="pd-map" />

## A king known only by two lines of praise

Kadungon's own reign is barely better documented than the interregnum
he ended. The one grant that describes him at all does so in a single
formula: that he "abolished by his strength the claim of others to the
earth and established his own claim on a firm basis." It is enough to
show that Kadungon had an active hand in the restoration, but not
enough to say what he actually did to bring it about, and his son
Maravarman Avanisulamani fares even worse in the record, described
only in terms general enough to suggest he held what his father had
won without adding much to it.

## Three centuries built on that one restoration

What the Pandya line built on this obscure beginning was real enough,
whatever the silence of its first two reigns. For three centuries
afterward, a run of Pandya kings expanded steadily against their two
great neighbors, the Pallavas of Kanchi and the rising Chola dynasty
of the Kaveri delta, in a contest that shifted the border back and
forth without ever settling for long. It ended only when a new Chola
king, still early in his own reign, turned the tables completely.

<Figure id="pd-vettuvan" />

## An empire ends in a single campaign

Parantaka I invaded the Pandya heartland within three years of his own
accession and, in a swift campaign, defeated the reigning Pandya king
outright, driving Pandya power back to Kanyakumari at the southern tip
of the peninsula. The victory earned Parantaka the title
"Madurai-konda" — captor of Madurai — and it closed out what later
historians would call the Pandya kingdom's First Empire. Two centuries
of Chola domination followed, long enough that a Pandya restoration
looked, for most of that time, no more likely than the one Kadungon
himself had once achieved.
