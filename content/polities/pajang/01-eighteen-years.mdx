When Sultan Trenggana of Demak died, the north-coast sultanate that had
dominated Java fell into a war over who would follow him. Jaka Tingkir won it.
He killed his rival Arya Penangsang in 1554, took the throne as Hadiwijaya, and
carried the centre of Javanese power off the coast and inland to Pajang, in the
middle Solo valley, where he ruled from 1568.

## The double claim

Having destroyed the dynasty he had served under, he argued his right to rule
from two directions at once. He claimed descent from Trenggana, whose line he
had just finished off. And he claimed descent from Brawijaya, the last king of
Majapahit — the kingdom Trenggana's own dynasty had absorbed thirty years
earlier.

Demak had made the same manoeuvre in its own founding, tracing Raden Patah to
the royal house it displaced. It is worth registering how the claim actually
worked in practice: it did not have to be believed so much as it had to be
sayable, by a man who already held the field, in terms the court chroniclers
could write down.

## Inland

Demak had been a port. It lived on land tax and on the tolls of its own
harbour, sold rice across the archipelago, and sent fleets against Portuguese
Melaka. Pajang sat up the Solo valley with no coast at all and lived on the
land alone. Mataram, which took it in 1586, lived the same way.

That is the change worth carrying away from eighteen years. The centre of
Javanese power went inland and never came back to the sea. Sultan Agung's
armies would later starve outside Batavia because the coast belonged to
somebody else, and the Dutch company would buy its way into Javanese
successions through the ports. Both of those follow from a short-lived kingdom
choosing a river valley.
