Almost everything known about the interior of Java in the sixteenth century
comes from Javanese chronicles written in the seventeenth and eighteenth, by
men who served the kings of Mataram and often belonged to their family. That
has to be allowed for, and in Pajang's case it has to be allowed for heavily.

The court that Mataram's founder destroyed is described in Mataram's histories
as feeble, and its sultan as a weak ruler whose defeat was only natural. It is
a tidy account and it is not what the same chronicles show him doing.

## What he actually held

As Trenggana's principal heir, Adiwijaya kept Demak's authority together across
a much wider area than his own valley: the interior of central Java, and
influence reaching west into Sunda country and east into the Brantas basin.

In the southwest he was collecting *bulu-bekti*, the annual tribute, from the
local lords of Kedu and Bagelen, which is the plainest surviving evidence of
what he governed — and the plainest evidence of how he lost it, because those
are exactly the men Senapati of Mataram talked into keeping their tribute at
home. One of them, the lord of Bocor, meant to stay loyal, tried to kill
Senapati, failed, and concluded from his failure that Mataram had the right to
rule Java after all.

He was also still enforcing. A Javanese chronicle dates the occupation of
Wirasaba to 1578, and the Banyumas chronicle records that the sultan of Pajang
ordered the killing of its lord. Late in the reign, the court was still
settling the interior by force.

Eastward his authority ran to Madiun, on the largest tributary of the Solo,
where the ruler was Trenggana's youngest son and his own brother-in-law.

## The assembly at Giri, 1581

The high point is a meeting, and it was held in somebody else's palace.

In 1581 Adiwijaya obtained recognition as Muslim king and sultan from the most
important rulers of east Java and the eastern coast, at a solemn assembly in
the court of the very old Sunan Prapen of Giri — the spiritual authority of the
eastern ports. The kings of Japan, Wirasaba, Kediri, Surabaya, Pasuruan,
Madiun, Sidayu, Lasem, Tuban and Pati were there.

Look at what that is. Ten courts, most of them on the coast, none of them
conquered, acknowledging an inland ruler as paramount at the invitation of a
holy man neither side controlled. Relations between Pajang and the eastern
princes were generally friendly; Surabaya's Panji Wiryakrama seems to have
emerged around then as their spokesman in dealing with him. This is a
paramountcy assembled by agreement, in a room, which is a different object from
an empire and did not outlive the man who assembled it.

## The one outside witness

Francis Drake called at Java in 1580 and got no further than Blambangan, at the
extreme eastern tip.

What he was told there was that the island had a very great number of kings,
but that there was one whom they acknowledged as supreme. He was not given the
name of the city.
