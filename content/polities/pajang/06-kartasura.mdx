Pajang did not stop existing in 1588. It stopped being the place the
paramountcy sat, and then it spent thirty years finding out what that meant.

Benawa made over his father's inheritance to Senapati, whom he treated as an
elder brother. Senapati said he preferred to stay in Mataram and asked only for
the regalia — the gold and jewels of the Pajang crown — and confirmed Benawa as
king of Pajang under his protection. Which is the whole transaction: the
ornaments went west, the title stayed, and the title was now something in
somebody's gift.

Benawa's reign lasted about a year. He died, or according to another tradition
left Pajang to give himself to religion at Parakan in northern Kedu. Senapati
then handed the kingdom to a young Mataram prince, Gagak Bening, who ordered a
large rebuilding and enlargement of the palace, and who was dead by about 1591.
After that Senapati appointed Adiwijaya's grandson, and under him and under
Senapati's successor the arrangement held quietly for a quarter of a century.

## 1617

Then Pajang rose against Sultan Agung, with help from the disaffected inside
Mataram itself.

The suppression of a revolt in the older and richer country next door was
carried out with massive destruction, and afterwards the villagers were carried
off to labour on the building of the new royal city. In 1618 the last king of
the Pajang house, beaten in the field, fled east to Giri and to Surabaya. The
Pajang family had always been on good terms with the princes of east Java, and
in the 1620s the resistance to Sultan Agung's expansion was concentrated in
exactly those coastal districts; one of Pajang's last vassal lords went with
him and made a place for himself at the Surabaya court.

For most of the seventeenth century the district was of no political or
economic consequence at all.

## The city that came back

In 1680, Sultan Agung's grandson Amangkurat II was driven out of Mataram, his
own inheritance, and had to build somewhere else. He built his new royal city,
Kartasura, in Pajang, not far from where the old sultanate had had its seat
about a century earlier.

The courts of Surakarta and Yogyakarta descend from that one. So the country a
kingdom held for eighteen years, and lost, and was flattened for, is where the
Javanese court eventually settled for good — twice, and by other people's
decisions both times.

There is one more scene worth keeping. In 1628, after his own inheritance had
been occupied, the last independent prince of Surabaya had to go to Mataram and
submit to Sultan Agung. On the way, it seems, he stopped at Butuh to pay his
respects at the grave of the sultan of Pajang.
