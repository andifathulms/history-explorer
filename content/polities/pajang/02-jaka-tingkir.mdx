The Javanese chronicles give Pajang's founder a career before the throne, and
it is worth setting out, because it is about as clear a case as survives of
what a dynastic chronicle is doing when it tells a founding story.

Jaka Tingkir is a young man from a village, of obscure but ultimately royal
descent, who goes to Demak, joins the sultan's guard, is expelled for killing a
rival applicant during a selection trial, is taken in by a holy man, and
crosses the Kedung Srengenge — where he is attacked by crocodiles and defeats
forty of them, who then tow his raft. He returns, marries Trenggana's daughter,
is given Pajang, and after the succession war becomes king.

## The three things it is doing at once

It establishes descent — the obscure origin is explicitly a disguise, and the
genealogy comes out later.

It establishes merit — he is physically formidable, and the killing that gets
him expelled is a display of the quality that later makes him fit to rule.

And it establishes sanction — the holy man, the ascetic interval, and the
animals that submit are the standard Javanese markers of *wahyu*, the light of
kingship, which is a thing that settles on a person rather than a thing a
person inherits.

What the story will not do is separate those three. A Javanese founder is
supposed to be all of them at once — born to it, worthy of it, and marked out
for it — and the version the chroniclers settled on gives him each in turn, in
the order that makes the next one plausible.

## Who was writing

The chronicles that carry it were compiled in the seventeenth century and
after, by men in the service of Mataram, and Mataram's own claim ran through
Pajang. Its kings needed Jaka Tingkir to have been legitimate, because their
ancestor had received Mataram from his hand and had then taken his kingdom.

So the legend is not an embellishment attached to a founder by admirers. It is
load-bearing, and the dynasty that wrote it down had a stake in every part of
it.
