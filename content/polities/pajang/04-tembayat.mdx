The eighteen years did one thing that outlasted everything else the kingdom
did, and it is not political. Under Adiwijaya, the court literature and court
art that had matured at Demak and Jepara moved inland, and southern central
Java — which would be the heartland of Javanese culture for the next three
centuries — began to be a place where such things were made.

Islam arrived there by the same road. Tradition credits Pengging's knowledge of
it to the legendary Syekh Siti Jenar, and further south to Sunan Tembayat, who
came from Semarang and was connected to the royal house of Demak.

## The buildings that date it

Tembayat is not only a story. The dated inscriptions carved on the buildings at
his grave establish that they went up during Adiwijaya's reign, and their style
belongs with the early Islamic work of the sixteenth century at Kudus and
Kalinyamat, and with the ruins of the same kind in east Java.

The tomb stayed a place of pilgrimage for Muslims of southern central Java for
centuries. Javanese tradition adds that near the end of his life the sultan
went there himself, to ask for help in his struggle against Mataram, which says
something exact about the relationship between his court and the community of
scholars that the man from Semarang had formed. It did not work.

## Apem thrown over the heads of the crowd

At Jatinom, at the foot of Merapi in what is now Klaten and was then Pajang,
there is an annual festival at which *apem* cakes are thrown over the heads of
the people who come for them.

It is called Angka Wiyu, and the name may come from an Arabic invocation — *ya
Kawiyu*, the Strong, the Mighty, the Praised — repeated like a litany by the
devout. Whether the rite itself is older than Islam in that place is an open
question and a good one.

## The poet of Pajang

Javanese tradition also holds that in the second half of the sixteenth century,
under Adiwijaya, Pangeran Karang Gayam wrote the moral poem *Niti Sruti*. He is
remembered in the literary tradition as *pujangga ing Pajang*, the poet of
Pajang.

His family came from around Taji, where a toll post stood on the road that had
for centuries been the main link between the Pengging-Pajang country and
Mataram — the same road Ki Pamanahan's settlers went west along, and the same
road the army came back down.

A poem, a road and a customs post. Between them they are most of what the
kingdom passed on.
