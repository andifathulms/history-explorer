The vassal who took the kingdom was the son of the man Adiwijaya had sent to
settle Mataram. Senapati began withholding what a vassal owed: he stopped
coming to court for the annual homage, and when the sultan ordered a punishment
carried out against the family of the *tumenggung* of Mayang — his own
brother-in-law — he gave the man asylum instead.

That forced the old sultan to march. The royal army broke up near Prambanan
before there was a battle, in large part because Merapi erupted.

On the way home he stopped at Tembayat, and the chronicles say that there he
understood his kingdom was finished and that the house of Mataram would rule
the whole of Java. He died soon after reaching his own palace — of illness, or
of an accident, or, in the version the chroniclers preferred, at the hands of
an unidentified gardener who wanted to do Senapati a service. He was buried at
Butuh, a little west of the royal garden, at a grave long known as Makam Aji.

The year was 1587.

## Four heirs and a holy man

There was no rule for what happened next, and so what happened next was decided
by whoever had the most authority in the room.

The sultan left three sons-in-law who were themselves rulers — of Tuban, of
Demak and of Aros Baya on Madura — and his own son, Pangeran Benawa, who was
young. Sunan Kudus used his standing to see that the court recognised not the
son but Arya Pangiri of Demak, who was a grandson of Trenggana and had married
a Pajang princess. What the scholar of Kudus seems to have wanted was to put
the sultanate of central Java back into the hands of a direct descendant of
Trenggana, forty years after Trenggana's death.

It failed within a year, and it failed on something ordinary. The new king
lived in the palace surrounded by officials he had brought from Demak, and the
grants and lands he handed to those coastal outsiders made the nobility and
landholders of Pajang itself resentful and jealous. Benawa, parked at Jipang
and unhappy there, made common cause with Senapati of Mataram and with the
disaffected at home, and in 1588 a short fight settled it.

Arya Pangiri's life was spared at the request of his wife, the Pajang princess.
He was tied with a silk sash and sent back to Demak — the same courtesy, and
the same sash, that Senapati would extend to the last claimant of Demak in the
same years.

## What the losing army was made of

One detail in the chronicles is worth more than the battle.

Arya Pangiri's force included hired soldiers he had brought from the coast:
slaves, Balinese, Bugis, Makassarese, and *peranakan* — Chinese of mixed
descent. They proved unreliable, and the men of Pajang went over to the other
side.

Mercenaries have to be paid, which means that by the last quarter of the
sixteenth century the coastal districts had an economy in which money did that
work. The interior — Pajang, and Mataram behind it — did not. The two halves of
Java were not only fighting each other, they were paying for it differently,
and the side that fought with obligations rather than coin won.

It is the third time in sixty years that a Javanese paramountcy passed from an
overlord to his own subordinate. Jaka Tingkir was Demak's man before he was
Demak's end; Senapati was Pajang's. It is also the last of them for a long
while, because what Senapati founded held Java until 1755.
