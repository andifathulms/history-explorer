The Javanese chronicles give this record's founder a career before the throne,
and it is worth setting out because it is the clearest example on this site of
what a dynastic chronicle is doing when it tells a founding story.

Jaka Tingkir is a young man from a village, of obscure but ultimately royal
descent, who goes to Demak, joins the sultan's guard, is expelled for killing a
rival applicant during a selection trial, is taken in by a holy man, and crosses
the Kedung Srengenge — where he is attacked by crocodiles and defeats forty of
them, who then tow his raft. He returns, marries Trenggana's daughter, is given
Pajang, and after the succession war becomes king.

## The three things it is doing at once

It establishes descent — the obscure origin is explicitly a disguise, and the
genealogy comes out later.

It establishes merit — he is physically formidable, and the killing that gets him
expelled is a display of the quality that later makes him fit to rule.

And it establishes sanction — the holy man, the ascetic interval, and the animals
that submit are the standard Javanese markers of *wahyu*, the light of kingship,
which is a thing that settles on a person rather than a thing a person inherits.

This record's `legitimation` field reads `descent` and `divine-sanction`, which
is what half this site's records read. Those two values are the first and third of
the three above, and the middle one — that he earned it — has no value in the
vocabulary at all.

## The comparison

This corpus's Singhasari record's first chapter is about Ken Arok, whose founding
story is a cursed dagger, a murdered smith and four generations of revenge
killing, and which that record's data comment declines to code as a succession
rule because what the *Pararaton* describes is a sequence of outcomes.

Three hundred and forty-six years apart, two Javanese founders of obscure origin
with a legend attached, in both cases recorded by the dynasty that descended from
them. Ken Arok's is a story about a curse. Jaka Tingkir's is a story about a
qualification.

The difference is not about the men. It is that the *Pararaton* was compiled by a
tradition that had watched the dynasty destroy itself, and the Javanese
chronicles of the seventeenth century were compiled under Mataram, which needed
Pajang to have been legitimate because Mataram's own claim ran through it.
