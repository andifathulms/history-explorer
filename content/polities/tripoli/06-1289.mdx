After Antioch fell in 1268 the northern Frankish position was one county, held
by the man who had been prince of Antioch, with Latakia as an outlying remnant
until 1287 and the Mamluk state to the south deciding when to finish it.

The county spent those years quarrelling with itself. Bohemond VII fought a war
against the Templars and against the commune of Tripoli, a body of townsmen and
Italian merchants who at one point ran the city in defiance of him. When he
died in 1287 without children the succession went to his sister Lucia, who was
in Apulia, and the commune preferred a republic under Genoese protection. The
dispute was still live when the Mamluk army arrived.

Qalawun besieged Tripoli in March 1289 and took it after a month. What happened
then is the part to be exact about. The city was not occupied. It was destroyed
— the population killed or enslaved, the buildings pulled down, and the
peninsula on which the old city stood abandoned. A new Tripoli was built inland
at the foot of Raymond's castle, and that is where the city is today. Removing
a port from the map is a deliberate policy and the Mamluks applied it along
that coast: a harbour the Franks could not use was a harbour no crusade could
land in.

Antioch 1268, Tripoli 1289, Acre 1291. The Frankish states of the Levant had
been there a hundred and ninety-one years when that sequence began, and
twenty-three years later there were none, and the same state did all three.

The county's end closes the northern half of that story two years before the
southern half. The last count of Tripoli died a hundred kilometres away in
Cyprus, and what remained of the four states — the titles, the law books, the
surviving families, the crown of Jerusalem itself — went to the island and
stayed there.

What continued from Cyprus was a kingdom and a claim. The county of Tripoli
made no claim to anything, and nothing continued from it at all.
