Tripoli fell in July 1109. Within a year the county had done something that
says more about how the Frankish states actually functioned than any battle
does.

Ibn al-Qalanisi records it from the other side. Having finished with Tripoli
and put the city and its districts in order, the Franks marched on Rafaniya.
The ruler of Damascus moved out to protect it and camped at Homs facing them.
The Franks could not invest the town.

So they wrote to each other.

## The treaty

The correspondence produced a settlement, and the terms are worth reading in
full because they are so unlike a holy war.

The Franks were to receive one third of the produce of the Biqa' — the great
valley between the Lebanon ranges, which neither side could hold outright and
both could raid. Two castles were to be handed over to them. In exchange they
were to stop plundering and ravaging in the provinces, and both parties were to
settle the boundaries of their respective territories and keep the peace.

A third of a harvest is not a frontier. It is a shared revenue over shared
ground, agreed in writing between a Muslim ruler and a Frankish one ten years
into the crusades, because the alternative — an annual campaign to decide who
got the crop — cost both of them more than the crop was worth.

## What a condominium implies

Arrangements of this kind are all over Qalanisi's chronicle and almost absent
from the Latin ones, which is itself informative about who wrote what down.

They imply a great deal. Somebody had to assess the harvest and divide it.
Somebody had to collect the Frankish third and hand it over. There had to be
people who could move between the two jurisdictions, count sacks, argue about
weights and be trusted enough by both sides to do it. None of those people
appear in the chronicles of either side, and the whole structure of coexistence
in twelfth-century Syria depended on them.

It also implies that a county of a hundred and eighty kilometres of coast, with
a Latin population that could be counted in thousands, was never going to live
by conquest. Tripoli's actual strategy for most of its existence was to hold
its castles, keep the ports open, take a share of what the interior produced,
and avoid provoking Damascus into a serious campaign.

That worked for a hundred and eighty-seven years, which is longer than the
county of Edessa managed by fighting and longer than Antioch managed by being
the strongest of the four.
