---
title: The godson who did not come
drafted_from: barber-2012

Raymond III never fully recovered from Hattin. He reached Tripoli, fell
ill — pleurisy, by one account — and died within months, in the autumn of
1187: the last ruler of the line that had held the county since Raymond of
Saint-Gilles founded it eighty-five years before.

He had no children, and he left the county by will to his godson: Raymond,
the elder son of Bohemond III, prince of Antioch. It was the natural choice,
a family already bound to Tripoli by two generations of marriage and
alliance, and it should have made the future ruler of Antioch the count of
Tripoli as well.

Bohemond III kept his heir at home. The man named in the will was not sent to
take up the inheritance; instead the prince sent his younger son, Bohemond,
to Tripoli in his brother's place, late in 1187. The county went to the
second son because the first one was needed at Antioch, and nobody with the
standing to contest the substitution was left alive to make the case.

That is how the union began, and it is worth being exact about what kind of
union it was at first. In 1187 it was one family holding two states, not one
ruler holding both: Antioch kept its own prince and Tripoli answered to a
different son of the same house. It became a single ruler's holding only in
1201, when Bohemond of Tripoli — by then established in the county for
fourteen years — succeeded his father at Antioch as well, over the claim of
his own dead elder brother's son, and held both titles as Bohemond IV.

From that point every count of Tripoli was also, or had been, prince of
Antioch, and the two states that had spent sixty years arguing their
separate cases to Byzantium and to Jerusalem answered to the same man. The
war Bohemond VII fought against his own commune was a war fought by a prince
who had inherited both titles as a single, undivided problem.
