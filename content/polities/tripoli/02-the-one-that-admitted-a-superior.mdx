The four Frankish states in the Levant are usually described as a system with
Jerusalem at its head. In law they were nothing of the kind. Antioch held that
it owed homage to nobody and spent sixty years arguing that with the Byzantine
emperors rather than with the Franks; Edessa was ruled by men who kept leaving
to become kings of Jerusalem, which is a connection of personnel and not of
tenure. Jerusalem's primacy over either was a matter of prestige and of who had
troops to lend.

Tripoli is the exception, and it is why this record carries a `vassal of` edge
from the Jerusalem page — the only one in this corpus that joins two Frankish
states.

The homage taken in 1109 was not ceremonial. Kings of Jerusalem arbitrated
Tripoli's succession disputes, and counts of Tripoli served in the kingdom's
armies and its politics as members of it. Raymond III, this record's `peak`, was
twice regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem, and in 1187 he was the man who argued
in council against marching out to relieve Tiberias — the advice that, had it
been taken, would have kept the field army in being. He was overruled, rode with
the army anyway, cut his way out of the encirclement at Hattin and died within
months.

The county itself was narrow: a hundred and eighty kilometres of coast with the
Lebanon mountains immediately behind it, and its inland frontier was never far
from the sea. What that country did contain was castles, and two of them
belonged to the military orders — Krak des Chevaliers to the Hospitallers,
Tortosa to the Templars — which is why this page's `military_basis` carries
`standing-professional` beside `land-grant` as the Jerusalem page does. In a
county this small, orders holding the two strongest fortresses in it were not a
supplement to the count's power. For much of the thirteenth century they were
more of it than he was.
