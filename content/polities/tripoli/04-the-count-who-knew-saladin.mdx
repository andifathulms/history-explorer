Raymond III of Tripoli spent the 1180s trying to keep the Latin East alive by
negotiating with the man who was going to destroy it, and the reason he failed
had nothing to do with Saladin.

## Taken, and released

He first appears in Lane-Poole's narrative as a prisoner. At Marj Uyun in June
1179 Saladin beat the king of Jerusalem and took seventy knights, and the list of
them is the whole leadership of the crusader states: the masters of the Temple
and the Hospital, Balian and Baldwin of Ibelin, Hugh of Tiberias — and Raymond of
Tripoli.

Captivity in this world was a negotiation rather than an ending. Men were
ransomed, and while they waited they talked. Whatever Raymond learned in those
months, he came out of it as the one Frankish magnate who dealt with Saladin as a
person rather than as a condition of the weather.

## The regent's truce

By the mid-1180s Raymond was regent of the kingdom of Jerusalem for the infant
Baldwin V, which made the count of Tripoli the effective head of the Latin East.

He used the position to make a four-year peace with Saladin — and Lane-Poole's
phrasing about why he could is the important part: Raymond was *personally on
terms of friendship if not actual alliance* with him.

That is an extraordinary thing to be able to write about a crusader prince, and it
was the county's best asset. Tripoli was a long thin coastal strip with the
Assassins and the Muslim interior behind it and no strategic depth at all. A
negotiated frontier was not a compromise for such a state; it was the only version
of it that could exist.

## Why it did not hold

Lane-Poole is clear that the truce was hollow from the start, and the reasons had
nothing to do with either signatory.

All Europe was arming. Knights from the Cheviots to the Pyrenees were taking the
cross. The two military orders were burning to strike a blow for the faith as
ardently as Saladin was. A settlement that suited the people who lived in Syria
was intolerable to the people who kept arriving in it.

And there was Reynald of Châtillon at Kerak, who had already sent ships into the
Red Sea to seize pilgrim vessels and had gone into Arabia with the declared
intention of destroying the Prophet's tomb at Medina and the Kaaba at Mecca. That
expedition was cut to pieces by the Egyptian fleet. Saladin twice besieged his
fortress and twice failed to take it.

Then, for the third time and in open breach of the treaty Raymond had negotiated,
Reynald fell on a peaceful merchant caravan passing his stronghold — rich, and
rumoured to include one of Saladin's sisters.

Lane-Poole's verdict on that is four words: *the provocation was ample*.

## What it cost the county

The war that followed took Jerusalem and broke the field army of the Latin East
at Hattin, and Raymond was in that battle too.

Tripoli survived it, because coastal cities with harbours could be relieved by sea
and inland kingdoms could not. But what survived was the strip and not the state.
The county kept its ports and lost the argument that had kept it alive: that a
small Frankish power could deal with its Muslim neighbours as neighbours.

For the hundred years remaining to it, Tripoli would go on making treaties. It
would never again have a count who could make one because the other man trusted
him.
