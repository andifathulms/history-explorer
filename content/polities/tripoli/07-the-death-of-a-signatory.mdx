The Mamluk sultans did not break their treaties with the Frankish states so
much as read them narrowly.

Lane-Poole's summary of Qalawun's method is blunt: his treaties were valid only
so long as he found them convenient, and his oath was no more sacred than
Baybars'. The mechanics were tidier than that makes them sound, and worse.

In 1285 he fell on the Hospitallers' great fortress of Marqab, in spite of a
ten-year engagement with them, and took it because it was unprepared. The count
of Tripoli was then made to surrender Maraqiya on the coast, a place whose
position made it proof against a siege from the land. The lady of Tyre bought
ten years of paper peace by handing over half her revenues and undertaking
never to repair her fortifications. Armenia bought a truce with a million
dirhams a year, the release of every Muslim prisoner, and a promise to abandon
its own defences.

Then Latakia was seized in 1287 in open defiance of the standing treaty with
Tripoli.

## The doctrine

And then Bohemond VII died, and the legal position changed, because the death
of a signatory was held at that point to void a treaty.

That is the sentence to sit with. The agreement protecting a state did not
survive the man who signed it; a ruler's death cancelled his country's peace.
Any Frankish lord in Syria in the 1280s was therefore governing a place whose
security expired with him, negotiating with a power that would honour the terms
exactly as long as it wanted to and had a legal theory ready for when it did
not.

In 1289, as described, the city was besieged, stormed and destroyed.

## What the destruction was for

The destruction was policy rather than fury. A fortified harbour on that coast
was somewhere a European fleet could land and hold, so the Mamluks were
removing the harbours — theirs as much as anyone's. They gave up the Levantine
ports on purpose, and the coast stayed poor for centuries as a result.

Two years later the same thing was done at Acre, and the Frankish century in
Syria was finished.

The county had lasted a hundred and eighty-seven years, longer than any of the
others, on castles, sea trade and a share of somebody else's harvest. It ended
in a month because the man whose name was on the paper had died.
