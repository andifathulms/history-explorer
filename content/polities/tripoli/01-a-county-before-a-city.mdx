---
title: A county before a city
drafted_from: barber-2012
phase: formation
---

Raymond IV of Saint-Gilles, count of Toulouse, was the richest and most senior
of the leaders of the First Crusade and the one who came out of it with nothing.
Bohemond had Antioch. Baldwin had Edessa and then Jerusalem. Godfrey had been
chosen at Jerusalem over Raymond's own candidacy. In 1102 he set about
manufacturing a principality for himself out of the one stretch of the Syrian
coast nobody else had claimed.

Tripoli was a wealthy Fatimid port on a peninsula with a fleet able to supply it
from the sea, and it did not fall to sieges. Raymond's answer was to build a
castle on the hill overlooking the city — Mount Pilgrim, which the Arabic
sources call Qal'at Sanjil after him, and which is still standing — and to sit
in it, taking the county's countryside and cutting the city's landward revenue
while he waited. He waited three years and died in 1105. The city held out four
more.

This is why the span on this record begins in 1102 rather than 1109. For seven
years there was a lordship with a fortress, a territory, a revenue and a
succession dispute, and no capital. The state existed before the city it is
named after was in it, which is unusual enough to be worth the field note.

Tripoli fell in July 1109 to a Genoese fleet and an army brought north by
Baldwin I of Jerusalem, and the king then did the thing that defines this
county. Raymond's son Bertrand and his cousin William-Jordan were both claiming
the inheritance; Baldwin arbitrated between them, divided the conquest, and took
their homage for it. The county got a capital and an overlord in the same week,
and the second is the more consequential.
