Around 1510 a Granadan diplomat travelling the Sudan for the sultan of Fez
passed through the kingdom whose ruler had, two centuries earlier, spent so much
gold in Cairo that the price of it was still depressed a decade later. His entry
on Mali is short and entirely matter-of-fact, and it is the clearest measure
anywhere of what had happened.

## What was there

> In this kingdome there is a large and ample village containing to the number of
> sixe thousand or mo families, and called Melli, whereof the whole kingdome is so
> named. And here the king hath his place of residence.

A village. Leo uses the word without irony and without comparison; he is simply
recording the size of the place, and six thousand families is not small. But the
same book calls Timbuktu a town and Gao a great city, and the seat of the mansas
gets the word for a settlement.

What he found there was prosperous. The region yields great abundance of corn,
flesh and cotton; there are many artisans and merchants everywhere; the
inhabitants are rich and have plenty of wares; and the king *honourably
entertaineth all strangers*. There are great numbers of mosques, priests and
professors — and then a detail a scholar would notice, because Leo was one:

> which professours read their lectures onely in the temples, bicause they haue
> no colleges at all.

Teaching in the mosque because there is no madrasa is not poverty; it is how most
of the Islamic world taught. But Leo had just come from Timbuktu, where there
were colleges and a book trade, and he mentions the absence because the
comparison was in front of him.

## The sentence that matters

Leo gives the Malians the highest praise in his whole account of the Sudan: they
*excell all other Negros in witte, ciuilitie, and industry*, and were the first
of them to take up Islam.

Then, in the same breath, the end of the story:

> At length Ischia subdued the prince of this region, and made him his
> tributarie, and so oppressed him with greeuous exactions, that he was scarce
> able to maintaine his family.

Askia Muhammad of Songhai had reduced the mansa to a tributary and taxed him to
the point where he could not keep his own household.

## What that is worth

It is worth being careful about the evidence. Leo is writing from memory in Italy
years afterwards, for a European readership, and the identification of every place
in his Sudan chapters has been argued over. He almost certainly did not see the
Malian court himself; the passage reads like a summary of what he was told on the
road.

But the shape of it is confirmed by everything else. Mali had lost the gold
routes to Songhai, lost the Sahel towns, lost Timbuktu, and by the 1540s was
sending embassies to Portugal asking for help against its neighbours. A century
later the mansas were one chiefdom among several on the upper Niger.

The empire whose ruler's pilgrimage is the single best-known fact about medieval
West Africa lasted, in its reduced form, longer than most of the states that had
taken pieces off it. What Leo saw was not a ruin. It was a rich agricultural
district with a market, a mosque, a court that received strangers properly, and a
ruler who could not pay his tax.
