This record's span runs to 1670 and its `ended.type` is `fragmentation` with an
empty `by`, which needs the same qualification the Aksumite record's ending does:
no conqueror, no date anybody would defend closely, and a long contraction rather
than a fall.

## What went, in order

Timbuktu in 1433, to Tuareg out of the desert — this record's second turning
point, and the first substantial loss. It went to a power with no state behind
it, which is the part worth noticing: the empire's frontier at the desert edge
had stopped being defensible before any rival empire existed.

Then Gao. Songhai had been a Malian province in the fourteenth century; by the
1460s it was taking the Niger bend, and this corpus carries it as the record two
entries along.

Then the Atlantic. Portuguese ships reached the Senegal in the 1440s and the Gold
Coast in the 1470s, and gold that had gone north across the Sahara for six
centuries began going west to the sea instead. Mali understood the problem
directly: an embassy went to Portugal in 1534 asking for help against Songhai,
which is the earliest recorded diplomatic contact between a West African empire
and a European state, and it got nothing.

Niani was sacked by Songhai in 1545 and held briefly. Morocco destroyed Songhai
in 1591 and Mali outlived it by decades without being able to take advantage.
The Bamana of Segu finished it in the seventeenth century.

## The measure this record does have

`reach_km2` reads 1,100,000 at 1380, from Taagepera, and it is one of only two
such figures anywhere in Africa on this site. The other is Songhai's, on the same
river a century later. The remaining ten African records — Ghana, Kanem-Bornu,
Asante, Kush, Makuria, Alodia, Aksum, Ethiopia, Kilwa and Great Zimbabwe — carry
no reach figure at all.

For scale: larger than the Holy Roman Empire at its widest, about four-fifths of
the Neo-Assyrian empire at its peak, and about four-fifths of the Byzantine
empire at *its* peak — which was in 555, eight hundred years before this figure's
date, by which time Byzantium had almost nothing left. Nothing in Latin Europe
was close.

The column itself is worth a caution, though, and this is as good a page as any
to give it. Only fifty-three of this site's records carry a reach figure at all,
because the figures come from tabulations of large empires and a polity has to be
big to get into one. Ranking within that column is therefore a ranking among the
already-large, and a record with no figure — Aksum's, three entries back — is not
below the ones with figures. It is outside the exercise.

The comparison is worth stating because the general-history version of this
empire is a pilgrimage and a gold price.

## What Africa has

Twelve records covering a continent, in a corpus of nearly two hundred.

There were three when this chapter was first written, and this paragraph has been
rewritten twice since, each time to strike names off its own list of absences.
Ghana, which preceded this record on the same routes, is here. So is
Kanem-Bornu, which lasted from the ninth century to the nineteenth around Lake
Chad; Kush, which ruled Egypt as its twenty-fifth dynasty and then held Meroë for
a thousand years; Makuria and Alodia on the middle Nile; Aksum's successor in
Ethiopia; Kilwa on the Indian Ocean coast; Great Zimbabwe on the plateau behind
it; and Asante in the forest.

Twelve was enough for the single `africa` region to stop making sense, and it has
been split into three: West Africa, which is this record's, the Nile and the
Horn, and the Zimbabwe plateau with its coast. That is a better shape and it is
not more coverage. Benin, Oyo, the Hausa city-states, the Sokoto caliphate,
Kongo, the Funj and Mutapa are all still absent, and two of those — the Funj and
Mutapa — are the reason two records in this corpus end against nobody.

This corpus's stated rule is that a polity never requires an edge and that
measurement does not require a predecessor, and the rule was written so that
entries like these could not be treated as second-class once they arrived. What
the rule cannot do is get them written. The thinness is a fact about where the
project has spent its attention, and this paragraph is on the page so that a
reader does not mistake it for a fact about Africa.
