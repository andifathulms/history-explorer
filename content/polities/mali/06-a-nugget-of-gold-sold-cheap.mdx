Ibn Battuta left Mali still under Mansa Sulayman, the king whose court
he had found stingy and correct in equal measure. What followed
Sulayman's own death, within a generation, turned an empire Ibn Battuta
had described as safer to travel than almost anywhere else into one
its own princes were actively looting.

## A wife's conspiracy, and a son who did not last

Ibn Battuta's own account already hints at the trouble to come: he
records that Sulayman's wife conspired against her husband during his
stay, an internal quarrel the traveler treated as scandal rather than
as the sign of institutional weakness it turned out to be. When
Sulayman died, his son Fomba inherited the throne and held it barely a
year before being deposed in 1359 by Mari Diata II, also known as
Sundiata II - a name that borrowed the founder's own, and delivered
nothing of his restraint.

## An empire run into the ground

Mari Diata II's own reign is remembered by Mali's own later chroniclers
as a catalogue of waste. He exhausted the royal treasury outright, and
in the single act later writers treated as emblematic of the whole
reign, sold off the dynasty's most famous treasure - a nugget of gold
so large it was reckoned at twenty kintars in weight - to Egyptian
merchants for a fraction of its worth. Whatever royal restraint had
kept Mansa Musa's own fortune intact through a pilgrimage that gave
away staggering quantities of gold in Cairo, Mari Diata II possessed
none of it. He was eventually incapacitated by illness - sleeping
sickness, by the account that survives - and removed from effective
power before his own death.

## A son on the throne, a general running the country

Mari Diata II's son, Mansa Musa III, held the throne from around 1374
to 1387, but the empire's actual governance during his reign belonged
to his general, who restored a measure of order and put down a
rebellion at the copper-trading town of Takedda. It was a pattern that
would recur for the rest of the century: princesses and court factions
intriguing over the succession, provincial governors increasingly
ignoring instructions from Niani, and an imperial prestige that,
however diminished in practice, still carried enough weight abroad that
its actual unraveling was not yet visible from outside. That
unraveling would become impossible to miss once Timbuktu itself, the
empire's own greatest trading city, fell to raiders from the desert its
own frontier had stopped being able to hold.
