Ibn Battuta reached the Malian court in 1352, stayed eight months, and disliked a
good deal of what he found. That is what makes his account useful.

He is impressed by the security — he says the people of Mali have a greater
abhorrence of injustice than any other, that the sultan pardons nobody guilty of
it, and that a traveller has no fear of thieves on a road of four months' length.
He is impressed by the observance of prayer, the memorisation of the Qur'an by
children, and the crowds in the mosques on Fridays.

He is appalled that women appear before men unveiled, that a man's heir is his
sister's son rather than his own, that female servants and slaves go naked, that
people put dust on their heads before the sultan, and that some subjects still
eat things he considers forbidden. He complains that a gift he was given was
small.

## Why a hostile witness is worth more

Ibn Battuta had spent thirty years travelling from Morocco to China and had seen
more courts than almost anybody alive. When he compares Mali's judicial security
favourably to everywhere else he has been, that is a comparison with the
Delhi sultanate, the Ilkhanate's successor states, Mamluk Egypt and the Yuan
empire — all records in this corpus.

And when he objects to matrilineal inheritance and to unveiled women, he is
recording practices that his own preconceptions made him notice. A sympathetic
visitor would have passed over them.

Gomez's book, which this record's institutions are coded from, uses exactly that
material, and its argument about gender and caste in the Niger empires rests
substantially on details that Arabic visitors reported because they found them
objectionable.

## The succession this record cannot code

The sister's son is the reason `succession_rule` on this page is null.

Ibn Battuta's observation is about inheritance generally rather than about the
throne, but the Malian royal succession shows the same shape: it runs to
brothers, to sons and to sisters' sons, in no order any source states, with
long disputes and at least two seizures by men outside the Keita line — including
a court slave who held power for several years.

Coding rule 7 takes the rule in force, and there is no statement of one. What
this record has instead is a pattern that mixes two principles, described by a
visitor who thought one of them was scandalous.

The Serbian record in this corpus is null on the same field despite a two-hundred
article law code that legislates everything except the throne. This one is null
because the tradition that held the rule was oral, professional, and not written
down in a form that survives — which is a different kind of silence and produces
the same empty field.

## The one thing everybody knows

Mansa Musa's pilgrimage is the next chapter and it is the only thing about this
empire that reliably appears in general histories written outside Africa.

It is worth saying here, before that chapter, that it is a poor summary of the
record. The pilgrimage is a single journey by one ruler in a three-hundred-year
span, it is famous because of its effect on the Cairo gold price and on European
cartography, and both of those are facts about how the rest of the world found
out about Mali rather than facts about Mali.

What this record is actually about is on the two chapters before this one: a
state founded in a song, taxing a commodity it had decided not to administer, and
holding a road four months long safely enough for a Moroccan lawyer to walk it
alone.
