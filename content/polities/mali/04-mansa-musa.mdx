---
title: The pilgrimage, and what it cost
drafted_from: levtzion-1973
phase: peak
---

Mansa Musa left for Mecca in 1324 with an entourage the Egyptian sources
describe in figures that should be treated cautiously, and gold in quantity. He
spent and gave it away in Cairo on a scale that the sources say depressed the
value of gold there for years afterwards.

Two things follow, and only one of them is usually told.

The first is reputation. Cairo was the informational hub of the Islamic world,
and after 1324 West Africa was on the map — literally: the Catalan Atlas of 1375
draws a crowned Black king holding a gold nugget on the southern edge of the
world, labelled as the lord of Mali. It is the first substantial European image
of a sub-Saharan state.

The second is that the pilgrimage was a fiscal event for somebody else's economy,
which is a good indication of the scale of the gold trade this site's Almoravid
page describes from the northern end. Mali and the Islamic west are the two ends
of one system, and the whole Mediterranean bullion supply ran through it.

Musa returned with scholars and an architect, endowed mosques at Timbuktu and
Gao, and made the pilgrimage route a channel for Maliki learning into the Niger
bend. That is the propagation this page's religion-carried count records: not the
ruling class adopting Islam, which had happened generations earlier, but the
institutions built afterwards that carried it further.
