Ibn Battuta reached the Malian court in 1352, stayed eight months, disliked a
good deal of it, and wrote the most detailed account anybody produced of a West
African empire at its height. The dislike is what makes it useful.

He arrived sick and was well treated. He lodged with the resident foreigners; the
qadi, a noted pilgrim, made him his guest and sent him a present and a cow; he
was ill for two months.

Then Mansa Sulayman gave a feast, and Ibn Battuta was presented, and afterwards
the sultan sent a gift to the judge's house. The qadi came hurrying to fetch him:
*Up, for the Sultan has sent you a present.*

> I hastened, expecting that a dress of honour, some horses, and other valuables,
> had been sent; but, behold! they were only three crusts of bread, with a piece
> of fried fish, and a dish of sour milk. I smiled at their simplicity, and the
> great value they set on such trifles as these.

Two months later he had still received nothing, so he stood up in the sultan's
presence and said so:

> I have travelled the world over, and have seen its kings; and now, I have been
> four months in thy territories, but no present, or even provision from thee,
> has yet reached me. Now, what shall I say of thee, when I shall be interrogated
> on the subject hereafter?

He got a house and provisions, and on leaving he got a hundred mithqals of gold.
His summary judgement of the man who gave it to him is *a most avaricious and
worthless man*.

## The prostration

What he describes of the court protocol is precise and hostile in equal measure.

Of all people, he says, the blacks debase themselves most in the presence of
their king. A man summoned before the sultan puts off his usual clothing and puts
on a worn-out dress; he prostrates himself; and when the sultan addresses
somebody, the man takes up dust and puts it on his head and shoulders. While the
sultan speaks, everybody present stands with his turban off.

That reads as humiliation to a fourteenth-century Moroccan lawyer accustomed to
the etiquette of Cairo and Delhi. It is a court ceremonial of extreme formality
serving the same function that kneeling and uncovering served in Europe, and the
useful thing about the passage is that he could not stop himself from writing
down exactly what he saw before delivering his opinion of it.

## What he admired

Two things, and both are worth having from a man who had been almost everywhere.

> One of the best things in these parts is, the regard they pay to justice, for,
> in this respect, the Sultan regards neither little nor much. The safety, too, is
> very great; so that a traveller may proceed alone among them, without the least
> fear of a thief or robber.

Ibn Battuta had crossed the Delhi sultanate, Ilkhanid Persia, Mamluk Egypt and
the roads of Yuan China. When he says the security of the Malian roads is the
best he has met, that is a comparison with most of the fourteenth-century world.

He also admired the observance of prayer, and the insistence that children commit
the Quran to memory — he describes seeing boys in chains until they had learned
their portion, which he approved of.

## What he condemned

The women, mostly. Female servants and slaves went naked; free women did not
clothe themselves until marriage; women, including the sultan's own daughters,
came into the royal presence unclothed. Some of the population still ate carrion
and dogs and asses.

And the inheritance. Among the merchants at Walata further north he had already
found that nobody was named after his father but after his maternal uncle, and
that a man's heir was his sister's son and not his own — *a custom I witnessed
nowhere else, except among the infidel Hindoos of Malabar*. What offended him was
not the practice but the combination: these were Muslims who knew the Quran by
heart and had an explicit inheritance law available to them, and were not using
it.

## Why the hostility is the evidence

A sympathetic visitor would have passed over all of it.

Almost everything specific that is known about how fourteenth-century Mali
actually worked — the ceremonial, the inheritance, the position of women, the
policing of the roads — survives because a foreign lawyer found it objectionable
and could not resist recording precisely what he objected to. He is the only
outsider who went, and the ways in which he was wrong about the place are the
reason there is anything to be right about.
