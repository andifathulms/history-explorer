---
title: The other end of the gold
drafted_from: levtzion-1973
phase: peak
---

Almost every state around the Mediterranean in this period struck gold coin, and
a great deal of the gold came from West Africa, up the trans-Saharan routes
through Sijilmasa and Awdaghust. The Almoravids were a Saharan movement sitting
on the northern end of those routes. Mali held the southern end.

Sundiata Keita assembled it in the 1230s out of the Malinke chieftaincies of the
upper Niger, on the ruins of Ghana. It controlled the goldfields of Bambuk and
Bure, the Niger as a transport artery, and the caravan termini at Timbuktu and
Gao. Mansa Musa's pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 — with an entourage and quantity of
gold that reportedly disturbed the Cairo market for years afterwards — is the
event that put a West African ruler on European maps, and the Catalan Atlas of
1375 draws him holding a gold nugget.

Mali at its widest, in 1380, is put at about 1,100,000 square kilometres — one of
the larger states on earth at the time, and larger than anything in Latin Europe.

That figure should be read with a caution the empire itself demands. Its
authority was strong along the river and the caravan routes and progressively
weaker away from them, and what it actually controlled was a network rather than
a territory. The area is the box the network sits in.

What it did propagate outward was Islam, and the distinction matters. The
merchants brought it and the ruling class adopted it generations before Mansa
Musa; what Mali did afterwards was endow institutions — Timbuktu and Djenné
became centres of learning drawing scholars from the whole Maghreb — which is a
different act from converting.
