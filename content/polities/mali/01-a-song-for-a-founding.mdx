Almost everything known about the founding of Mali comes from a poem that was
performed for seven centuries before anybody wrote it down.

The Sundiata epic is the property of the *jeliw* — griots, hereditary
specialists in genealogy, praise and history, whose families have been attached
to the same lineages for generations. It tells of a crippled boy, son of a
Malinke king and a hunchbacked woman, exiled by a rival queen, who returns to
overthrow Sumanguru of Sosso — a sorcerer-king — at a battle called Kirina, and
founds an empire.

It was first transcribed in the twentieth century.

## What is and is not in doubt

The event is not seriously doubted. There was a Sosso power, Mali replaced it,
and the Arabic sources writing a century later know Sundiata's name — Ibn Khaldun
has him as Mari Jata, founder of the dynasty. What is uncertain is the date,
which is conventional; the sequence; and how much of the narrative is thirteenth
century and how much is the accumulated shaping of a text whose whole purpose is
to be recited well.

Gomez treats the oral tradition as evidence to be handled carefully rather than
discarded, which was not the default when the older narratives of West Africa
were written, and it changes a great deal about what can be said.

## What a griot is for

It is worth being precise, because "oral tradition" invites a reader to imagine
folklore.

The *jeliw* are a caste. They marry within it, they hold hereditary attachments
to particular noble families, and their function is professional: to know the
genealogy, to recite it accurately, to praise, and on occasion to speak to a
ruler in terms nobody else may use. A dynasty's legitimacy is partly held in the
memory of specialists who are paid to hold it.

The Keita descent from Sundiata is a claim in the ordinary sense — publicly
made, and the ground of a right to rule — and the institution that maintains it
is a profession rather than a chancery.

There is a second descent claim beside it and it points the other way entirely.
In the Arabic sources the Keita trace themselves to Bilal ibn Rabah, the
Abyssinian whom the Prophet made the first muezzin. That makes the dynasty's
Islam ancestral rather than adopted, and it is aimed at an audience in Cairo and
Fez rather than on the Niger.

A dynasty maintaining two genealogies for two audiences is not unusual — the
kings of Pontus claimed Darius and Alexander at the same time, for the same
reason. What is unusual here is that one of Mali's was kept by a hereditary
profession, in performance, and the other was written down in a language most of
the empire could not read.

## The shape of the thing

The empire that followed Kirina was not a territorial administration in the way
a Mediterranean empire was.

It was a core on the upper Niger, a set of provinces under appointed governors,
and beyond them a wide zone of tributaries, allied chiefs and trading towns that
paid, sent contingents, and otherwise ran themselves. Al-Umari, writing in Cairo
from informants, describes fourteen provinces. Ibn Battuta, who was actually
there in 1352, describes a court with elaborate protocol, a functioning judiciary
and roads safe enough for a traveller to use alone — which he says explicitly and
which he did not say about most places he went.
