The empire ended in the seventeenth century and the poem did not.

The Sundiata epic went on being performed, by the same hereditary families, for
another three hundred years after there was no Mali to perform it for — through
the Bamana states, through the Muslim jihads of the eighteenth and nineteenth
centuries, through French colonial rule, through independence, and it is being
performed now.

That is a longer continuous run than the empire managed, and it is the reason
anybody can say anything at all about how Mali was founded.

## What a griot is for

Not entertainment, and not history in the sense a chronicler would recognise.

The *jeliw* are a hereditary occupational group, attached by descent to
particular lineages, whose professional business is genealogy, praise and the
public memory of who is descended from whom. A family has its griots; the griots
have that family's past; and the arrangement is inherited on both sides.

That produces a very specific kind of record. It is maintained by people with a
professional stake in its being impressive, performed to audiences who know it
and would notice a change, and transmitted with enough formal structure that it
does not simply drift into invention — while being under continuous pressure to
serve the present, because the point of a genealogy is what it entitles somebody
to now.

Everything strong and everything unreliable about the Sundiata tradition follows
from that. It is not a document, and it is not folklore either.

## What was written down, and when

The epic reached print in the twentieth century, and the versions differ, which
is what one would expect of a performed text with hundreds of performers.

The best-known English text descends from a recitation by Djeli Mamoudou
Kouyaté, of Guinea, published in the 1960s — a man from a griot family, giving a
version his family had held, at the moment several West African countries were
becoming independent and looking for a usable past. Others have been recorded
since, and they are recognisably the same story with different emphases and
different lengths.

So the founding of the empire is known from a text that was fixed on paper seven
hundred years after the events, by the profession that had kept it, in the decade
the region was being decolonised.

## Why that is not a disqualification

It would have been treated as one for most of the twentieth century, and that
assumption has been going out for about fifty years.

An oral tradition maintained by professionals with a formal structure and a
critical audience is not the same thing as a rumour, and it is not obviously
worse evidence than a chronicle written up at a court two hundred years after the
fact by somebody with a patron. Both need handling. Only one of them was
routinely dismissed.

Gomez's argument, and it is now the ordinary position, is that the material has
to be worked rather than either accepted whole or thrown away — and when it is
worked, a good deal of it lines up with what Ibn Khaldun heard in Cairo and what
Ibn Battuta saw on the Niger.

## And the other inheritance

The instrument, not the story.

Ghana had taxed the transit and taken a royalty on the metal without owning the
mines. Mali did the same thing. Songhai did the same thing after Mali. Every one
of the three collected at the desert edge and let the goldfields alone, because
the goldfields belonged to people who were not their subjects and who would stop
digging if pressed.

Three empires, six hundred years, one fiscal system, and the only thing that
ever broke it was a ship.
