This record's `revenue_basis` reads `mining, trade-toll`, and the first value
needs a correction that changes the shape of the empire.

Mali did not own the goldfields.

The gold came from Bambuk, between the Senegal and the Falémé, and from Bure on
the upper Niger, and later from the Akan forest further south. The people who
worked it were not Malian subjects in the ordinary sense and were not Muslim, and
the empire's authority over them was a matter of arrangement rather than
administration.

The story everybody tells about this comes from al-Umari, writing in Cairo in
the 1330s from people who had come with Mansa Musa. A mansa attempted to bring
the gold-producing country under direct rule and to convert its people; the
producers responded by not producing; the arrangement was restored and left
alone thereafter.

Whether it happened is less important than that it was the account the Malians
themselves gave of why the empire stopped at a particular line on the map.

## What the state took instead

A royalty on production and a tax on movement.

All gold *nuggets* belonged to the mansa; only gold dust circulated. That is a
claim on the product rather than on the mine, and it is enforceable at the point
where the gold enters the empire rather than where it comes out of the ground.
Beside it were the tolls on the trans-Saharan trade — the salt coming south from
Taghaza, the gold going north, and the copper, cloth, horses and books coming
back.

The `mining` value is coded on the royalty and the chapter is the argument for
it. A reader who assumes Mali owned the mines has the empire's whole
constitution wrong: it was a state that taxed a commodity produced by people it
had deliberately decided not to govern.

## Salt

The other half of the trade is the one that gets less attention and was worth
more per journey.

Salt came from Taghaza in the middle of the Sahara, where the houses and the
mosque were reportedly built of salt blocks because there was nothing else, and
was carried south in slabs. In the savanna it was scarce and necessary, and the
exchange rate against gold at the southern end was close enough to parity in some
accounts to have generated a good deal of European legend about it.

Mali sat between a region with gold and no salt and a region with salt and no
gold, and taxed the traffic in both directions.

## And the third commodity

Slaves, moving north.

The trans-Saharan slave trade ran for a millennium before the Atlantic one and
continued alongside it, and the Niger empires were both suppliers and consumers.
This record's `military_basis` carries `slave-soldier` and Gomez's account, which
that field is coded from, is direct about what the institution rested on: an
empire whose free troops came through intermediaries who could withhold them
acquired a force that could not, by buying and capturing people.

That is a fact about this record's coded fields and not an aside. Two of the four
institution values on this page — the slave soldiery and the royalty on gold —
describe a state whose power came from controlling the movement of things and
people rather than from administering territory.
