Mali did not own the goldfields.

The gold came from Bambuk, between the Senegal and the Falémé, and from Bure on
the upper Niger, and later from the Akan forest further south. The people who
worked it were not Malian subjects in the ordinary sense, and were not Muslim,
and the empire's authority over them was a matter of arrangement rather than
administration.

The story everybody tells about this comes from al-Umari, writing in Cairo in the
1330s from people who had travelled with Mansa Musa. A mansa attempted to bring
the gold-producing country under direct rule and to convert its people. The
producers responded by not producing. The arrangement was restored, and left
alone thereafter.

Whether it happened as told matters less than the fact that this was the account
the Malians themselves gave of why their empire stopped at a particular line on
the map.

## What the state took instead

A royalty on production, and a tax on movement.

All gold *nuggets* belonged to the mansa; only gold dust circulated. That is a
claim on the product rather than on the mine, and it is enforceable where the
gold enters the empire rather than where it comes out of the ground. Beside it
were the tolls on the trans-Saharan trade: salt coming south from Taghaza, gold
going north, and copper, cloth, horses and books coming back.

A reader who assumes Mali owned the mines has the empire's whole constitution
wrong. This was a state that taxed a commodity produced by people it had
deliberately decided not to govern.

## Salt

The other half of the trade gets less attention and was worth more per journey.

Salt came from Taghaza in the middle of the Sahara, where the houses and the
mosque were reportedly built of salt blocks because there was nothing else to
build with, and it went south in slabs. In the savanna it was scarce and
necessary, and the exchange rate against gold at the southern end was close
enough to parity in some accounts to have generated a good deal of European
legend.

Mali sat between a region with gold and no salt and a region with salt and no
gold, and taxed the traffic in both directions.

## And the third commodity

Slaves, moving north.

The trans-Saharan slave trade ran for a millennium before the Atlantic one and
continued alongside it, and the Niger empires were both suppliers and consumers.
Mali's own army rested on it: free troops came through intermediaries who could
withhold them, and a ruler who wanted a force that could not be withheld acquired
one by buying and capturing people.

Put the two together — the royalty on gold and the purchased soldiery — and the
shape of the state is clear enough. Its power came from controlling the movement
of things and of people, not from administering territory.
