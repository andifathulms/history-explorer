Frankincense and myrrh are the dried resins of trees that grow in a narrow band
of country in southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa and almost nowhere else.
The ancient Mediterranean burned them in quantity — in temples, at funerals, in
medicine, as perfume — and had no way to produce them. That single fact made the
southern corner of the Arabian peninsula one of the wealthiest regions of the
ancient world, and gave it the name Roman geographers used: Arabia Felix,
fortunate Arabia, as against the rocky Arabia to the north and the desert
between.

The resin came from Hadramawt and Dhofar. It travelled north by camel caravan up
the western edge of the peninsula, through a string of oases and kingdoms, to
Gaza and the Mediterranean — a journey of two thousand kilometres taking
months, through country where a caravan needs water, fodder, protection and
permission at every stage. Whoever controlled a stretch of that road taxed it.

Several kingdoms did. Saba' around Marib, Ma'in to its north, Qataban to the
south-east, Hadramawt in the east where the trees actually grew, Awsan on the
coast, and later Himyar in the highlands. They fought each other, absorbed each
other, and are known to us overwhelmingly through their own inscriptions —
tens of thousands of them, cut in stone in a monumental alphabet, recording
dedications, building works, campaigns and laws.

Saba' is the largest and best documented of them, and it is the one outsiders
had heard of. The Hebrew Bible has a queen of Sheba visiting Solomon;
Assyrian royal inscriptions from the eighth century BC record tribute from a
king of Saba'; Greek and Roman writers from Theophrastus to Pliny describe the
Sabaeans as the richest people on earth, on evidence they had mostly not seen.

The site's chronology for the kingdom carries a two-hundred-year range at the
start, and the range is a real disagreement rather than a hedge: South Arabian
epigraphy has a long and a short chronology, and the earliest Sabaean rulers sit
several centuries apart depending on which one is used.
