title: The dam at Marib

Marib sits at the mouth of a wadi where the highlands drop to the desert. Twice
a year, when the monsoon rains fall on the mountains, the wadi runs — briefly,
violently, and then not at all. The whole of Sabaean civilisation is what was
built to catch that water.

The dam was not a reservoir in the modern sense. It was a barrier across the
wadi with sluices at either end, designed to divert the flood into two networks
of stone-lined canals that spread it over the fields on both banks — perhaps
ninety square kilometres of irrigated land in the middle of a desert, feeding a
population that had no other way to exist there. Work on it begins in the eighth
century BC or earlier and continues for over a millennium; the surviving
structures are successive rebuildings, and the inscriptions recording repairs
are a large part of what is known about later Sabaean and Himyarite politics,
because keeping it working required a state and saying so was worth cutting in
stone.

It is the largest hydraulic work of the ancient world outside Rome and China,
and it was maintained for something like a thousand years.

The god of Marib was Almaqah, and the early Sabaean rulers are styled *mukarrib*
rather than king. The word's meaning is argued over — federator, unifier,
priest-king are all proposed — and the consensus is that it combined political
leadership over a confederation of tribes with a role in Almaqah's cult and in
the pilgrimage to his temple. This site codes the legitimation `divine-sanction`
on that basis, with the argument recorded here rather than smoothed away. Later
rulers dropped the title and used *malik*, king, which is either a
secularisation or a change in what a confederation had become.

The dam failed for the last time in the sixth century AD, long after this
record's span, and the Qur'an refers to it: the flood of the dam, sent against a
people who had turned away, and gardens that became bitter fruit and tamarisk.
By then Saba' had been part of Himyar for three centuries, and the failure was
the end of the irrigation rather than of the kingdom.
