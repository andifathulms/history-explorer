Almost everything known about Saba' was written by Sabaeans, in Sabaic, on
stone, about themselves. There are two early exceptions, and they are worth
setting out at length because they show what the kingdom looked like from the
other end of its road.

Sargon II of Assyria, listing his receipts in the annals of his reign, records
tribute from Pir'u king of Egypt, from Samsi queen of Arabia, and from It'amra
the Sabean: gold, herbs of the mountain, horses and camels. That is the company
Saba' is put in — Egypt and the strongest of the northern Arabian confederacies,
named in one breath, as powers that sent things to Assyria rather than as
places Assyria had been.

<Figure id="sargon" />

A generation or so later Sennacherib, laying the foundations of a temple for
the New Year's festival, describes what he buried in them. Into the footings
went silver and gold, carnelian and lapis lazuli, and among it all the treasure
that Karibi-ilu king of Saba' had ordered to be brought: stones, precious
stones, choice herbs. The gift of a South Arabian king is in the ground under
an Assyrian temple.

## What the two notices actually prove

Neither is a description of Saba'. Both are inventories, and Assyrian
inventories are written to make the Assyrian king look like the centre of the
world.

But they establish three things that the inscriptions at Marib cannot establish
about themselves. Saba' existed under that name, with a king, by the late
eighth century. Its goods reached northern Mesopotamia — not by conquest and
not by an embassy travelling once, but as a regular enough matter for a scribe
to file it under tribute alongside Egypt's. And what travelled was aromatics
and precious stone: the herbs of the mountain are incense, and incense from
Arabia is in an Assyrian foundation deposit two thousand kilometres from where
the trees grow.

The names help too. It'amra and Karibi-ilu are Assyrian spellings of Yatha'amar
and Karib'il, and both are Sabaean royal names of exactly the kind the
inscriptions carry. They are the earliest rulers of this kingdom who can be
dated by anything outside its own epigraphy, which in a chronology argued over
by centuries is not a small thing.

## Expansion measured in a road

This is the shape of Sabaean power and it is easy to mistake. The kingdom did
not grow the way a land empire grows, by pushing a frontier outward until it
meets another one. It grew along a line.

The incense went north from Hadramawt and Dhofar up the western edge of the
peninsula, and every stage of that route needed water, fodder, a place to
change animals, someone to guarantee the caravan's safety and someone to take a
percentage. What Saba' extended was its ability to guarantee and to charge:
outposts, allied and subject towns, colonies of Sabaean speakers far up the road
in the Hijaz and beyond, and the reputation that made a caravan safe because of
whose name it travelled under.

A state of that kind is not well described by area. It is better described by
the fact that a king of Assyria could file its produce under tribute, and that
another king of Assyria thought a Sabaean gift the right thing to put under a
temple.
