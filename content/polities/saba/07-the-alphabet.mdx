---
title: The alphabet that went to Africa
drafted_from: fisher-2015
phase: aside
---

South Arabian is written in an alphabet of its own — the *musnad*, a monumental
script of tall geometric letters, cut in stone with a precision that makes the
inscriptions legible after two and a half thousand years. It derives, like the
Greek and Aramaic and Hebrew alphabets, from the same North-West Semitic
ancestor, and it went its own way early.

Tens of thousands of these inscriptions survive, and they are effectively the
whole of the evidence for this polity. There is no Sabaean history, no chronicle,
no literature; there are dedications to Almaqah, records of building and repair,
campaign accounts, legal texts and boundary markers, and everything anyone knows
about the kingdom is assembled out of those and out of what outsiders wrote.

The script crossed the Red Sea. Ancient South Arabian letterforms are the
ancestor of the Ge'ez script of Ethiopia and Eritrea, which developed vowel
marking that South Arabian did not have and which is used to write Amharic and
Tigrinya today.

The Ethiopians did something with it that the Sabaeans never had. South Arabian
wrote consonants and left the vowels to the reader, as the Semitic alphabets
generally did; Ge'ez developed a system of marking the vowel on the consonant
itself, turning each letter into a set of forms. That is a real invention, and
it is why Ge'ez is a syllabary made out of an alphabet rather than a version of
one. It is used to write Amharic and Tigrinya today.

So the writing survived the language, the language survived the kingdom, and
both crossed a sea. Saba' stopped being a polity in about 275. The letterforms
its masons were cutting eleven centuries earlier are on shop fronts in Addis
Ababa and Asmara, which is a longer working life than anything else this
kingdom made — longer than the dam, longer than the caravan road, longer by far
than the state.
