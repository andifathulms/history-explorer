---
title: Sixty-five stages
drafted_from: pliny-bostock
phase: aside
---

Pliny wrote down what the incense road cost, item by item, and the list is the
best surviving description of how a South Arabian kingdom actually made money.

He begins with the geography and gets it roughly right. No country in the world
produces frankincense except Arabia, and not all of Arabia: near the centre of
the region are the Atramitae, a community of the Sabaeans, whose capital Sabota
stands on a high mountain, and eight stages beyond it is the incense-bearing
district, which the Greeks were told means a secret mystery. It looks north-east,
it is walled in by rock on every side and shut off from the sea by enormous
cliffs, the soil is a milky white inclining to red, and the forests run twenty
*schoeni* long and half that broad. Adjoining it are the Minaeans, through whose
country lies the sole transit for the frankincense, along a single narrow road.

That last clause is the whole economy. One commodity, one growing district, one
road.

What happens on the road is a sequence of people taking a cut, and Pliny lists
them in order. First the priests take a tenth, measured, before any of it may be
sold at all; out of that tenth the public expenses are defrayed, because the god
generously entertains every stranger who has travelled a certain number of days
to get there. Then the incense can only be exported through the country of the
Gebbanites, so a tax is paid to their king as well. Then portions go to the
priests and to the king's secretaries, and after them to the keepers of the
incense, the soldiers guarding it, the gate-keepers and various other employees.
Then the road itself: at one place water to be paid for, at another fodder, and
lodging at the stations, and taxes and imposts besides.

Thomna, the Gebbanite capital, is 4,436 miles from Gaza — a figure Pliny's
translators think must be corrupt — and the journey is divided into sixty-five
stages by camel.

The total, Pliny says, is six hundred and eighty-eight denarii per camel before
it reaches the Mediterranean at all. And then there are still payments to be made
to the farmers of the Roman revenue. Which is why, he concludes, a pound of the
best frankincense sells at six denarii, the second quality at five and the third
at three.

Two things follow from that accounting and they run in opposite directions.

The first is what the wealth of Arabia Felix actually was. It was not
agricultural surplus and it was not manufacture. It was a toll regime: a resin
that grew in one inaccessible valley, moving along one road, past a series of
authorities each of whom could stop it, and every one of whom charged. The
temple at the head of the road is part of the same machine as the gate-keeper at
the twentieth stage — the tithe taken by the god pays the state's bills, and the
hospitality offered to arriving strangers is what a tax looks like when it is
also a pilgrimage.

The second is what happens to such a state when the road stops being the only
way. Pliny was writing in the 70s AD, at the moment Greek and Roman shippers were
learning to use the monsoon and sail from Egypt to India and back inside a year.
A sixty-five stage camel road with sixty-five tolls on it cannot compete with a
hull. The kingdoms of the incense road did not fall to anybody. They were
bypassed, over about two centuries, by people who had found out how the wind
worked.
