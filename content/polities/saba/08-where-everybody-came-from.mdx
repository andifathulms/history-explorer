The dam at Marib broke for the last time in the sixth century, three hundred
years after the kingdom had stopped being one, and the country below it went
back to being desert. What happened next is not really an event in the history
of Saba'. It is the beginning of a story that the Arabs told about themselves
for the next thousand years, and it made the ruined oasis the starting point of
almost everybody.

The version the Arabic genealogists worked with runs like this. The people who
inhabited the country of Saba' and its capital Marib — the Azd, the most
important of them — abandoned it at the rupture of the dyke of 'Arim, all but a
small remnant. A portion went to Oman. The main body went down to the Tihama,
the hot coastal plain, and settled near a pool called Ghassan between the wadis
of Zabid and Rima', where the tribes already in possession eventually quarrelled
with them and pushed them out again. From there they scattered.

One section stopped at Najran. Another, led by Haritha son of 'Amr, attacked the
Jurhum at Mecca, beat them, and took the sanctuary; they were afterwards called
Khuza'a, the separated ones, because they had split from the branch that
followed Tha'laba son of 'Amr. Tha'laba's own descendants went north to Yathrib
and took it: his two grandsons gave their names to the Aws and the Khazraj.
And the group that had camped at the pool went north again, all the way to
Syria, where they became the kings of Ghassan and held the desert frontier for
Rome until the Muslim armies came.

## What the genealogy is doing

Follow that out and see where it lands.

The tribe that held Mecca and its sanctuary before the Quraysh came from Marib.
The two tribes of Medina who took Muhammad in when nobody else would, and whom
he named the Ansar, the Defenders — they came from Marib. The Christian Arab
kingdom that fought Persia's Arabs on Rome's behalf for two centuries came from
Marib. So did the Azd of Oman, at the far corner of the peninsula.

None of this is a chronicle. It is a family tree of nations, assembled by
scholars centuries later out of tribal tradition and poetry, and it has the
problems family trees of nations always have: the dates do not work — the taking
of Mecca from the Jurhum is placed by one reckoning in the second century AD,
four hundred years before the last breach — and the dispersal is made a single
migration when what it describes must have been a slow drift over many
lifetimes, if it happened at all.

But that is the wrong thing to ask of it. The genealogies were not written to
record a migration. They were written to answer a live political question in
the early Islamic centuries: who are the southerners, and by what right do they
stand where they stand? And the answer they give is that the south is not the
periphery of Arabia. It is the source. The Qahtani tribes descend from a
kingdom with cities and a script and a thousand years of inscriptions, and they
left it because a great work of engineering failed, and everything they
afterwards founded — in the Hijaz, in Syria, in Oman — was founded by people
carrying that memory.

The Qur'an gave the story its permanent form: the flood of the dam sent against
a people who turned away, gardens turned to bitter fruit and tamarisk. That
sealed it. A hydraulic failure at the edge of a desert became a judgement, and
the judgement became a pedigree.

## The oasis afterwards

Marib itself stopped mattering almost entirely.

It appears in the medieval Yemeni chronicles as a place things occasionally
happen near. In 1027 an unknown claimant turned up there, proclaimed himself
imam under a title meaning He who brings the people back to the religion of
God, made himself master of San'a, and was killed four years later. That is
about the scale of it. A man with no name anyone recorded, using a ruined oasis
as a place to start from, because it was empty and it was famous.

The name did better than the place. Marib was the city everybody had left, and
it went on being that in poetry and in argument long after there was nothing at
it worth taking — which is a peculiar kind of survival, and the only one this
kingdom got.
