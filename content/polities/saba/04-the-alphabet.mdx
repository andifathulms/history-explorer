---
title: The alphabet that went to Africa
drafted_from: fisher-2015
phase: aside
---

South Arabian is written in an alphabet of its own — the *musnad*, a monumental
script of tall geometric letters, cut in stone with a precision that makes the
inscriptions legible after two and a half thousand years. It derives, like the
Greek and Aramaic and Hebrew alphabets, from the same North-West Semitic
ancestor, and it went its own way early.

Tens of thousands of these inscriptions survive, and they are effectively the
whole of the evidence for this polity. There is no Sabaean history, no chronicle,
no literature; there are dedications to Almaqah, records of building and repair,
campaign accounts, legal texts and boundary markers, and everything on this page
is assembled out of them and out of what outsiders wrote.

The script crossed the Red Sea. Ancient South Arabian letterforms are the
ancestor of the Ge'ez script of Ethiopia and Eritrea, which developed vowel
marking that South Arabian did not have and which is used to write Amharic and
Tigrinya today.

That is why this record's `descendant_scripts` count reads 1, and it is the only
influence count on the page. This site's Aksum record says twice that the Ge'ez
script derives from South Arabian letterforms, and has said it since the page
was written, pointing at a polity that was not in the corpus. It is now, and the
count is on it.

The coding rules make this a legitimate entry rather than a courtesy. A
descendant script is counted where a script is derived from or newly adapted out
of the polity's own, and where a source attributes it; what is excluded is a
change of language without a change of script, and adaptations a source assigns
elsewhere. Ge'ez is a genuinely distinct script that developed from these
letterforms, the derivation is attributed, and the script is conventionally
named Sabaean after this kingdom.

It also makes the count one of the longest-running consequences anywhere in this
corpus. Saba' ended as a polity in about 275. The alphabet its scribes cut is
on Ethiopian shop signs.
