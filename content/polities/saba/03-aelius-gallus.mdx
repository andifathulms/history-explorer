In 26 BC Augustus sent an army to take Arabia Felix. Strabo, who knew the
commander personally, is the main source, and his account is the reason we have
any of it.

Aelius Gallus, prefect of Egypt, crossed the Red Sea with something over ten
thousand men and marched south down the peninsula. The march took six months.
The guide, a Nabataean, was later accused of leading them deliberately astray;
the army lost men steadily to thirst, disease and the country rather than to
fighting, took several towns, reached Marib, besieged it for some days, and
turned back. The return march took two months. Strabo says the campaign lost
seven men to the enemy and the rest to hunger and sickness, which is a rhetorical
figure and is also roughly the shape of what happened.

Rome did not try again by land. What it did instead is the reason this chapter
is filed under contraction rather than as a Sabaean victory.

Within a generation of Gallus, Greek and Roman shipping out of Egyptian Red Sea
ports was sailing directly to India on the monsoon, in numbers the *Periplus of
the Erythraean Sea* describes in the middle of the first century AD and Pliny
complains about the cost of. Goods that had gone up the caravan road, paying a
toll at every kingdom on it, could now go by sea past all of them.

The incense kingdoms did not collapse — the trees still only grew in one place,
and the sea route had its own middlemen — but the structural advantage that made
Saba' rich was a monopoly on a road, and the road stopped being the only way.
The following two centuries are of Sabaean decline relative to the highland
power that did not depend on the caravan: Himyar, which had the agriculture and
the coast, and which absorbed Saba' in the end.
