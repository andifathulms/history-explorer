Himyar appears in the record in the last century BC, in the highlands south-west
of Saba', with its capital at Zafar. It had what Saba' did not: terraced
agriculture in high country with reliable rainfall, and a position facing the
coast and the Red Sea rather than the caravan road.

The two kingdoms fought and made alliances for three centuries, along with
Hadramawt and what was left of Qataban, in a period the inscriptions document
densely and confusingly. The decisive change is in the titles. Sabaean and
Himyarite rulers each at various points styled themselves king of Saba', which
was the prestigious claim; by the late third century the ruler at Zafar was
styling himself king of Saba' and of dhu-Raydan — Raydan being the citadel at
Zafar — and then king of Saba', dhu-Raydan, Hadramawt and Yamanat, and the
titulature is the evidence that the unification had happened.

This site dates the end to about 275 and types it `gradual absorption`, which is
what the titles describe: not a conquest with a date but a competition for the
same crown that one side stopped losing.

What Saba' left is on the next record and under the ground. Himyar governed a
unified South Arabia for two and a half centuries, kept the Sabaean royal title
at the head of its own, and maintained the dam. The inscriptions continue in the
same script and the same language for public purposes long after Sabaic had
stopped being what most people spoke.

And the name did not go away. Saba' is Sheba, and the queen who visited Solomon
in the Book of Kings became, in the Ethiopian national epic, the ancestress of a
dynasty that claimed descent from her and Solomon and ruled until 1974 — a claim
made in the script this kingdom's alphabet produced, about a queen this kingdom
may or may not have had, by an empire on the other side of a sea.
