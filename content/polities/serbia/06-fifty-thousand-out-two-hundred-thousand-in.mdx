In 1433 a Burgundian sent east to work out whether a crusade could be got through
rode across Serbia, and his notes are a balance sheet. Bertrandon de la
Broquière was surveying a route, so he counted what a quartermaster counts: what
the ruler paid out, what he took in, where the forts were, and who would fight.

## The arithmetic

The despot was Đurađ Branković, and Broquière sets out his position in two
figures.

He pays the Turk an annual tribute of fifty thousand ducats. And on the river
towards the borders of Bulgaria, Slavonia, Albania and Bosnia, he holds a town
with a mine producing gold and silver at the same time, which yields him more
than two hundred thousand ducats a year — *as well informed people assured me*.

Then the conclusion, which is the whole history of the despotate in one clause:

> without this, he would be soon driven out of his dominions.

That is Novo Brdo, and it is the same silver the Serbian state had been built on
for two centuries, now doing a different job. The mines that had once paid for
Dušan's armies and his code were paying the Ottoman tribute that bought the
despot his remaining years.

## What the country looked like

Broquière rode up the Nišava and the Morava through what he calls a pleasant,
well-peopled country full of villages where all your wants may be supplied, and
past the castle of Kruševac at the confluence — once a strong place, now part of
a wall and a tower.

From there, he notes, it is a hundred miles to the Danube, and *in all this
distance, there does not subsist any fort, or place of defence*, except a village
and a house with a mosque built by a Turkish bey on a hillside.

At the river mouth the Turk kept eighty to a hundred galleys and rafts to ferry
his cavalry across in time of war, guarded by three hundred men relieved every
two months. Broquière could not see them, because no Christian was allowed near
them, but a man worthy of belief told him.

## Whose subjects

The other thing he recorded is the muster.

Everywhere from Macedonia through Bulgaria and Serbia, the Turk had proclamation
made that whoever owed service should hold himself ready to march. Those who fed
a horse were exempt from the tax; Christians who preferred not to serve paid
fifty aspers a head; and some were compelled to go when reinforcements were
needed.

Belgrade had belonged to the despot and he had ceded it to the king of Hungary
four years earlier, for fear of losing it to the Turk as he had lost Golubac —
which Broquière calls a heavy loss to Christendom, and thinks the loss of
Belgrade would be heavier still, the place being stronger and able to hold five
or six thousand horse.

Two days after he arrived there, twenty-five men came in as garrison. They told
him they were Germans, and explained why Germans had been sent for when Serbs and
Hungarians were next door: the Serbs were subjects and tributaries of the Turk
and could not be trusted, and the Hungarians were so frightened of him that if he
appeared they would not dare defend the place however strong it was.

## What the survey adds up to

A ruler with the richest mine in Europe paying a quarter of its output to an
empire whose musters were already being proclaimed in his own villages; a hundred
miles of open country between his second river and the Danube with nothing on it;
a fortress ceded to Hungary to keep it out of Turkish hands; and a garrison of
foreigners hired because nobody local could be relied on.

Broquière was writing to advise a crusade, and everything he set down argues that
one was needed and that it would be difficult. Twenty-six years after he rode
through, the despotate ended at Smederevo, and the mine went on producing for the
people who took it.
