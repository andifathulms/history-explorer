For five centuries after Smederevo there was no Serbian state, and the country's
history was maintained instead by men with one-stringed fiddles.

The *gusle* is a bowed instrument with a single string, and what was recited to
it was a body of unrhymed decasyllabic verse with a caesura in the middle of each
line, transmitted orally, without authors, revised continuously by whoever was
singing. A whole cycle of it centres on the battle of 15 June 1389 — 28 June by
the modern calendar, Vidovdan, the feast of Saint Vitus — on the field of
Kosovo, the field of the blackbirds.

Seton-Watson's summary of what it was for is exact: during five centuries of
Turkish rule the songs kept alive the memory of Serbia's lost glories, surrounded
the dim figures of her medieval heroes with a halo of romance, and encouraged the
faith of a downtrodden people in a resurrection that was certain to come.

## What the ballads say happened

The cycle opens with Murad's letter to Lazar demanding the keys of the fortresses
and seven years of taxes, on the ground that it never was known and never can be
that two should rule in one empire. Lazar reads it and weeps, and then curses
anyone who fails to come to the field:

> He who does not come to the Field of Blackbirds,
> Let nothing prosper in his hands,
> Neither the gold-white wheat in the field,
> Nor the vine on the mountain side,
> Nor the children playing at home.

Then he holds his nameday feast at Kruševac and raises the cup, and works through
who deserves it — by age, by rank, by affection, by beauty, by height — before
drinking it to Miloš Obilić for prowess and accusing him, in front of the table,
of intending to betray the army in the morning. Miloš stands up, thanks him for
the toast and not for the speech, swears he will kill the sultan on the field and
plant his foot on his throat, and names the man sitting at the prince's own knee
drinking wine from a silk-draped glass as the real traitor.

Both men keep their word. Miloš kills Murad. Vuk Branković is remembered as the
betrayer, on the strength of the poem, for six hundred years.

## The mother of the Jugović

The other pole of the cycle is not heroic at all.

The mother of the nine Jugović brothers prays for the eyes of a falcon and the
wings of a swan so that she can fly to Kosovo, and God grants it, and she finds
her nine sons and their father dead with nine spears above them and nine falcons
on the spears. She does not weep. She takes the horses and the falcons home. She
does not weep when the nine widows and the nine orphans come out to meet her, nor
when the horses whinny, nor at midnight when Damian's grey horse begins to groan
for a master it will never carry again.

In the morning two ravens come, bloody to the shoulders, with foam round their
beaks, and drop a hero's hand into her lap with a wedding ring on it. Damian's
wife identifies the ring. The mother turns the hand over and fondles it and
speaks to it:

> O my hand, my fresh green apple,
> Where didst thou grow, where wert thou plucked?
> 'Twas on my bosom thou didst grow.
> The plucking, 'twas on Kosovo's plain.

Then she dies.

That is the poem a people without a state recited to itself for five hundred
years, and it is worth noticing what it is not. It is not a call to arms and it
does not promise anything. It is a woman holding a piece of her son.

## What the ballads got wrong, and why it did not matter

Lazar was a prince and the songs make him a tsar. The Serbian nobility at Kosovo
was not a unified nation and the songs make it one. Vuk Branković's treachery is
not in any contemporary source and is probably an invention of the fifteenth or
sixteenth century, looking for someone to blame. The battle's outcome, which
nobody at the time could agree on, becomes in the cycle an unambiguous
catastrophe and simultaneously a moral victory — Lazar choosing the heavenly
kingdom over the earthly one, which is a theological idea and not a military
report.

None of that made the songs less useful. A people with no schools, no
aristocracy, no state and no written history had a portable account of who they
had been, in a metre that could be memorised, performed anywhere, by anybody, for
free. It is not history and it was never trying to be. It was a nation kept in
the only container available.

## Threepence, London, June 1916

Which brings the cycle to its strangest appearance.

These translations were made by R. W. Seton-Watson and published in London for
the Kossovo Day Committee, at threepence, dated the 5th of June 1916. Serbia at
that point had been overrun; its army had retreated across the Albanian mountains
in winter with catastrophic losses; and a British committee organised a public
commemoration of the five hundred and twenty-seventh anniversary of a defeat, to
raise sympathy and money for the country's survivors.

The choice of anniversary was not sentimental. Vidovdan was already the date on
which a Bosnian Serb had shot the Austrian archduke in Sarajevo two years
earlier, which is what had started the war the pamphlet was published in the
middle of.

Seton-Watson's preface notes that the last generation of Serbian bards produced a
whole new cycle about the war of liberation a century before — Karađorđe, Hajduk
Veljko, Miloš Obrenović — with the old fire and the same vivid word-painting.
That is the thing to end on. The form did not stop when the state came back. It
simply took the new leaders and put them in the metre, beside the old ones, on
the same one string.
