In May 1349, at a council at Skopje, Dušan promulgated a law code. He enlarged it
at a second council in 1354. Together the two parts run to something over two
hundred articles, and they survive in a couple of dozen manuscript copies.

It is a working code rather than a statement of principle. It legislates the
jurisdiction of church and secular courts; the status and obligations of the
dependent peasantry, the *sebri*, including how many days a week they owe; the
protection of church property; robbery and the collective liability of villages
for it; judicial procedure and the standing of witnesses; the Saxons and the
Ragusans, whose privileges it both confirms and limits; and heresy, which it
treats severely.

## What it does not say

Who inherits the throne.

Two hundred articles on the jurisdiction of courts, the days of labour owed by a
peasant, the liability of a village for a robbery committed on its road, the
privileges of German miners and Ragusan merchants, the punishment of heretics —
and not one word about the succession to the imperial crown its author had put on
his head three years earlier.

The silence is not an accident of what survived. The document is here, complete,
in two dozen copies.

## Why that is remarkable

States do sometimes write down who inherits, and there is a pattern in which ones
do. Telipinu legislated the Hittite succession after a century of murdered kings.
Esarhaddon made the Assyrian nobility swear to his arrangements after a civil war
caused by his father's assassination. The Golden Bull of 1356 — issued seven
years after this code and four hundred kilometres away, and about almost nothing
else — was Charles IV settling three centuries of disputed imperial elections.

A written succession rule is nearly always a scar. It gets written by people who
have watched the alternative.

Dušan had watched the alternative from closer than any of them. He deposed his
own father three years into that father's reign and had him strangled in the
fortress at Zvečan; his grandfather had been deposed by a son as well. The man in
Europe best placed to know exactly what an unregulated succession costs a dynasty
sat down and legislated the hire of oxen.

Nine years later he died at forty-seven, leaving a nineteen-year-old son and an
empire assembled out of somebody else's provinces in under a decade. The great
lords stopped obeying inside five years, and there was no rule anybody could
appeal to, because the one man who could have written one had chosen not to.

## What happened next

Dušan died in 1355 and was succeeded by his son Uroš, who was nineteen and
inherited an empire assembled in nine years out of provinces taken from a
neighbour in civil war.

It held together for about five years and then did not. The great lords of
Macedonia, Epirus and Thessaly — most of them Dušan's own appointees and several
of them his relatives — stopped obeying, and Uroš spent the rest of his reign
being nominally emperor of territory that other men governed. He is called Uroš
the Weak, which is the traditional verdict and is at least partly a judgement on
somebody else's arrangements.

The men who ended the arrangements had by then been in Europe for seventeen
years.
