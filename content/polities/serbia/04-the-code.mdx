In May 1349, at a council at Skopje, Dušan promulgated a law code. He enlarged it
at a second council in 1354. Together the two parts run to something over two
hundred articles, and they survive in a couple of dozen manuscript copies.

It is a working code rather than a statement of principle. It legislates the
jurisdiction of church and secular courts; the status and obligations of the
dependent peasantry, the *sebri*, including how many days a week they owe; the
protection of church property; robbery and the collective liability of villages
for it; judicial procedure and the standing of witnesses; the Saxons and the
Ragusans, whose privileges it both confirms and limits; and heresy, which it
treats severely.

## What it does not say

Who inherits the throne.

This record's `succession_rule` is null, and this is the seventh record in the
corpus where a written law code survives and the third where the code does not
answer this question.

The other two are worth naming, because the pattern has now crossed three
millennia, two religions and four writing systems. The Hasmonean record's decree
of 140 BC, engraved on bronze and set up in the Temple precinct, vested the
offices in Simon and his sons and named no order among them. The Ur III and Old
Babylonian records legislated theft, marriage, injury, the hire of oxen and the
liability of a builder whose house falls down, and never this.

Against them stand the records where a state did write it down: Telipinu's Edict
in Hatti about 1500 BC, Esarhaddon's succession oath of 672, the Venetian
procedure of lot and ballot, the Golden Bull of 1356 — issued seven years after
this code, four hundred kilometres away, and about nothing else.

The Urartian record draws the conclusion the others support: a state writes a
succession rule after being badly hurt by not having one. Telipinu legislated
after a century of murdered kings and Esarhaddon after a civil war caused by his
father's assassination.

Serbia in 1349 had been hurt by exactly that and did not legislate. Dušan had
deposed his own father three years into that father's reign and had him strangled
in a fortress; his grandfather had been deposed by his own son as well. The man
best placed in Europe to know what an unregulated succession costs wrote two
hundred articles about the days of labour owed by a peasant and said nothing
about the throne.

Coding rule 7 takes the rule in force and there was not one. The field is null
and this chapter is the reason, and the reason is not an accident of survival:
the document is here, complete, and the silence in it is the evidence.

## What happened next

Dušan died in 1355 and was succeeded by his son Uroš, who was nineteen and
inherited an empire assembled in nine years out of provinces taken from a
neighbour in civil war.

It held together for about five years and then did not. The great lords of
Macedonia, Epirus and Thessaly — most of them Dušan's own appointees and several
of them his relatives — stopped obeying, and Uroš spent the rest of his reign
being nominally emperor of territory that other men governed. He is called Uroš
the Weak, which is the traditional verdict and is at least partly a judgement on
somebody else's arrangements.

The next chapter is the year the arrangements ended, and the men who ended them
had been in the Balkans for seventeen years by then.
