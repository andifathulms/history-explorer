In the 1240s, after the Mongols had wrecked Hungary and moved on, Serbian rulers
invited German miners in.

They are called the *Sasi* — Saxons — in the Serbian sources, they came from the
mining districts of Hungary and Transylvania, and they arrived with their own
law, their own courts, their own technical vocabulary and their own terms. The
charters granting them privileges survive. Within a generation there were mines
at Brskovo, then Rudnik, Trepča, Janjevo, Srebrenica — the name means silver —
and finally Novo Brdo, which was the largest.

## What that did

Serbia became a mining state, which almost nothing else in medieval Europe
outside the Alps and Bohemia can be called without qualification.

Serbian silver was a substantial share of European production in the fourteenth
and fifteenth centuries. Novo Brdo alone is estimated to have produced several
tonnes a year at its height, some of it *glama* — silver naturally alloyed with
gold, which sold at a premium. The mines were royal, the crown took a share of
production, and Ragusan merchants ran the export.

Ćirković treats that as the engine of everything else. A mountainous
principality of no particular consequence acquired, within about fifty years, an
income that let it retain foreign cavalry for decades, mint its own coin in
quantity, endow monasteries at a rate that is still visible on the ground, and
finance an expansion into Byzantine territory at exactly the moment Byzantium
could not pay its own troops.

## The other side of the same fact

It also brought in people the state did not govern in the ordinary way.

The Saxons had their own law and were not subject to Serbian courts in mining
matters. The Ragusans who bought the silver had colonies with their own consuls
in the mining towns and along the roads to the coast. Dušan's Code has articles
about both, which is a state legislating around communities it has invited and
cannot simply command.

It is the ordinary price of an economy that runs on somebody else's expertise
and somebody else's ships, and it was being paid all over the eastern
Mediterranean in these decades — by the Byzantine emperor whose customs the
Genoese were collecting across the Golden Horn, and by every crusader port with
an Italian commune holding streets inside it.

## And what it could not do

Silver buys soldiers and does not make them.

Serbia's army was a feudal levy on land grants — the *baština* held outright and
the *pronija* held on condition of service, the second borrowed under its own
Greek name from the Byzantine administration Serbia was expanding into. Beside it
were the mercenaries the mines paid for, of whom the best documented is a
German captain called Palman who commanded Dušan's heavy cavalry for something
like twenty years.

It was enough to beat Bulgarians, Byzantines and Epirotes. Whether it would ever
have been enough against the Ottomans is a question that never got asked in the
form that would have answered it, because by the time the Ottomans arrived the
empire the silver had built had already come apart for reasons of its own.
