On 15 June 1389, on the field of Kosovo, Prince Lazar met Murad I. Both rulers
died. Murad was killed in his own camp, which is the only occasion on which a
reigning Ottoman sultan was killed by an enemy; the Serbian tradition names Miloš
Obilić and describes a feigned defection and a concealed knife. Lazar was
captured and executed on the field.

Both armies were wrecked. Bayezid, Murad's son, had his brother strangled in the
camp to secure the succession and withdrew.

## `contested: true`

This record marks the turning point contested, and the reason is not the usual
one.

Coding rule 4.4 says the flag marks a dispute about whether an event was a hinge
rather than about its date or details. Here the hinge is not in doubt. What is
disputed is the outcome. News of Kosovo reached western Europe as a Christian
victory and was celebrated as one — there was a Te Deum in Paris; the Florentine
senate wrote to the Bosnian king congratulating him. Contemporary Ottoman
accounts claim it. Modern historians mostly call it a draw at best, and some call
it an Ottoman victory that both sides took time to recognise.

The flag is being used slightly outside its written scope and this paragraph
exists so that a reader knows that rather than inferring a rule that is not there.
The First Bulgarian record uses it the same way for Kleidion, where the substance
of the famous detail is partly a later construction.

## What is not disputed

The result. Serbia had no army left and Lazar's son Stefan became an Ottoman
vassal within a year, married his sister to Bayezid, and fought for the Ottomans
at Nicopolis in 1396 against the crusade — and at Ankara in 1402 against Timur,
where this corpus's Timurid record has the Serbian heavy cavalry singled out by
the Ottoman chroniclers as the only contingent that did not break.

The Ottomans replaced their sultan inside a day and their losses inside a season.
That asymmetry is the whole of it: a state that can absorb the death of its ruler
and the loss of an army, against one that cannot.

## Seventy more years

The Despotate that followed is the part nobody remembers and it lasted longer
than Dušan's empire by a factor of seven.

Stefan Lazarević took the title *despot* from Constantinople in 1402, moved the
capital to Belgrade, and ran a functioning state with a legal code for the mines,
a mint, and a court that produced a good deal of literature. His successor Đurađ
Branković built the fortress at Smederevo in the 1430s — this record's third
capital — with money that was still coming out of Novo Brdo.

The silver held out almost to the end. The Ottomans took Novo Brdo in 1455 and
Smederevo in June 1459, six years after Constantinople, and this record's edge to
the Ottoman one is dated to that.

## The memory

Kosovo became the central event of Serbian national memory, and the form it took
— the cycle of epic poems, the choice of the heavenly kingdom over the earthly
one, Lazar as martyr — was assembled over the following centuries and given its
modern shape in the nineteenth.

That belongs in a chapter rather than in a field, and coding rule 1's exclusion of
modern claims is the reason this record's `successor_claims` is zero. Nation-states
invoking a medieval polity are out of scope, because the count measures political
succession within the period rather than heritage. It is the clearest case on this
site of a rule doing unglamorous but necessary work: a corpus that counted modern
national memory would end up ranking polities by how much they are argued about
now, which is a fact about the present.

What this record can say is the fourteenth-century part. A state built on silver,
a church it obtained from a government in exile, an imperial title taken from a
neighbour in civil war, a law code that legislated everything except the throne,
and a battle whose outcome its own contemporaries could not agree on.
