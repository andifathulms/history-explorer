Stefan Nemanja was one of several Serbian princes in the Byzantine orbit in the
1160s, and by his abdication in 1196 he had made his family the only one that
mattered. What he did afterwards is the more consequential half.

He became a monk. He went to Mount Athos, where his youngest son Sava was
already living as one, and the two of them founded Hilandar — a Serbian monastery
on the Holy Mountain, granted by an imperial charter, which still exists and has
been a Serbian institution for eight hundred and thirty years. Nemanja died there
in 1199 and was canonised as Saint Simeon.

## The argument the dynasty made

This record's `legitimation` field carries `divine-sanction` and it means
something narrower here than on most pages of this site.

The usual sense is that the right to rule comes from above: the mandate of
heaven, the king as the god's steward, an Assyrian king reporting to Ashur. What
the Nemanjići asserted is that the dynasty itself was holy. Nemanja was Saint
Simeon; Sava was Saint Sava, the first Serbian archbishop and the country's
patron; and most of the line after them was canonised too, with a cycle of royal
saints' lives written by their own descendants and a set of monastery churches —
Studenica, Žiča, Mileševa, Gračanica, Dečani — endowed by successive rulers, each
containing a fresco of the family tree with the founder at the root.

It is a genealogy that is also a hagiography, painted on the wall of a building
the subject paid for. Coding rule 1 excludes descent on its own and counts it
where it is publicly invoked as the ground of a right to rule; here the descent
and the sanctity are the same claim and the frescoes are the publication.

## 1219

Sava's other achievement is this record's first turning point and it came from an
unexpected direction.

In 1219 he travelled to Nicaea — where this corpus's Nicaean record's government
in exile was sitting, having lost Constantinople to the Fourth Crusade fifteen
years earlier — and obtained from the exiled patriarch an autocephalous Serbian
archbishopric, with himself as its head.

The Greek hierarchy at Ohrid, which had jurisdiction over Serbia and was not
consulted, protested at length. It made no difference. A patriarch with no city
and a claim to be the Roman empire had one thing left to dispose of, which was
ecclesiastical authority, and he disposed of it to a Serbian monk on the strength
of an argument about the church's needs.

Serbia therefore had a church answerable to nobody in the region a hundred and
twenty-seven years before it had an emperor, and the two together are the whole
of the third chapter.

## What kind of state

Small, mountainous, and between things. Serbia in 1200 was a principality in the
Morava and Ibar valleys with a Catholic Hungary to the north, a Byzantine world
to the south and east, and the Adriatic ports of a Venetian sphere to the west.
Nemanja's sons quarrelled over which way to face — one was crowned by a papal
legate in 1217 and is known as the First-Crowned, and his brother the monk got
the Orthodox archbishopric two years later — and the state ended up with both,
which is a fair description of where it sat.

What changed the scale of it was not a decision. It was silver.
