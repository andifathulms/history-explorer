On 15 June 1389, on the field of Kosovo, Prince Lazar met Murad I. Both rulers
died. Murad was killed in his own camp, which is the only occasion on which a
reigning Ottoman sultan was killed by an enemy; the Serbian tradition names
Miloš Obilić and describes a feigned defection and a concealed knife. Lazar was
captured and executed on the field.

Both armies were wrecked. Bayezid, Murad's son, had his brother strangled in
the camp to secure the succession and withdrew.

## Who won

Nobody could agree at the time, and nobody agrees now.

News of Kosovo reached western Europe as a Christian victory and was celebrated
as one. There was a Te Deum in Paris. The Florentine senate wrote to the
Bosnian king congratulating him. Contemporary Ottoman accounts claim it just as
firmly for the other side. Modern historians mostly call it a draw at best, and
some call it an Ottoman victory that both sides took time to recognise.

It is an unusual kind of dispute. The battle's importance has never been in
question; what is in question is the result — which is roughly the reverse of
the normal situation, where everyone knows who won and argues about whether it
mattered.

## What is not disputed

The result. Serbia had no army left and Lazar's son Stefan became an Ottoman
vassal within a year, married his sister to Bayezid, and fought for the
Ottomans at Nicopolis in 1396 against the crusade — and at Ankara in 1402
against Timur, where the Ottoman chroniclers single out the Serbian heavy
cavalry as the only contingent that did not break.

The Ottomans replaced their sultan inside a day and their losses inside a
season. That asymmetry is the whole of it: a state that can absorb the death of
its ruler and the loss of an army, against one that cannot.

## Seventy more years

Serbia after Kosovo was a vassal state, and then for two generations something a
good deal better than that.

Stefan Lazarević took the title *despot* from Constantinople in 1402, moved the
capital to Belgrade, and ran a working state with a legal code for the mines, a
mint, and a court that produced a good deal of literature. Đurađ Branković built
the fortress at Smederevo in the 1430s with money still coming out of Novo Brdo.
The Despotate is the part nobody remembers, and it outlasted Dušan's empire by a
factor of seven.

The silver held out almost to the end. The Ottomans took Novo Brdo in 1455 and
Smederevo in June 1459, six years after Constantinople. The state that had been
built on a mine ended four years after the mine was taken.
