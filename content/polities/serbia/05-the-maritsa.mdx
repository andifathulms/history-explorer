On the night of 26 September 1371 an Ottoman force surprised the camp of the
Serbian lords of Macedonia on the river Maritsa near Chernomen and destroyed
them. Vukašin Mrnjavčević, who had made himself king in the Serbian lands, and
his brother Uglješa, who governed Serres, were both killed. Many of the men who
drowned in the river gave the battle its Turkish name, the rout of the Serbs.

Uroš V died two months later without children, and the Nemanjić dynasty ended
two hundred and five years after Stefan Nemanja took the grand principality.

## Two endings, and only one of them Serbia's

It is worth being precise about what did and did not end in 1371, because a
reader arriving at a page called Serbia will reasonably expect the state to stop
when the dynasty does.

The empire of 1346 ended. Macedonia south of the Šar passed under Ottoman
suzerainty within a few years, Thessaly and Epirus went their own way, and the
imperial title lapsed because nobody was in a position to use it.

The state did not end. Serbian principalities north of the Šar continued under
their own rulers, and within about a decade Prince Lazar Hrebeljanović had
assembled most of them into something governable again, with a capital at
Kruševac and the church's excommunication finally lifted.

The distinction matters and it is easy to blur. Bulgaria next door genuinely did
stop: a hundred and sixty-seven years of direct Byzantine administration with no
Bulgarian ruler anywhere, and then a restoration that had to claim continuity
because there had visibly been a gap. Serbia had no gap. The Nemanjić line ended
and Serbian rulers went on governing Serbian territory without a break, and the
country's most impressive stretch of institutional life — a legal code for the
mines, a mint, a fortress at Smederevo, seventy more years — is on the far side
of 1371.

## The seventeen years

The other thing worth stating plainly is that the Ottomans were not a surprise.

They had crossed into Europe permanently in 1354, ferried across the straits by
one side in a Byzantine civil war that had hired them, and had taken Adrianople
by about 1369. The Maritsa is seventeen years after the crossing and
two years after the new capital.

What they arrived into is the point. Byzantium was fighting itself. Serbia had
come apart on the death of an emperor who had left no rule for who came next.
Bulgaria had divided itself between two of Ivan Alexander's sons. Nobody in the
Balkans was in a position to face a unified power, and not one of them had become
disunited because of the Ottomans.

Ćirković's account and Fine's agree on this and it is worth resisting the tidier
version. The Ottoman conquest of the Balkans was not a collision between a rising
power and a settled order. It was a rising power arriving in a place that had
just dismantled itself for reasons of its own.
