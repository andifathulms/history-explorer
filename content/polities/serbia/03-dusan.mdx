Stefan Dušan deposed his father in 1331 and had him strangled at Zvečan the
following year. He was twenty-three. He then ruled for twenty-four years and
roughly tripled the state.

## Velbazhd, first

The year before he took the throne, in 1330, a Serbian army destroyed a Bulgarian
one at Velbazhd and the tsar Michael Shishman was killed in the field. Dušan
commanded a wing of it.

The balance in the Balkans passed to Serbia for the next thirty years, and
Bulgaria spent them as a junior partner. It was the second time in a century that
one battle between these two states had settled which of them mattered, and the
first had gone the other way: at Klokotnitsa in 1230 a Bulgarian tsar destroyed
the army of Epirus and held everything from the Danube to the Aegean for a
decade.

## What he took

Almost all of it from Byzantium, and almost all of it while Byzantium was fighting
itself.

The Byzantine civil war of 1341 to 1347 pitted a regency in Constantinople
against John Kantakouzenos, and both sides went looking for outside help. Dušan supplied it to whichever side suited him and took payment in
territory: Albania, most of Macedonia, Epirus, Thessaly. By 1348 the Serbian
state ran from the Danube to the Gulf of Corinth and Dušan held more of the
Byzantine empire's European territory than the Byzantine emperor did.

Thessalonica held out and Constantinople was never seriously attempted, for the
reason that had stopped Krum and Simeon three and four centuries earlier and had
not changed since. Serbia had no fleet. An army that cannot cross water cannot
take a city on a strait, however much of the empire behind it that army holds.

## 1346

On Easter Sunday 1346 at Skopje, Dušan was crowned *basileus and autokrator of
the Serbs and the Greeks*.

Three weeks earlier he had raised the Serbian archbishopric — Sava's, from the
first chapter — to a patriarchate, because a patriarch was needed to perform the
coronation and Constantinople was not going to provide one. The Bulgarian
patriarch and the archbishop of Ohrid attended, which supplied the appearance of
canonical assent.

Constantinople excommunicated the Serbian church for it, and the excommunication
was not lifted for twenty-nine years.

## What the title was for

Four rulers in six centuries claimed the Roman imperial office from outside
Constantinople without holding the city: Simeon of Bulgaria in 913, the Latin
emperors in 1204 who at least had the ceremonial, Dušan in 1346, and Mehmed II in
1453, who had the city and therefore does not quite belong on the list. Dušan's
is the most elaborately staged of them — a coronation, a title on seals and
charters, and a patriarchate manufactured three weeks beforehand for the sole
purpose of performing it.

The excommunication is the best evidence of how it was read. Constantinople did
not treat this as a Slavic ruler translating a local dignity into Greek. It
treated it as a man taking the emperor's office, and responded with the heaviest
instrument it had left.

What Dušan meant by it is argued about. The maximal reading is that he intended
to replace the emperor in Constantinople and was assembling the means. The
minimal one is that *Serbs and Greeks* is a description of the population he
actually ruled, which by 1346 included more Greeks than Serbs. Ćirković is
cautious, and there is no settling it: the man died with an army on the road and
left no statement of what the army was for.

He died in December 1355, aged forty-seven, on his way somewhere with an army.
The empire lasted nine years after the coronation and did not survive him by
much.
