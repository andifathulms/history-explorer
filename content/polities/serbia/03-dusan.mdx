Stefan Dušan deposed his father in 1331 and had him strangled at Zvečan the
following year. He was twenty-three. He then ruled for twenty-four years and
roughly tripled the state.

## Velbazhd, first

The year before he took the throne, in 1330, a Serbian army destroyed a Bulgarian
one at Velbazhd and the tsar Michael Shishman was killed in the field. Dušan
commanded a wing of it.

This corpus's Second Bulgarian record carries the same day as its own fourth
turning point and says the balance in the Balkans passed to Serbia for the
following thirty years. That record was written before this one, and this is the
page it was pointing at. Two records, one battle, one describing what it lost and
the other what it did with the gain — which is the second such pairing between
these two states, the first being Klokotnitsa in 1230 running the other way.

## What he took

Almost all of it from Byzantium, and almost all of it while Byzantium was fighting
itself.

The Byzantine civil war of 1341 to 1347 — this corpus's Byzantine record's ninth
chapter — pitted a regency against John Kantakouzenos, and both sides looked for
outside help. Dušan supplied it to whichever side suited him and took payment in
territory: Albania, most of Macedonia, Epirus, Thessaly. By 1348 the Serbian
state ran from the Danube to the Gulf of Corinth and Dušan held more of the
Byzantine empire's European territory than the Byzantine emperor did.

Thessalonica held out, and Constantinople was never seriously attempted. This
record has no naval value in its `military_basis` field and, as on the Bulgarian
records three centuries earlier, that is the whole explanation: an army that
cannot cross water cannot take a city on a strait.

## 1346

On Easter Sunday 1346 at Skopje, Dušan was crowned *basileus and autokrator of
the Serbs and the Greeks*.

Three weeks earlier he had raised the Serbian archbishopric — Sava's, from the
first chapter — to a patriarchate, because a patriarch was needed to perform the
coronation and Constantinople was not going to provide one. The Bulgarian
patriarch and the archbishop of Ohrid attended, which supplied the appearance of
canonical assent.

Constantinople excommunicated the Serbian church for it, and the excommunication
was not lifted for twenty-nine years.

## What the site does with the title

This record's `legitimation` carries `titulature`, and the Byzantine record's
`successor_claims` count goes to four with Dušan in it, alongside Simeon of
Bulgaria in 913, the Latin emperors of 1204 and Mehmed II in 1453.

Coding rule 1 counts a claim when a later polity asserts an earlier polity's
legitimacy in its own public self-description, and counts unrecognised claims
exactly as it counts recognised ones. This is about as public as a claim gets —
a coronation, a title on seals and charters, a patriarchate created to perform
it — and the excommunication is the clearest possible evidence that
Constantinople read it as a claim rather than as a translation.

What Dušan meant by it is argued about. The maximal reading is that he intended
to replace the emperor in Constantinople and was assembling the means. The
minimal one is that *Serbs and Greeks* is a description of the population he
actually ruled, which by 1346 included more Greeks than Serbs. Ćirković is
cautious; this record codes the claim and does not adjudicate the ambition.

He died in December 1355, aged forty-seven, on his way somewhere with an army.
The empire lasted nine years after the coronation and did not survive him by
much.
