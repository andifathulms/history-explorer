The kings later inscriptions call the Snake dynasty, kaan or kaanul,
were already a power in the lowlands generations before any of them
raised a monument at the city archaeologists now call Calakmul. The
earliest known Snake rulers, men like Yuknoom Ch'een I and Sky
Witness, are attested chiefly at other sites - a carved stairway of
bound captives at Dzibanche names Yuknoom Ch'een I directly, and Sky
Witness's own conquests, including the very defeat that broke Tikal
in 562, survive only in inscriptions raised by other kings recording
what he had done to them. Not a single monument from any of these
early Snake kings stands at Calakmul itself.

## The dynasty's arrival at Calakmul

The pattern held through the reigns of Scroll Serpent, whose armies
reached as far as Palenque in 599 and 611, and his successors
Yuknoom Ti' Chan and Tajoom Uk'ab K'ahk'. Only with Stelae 28 and
29, raised in 623, do dated Snake-era monuments finally appear at
Calakmul itself - though even these do not preserve a legible royal
name. Whether this marks the dynasty's actual relocation to the city
or simply the point at which its kings began commemorating
themselves there, the effect was the same: Calakmul, not Dzibanche,
became the seat from which the Snake kings would build the network
of allied and subject kingdoms that made them Tikal's great rival
for the rest of the Classic period.
