Yuknoom the Great's son Yich'aak K'ahk' inherited a kingdom whose
authority still ran from El Perú and Dos Pilas to a five-year-old
client king installed at Naranjo, but in August 695 he led Calakmul's
forces against Tikal's Jasaw Chan K'awiil I and was decisively
beaten, losing the effigy of a Calakmul patron deity in the fighting.
The defeat did not destroy Calakmul's power outright, but the
diplomatic relationships that had sustained the golden age fell away
sharply afterward; only one or two statements of overlordship can be
identified in the reigns that followed.

## Decline into silence

The decline that set in after 695 continued for generations. A lone
monument was raised at Calakmul in 790, two more in 800, three in
810 - none naming its own patron - and, as at Tikal, the key
calendar-ending of 830 passed without commemoration at all. By 849
a foreign inscription at Seibal offered what may be the last outside
reference to the Snake kingdom, and archaeologists have questioned
whether a state worth the name still existed there in any real sense.

## The last stone

One final rally left its mark. Stela 61, its portrait almost entirely
gone, preserves the name of a king called Aj Took', "He of Flint" -
and, in place of a fuller text, a single ceremonial date most likely
placing it at the calendar-ending of 909. A few even later stones
survive, but by then Maya writing at the city had degraded into
sequences that only resembled dates, or, in the case of one
particularly crude stela, seemed almost to parody the great monuments
still standing around it from centuries before.
