Harsha met the Chinese pilgrim Hiuen Tsang while in camp in Bengal, and was
so taken with the Master's account of Buddhist teaching that he resolved to
give it the widest publicity a reigning north Indian paramount could
provide: a special assembly at Kanauj, by then his capital, with the
pilgrim himself as its centrepiece.

## Ninety days to arrive

Harsha marched along the southern bank of the Ganges at the head of an
enormous train, while his vassal Kumara, king of Kamarupa, kept pace on the
opposite bank with a smaller following of his own. The two processions took
ninety days to reach Kanauj, arriving in the early spring of 644. Waiting
to receive them were the king of Valabhi, connected to Harsha by marriage,
eighteen further tributary rajas, four thousand learned Buddhist monks —a
thousand of them from Nalanda alone — and some three thousand Jains and
orthodox Brahmans besides.

## A king dressed as a god

A monastery and shrine had been built specially on the riverbank, its
central tower a hundred feet high, housing a golden image of the Buddha
cast to the king's own height. Each day a smaller image was carried in
procession, escorted by all twenty vassal rajas and three hundred
elephants, its canopy borne by Harsha himself, dressed as the god Indra,
while Kumara walked beside him costumed as Brahma. The king scattered
pearls and golden flowers as he went, washed the great image with his own
hands, and carried it on his shoulder to its tower, where he heaped it with
thousands of jewelled silken robes. Each afternoon closed with a public
disputation; each evening, the king withdrew to his travelling palace a
mile distant.

## What the ceremony nearly cost him

The celebration, which ran for many days, ended in violence nobody had
planned for. The great temporary monastery caught fire and came close to
total destruction before Harsha's own intervention — read by the pious
crowd as a miracle — brought the flames under control. Then, descending the
steps of the stupa he had climbed to survey the scene, Harsha was rushed by
a man with a dagger. The would-be assassin was seized on the spot, and
under the king's own interrogation confessed that "heretics" — rivals of
the Buddhist establishment Harsha had so conspicuously favoured — had put
him up to it.

Five hundred prominent Brahmans were arrested on the strength of that
confession and, "straitly questioned" in turn, admitted to having set the
fire themselves with burning arrows, hoping to kill the king in the
resulting panic. That confession too was almost certainly extracted under
torture and almost certainly false in substance — but true or not, it was
accepted at face value. The alleged ringleaders were executed and five
hundred Brahmans sent into exile, on evidence that would not survive
serious examination by any standard except the one that mattered at the
time: the king's own.
