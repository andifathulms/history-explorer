---
title: A borrowed army
drafted_from: smith-1904

Wang Xuance, a Chinese envoy with no troops of his own, no authority beyond
his imperial commission, and a usurper's army between him and home, did not
go home. He went to the court of Srong-tsan Gampo, the Tibetan king married
to a Chinese princess, and asked for help.

## Two kingdoms, borrowed for a war of revenge

Srong-tsan Gampo gave it: a thousand Tibetan horsemen, joined by a Nepalese
contingent of seven thousand more, placed under the command of a Chinese
diplomat whose actual mission had been to deliver imperial courtesies, not
to fight a war. With this small, improvised, entirely borrowed army, Wang
Xuance descended into the Gangetic plain and besieged the chief city of
Tirhut. It fell after three days. Three thousand of the garrison were
beheaded; ten thousand more drowned in the neighbouring river attempting to
escape.

## Arjuna's defeat, twice over

Arjuna fled, gathered a fresh force, and offered battle again. He was
beaten a second time and taken prisoner. Wang Xuance beheaded a thousand
more captives on the spot, and in a further action captured Arjuna's entire
royal family, twelve thousand additional prisoners, and thirty thousand
head of cattle. Five hundred and eighty walled towns submitted without a
further fight. Kumara, the king of eastern India who had walked beside
Harsha at Kanauj four years earlier, sent supplies of cattle, horses and
equipment to support the victorious army — an old ally of the dead king
backing the foreign envoy who had, in effect, taken up the dead king's own
unfinished business.

## What became of the usurper, and of the episode

Wang Xuance brought Arjuna to China as a prisoner and was promoted for his
service. He returned to India once more in 657, this time on an entirely
peaceful mission to make offerings at the Buddhist holy places, entering
by the same Nepalese route that had once been his only way out alive.

The whole affair — a Chinese ambassador defeated and humiliated, raising a
foreign army on his own initiative, invading a country he held no
commission to invade, and returning its usurping king to China in chains —
went essentially unnoticed by the historians of India for centuries
afterward, surviving instead in Chinese sources that had no reason to
connect it to the broader question of what followed Harsha's reign. No
paramount power replaced his in the north. The region that Harsha had held
under one umbrella for four decades broke, after Wang Xuance's brief and
strange intervention, into the regional kingdoms it would remain divided
among for centuries — the closing, and by some distance the strangest,
chapter in the history of the dynasty a fifteen-year-old prince had once
hesitated even to call his own.
