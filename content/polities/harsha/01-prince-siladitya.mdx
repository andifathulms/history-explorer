Thanesar had been holy ground since legend's own memory, the "Land of Kuru"
and battlefield of heroes long before it became, in the late sixth century,
the seat of a raja named Prabhakaravardhana — a man who raised himself to
real regional power through successful wars against the Hun settlements of
the north-western Punjab and the Gurjara country, helped along by a
politically useful advantage: his mother was a princess of Gupta lineage,
giving his ambitions a pedigree his own conquests alone would not have
supplied.

## Two sons, one campaign each

In 604 Prabhakaravardhana sent his elder son Rajyavardhana, a young man
just entering manhood, north with a large army against the Huns; his
younger son Harsha, four years junior, followed at a distance with a
cavalry force. While Rajyavardhana pressed the campaign into the hills,
Harsha — fifteen years old — lingered in the foothill forests enjoying the
hunting, until a courier reached him with news that changed everything: his
father lay dying of a violent fever.

## A death, a crown, and a hesitation

Harsha rode back to the capital at speed and found the king past saving.
Prabhakaravardhana died before Rajyavardhana, victorious in the field,
could return to claim what was his by right. Some at court, in the
interval, appear to have favoured the younger son's succession instead —
but the intrigue came to nothing once Rajyavardhana arrived and took the
throne without contest.

He did not hold it long. Within days a courier brought word that Grahavarman,
king of Kanauj and husband of the princes' sister Rajyasri, had been killed
by the king of Malwa, who had the princess "confined like a brigand's wife,
with a pair of iron fetters kissing her feet." Rajyavardhana marched at once
with ten thousand cavalry, left the elephants and heavy troops with his
brother, defeated the Malwan king with little difficulty — and was then
treacherously murdered under cover of a peace conference by Shashanka, king
of Bengal and an ally of the man he had just beaten.

## A throne nobody was sure belonged to him

The murdered king left no son old enough to rule, and the nobles of
Thanesar hesitated visibly before offering the crown to his younger
brother — a hesitation the sources never fully explain, though it is
consistent enough with an accession that had no automatic claim behind it.
On the advice of Bhandi, a cousin raised alongside the two princes, the
council finally settled on inviting Harsha to take up the burden of rule.
Harsha, for reasons the record leaves unstated, hesitated in turn — by one
account consulting a Buddhist oracle before accepting — and even then
declined the royal title itself, styling himself merely Prince Siladitya.
He did not formally assume the kingship until 612, five and a half years
into what history nonetheless dates, from his actual accession, to October
606.
