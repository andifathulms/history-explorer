After the Kanauj assembly closed, Harsha invited his Chinese guest to one
further ceremony before letting him begin the long journey home: a
quinquennial assembly at Prayaga, the sacred confluence of the Ganges and
Jumna at modern Allahabad, where — he explained — it had been his own
practice for thirty years, following the custom of his ancestors, to give
away everything he had accumulated to the poor and to the religious of
every denomination.

## Seventy-five days of giving

The 644 assembly, the sixth of the series, drew all of Harsha's vassal
kings and a crowd estimated at half a million — the poor, orphans, the
destitute, and Brahmans and ascetics of every sect invited from across
northern India. The proceedings ran seventy-five days. On the first day an
image of the Buddha was set up on the sands and showered with costly
clothing and valuables; the Sun and Shiva received similar honour on the
second and third days, each at half the value given to the Buddha. Ten
thousand selected Buddhist monks received gold coins, pearls and cotton
garments on the fourth day. The next twenty days belonged to the Brahmans
in their thousands; ten more to Jains and other sects the Chinese
pilgrim's own account calls "heretics"; a further ten to mendicants from
distant regions; a full month to the poor, the orphaned and the destitute.

## What was left when it was over

By the end, in the Chinese record's own words, "the accumulation of five
years was exhausted. Except the horses, elephants, and military
accoutrements, which were necessary for maintaining order and protecting
the royal estate, nothing remained." Harsha gave away his gems, his
clothing, his necklaces, his earrings, his bracelets, his chaplets, his own
head-jewel — everything, without stint. When it was all gone, he asked his
sister Rajyasri for an ordinary second-hand garment, put it on, and paid
worship to "the Buddhas of the ten regions," rejoicing that his treasure had
been given in the field of religious merit.

## An emperor who owned nothing but his army

That is not a ceremonial flourish added to a chronicle for effect — it is
the plain, repeated practice of a paramount ruler who had done this five
times before and would, had he lived, likely have done it again. Harsha
kept enough of a standing military establishment to hold what he had
conquered and nothing else on a permanent basis; wealth accumulated
between assemblies existed, on his own explanation, in order to be given
away at the next one, in public, in a fixed and elaborate order of
precedence, until a man who had put a hundred thousand cavalry in the field
stood on the sands at Prayaga in a garment he had borrowed from his own
widowed sister.
