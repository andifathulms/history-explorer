Two tasks faced the new king immediately: avenge his brother's murder, and
find his sister. He judged the second more urgent, and pursued it even at
the cost of letting Shashanka's assassin escape.

## A princess found in the forest

Rajyasri, despairing of rescue, was on the point of burning herself alive
with her attendants when Harsha, guided by local tribal chiefs, traced her
into the depths of the Vindhyan jungles in time. The campaign against
Shashanka himself went nowhere near as decisively — no details of it
survive, and Shashanka appears to have escaped with little loss, still
ruling Bengal as late as 619. Rajyasri, recovered and safe, turned out to
be a young woman of real accomplishment, learned in Buddhist doctrine — and
her rescue, rather than the unfinished reckoning with her husband's
murderer, is what Harsha's reign actually pivots on next.

## A deliberate scheme, not a reaction

With his sister recovered, Harsha turned his considerable energy toward a
methodical, sustained campaign aimed at bringing all of northern India, in
the phrase his own era used, "under one umbrella." He began the effort with
a force of five thousand elephants, twenty thousand cavalry and fifty
thousand infantry — already discarding the war-chariots ancient tradition
still counted as a fourth arm of a properly organised Indian army, in favour
of speed.

Hiuen Tsang, the Chinese pilgrim who would later travel at Harsha's own
court, described the resulting campaigns in language that has outlived the
battles themselves: "he went from east to west subduing all who were not
obedient; the elephants were not unharnessed, nor the soldiers unhelmeted."
Within five and a half years the north-west and much of Bengal were his,
and his army had grown to match the conquest — sixty thousand war elephants,
a hundred thousand cavalry. He kept fighting for thirty years beyond that,
his last recorded campaign an assault on the coastal region of Ganjam as
late as 643.

## The one campaign that did not go his way

Only one adversary stopped him outright. Pulikesin II, the greatest of the
Chalukya kings, had built a comparable paramountcy over the Deccan, and
Harsha could not tolerate so powerful a rival existing unchallenged.
Marching south in person with, in the Chinese pilgrim's phrase, "troops
from the five Indies and the best generals from all countries," he found
the Chalukya king guarding the passes of the Narmada too effectively to be
dislodged. Harsha was forced to retire and accept the river as his
southern frontier — the single check on thirty years of otherwise
unbroken success, and the boundary his empire never crossed again.
