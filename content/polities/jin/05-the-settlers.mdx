The Jin held north China with a settlement scheme, and the scheme was called the
*meng'an-mouke*.

Jurchen households were organised in units of a hundred and of a thousand and
moved south in enormous numbers after the conquest — hundreds of thousands of
people — and settled on confiscated land across north China. They held it in
exchange for military service, kept their own law and their own officers, and
lived among a Chinese population that outnumbered them perhaps forty to one.

## What happened to them

What happens to every land-for-service arrangement anywhere. Within three
generations the settlers were landlords who rented out their allotments and did
not soldier; the state issued edicts about it; the edicts did not work; and when
the Mongols came the Jin fought them with hired troops.

That decay is so regular it is almost a law — Roman limitanei, Byzantine
pronoia, Ottoman timars, Ming garrison colonies, all of them the same three
generations. What makes this one specific is the population transfer.
The Ming *weisuo* garrisons were Chinese soldiers on Chinese land. The Jin moved
a people.

The other half of that is the harder problem: a dynasty
deliberately trying to preserve a Jurchen identity — the language, the script,
the hunting, the names — in a population that was assimilating anyway, and
legislating about it with increasing urgency for a century.

The two things are one policy seen from two ends. Settling the Jurchen among the
Chinese was meant to hold north China with a garrison that fed itself; what it
actually did was dissolve the garrison into the country it was garrisoning.

## And then 1214

With Mongol armies in the north the court left Zhongdu — the city that is now
Beijing — for Kaifeng, the capital it had taken from the Song eighty-seven years
earlier.

The move gave up the Jurchen homeland and the settled Jurchen population of the
north in order to hold the Yellow River plain. It held neither. Twenty years
later the last emperor died at Caizhou with a Mongol and a Song army outside the
walls together.
