The Jin was destroyed by the Mongols in twenty-three years, and it was destroyed
in exactly the way it had destroyed the Liao — including the part where the
Chinese dynasty in the south allied with the northern newcomer against the
northern incumbent, and got the same result.

## The wars

Chinggis Khan attacked in 1211. The Jin field armies were beaten in the passes;
the Mongols got through the Great Wall line, and in 1214 the court bought peace
with a princess, gold, silk and horses, and then immediately moved the capital
from Zhongdu — the future Beijing — south to Kaifeng.

Moving the capital was read, correctly, as an admission that the north could not
be held. Zhongdu fell in 1215. Jurchen and Khitan units defected. The territory
north of the Yellow River was progressively lost over the next fifteen years,
and the state that had ruled forty million people was reduced to the country
between the Yellow River and the Huai.

## What the Jin did with its last decade

It attacked the Song.

That is the decision that finishes the argument about whether the dynasty had
become a Chinese one. Facing annihilation from the steppe, the Jin court spent
the 1220s campaigning south against the Southern Song, on the reasoning that
territory lost in the north had to be replaced from somewhere.

It failed, it exhausted the army, and it guaranteed that when the Mongols asked
the Song for a joint attack, the answer would be yes.

## The last emperor

The Mongols took Kaifeng in 1233 after a siege in which, the accounts say,
plague killed a very large part of the population. The emperor Aizong had
already left for Caizhou, a small city further south, with what was left of the
court and the army.

Caizhou was invested by a Mongol army and a Song army together in the winter of
1233. It held for three months. In February 1234, with the walls being taken,
Aizong abdicated in favour of a general — reportedly on the grounds that he was
too heavy to ride and escape, and that the man he was handing it to was a better
soldier and might get away — and hanged himself.

The general was killed in the fighting the same day. He had held the throne for
a few hours and is counted in the dynastic lists.

## What the Song got for it

The Song had supplied grain and troops for the siege and was promised territory
in return.

In 1234, with the Jin gone, a Song army marched north to occupy Kaifeng and
Luoyang and found them empty, ruined and unable to feed an army. The Mongols
counter-attacked, drove them out, and treated the advance as a breach of
agreement.

The Mongol war on the Southern Song began the following year and took another
forty-five.

That is the second time in a hundred and twenty years that a Chinese dynasty in
the south allied with a rising northern power against a declining one, got a
fraction of what it was promised, and found itself facing something far worse
than what it had helped destroy. The Song did it twice, to the same result, in
living memory of the first.
