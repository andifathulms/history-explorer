In the autumn of 1220 a vice-president of the Jin Board of Rites named Wuguxun
Zhongduan set out from a court that had already lost its capital, to find an
emperor who was in the middle of destroying an empire two thousand miles further
west than anyone in Kaifeng had reason to expect him to be.

## The mission

Chinggis Khan had broken with the Jin in 1211 and taken Zhongdu in 1215. His
general Muqali was still fighting through north China, but the khan himself had
turned west against Khwarazm. The Jin court, in its distress, sent Wuguxun to sue
for peace — travelling, nominally, to "the northern court," though by the time he
reached it the northern court was in Central Asia.

He set out in the twelfth month of 1220, crossed the northern edge of the Tangut
kingdom, and kept going. Bretschneider's translation preserves his own account,
given afterward to a colleague who wrote it down at his request: *I have been sent
a distance of ten thousand li west of the border of heaven, and not wishing all the
curious things I saw in my travels to remain unrecorded, I therefore request you to
write down my narrative.*

## What he saw on the way

He rode along a mountain range where the rivers split — east of it running east,
west of it running west, the ground falling away beyond. He passed more than a
hundred cities, and none of them had a Chinese name.

He reached the Uyghur country and a city he calls Ili, and gives, unprompted, the
history of the Qara Khitai as it was told to him there: how a prince of the fallen
Liao house named Yelü Dashi had fled west with a following, wandered from pasture
to pasture, crossed a mountain range his party could barely get their carts over,
and founded an empire in the land of the Muslims that lasted some ninety years
before falling into decline and finally being destroyed by them in turn — the very
event, as it happened, that Chinggis Khan was in the process of finishing off
further south.

He describes the Muslim world he was crossing with the eye of a Chinese official
who has never seen anything like it: the grass drying up in early summer as if it
were Chinese winter; snow on the mountains in the hottest months; men whose beards
are dyed orange with henna and go black over it, so that only the nose and eyes show
through the hair; women who cover their faces and do nothing but sing and dance,
while the men do the sewing and embroidery; houses roofed in clay instead of tile;
windows and vessels of white glass; watermelons of sixty pounds' weight; bridges
built of great bricks; camels with one hump instead of two; oxen with a hump on the
neck; sheep with enormous tails; a four-legged snake; a spider whose bite makes a
man cry out and die.

None of it is quite right and all of it is exactly what a startled envoy from
Kaifeng would notice.

## The audience

He found Chinggis Khan somewhere in the mountains of what is now Afghanistan, in
the middle of a war, and delivered the letter he had carried across a continent — an
offer to become the khan's younger brother, his vassal, on condition of keeping the
title of emperor.

The *Yuan Shi* records what the khan said to him, and Bretschneider translates it
in full because Wuguxun's own account, oddly, says nothing about it:

> I formerly asked of your sovereign to cede the land north of the Yellow River,
> and to reign over the country south of it with the title of wang. On these
> conditions I would have suspended the war. But now Muqali has already conquered
> all these countries, and you are compelled to sue for peace.

Wuguxun asked for pity. The reply is the whole relationship in three sentences:

> It is only in consideration of the great distance you have come that I can be
> indulgent. The land north of the Yellow River is in my possession, but there are
> still some cities in Kuan-xi which have not surrendered. Tell your sovereign to
> surrender these cities, and then he may reign south of the Yellow River, with the
> title of wang.

Not emperor. King. The offer that had been on the table when the war began was
gone; what remained was smaller, because Muqali's campaign had made it smaller
while the envoy was still travelling to ask for the old terms.

Wuguxun turned round and rode home.

## Zhang Qian's road, run in reverse

The colleague who wrote his account down could not help the comparison, and the
Han envoy he reaches for is the same one whose own road west opened this whole
stretch of history, thirteen centuries earlier.

*Anciently, in the time of the Han dynasty, Zhang Qian and Su Wu were entrusted
with missions to far countries. They returned after several years, having endured
much suffering and risked their lives. Like these men you penetrated to unknown
countries, travelled amongst enemies more than ten thousand li, crossed deserts,
and accomplished all this for the sake of saving the people.*

Zhang Qian went west in the second century BC to find allies against the Xiongnu,
and opened the road that carried silk to Parthia. Wuguxun went west fourteen
hundred years later, on the same roads in outline, to beg the people who now
controlled them for terms. One envoy had gone out to build a frontier. The other
went because the frontier no longer existed.

He got his terms — such as they were — and delivered them, and the Jin court had
another thirteen years.
