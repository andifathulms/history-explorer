Between 1114 and 1127 the Jurchen destroyed two empires. The first took eleven
years, the second took two, and the second was easier.

## The Liao

Aguda's revolt began with a few thousand men against a state that had held the
northern frontier for two centuries, and it went faster than anyone including
Aguda seems to have expected.

The reason is that the Liao was a composite. It governed a steppe population and
a settled Chinese one through two separate administrations, held a conquered
Bohai kingdom in the east, and drew tribute from Jurchen, Tanguts and Koreans on
its edges. Every one of those was a joint, and once the centre was seen to be
losing, they came apart in order: Bohai first, then the tribal contingents, then
the Chinese prefectures, each defecting or standing aside rather than dying for
a dynasty that was going to lose.

The Liao emperor spent the last years of it in flight. The capital fell in 1120,
the Southern Capital in 1122, and Tianzuo was captured in 1125.

## The alliance that killed the Song

The Song had wanted the sixteen prefectures back for a hundred and eighty years,
and in about 1120 it made an arrangement with the Jurchen to take them: the Jin
would attack the Liao from the north, the Song from the south, and the
prefectures would go to the Song.

The Song armies performed badly enough that the Jurchen had to take the Southern
Capital themselves, and then handed over a few of the prefectures, stripped, in
return for the tribute payments the Song had been making to the Liao.

That is the whole of it, and everything after follows. A dynasty that had spent
a century buying peace from a northern neighbour it could not beat had just
demonstrated, to a new and far more capable northern neighbour, that its armies
could not fight.

## 1127

The Jin invaded in 1125, the year they finished the Liao, reached Kaifeng, were
bought off with an enormous indemnity, and came back the following year when
the payments stopped. The second time they took the city, and what they did with
its court is the subject of the next chapter.

## What they had acquired

The whole north China plain: the old imperial heartland, both Song capitals, and
a population of perhaps forty million people, held by a ruling group of at most
a few million.

A surviving Song prince set up a court in the south, and after fifteen years of
fighting the two states settled a border along the Huai river in 1142, with the
Southern Song as a tributary paying annual silver and silk.

The Jurchen had gone from a forest tribute-people to the ruler of northern China
in one generation. What that did to them is the subject of the next chapters,
and it happened faster than the conquest had.
