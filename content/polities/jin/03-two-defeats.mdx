In 1127 the Jin took Kaifeng and carried off two Song emperors, the imperial
family, the court, and the palace collections into captivity in the north. It is
called the Jingkang incident, and it is the most consequential single event in
Chinese history between the An Lushan rebellion and the Mongol conquest.

A surviving prince re-established the dynasty south of the Yangtze. The hundred
and fifty years that follow are conditioned by a court that wanted the north back
and a faction at that court that did not, and by the execution of the general who
most wanted it — which Chinese popular memory has never let go of.

## 1141

The treaty of Shaoxing fixed the border at the Huai river, made the Song emperor
formally the subject of the Jin emperor, and set annual payments in silver and
silk.

This corpus's Liao record's second chapter is about the same arrangement with the
parties changed: the treaty of Chanyuan in 1005, under which the Song paid the
Liao and both courts agreed to call it something other than tribute.

The Jin had watched that arrangement from the outside as a tributary of the Liao.
Within twenty-seven years of destroying them they were on the collecting end of
the institution they had inherited, on worse terms for the payer, having improved
it.

`revenue_basis` on this record reads `land-tax` and `poll-tax` and not `tribute`,
which is a judgement worth stating: the Song payments were large and they were
not what this state ran on. The Liao record is coded the other way round for the
same reason in reverse — its Chinese tax base was substantial and its
`revenue_basis` said `tribute` alone until this pass, and it now says both.

Two neighbouring records, one relationship, and the coding of it was wrong on
both of them in opposite directions.
