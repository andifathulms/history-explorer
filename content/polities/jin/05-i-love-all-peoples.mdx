Wanyan Yong took the throne in 1161 the way most of his predecessors had -
in the chaos left by another man's crimes - and spent the next twenty-eight
years proving that was not the only way to hold it.

## An inherited throne, not an undisputed one

His predecessor, the tyrant Hailingwang, was murdered by his own guards
mid-campaign against the Southern Song, and Wanyan Yong, proclaimed emperor
in the disorder that followed, still had to fight for the succession he had
been handed. A Khitan general named Yelu, entrusted with keeping his own
people in order, used his position and his standing among the Khitan tribes
to proclaim himself emperor of a restored Liao instead. It took a
considerable army to run him down; his capture and execution were what
finally let the new Jin emperor govern without looking over his shoulder at
his own frontier troops.

## A peace he had to defend from his own side

Wanyan Yong's own instinct, once the throne was secure, ran toward peace
with the Song rather than renewed war - a preference his own generals did
not share. A faction of Jin commanders, unwilling to accept the inactivity
peace required, deliberately massed frontier garrisons and spread rumors of
a coming invasion to goad the Song into striking first, calculating that
once fighting began their own emperor would have no choice but to back them.
It worked, in the narrow sense: a fresh war broke out the same year peace had
seemed settled. It did not work in the sense the hawks wanted - Jin forces
were driven from the field, and the war ended in a peace on terms more
favorable to the Song than any earlier settlement had been. Wanyan Yong got
the outcome he had wanted in the first place, just by a route he had not
chosen.

## Two tests of the same principle

Two episodes from later in the reign show what that preference for peace
actually meant in practice, rather than as a slogan. In 1170 a Xia minister
tried to force his own king into a private arrangement dividing the kingdom
- and needed Jin's consent as overlord to make it stick. Wanyan Yong saw
through the scheme immediately, refused to ratify an agreement he judged
had been extracted rather than freely made, and wrote to the Xia king
directly making the case plainly enough that the king had his own scheming
minister arrested and executed.

A Korean rebel offered a cleaner temptation: having seized his own kingdom's
western provinces by force, he proposed becoming Jin's vassal in exchange
for recognition. Wanyan Yong turned him down in terms his own court
recorded as remarkably direct: "You and your master deceive yourselves if
you believe me capable of approving an act of treason, whatever the
personal advantage it might procure me. I love all peoples of whatever
nation they may be, and I wish to see them at peace with one another. How
have you imagined me capable of so mean an act as that you propose to me?"
Both refusals cost him an easy opportunity for gain. Both were consistent
with the same standard applied in his own reign's first year, against his
own generals.

## What the reign left behind

His later years turned toward a sustained programme of Jurchen cultural
preservation - the language taught and examined, the Chinese classics
translated into it,
Jurchen dress and archery mandated for an aristocracy already drifting
toward Chinese habits it could not be legislated out of. He died in 1189,
and Jin's own historians ranked him second only to Akouta, the dynasty's
founder, among the great figures of their whole ruling house - the first
Jin emperor, in their own judgment, to understand that war was only ever
justified as a means to a worthy end, rather than an end an emperor was
entitled to pursue for its own sake.
