A dynasty that had been dead for three hundred and eighty-two years acquired a
successor who took its name on purpose.

In 1616 a Jurchen leader named Nurhaci declared a state in the forests of the
north-east — the same country the Jurchen empire had come out of, in the same
language group, among the descendants of the same people — and called it the Later Jin.
The claim was explicit: descent from this dynasty, and its name.

<Figure id="jn-nurhaci2" />

His son renamed it Qing in 1636, dropped the Jurchen ethnonym in favour of
Manchu, and in 1644 walked through Shanhai pass at the invitation of a Ming
general and took China. It lasted until 1912.

<Figure id="jn-tianming" />

## The pattern

Put the Liao, the Jin and the Mongols in order and they are one argument.

A conquest dynasty holds north China by governing two populations under two
systems. It is destroyed by a frontier people it had been extracting from and
managing. That people then holds north China by governing two populations under
two systems. Three iterations — Liao to Jin to Mongol — and then a fourth, in
1616, by a man deliberately invoking the second one.

Nurhaci was not making a historical allusion. He was making an argument about
entitlement to a specific piece of ground, addressed to the people who lived on
it, in the name of the last people from his forests who had held it — and it
worked well enough that his grandson was ruling from Beijing twenty-eight years
later.
