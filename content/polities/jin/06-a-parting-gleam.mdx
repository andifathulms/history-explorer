Zhangzong inherited the throne from his grandfather Shizong in 1189 and, for
most of his own nineteen-year reign, inherited his policy along with it -
the record's own verdict is that he preserved the salient features of
Shizong's government without needing to reinvent them.

## Peace tested once more, and won

That inheritance was tested from the Song side rather than the Jin's own.
Song's Emperor Ningzong, hearing repeated reports of Jin decline - a
discontented, underpaid army, a population with no particular loyalty to an
alien dynasty the moment it looked weak, Genghis Khan's own Mongols already
striking at Jin's reputation on the northern steppe - let those reports
convince him the moment had come to recover territory lost to the Jin
generations earlier. In 1206 he let himself be talked into a war his own
advisers had oversold.

The campaign went the opposite way from what Ningzong had been promised.
Jin forces won it decisively, and the peace that followed in 1208 simply
confirmed the existing treaties rather than conceding anything - Ningzong's
own advocate for the war was executed for the miscalculation once the
fighting was over. It was, the record notes, the last real triumph the Jin
dynasty would ever win over its old southern rival: "a parting gleam of
glory" around a dynastic name whose own signs of decline the war itself had
been fought to test.

## What the victory actually proved

The 1208 peace was real, and so was the decline it had been fought over.
Zhangzong's own generation held the frontier competently enough to beat
back a war Song had started expecting an easy win. What neither he nor his
own court fully reckoned with was the power gathering on Jin's other
border, in the Mongolian steppe, where the confederation the Song had
correctly identified as a threat to Jin was still, in 1208, treated as a
secondary concern next to the older, more familiar rivalry with the south.

## The last quiet year

Zhangzong died the same year the Mongol threat was still being measured
against the wrong neighbour, and it was his own less capable successors who
would have to answer for that miscalculation directly. His reign closes out
what later observers would come to treat as the dynasty's own settled
middle age - two capable rulers in succession, Shizong and Zhangzong,
governing a conquest state that had made its peace with the Chinese
population it ruled and mostly kept the peace with its Chinese rival to the
south. The war Genghis Khan brought to the Jin frontier within a few years
of Zhangzong's own death would show, in short order, how little of that
settled competence had prepared the dynasty for what was actually coming.
