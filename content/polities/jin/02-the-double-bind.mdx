---
title: Preserving what conquest dissolves
drafted_from: franke-twitchett-1994

Emperor Shizong's reign in the second half of the twelfth century contains a
sustained official campaign to preserve Jurchen identity: the language taught and
examined, translations of the Chinese classics into Jurchen commissioned, dress
regulated, hunting and archery mandated for the elite, Jurchen names required.

Programmes like that are evidence of the opposite of what they intend. By the
1180s the Jurchen aristocracy in northern China was speaking Chinese, marrying
Chinese, living in cities and reading for examinations, and the emperor could see
it happening and could not stop it.

The problem is structural and this site has now seen four versions of it. The
Liao kept the two populations under two administrations and a mobile court. The
Yuan legislated a racial hierarchy and suspended the examinations. The Chagatai
khanate split in half over it. The Jin tried cultural preservation by decree and
got a century.

What none of them could escape is that governing a large settled population
requires the methods that population already has, and using those methods for
two or three generations produces administrators who are at home in them. The
conquest elite either stays on the steppe and stays small, or comes inside and
stops being distinct. The Jin came inside.

The Mongols destroyed them between 1211 and 1234, which took over twenty years —
longer than the conquest of Khwarazm, and a reminder that what this corpus
records as the Islamic world's catastrophe was one front among several.
