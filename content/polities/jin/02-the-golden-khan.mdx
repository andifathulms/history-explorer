Rashid al-Din, writing the history of the world in Tabriz a century after Aguda's
revolt, knew the Jin dynasty by a title rather than a name, and got its origins
tangled with the empire it had actually destroyed.

<Figure id="jn-siege" />

## What the Persian chroniclers had

Benakati's *Tarikh-i Khata*, itself drawn from Rashid, describes a settled,
agricultural people next to the Khitan whom the Khitan called Miu-che and everyone
else called Churche — the Jurchen, filtered through Mongol pronunciation into
Persian spelling. Among them, it says, was a man called Huonyen Agudai, surnamed
Daigim, whom the Mongols called Akuda.

*He overthrew the dynasty of Kara Khitai in Khatai.*

That sentence is wrong in a specific and informative way. Aguda overthrew the
Liao — the Khitan empire in China and Manchuria. Kara Khitai, the Western Liao, was
the state Yelü Dashi founded in Central Asia after fleeing Aguda's own conquest.
The Persian sources, writing generations later and a continent away, had folded
the destroyer of the Liao and the destroyer of its western remnant into the same
confusion of names, because by the time the story reached Tabriz both empires were
called Khitai and both had gone down to somebody.

## Huangdi, and the golden khan

Bretschneider untangles one more piece of it. *Huonyen* is the Persian rendering
of Wanyan, the Jurchen imperial clan name, used constantly through the Chinese
history of the dynasty. *Daigim* is Persian for *Da Jin* — Great Jin, the dynastic
name itself, which means Golden.

And the Persian sources give the same title, Daigim, to two different men: the
founder Aguda, and — separately — "the Altun Khan who lived at the time of
Chinggis, and the one who was destroyed under the reign of Ögedei." *Altun* is
Turkic and Mongol for gold. The Persian chroniclers had translated the dynasty's
own name into their own language and then used the translation as though it were a
separate title held by whichever Jin emperor happened to be reigning when a Mongol
army was fighting him — first the emperor Chinggis fought, then the one Ögedei's
armies finished off at Caizhou.

## Why the confusion is worth keeping

Because it shows, precisely, how information about a Chinese dynasty actually
moved through the Mongol world in the thirteenth century — not as a written
Chinese record consulted directly, but as an oral report, translated once from
Chinese into Mongol and then again from Mongol into Persian, each translation
keeping the sound of a name or the sense of a title and losing the rest.

The Jin never called any of their own emperors Altun Khan; it is a Mongol
description of a Chinese title, carried west by envoys and merchants and soldiers
who had fought them, and written down by a vizier who had never seen China and had
no way to check it against the dynastic history the Jin themselves kept.

It is also, incidentally, a demonstration of the same mechanism working on the
Jin's own name for itself. Jin means gold. Altun means gold. Everyone in the
chain — Jurchen, Mongol, Persian — agreed on what the dynasty was called; only the
history attached to the name came apart in transit.
