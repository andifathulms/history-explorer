For about eighty years the Zirids governed more of North Africa than anybody had
since Rome, and did it as somebody else's deputies.

<Figure id="lantern" />

What al-Mu'izz handed over in 972 was the whole Fatimid west: Ifriqiya, the
central Maghreb, and a claim on everything as far as the Atlantic. The caliph
kept Egypt, Syria and the Hijaz, and the Zirid amir kept the province the
caliphate had been built out of, on the understanding that he would hold it, pay
for it and say the right name on Fridays.

## What a viceroyalty of that size involves

Fighting Zanata, mostly, and doing it continuously.

The Sanhaja confederation the Zirids led and the Zanata confederation that held
the central and western Maghreb had been enemies before either of them had a
caliph to fight for, and the Fatimid–Umayyad rivalry gave them one each. Córdoba
paid Zanata; Cairo paid Sanhaja; and the Maghreb spent the tenth century as a
proxy war conducted by two Berber confederations with two caliphates' money.

Buluggin ibn Ziri campaigned west repeatedly and got as far as the Atlantic
plain. His successors held rather less. Nobody could hold it all, because holding
it all meant garrisoning a thousand miles of mountain and steppe against people
who lived there, and nobody had that much money.

## The other half

Which is why the family divided it.

By 1015 a cadet branch — the Hammadids — was ruling the central Maghreb from a
new fortified capital in the mountains, independently in fact and eventually in
name. Ifriqiya faces the sea, lives on its coastal plain and looks to Sicily and
Egypt. The central Maghreb is mountain and steppe and lives on the routes
crossing it. A single dynasty holding both was a Fatimid administrative
convenience, and it survived the Fatimids' departure by forty-three years.

## And the wealth

Ifriqiya in this period was still the richest province of the Maghreb and had
been since Rome.

Olives and grain on the Tunisian plain; the ports at Mahdia, Sousse, Sfax and
Tunis; the Sicilian trade; and the caravan routes to the Sahara. The Zirids
minted, built, endowed and paid an army out of it, and Kairouan under them
remained the intellectual centre of the western Islamic world — Maliki
jurisprudence, and a Jewish community whose correspondence with Fustat survives
in the Cairo Geniza and is some of the best evidence anywhere for how eleventh
century Mediterranean commerce actually worked.

<Figure id="chushiel" />

All of which rested on there being an agricultural interior to tax, and that is
the thing that stopped.
