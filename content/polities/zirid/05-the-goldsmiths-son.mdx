The court poet of al-Mu'izz ibn Badis was the son of a Greek slave who worked as
a goldsmith in a small town on the Tunisian coast, and he trained as a goldsmith
himself before he found out what he could do with words.

Ibn Khallikan's entry on Ibn Rashiq of Kairouan is the closest thing the Zirid
century has to a witness, and its most important sentence is a subordinate
clause.

## The career

He was born about the year 1000 — at Mahdia by one account, at Masila by another
— and his father was a Greek *mamluk*, a white slave, attached as a client to an
Arab tribe. The father was a goldsmith at Muhammadiya and taught his son the
trade. The son studied letters there instead, began to compose, and *conceived a
strong desire to augment that talent and meet with literary men*, which brought
him to Kairouan.

At Kairouan he got into reputation, composed poems in honour of the ruling
prince, and was attached to his service. The prince was al-Mu'izz ibn Badis, the
Zirid amir who took the province out of Fatimid allegiance.

Ibn Rashiq wrote the *Umda*, a treatise on the art of poetry and on poetical
criticism, and it became the standard work on the subject in Arabic; a century
and a half later Ibn Khallikan cites it in entry after entry as the authority
that settles a question about a verse. He wrote a specimen anthology, a
collection of epistles, a philological treatise on words used in rare senses, and
a small book called *Grains of Gold* — the goldsmith's son's title for something
short and highly instructive.

The verses Ibn Khallikan chooses to preserve are not court panegyric. They are
domestic and slightly sour:

> I love my brother, though I turn away from him, and though my words rarely
> strike his ear. At the sight of his face I frown with pleasure, as you frown at
> the aspect of generous wine. Frowns do not always proceed from hatred, and
> secret hatred is often concealed by smiles.

And, on being old:

> When I wish to be light and gay as in the days of my youth, five and forty
> years refuse their consent. My steps falter, not with age, but with the years
> which I drag after me.

## The clause

Then the line that puts the whole Zirid century into one sentence of a reference
book:

> He continued in that city till it was carried and destroyed by the Arabs, and
> its inhabitants massacred; on which he passed over to Sicily and settled at
> Mazara, where he died.

That is Kairouan in 1057, from a man who was in it. The Banu Hilal took the
intellectual capital of the western Islamic world, and the court poet of the
prince whose change of allegiance had brought them west got out and crossed to
the other side of the strait.

He died at Mazara del Vallo — Ibn Khallikan settles on 1070 and notes that a
learned hand he consulted put it six years earlier — in a Muslim Sicily that had
been conquered from Kairouan two hundred and thirty years before, and that the
Normans began taking while he was living on it.

## What is worth noticing about the shape

Nothing in this life was planned by anybody, and all of it depended on the state.

A Greek taken as a slave into Ifriqiya makes a living as a goldsmith and puts his
son into the trade. The son goes to the capital because that is where the patrons
are, and the patrons exist because the Zirids were rich enough to keep a court.
The court is destroyed by a migration the prince's own politics invited. The poet
leaves for the nearest place where Arabic is still the language of a government,
and that place is an island the province had conquered when it was strong.

And the book survived all of it. The *Umda* went out of Kairouan before the
Arabs arrived, and was still being quoted as the authority on Arabic verse two
hundred years later by a judge in Cairo who knew the city it was written in only
as a place that had been destroyed.

One of the poems Ibn Khallikan keeps is a complaint to God about being harassed
by small enemies, and it reads differently once the date is known:

> O Lord! I am not able to repel this evil, and to thee do I apply for succour
> against this feeble worker of evil. Why hast thou sent against me a thousand
> gnats, whilst thou didst send only one against Nimrod?
