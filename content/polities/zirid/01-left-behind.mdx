---
title: Left behind
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987
phase: formation
---

When the Fatimids took Egypt in 969 and moved their caliphate to a new city
called Cairo, they left behind the province they had spent sixty years building
it out of.

They left it to the Zirids. Buluggin ibn Ziri was the son of a Sanhaja Berber
chief who had held the central Maghreb for the Fatimids against their enemies,
and when al-Mu'izz went east in 972 he took up residence as viceroy of Ifriqiya
with the whole western half of the caliphate under him.

The arrangement is the same one the Aghlabids had held from Baghdad a century and
a half earlier: a distant caliph, a local family, a tribute, and a name in the
Friday sermon. What is different here
is that the caliph in question had personally been in Kairouan a few years
before, and that the family holding the province had been the instrument of his
own rise.

It worked for two generations and then divided. In 1015 a cadet branch under
Hammad, holding the Qal'a in the mountains of the central Maghreb, refused
subordination and set up on its own — and, being in rebellion against a Fatimid
viceroy, put the *Abbasid* caliph's name in its own sermon. That is the
Hammadid dynasty, which held the central Maghreb for a century and a half and
built a mountain capital whose ruins are still there.

So by 1016, when al-Mu'izz ibn Badis came to the throne at Kairouan, the
Fatimid west was already two polities, one of them Sunni in its public
allegiance. Ifriqiya itself was overwhelmingly Sunni and Maliki — the Fatimids
had never converted it, any more than they
converted Egypt — and the province's scholars and townsmen had spent eighty
years being governed in the name of a caliph they regarded as a heretic.

Al-Mu'izz ruled for forty-six years, and somewhere around the middle of them he
did the popular thing.
