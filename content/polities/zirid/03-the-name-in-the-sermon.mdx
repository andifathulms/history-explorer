In 1048 al-Mu'izz ibn Badis stopped saying the Fatimid caliph's name in the
Friday sermon at Kairouan and said the Abbasid caliph's instead.

It is a small liturgical act and it was the most consequential decision anybody
in Ifriqiya made in three hundred years.

## What it meant

Everything, in a system where the sermon was the only regular public statement of
who was sovereign.

The *khutba* named the ruler in whose name authority was exercised, in every
congregational mosque, every week, to everybody. Changing the name was not a
gesture: it was the formal transfer of allegiance from a Shi'i caliph in Cairo to
a Sunni one in Baghdad, announced simultaneously in every town, and it could not
be done quietly or taken back.

## Why he did it

Because it was popular, and because he had been governing an overwhelmingly Sunni
country in the name of a caliph its scholars regarded as a heretic for eighty
years.

The Fatimids had never converted Ifriqiya. Their Isma'ilism had been the doctrine
of a court and a movement, not of the province, and the Maliki establishment of
Kairouan — the most prestigious body of scholars in the western Islamic world —
had spent the better part of a century in a state of tolerated dissent. There
were anti-Shi'i riots in the towns. The Zirid amir who put himself at the head of
that feeling was doing what every ruler in his position eventually does, which is
stop paying for other people's unpopularity.

The Abbasid caliph, who could do nothing for him and nothing to him, sent a
diploma of investiture. It cost Baghdad nothing and it gave al-Mu'izz what he
wanted, which was to be the legitimate Sunni ruler of a Sunni country.

## The peak, and what it was standing on

This is the high point of the dynasty by every measure that would have been
visible at the time. An independent, wealthy, orthodox state, holding the richest
province in North Africa, ruling from the intellectual capital of the Islamic
west, with its allegiance transferred to a caliph nobody objected to and its own
scholars finally on its side.

It lasted four years.

The thing al-Mu'izz had not weighed is that the caliph he had abandoned could not
send an army across Libya, and did not need one. What Cairo could do was send
people — and the difference between an army and a migration is that an army can
be defeated.
