---
title: The answer was a migration
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987

---

Between 1041 and 1051 al-Mu'izz ibn Badis renounced the Fatimids and had the
Abbasid caliph's name pronounced in the sermon at Kairouan. The city was
delighted. It was the correct move for a Sunni ruler of a Sunni province with a
Sunni scholarly establishment, and it was the last comfortable decision the
dynasty made.

Cairo could not send an army across Libya. What it did instead was give
permission.

The Banu Hilal and the Banu Sulaym were Arab bedouin confederations settled in
Upper Egypt, where they were a nuisance. The Fatimid government encouraged them
to go west into Ifriqiya, reportedly granting them the country and telling them
to take it. They went — not as an army with a campaign but as a migration, with
families and herds, over years, in numbers the sources put in the tens of
thousands and modern estimates argue about.

In April 1052 a Zirid army was destroyed at Haydaran by a much smaller Hilali
force. After that there was nothing to stop them, and the thing they did was
not conquest in the sense this site's other pages use the word. They took no
capital and founded no dynasty. They moved into the country, and the settled
agriculture of Ifriqiya — the olive and grain economy that had made it the
richest province of the Maghreb since Rome — could not survive being pastured
over.

Kairouan was abandoned in 1057 and sacked. The city that had been the capital
of the Islamic west since the conquest, whose Great Mosque this site's Aghlabid
page describes, stopped being a capital and never became one again.

The long consequence is the one Ibn Khaldun wrote about three centuries later
from Tunis, on this site's Hafsid page: he treats the Hilali migration as the
event that ruined the Maghreb's agriculture and made its politics tribal, and
his account has been argued over ever since — modern historians are divided on
how much of the region's decline it caused and how much it was blamed for. What
is not disputed is the linguistic outcome. Before the Banu Hilal the Maghreb
was a Berber-speaking country with Arabic in the cities. Afterwards Arabic
spread into the countryside, and the language map of North Africa today is
substantially the result.

A caliphate that could not punish a defection with an army punished it with a
demographic event, and the map still shows it.
