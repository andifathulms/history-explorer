The Zirids lost Mahdia in 1148 and the last of them died under Almohad
protection in 1167. What they left is a country and a language, and neither of
them was anything the dynasty intended.

## The country

Ifriqiya after the Zirids is a different kind of place from Ifriqiya before them,
and it stayed that way.

Before 1050 it was what it had been since Carthage: an agricultural province with
a dense settled interior of olives and grain, taxed by a government in a city on
the plain. After the Hilalian migration it was a coastal strip of ports with a
pastoral hinterland that no state administered — that negotiated with tribes,
subsidised them, married into them and occasionally fought them, but did not
collect a land tax from them in any systematic way.

Every government that followed inherited that arrangement and none of them
reversed it. The Almohads took the coast and dealt with the interior. The Hafsids
ruled Tunis for three centuries on exactly the same basis. So did the Ottomans,
and so, in its own way, did the French protectorate.

A country's political geography was reset in about twenty years by people who
were not trying to govern it.

## The language

The larger change is the one Ibn Khaldun argued about and the maps still show.

Before the Banu Hilal, the Maghreb was a Berber-speaking country with Arabic in
the cities — the language of religion, law, administration and long-distance
trade, spoken by townsmen and scholars, in a countryside that spoke something
else. Four centuries of Arab rule had not altered that, because conquest and
government do not change what people speak at home.

What changed it was the arrival of large numbers of Arabic-speaking pastoralists
who lived in the countryside, moved through it and married into it. Within a few
generations Arabic was a rural language in North Africa, and the Berber-speaking
areas had contracted to the mountains and the desert margins where they largely
remain.

Ibn Khaldun, writing at Tunis three hundred years later, treats the migration as
the catastrophe that ruined the Maghreb, and his account has been argued over
ever since — modern historians are divided about how much decline it actually
caused and how much was blamed on it afterwards. The linguistic outcome is not in
dispute. The language map of North Africa today is substantially what the
eleventh century made.

## What it was retaliation for

A liturgical formula.

Al-Mu'izz ibn Badis changed the name in the Friday sermon in 1048 because his
subjects were Sunni and he was tired of paying for a Shi'i caliph's unpopularity.
Cairo could not reach him with an army and did not try. It gave two Arab
confederations that were causing trouble in Upper Egypt permission to go west,
and their grandchildren were pasturing sheep over the olive groves of the richest
province in Africa.

Whether the Fatimid vizier who arranged it foresaw any of that is not
recoverable. The likeliest reading is that Cairo was solving a problem in Upper
Egypt and was pleased to have a use for it. The Maghreb got the consequences,
and has them still.
