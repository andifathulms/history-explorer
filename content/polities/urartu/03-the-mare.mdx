By the middle of the eighth century BC Urartu was not a mountain kingdom holding
its valleys. It was contesting northern Syria with Assyria, and it had allies
there.

That is the measure of the peak, and the evidence for it is Assyrian, because
Tiglath-Pileser III had to go and undo it.

## How far it had come

Sarduri of Urartu, the annals say, *revolted against me and made common cause
with Mati'-ilu* — Mati'-ilu being the king of Arpad, in what is now northern
Syria, six hundred kilometres from Lake Van.

The word *revolted* is Assyrian bookkeeping and should be read as such: Urartu
had never been an Assyrian possession, and its king owed Assyria nothing. What
the phrasing conceals is the real situation, which was that a highland kingdom
had built a coalition across the top of the Fertile Crescent and was in a
position to decide who held the north Syrian trade routes.

The battle happened at Kishtan and Halpi in Commagene, on the Euphrates — far
from both capitals, which is where the two states had come to meet.

## The flight

Tiglath-Pileser's account survives in more than one version, and the differences
are the interesting part.

Both agree that he broke Sarduri and took his whole camp. One version says
Sarduri became frightened at the fury of Assyrian arms and *ran away, alone, to
save his life*. The other is more specific, and better: to save his life he
*mounted a mare and escaped to Mount Sibak, a steep mountain, at night, and
ascended it*.

A mare rather than a stallion, at night, up a mountain. It is the kind of detail
that survives because it was humiliating and because somebody saw it, and it has
outlasted every inscription Sarduri put up about himself.

He was then shut inside Tushpa, his own capital on the lake, with large numbers
of his men killed in front of the gate. Tiglath-Pileser did not take the city.
Instead he had his royal image made and set up in front of it, which is what you
do when you cannot get in and want the failure to look like a decision.

## What he did take

The annexation list is the part that shows what a great state losing a battle
actually costs.

He marched, he says, through the whole width of Urartu from its upper border to
its lower — sixty double-hours in one version, eighty in the other — and *found
none to oppose*. Then the strongholds: Ulluba entire, and Kashtirru, Parisu,
Tashuha, Galananal, Sikibsa, Asurdai, Babutta, Lusia, Bisia; and Kuta, Urra,
Arana, Taba, Uallia as far as the Euphrates; and Kilissa, Ezieda, Abbissa,
Harbisinna, Tasa, Enzi, Anganu, Benzu.

They were not sacked and left. They were *brought within the Assyrian border*,
assigned to the provinces of the commander-in-chief and of Nairi. A new city was
built in Ulluba, named Ashur-ikisha, populated with deportees, and given an
Assyrian governor.

Note also, in the same list, a stronghold called Sardaurri. Urartian kings named
fortresses after themselves; this one now belonged to Assyria under the name of
the king who had lost it.

## The high point, read backwards

Nothing in Urartian inscriptions describes a defeat, because royal inscriptions
in this world never do. Almost everything we can date about the kingdom's extent
comes from the people who fought it.

So the peak has to be read out of an enemy's annals, and it reads clearly: to be
worth a campaign of this size, to hold allies in Syria, to require a march
across the entire kingdom to subdue, Urartu had to have been the second power in
the region. Its capital held. Its frontier provinces did not, and it never got
them back.

Thirty years later Sargon II would come up the same valleys and write the most
detailed military narrative in the ancient Near East about it.
