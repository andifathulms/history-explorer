The kingdom disappeared around 590 BC without anybody recording how, and what
outlived it was a word in somebody else's book — attached, eventually, to the
wrong mountain.

## Where Ararat was

Ararat was what the Assyrians called the country around Lake Van and the
district south of it. That is where the name sits in the Hebrew scriptures,
where the ark comes to rest on the mountains of Ararat and where the sons of
Sennacherib flee after murdering their father.

It was not, Sayce notes, extended northward to the peak now called Mount Ararat
until post-biblical times. The title originally belonged to the Kurdish range
in the south. A country's name became a mountain's name, and then moved about a
hundred and fifty kilometres to a different mountain, and stayed there.

So the most famous thing about Urartu is a misattribution of its own name, and
the state that carried the name for two and a half centuries had nothing to do
with a flood story.

## What survived to be read

The inscriptions did, scattered across eastern Anatolia in a borrowed script
and an unrelated language, and they were unreadable for a very long time.

The script could be sounded out once Assyrian cuneiform was deciphered, which
is why nineteenth-century scholars could copy the texts and pronounce them
without knowing what they said. Sayce, writing in 1888, was among the first to
work through them and to establish what they contained: the buildings, the
offerings, the campaigns, the names of Menuas and Argistis and the god Khaldi.

Everything now known about the kingdom's own view of itself comes from that
material, and the material was standing on cliff faces and fortress walls in
plain sight the whole time.

## A state with no successors, and a country with one

Nobody claimed to continue Urartu. No later kingdom took its titles and no
dynasty traced itself to its kings, and the god Khaldi disappears from the
record altogether — which is the strangest part, because gods normally outlast
the states that endow them.

The country carried on under another name. When Darius had his account of his
accession carved at Behistun in three languages, the province the Old Persian
version calls Armina is called Urashtu — Urartu — in the Babylonian one. The
Persians had a new name for the place and the Armenians were becoming its
people, and Babylonian scribes went on writing the old name for a century after
there was no kingdom left to attach it to.

The kingdom was built for one purpose — to survive the Assyrian army — and it
achieved that for two hundred and seventy years, which is longer than the
empire it was built against lasted. Then Assyria fell, the reason for the
fortresses went with it, and within a generation or two Urartu was gone as
well, with no enemy left to explain it.

What is left is a lake with the kingdom's own name in it, a mountain in the
wrong place carrying the name its enemies used, and several hundred
inscriptions in a language that has no relatives.
