The kingdom did not call itself Urartu. That was the Assyrian name, and the
Hebrew scriptures took it from the Assyrians as Ararat.

Its own name for itself was Biainas, and that name has survived in the one
place it could: the lake. Van is what is left of Biainas.

## Reading the kings

The kingdom left inscriptions all over eastern Anatolia, and Sayce, who was the
first person to read them systematically, describes exactly what they are.

They are written in cuneiform borrowed from Nineveh — taken over, on his
reckoning, in the time of Ashurnasirpal or his son Shalmaneser — and in a
language that bears no resemblance whatever to Assyrian. That is the whole
kingdom in one observation: an enemy's writing system used to record a language
the enemy could not read.

What the inscriptions record is buildings, offerings and campaigns. Temples and
palaces put up; gifts made to the gods; and the marching, in the terms the
kings themselves used.

## Menuas at Palu

Menuas reigned in the ninth century and went west. He overran the land of Alzi
and found himself among the Hittites — the successor kingdoms of northern Syria
and the Euphrates bend — where he plundered two cities belonging to a Hittite
prince and captured a number of soldiers, whom he dedicated to the service of
his god Khaldi.

On another occasion he marched as far as Malatya, and on the way back he had an
inscription commemorating his conquests cut into the cliffs at Palu, on the
north bank of the Euphrates, about halfway between Malatya and Van.

His son Argistis made war on the same people again, and one of his inscriptions
shows Malatya itself counted among their fortresses.

## What the reach was for

Set that beside what the Assyrians were doing at the same moment and the shape
of the kingdom becomes clear.

Urartu's expansion runs north and west, into Transcaucasia and up the Euphrates
— away from Assyria, along the routes that carried metal, horses and timber. It
takes prisoners and dedicates them to the state god. It records the taking of
towns and does not annex most of them.

This is a state doing to its own neighbours precisely what Assyria was doing to
it, on a smaller scale and for the same reason: a fortress kingdom in high
country cannot feed itself out of its valleys alone, and everything it needed
had to be brought in.

The cliffs at Palu are as far west as the writ ran. A king who could not stop
the Assyrian army coming north every few years could still put his name on a
rock four hundred kilometres from his capital, in a script he had taken from
the people he was hiding from.
