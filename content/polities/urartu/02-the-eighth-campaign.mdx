In 714 BC Sargon II went into the highlands, and afterwards had an account of it
written as a letter to the god Ashur. It runs to something over four hundred
lines and it is the most detailed military narrative surviving from the ancient
Near East.

It is detailed in ways royal inscriptions usually are not. The terrain is in it —
gorges the chariots had to be taken apart to cross, mountains described as
standing like the blade of a dagger. The exhaustion is in it: the troops
complaining, the water problem, the king on foot. The intelligence is in it,
including reports from informers. It reads, in places, like a campaign diary that
somebody afterwards addressed to a god.

The campaign avoided the Urartian fortresses, which Sargon evidently did not
propose to besiege, met Rusa I in open country in the mountains and beat him,
marched through the country burning, and then turned aside on the way home to
sack Musasir.

## Musasir

Musasir was the principal sanctuary of Haldi, the god in whose name every
Urartian king says he does everything. It lay outside the kingdom's core, in the
hills on the Assyrian side, which is why it was reachable. Sargon took the temple
treasury, and the letter itemises it at extraordinary length — the weights of
gold and silver, the vessels, the furniture, the statue of the god himself,
carried off to Assyria. A relief in Sargon's palace at Khorsabad showed Assyrian
soldiers stripping the temple, and it is one of the few images of an Urartian
building made by anyone.

Rusa, the letter says, heard what had happened, threw himself on the ground, tore
his clothes and took his own life with his own iron dagger.

## What actually happened to Urartu

It survived. That is the part worth holding onto, because the document is
overwhelming and the outcome was not.

Urartu was not annexed, not made tributary, and not destroyed. Its kings went on
building fortresses for another century — some of the largest, at Ayanis and
Bastam, are later than this campaign. What it stopped doing was contesting
Assyria. After 714 the two states are largely at peace, and by the seventh
century they appear to have had a working relationship against the nomadic
pressure coming down from the steppe, which had become the more serious problem
for both.

So the two turning points on this record are both Assyrian defeats of Urartu and
neither of them ended it. The kingdom was built to survive the Assyrian army and
it did exactly that, at the cost of never being anything more than a state that
survived. This site has no field for that outcome. It codes reach, institutions
and influence, and a polity whose achievement was continuing to exist scores
nothing on any of them.
