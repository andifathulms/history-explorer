This record's `ended.type` is `still contested`, and it is the first time that
value has been used anywhere in this corpus. Since the site argued against using
it three records ago, the choice has to be defended.

On the Hittite record, `still contested` was rejected on the ground that it would
describe the scholarship rather than the polity. Everyone agrees that Hattusa was
emptied and burned around 1180 and that the central state stopped; what is
disputed is the cause. A vocabulary of endings should say what happened, and what
happened there was fragmentation.

Here there is no agreed event. Whether Urartu was conquered at all is open.
Whether the destruction levels at the great fortresses represent one episode or
several spread over decades is open. Who the agent was — Medes, Scythians,
Cimmerians, some combination, or nobody — is open, and each of the candidates
rests on a late Greek or biblical source rather than on anything contemporary.
Even the date is a convention: 590 is a round number standing in for a process
somewhere in the first third of the sixth century.

There is no cause to dispute because there is no event to attach one to. That is
what this value is for, and if it is never used for a case like this it will
never be used at all.

## Nineteen years

The one comparison worth making is with the neighbour. Assyria ended in 609,
comprehensively: the great cities destroyed and never reoccupied, the language of
the court no longer written, and within two centuries Greeks passing the mounds
being told they had been built by somebody else.

Urartu, a seventh of Assyria's size at the peak, built specifically to survive
Assyria, outlasted it — by about nineteen years on the conventional dating, which
is close enough to be a coincidence and satisfying anyway. What it did not
outlast was whatever came out of the steppe afterwards, and the same
disappearance of the written record that makes its ending unknowable is why we
cannot say what.

## What did not carry over

Armenia occupies this ground later. This site's Greater Armenia record starts
about 190 BC, four hundred years after this one ends, in a different region of
the site, and there is no edge between them and no successor claim counted.

That is deliberate. The Armenian language is Indo-European and Urartian is not —
it is related to Hurrian, which puts it with this corpus's Mitanni record and
nothing else. No source has an Armenian dynasty asserting Urartian legitimacy,
and coding rule 1 does not count continuity of territory. The Assyrians' word for
the country, *Urartu*, survives as the biblical Ararat and as a mountain's name,
and a name on a map is not a claim either.

What did carry over is on the Assyrian page, not this one. Urartian cuneiform is
Neo-Assyrian cuneiform adapted to write a language unrelated to Akkadian, and
rule 3 puts that on the record of the polity whose script was adapted. The
Neo-Assyrian record's `descendant_scripts` count was null until this page was
written and is now 1. Urartu's own is 0.

Which is the region's recurring joke at this point. The Hittite record's script
claim belongs to a Babylonian page. The Middle Elamite record's belongs to a
Mesopotamian one. Here, for once, the beneficiary is actually in the corpus — and
it is the state this one was built to keep out.

## Four nulls

One last pattern, which this record completes and which is not the one the site
expected.

`succession_rule` is null on eight of this region's thirteen records, and four of
those are null for the ordinary reason: nobody has a source. The other four are
not. On the Ur III record, the Old Babylonian record, the Egyptian Old Kingdom
record and this one, the dynasty passed the throne down for centuries without
recorded difficulty and no text states a rule. Three writing systems, two
thousand years, four dynasties whose successions worked, and not one of them
wrote down how.

The two records in this region that did — Hatti, with Telipinu's Edict around
1500, and Assyria, with Esarhaddon's succession oath in 672 — are the two whose
successions had already gone catastrophically wrong. Telipinu legislated after a
century in which nearly every accession was settled by murder. Esarhaddon drafted
his oath after taking the throne in a civil war caused by his father's
assassination.

So the region's evidence does not show that some states had succession rules and
others did not. It shows that a succession rule is something a state writes after
it has been badly hurt by not having one, and that the dynasties whose
successions worked never saw the need. Coding rule 7 says the field takes the
rule and not the outcome, and following it strictly across thirteen records
produced that finding, which is not visible in any single one of them.
