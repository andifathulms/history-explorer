The Armenian highlands are not a place that produces empires. High, cold,
mountainous, cut into valleys that do not communicate easily, with a short
growing season and no great river plain — the kind of country that produces
chiefdoms and keeps producing them. From the middle of the ninth century BC it
produced instead a centralised state with a royal dynasty, a chancery, a
territorial administration and a network of vast fortresses, which held together
for two hundred and seventy years and then vanished.

Zimansky's account, which this record is coded from, is that the anomaly has an
external explanation. Urartu is not what that landscape generates on its own. It
is what that landscape generates when the Assyrian army starts coming north every
few years and there is no alternative to organising.

## The fortresses

The state is legible chiefly as architecture. Along the valleys the kings built
citadels on rock outcrops — Tushpa on the crag at Van, Erebuni above what is now
Yerevan, Argishtihinili, Ayanis, Bastam, Karmir Blur — each with cyclopean walls,
each with an inscription naming the king who built it, and each with storage on a
scale that is the point of the whole thing. Rows of magazines holding grain and
wine in jars sunk to the shoulder in the floor, capacities recorded in the
inscriptions, running to hundreds of thousands of litres in a single fortress.

Around them, state-built infrastructure: irrigation canals, orchards, vineyards
established by royal order and recorded as such. The canal Menua cut to bring
water to Tushpa runs for over fifty kilometres and parts of it still carry water.

What is thin, by comparison, is everything not royal. The countryside between the
fortresses has produced remarkably little; there is not much in the way of
Urartian villages, ordinary cemeteries or private inscriptions. This is a state
that appears in the archaeological record almost entirely as the works of its
kings, which is why Zimansky calls it a superstructure — an apparatus laid over
a landscape rather than grown out of one.

## Where the wealth came from

This record's `revenue_basis` reads `land-tax, plunder`, and the second value is
coded in coding rule 6's strict sense, on Zimansky's argument.

The royal inscriptions are relentlessly quantitative about campaigning. King
after king records marching north and west into Transcaucasia and eastern
Anatolia, burning towns, and bringing back the count: so many thousand cattle, so
many thousand sheep, so many thousand horses, so many thousand men and women. The
territories raided were mostly not annexed. The people were resettled.

Rule 6 wants a source that treats campaign proceeds as structural income rather
than as windfall, and this is what that looks like: the deported population is
what worked the fortress estates, and the herds are what stocked them. The
raiding is not incidental to the agricultural base; on this reading it is the
mechanism by which a fortress in a high valley had anything to store. That is the
same value this site codes on the Neo-Assyrian record for the same kind of reason,
and here it is being applied to the state Assyria was raiding.
