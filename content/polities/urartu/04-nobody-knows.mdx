Urartu is one of the very few kingdoms of the ancient world whose ending cannot
be described at all.

Whether it was conquered is open. Whether the destruction levels at the great
fortresses represent one episode or several spread over decades is open. Who
the agent was — Medes, Scythians, Cimmerians, some combination, or nobody — is
open, and each candidate rests on a late Greek or biblical source rather than
on anything contemporary. Even the date is a convention: 590 is a round number
standing in for a process somewhere in the first third of the sixth century.

This is a stronger kind of ignorance than usual. For most vanished states the
event is known and the cause is argued — everyone agrees Hattusa was emptied
and burned around 1180, and the dispute is about why. Here there is no cause to
dispute, because there is no event to attach one to.

## Nineteen years

The one comparison worth making is with the neighbour.

Assyria ended in 609, comprehensively: the great cities destroyed and never
reoccupied, the language of the court no longer written, and within two
centuries Greeks passing the mounds were being told they had been raised by
somebody else entirely.

Urartu was a seventh of Assyria's size at its peak and was built specifically
to survive Assyria. It did — by about nineteen years on the conventional
dating, which is close enough to be a coincidence and satisfying anyway. What
it did not survive was whatever came out of the steppe afterwards, and the same
disappearance of the written record that makes its ending unknowable is why
nobody can say what that was.

## What did not carry over

Armenia occupies this ground later, and the connection is weaker than the
geography suggests. Armenian is Indo-European; Urartian is not, being related
to Hurrian and to very little else. No source shows an Armenian dynasty
claiming Urartian legitimacy. The Assyrians' name for the country, *Urartu*,
survives as the biblical Ararat and as a mountain's name, and a name on a map
is not an inheritance.

What did carry over went to the enemy. Urartian cuneiform is Neo-Assyrian
cuneiform adapted to write a language unrelated to Akkadian — so the one thing
this kingdom's writing passed on, it passed to the state it had been built to
keep out.

## The rule nobody wrote

There is a pattern across this whole region worth ending on, and Urartu
completes it.

Look at the dynasties whose successions are undocumented: Ur III, Old
Babylonian, the Egyptian Old Kingdom, and this one. Four dynasties across three
writing systems and two thousand years, each of which passed the throne down
for centuries without recorded difficulty, and not one of which wrote down how
it was supposed to work.

Now look at the two that did write it down. Hatti produced Telipinu's Edict
around 1500, after a century in which nearly every accession had been settled
by murder. Assyria produced Esarhaddon's succession oath in 672, drafted by a
king who had taken the throne in a civil war caused by his father's
assassination.

So the ancient Near East does not divide into states that had succession rules
and states that did not. It divides into states that had been badly hurt by not
having one and states that had not yet been. A written constitution for the
throne is a scar, and the dynasties whose successions worked never saw the need
to acquire one.
