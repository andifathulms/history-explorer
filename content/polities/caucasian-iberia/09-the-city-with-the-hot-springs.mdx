The kingdom ended in 580. The country did not, and the best evidence for that is
what Muslim geographers found when they got there and started writing it down.

## Tiflis

Vakhtang's city on the Kura was taken by the Arabs in the 640s and became an
emirate under a Muslim amir, which it stayed for the better part of five
centuries. Ibn Hawkal, describing it in the tenth century, gives a town with
double walls, strongly fortified, and three gates. It sat on both banks of the
river, and Muqaddasi says a bridge of boats joined the two halves. The country
round about was extremely fertile.

And there were natural hot baths, where the springs came up in the bed of the
river itself. That is the thing the city is named for — *tpili* is Georgian for
warm — and it is the detail the Arabic geographers thought worth recording about
a place otherwise notable for being the frontier.

## Not a Muslim country

The striking fact in Le Strange's account is the one he states in a single
sentence and does not dwell on. Gurjistan — Georgia — and Abkhazia only enter
the Muslim geographies as Muslim districts after Timur's campaigns at the end of
the fourteenth century.

Eight hundred years. An Arab amir held the capital from the 640s; the Georgian
countryside outside it was governed by Georgian presiding princes and remained
Christian throughout; and when the geographers of the tenth century listed the
provinces of the caliphate, Georgia was a place they knew about, described, gave
distances to, and did not count as theirs.

Muqaddasi files Abkhazia under the Caucasus proper and lists what is in it: a
village called the Village of Jonah, inhabited by Muslims, and around it the
tribes of the Georgians and the Alans and others. One Muslim village, named in
an itinerary, surrounded by people who were not.

## What that is

It is the answer to the question the abolition of the monarchy raises.

Removing a king removes the one institution an empire can see. What it does not
touch is a nobility holding its own valleys, a church older than the empire
doing the removing, an alphabet, and a language nobody else speaks. Those four
things carried Iberia through Sasanian, Byzantine and Arab suzerainty for three
hundred years of presiding princes, and then in 888 a Bagratid in the
south-western marches took the title king of the Kartvelians and there was a
Georgian monarchy again.

Three hundred and eight years without a crown, and the country that got one back
was recognisably the same country, worshipping in the same church, writing in
the same alphabet, ruled by the same houses in the same valleys, from a city
built round a hot spring by a king called wolf-head.
