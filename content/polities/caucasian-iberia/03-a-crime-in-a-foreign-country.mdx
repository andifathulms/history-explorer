Tacitus names the Iberian royal house directly in the middle of the
first century AD, and the story he tells about it is one of the
ugliest in his whole account of Rome's eastern frontier - a king who
destroyed his own brother to get at a throne, and a son who went
further still.

## A brother given Armenia, and a son who wanted more

Pharasmanes had long held Iberia; his brother Mithridates ruled
Armenia with Roman backing after Pharasmanes had helped drive the
Parthians out on his behalf. Pharasmanes's own son, Rhadamistus - tall,
strikingly handsome, and famous among his neighbors for his physical
strength - grew impatient waiting to inherit a kingdom he considered
too small for him, and made his ambition obvious enough that his aging
father began to fear him. Rather than risk open conflict with a
popular, capable son, Pharasmanes pointed him toward Armenia instead:
the throne his own brother held. Rhadamistus staged a public quarrel
with his father, fled to Mithridates's court pretending to be a
refugee from his stepmother's hatred, was welcomed and showered with
honors by his own uncle, and spent his time there quietly recruiting
Armenian nobles to his cause without Mithridates ever suspecting it.

## A handshake used as a trap

When Mithridates finally took refuge in a Roman-garrisoned fortress
after Pharasmanes provoked open war, the siege dragged on until a
truce was arranged, sealed by the traditional steppe ritual of binding
right hands together at the thumb and letting the blood mingle. As the
man administering the ritual bound Mithridates's hand, he pretended
the knot had slipped, seized Mithridates by the knees, and threw him
to the ground; armed men rushed in and chained him. Mithridates was
dragged off in fetters - a public humiliation for a king - while his
wife followed with their children, filling the air with her cries.
Pharasmanes, Tacitus writes, valued power over his own brother and
daughter and had the stomach for any crime, though he could not bring
himself to watch the killing in person. Rhadamistus, keeping a
technical oath not to use blade or poison on his own uncle and aunt,
had them thrown to the ground and smothered under piles of heavy
cloth instead. Mithridates's own young sons were killed too, for
having wept over their father's murder.

## A wife stabbed and left for the river

Rhadamistus held Armenia only briefly. The Parthian king Vologeses
invaded to install his own brother Tiridates on the throne instead,
and when Rhadamistus's Iberian garrison collapsed without a fight, he
was reduced to fleeing the kingdom on horseback with his pregnant wife
Zenobia riding beside him. Exhausted and terrified of capture, Zenobia
begged her husband to kill her rather than let her fall into enemy
hands. Rhadamistus, torn between admiration for her courage and horror
at abandoning her, finally stabbed her with his own sword and threw
her body into the Araxes river to keep it from being found - then rode
on alone into Iberia, his father's kingdom, to save himself. Zenobia
was not dead. Local shepherds found her still breathing at the river's
edge, bandaged her wound, and, once they learned who she was, carried
her to Artaxata, from where she was eventually restored with honor -
the one person in the whole affair who survived it with anything like
dignity.
