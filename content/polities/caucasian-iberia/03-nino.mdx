Iberia adopted Christianity under Mirian III, conventionally in 337 and probably
in the 330s. The Georgian tradition attributes the conversion to a woman: Nino,
a captive from Cappadocia, who healed the queen and then the king, and whose
shrine is at Bodbe and whose cross — made of vine-wood bound with her own hair —
is in Tbilisi.

That a national conversion is credited to a foreign woman with no office is
unusual enough to be worth stating, and it is not the only thing in this corpus
that is: Arwa al-Sulayhi held religious rank in the Isma'ili hierarchy, and
Tamar on this site's Georgia record was styled king. The Caucasus and Yemen
supply most of the corpus's exceptions on this point.

Iberia is the second or third of the three Caucasian conversions, depending on
which dating of the Armenian one is used, and this site now carries all three
side by side: Armenia around 301, Iberia around 337, Albania around 313 by its
own tradition. Within four decades the Sasanian empire's entire northern
frontier had become Christian, at the moment the Sasanians were consolidating
Zoroastrianism as a state religion with a state priesthood.

Everything afterwards follows from that. The Sasanians attempted to reverse it
in all three countries and failed in all three; the countries aligned with Rome
and then with Byzantium and discovered that Byzantium wanted their churches as
well as their loyalty; and when the Arabs arrived in the seventh century they
found three Christian populations who had spent three hundred years learning how
to be governed by an empire of a different religion without ceasing to be
themselves — which is a substantial part of why the Caucasus is Christian today
and the countries south of it are not.

The Georgian church established here is the most continuous institution in
Georgian history. It is older than the Bagrationi, older than the kingdom on
this site's Georgia record, older than Tbilisi, and it is still there.
