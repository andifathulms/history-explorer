Vakhtang I, called Gorgasali — wolf-head, from the device on his helmet — ruled
for most of the second half of the fifth century and is the figure Georgian
tradition treats as its Charlemagne: the king who reorganised the country, fought
the Persians, and founded Tbilisi.

The tradition is thick and the evidence is thin, and Suny's account is careful
about the difference. What can reasonably be said is that Vakhtang campaigned
against and with the Sasanians in the pattern the position required, that he
obtained or asserted autocephaly for the Georgian church — which had until then
been under Antioch, and which becoming self-governing turned into a national
institution rather than a provincial one — and that Tbilisi, on the Kura where
the sulphur springs are, was founded or refounded in his reign and became the
capital under his son Dachi.

That last fact is on this site twice, six centuries apart, and the pair is worth
setting beside each other. Tbilisi is founded by a Christian Georgian king in
about the 490s. It is taken by the Arabs in 736 and is a Muslim emirate for the
following three hundred and eighty-six years. And this corpus's Georgia record
has David IV taking it back in 1122 and keeping the mosque, the qadi and the
Muslim population, and setting their taxes below the Christians'.

Vakhtang's other legacy is the one this record ends on. He spent his reign
strengthening the crown against the eristavi houses, and it did not hold: within
sixty years of his death the Sasanians were able to abolish the monarchy without
a war, because the nobility preferred a Persian governor to a Georgian king who
might tax them. The Armenian nobles had made the same calculation in 428 and the
Albanian ones in 510.

Three Caucasian monarchies removed by the same empire within a hundred and fifty
years, in each case with the local nobility's acquiescence, and in each case the
nobility surviving the crown. That is the region's argument, and this is the
third instance of it.
