---
title: First fight with me, and then with the Greeks
drafted_from: georgian-chronicle-bedrosian
phase: contraction
---

The Iberian court's own chronicle records the moment the kingdom stopped being
worth having, and it records it as a triumph.

A new shah had come to the throne in Iran and sent to Vakhtang requiring him to
act as guide for a Persian army going to war against Byzantium — which is what a
client king on that frontier was for. The chronicle has Vakhtang laying the
foundations of Tiflis at the time and building it vigorously, and then sending
the messenger back with two sentences. The first is a proverb: *blacksmith,
sharpen the sword so that I may cut off your head.* The second is the answer:
go and tell the man who sent you, first fight with me, and then with the Greeks
— for we spared and preserved you.

The country fortified itself. Vakhtang took his family up the Ujarma valley; his
son went east into the cliff-caves of Lopnia among people the chronicle
describes as a great multitude of barbarians who worshipped fire and water. The
shah came, destroyed the city of Kambechovani and the fortress of Cheremi,
reached Kakheti and camped on the river. Then Vakhtang came down against him.

The numbers the chronicle gives are 240,000 against 740,000, which is not
arithmetic but scale — a chronicler telling his readers that the whole of Iran
came, and that his king went out to meet it anyway. The Iberians broke them,
killed the shah's son, and had the shah begging before it stopped; and in the
middle of it an Iranian put a spear into Vakhtang's side. He took heart, the
chronicle says, and left the field triumphantly, killing a hundred and thirty
thousand of them on the way, and rode to Ujarma, and died there of the wound.

Everything after that sentence is the consequence. The emperor Zeno had set out
to help him and got as far as Speri before the news reached him, and turned
round and went home. The shah came back through Iberia, destroyed Tiflis and
Armazi and the country round Mtskheta, went on to fight the Byzantines
inconclusively, and returned through Kartli again. The kingdom had won its
battle and lost its two cities and its king in the same season, and had learnt
what it cost to say no.

The chronicle's next paragraphs are the whole of the sixth century and they are
about learning to say yes. Dachi rebuilt what the Iranians had destroyed. Two
reigns later the Ossetians came down and ravaged Kartli, and Parsman found that
Byzantium was too occupied in the west to help him — so he went to the Iranians
instead, with an offer: he would submit to them in the matter of taxation,
provided they used no force in the matter of faith and the Church. The Iranians
accepted it, and the chronicle records with evident relief that they in no way
harassed the Iberians about their religion.

Then one further sentence, easy to miss, which is the real terms of the bargain.
When the next catholicos died, the Iberians seated his successor without
ordination from Antioch — and thereafter, says the chronicle, they designated
their own catholicoi from the line of the *naxarars*, the noble houses.

A kingdom that had refused to march for a shah in 522 was paying him tribute
within a generation and had given up nothing except the ability to refuse. What
it kept, and quietly made self-perpetuating in the same years, was a church
staffed from its own aristocracy and answering to nobody outside the country.
When the monarchy was abolished later in the century, that was the institution
still standing.
