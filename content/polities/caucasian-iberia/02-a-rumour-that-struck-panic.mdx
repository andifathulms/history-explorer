Fifteen years before Rhadamistus murdered his own uncle for Armenia's
throne, his father Pharasmanes had already fought a pitched battle
over the same kingdom - on the same family's behalf, against the
Parthian empire directly, with Rome's tacit encouragement behind it.

## A throne contested by proxy

The emperor Tiberius, unwilling to fight Parthia's king Artabanus
directly, settled on an indirect strategy: he reconciled the Iberian
king Pharasmanes with his brother Mithridates and backed Mithridates's
bid to take Armenia for himself. Mithridates persuaded Pharasmanes to
back the scheme with force, Parthian officials were bribed with gold
to look the other way, and an Iberian army burst into Armenia and
seized the city of Artaxata outright. Artabanus answered by sending
his own son Orodes with the Parthian army to recover it, while
Pharasmanes brought in allies of his own - Albanian troops and hired
Sarmatian horsemen from north of the Caucasus, whose chiefs, Tacitus
notes dryly, took bribes from both sides at once in the usual fashion
of their people.

## A battle decided by a false rumor

The two armies met in a set battle in which the tactical distinctions
mattered as much as the outcome: the Parthians were purely cavalry,
built for the charge and the disciplined retreat, while the Iberians
and Albanians, hardened by life in wooded, difficult country, brought
real infantry to the field as well - a combination that let them pull
Parthian riders bodily from their horses in the close fighting. The
two commanders, Pharasmanes and Orodes, recognized each other in the
melee and rode straight at one another; Pharasmanes struck first,
denting Orodes's helmet with a blow that would likely have killed him
outright had his own horse's momentum not carried the Iberian king
past before he could finish the stroke. Orodes's own guards shielded
him and got him away, but word spread through the Parthian ranks -
mistaken, but believed - that their commander had been killed
outright. The panic that followed cost Artabanus the battle.

## A war Rome never had to fight

Artabanus marched into Armenia himself soon after with the full
strength of his kingdom, determined on revenge, and the Iberians,
fighting on ground they knew, held their own against him. What finally
forced Artabanus to abandon the campaign was not Iberian resistance
but the Roman governor of Syria, Vitellius, who mobilized his legions
and let a rumor spread that Rome itself meant to invade Mesopotamia -
a bluff serious enough that Artabanus's own subjects began deserting
a king they already resented as a tyrant. Tiberius had gotten exactly
what he wanted without committing a single Roman soldier to the field:
a client dynasty doing Rome's fighting for it, and a Parthian king
driven out of Armenia by an Iberian army acting, in every way that
mattered to Rome, as its own.
