title: Not that Iberia

The Greeks and Romans called eastern Georgia Iberia. They also called Spain
Iberia, and no connection between the two names has ever been established. The
country here is the one in the Caucasus.

The Georgians call it Kartli, and their own tradition — the *Kartlis
Tskhovreba*, assembled over centuries out of older material — gives the kingdom
a founder and, unusually, gives him something specific to have founded it
against.

In the chronicle's account Alexander crosses the Caucasus, takes the fortresses
of Kartli one by one over six months, razes the walls of Mtskheta, and leaves a
*patrik* named Azon to govern the country with a hundred thousand men. Azon
holds the four fortresses around the capital, takes tribute from the Ossetians
and the Leks and the Khazars, and turns out to be a bad ruler in the particular
way garrison commanders are: he orders his soldiers to kill any Georgian found
carrying a weapon, and then starts killing his own men too. Parnavaz raises the
country against him. A thousand of Azon's own Roman soldiers change sides.
Parnavaz kills him in battle at Artaani and takes the kingdom.

He then appoints eight *eristavis* and a *spaspet* over them, sets the
boundaries of each of their provinces, walls Mtskheta again, and sets up an idol
of Armazi on the mountain above the city and calls it after himself. The
chronicle says the arrangement was modelled on Persian practice, which is the
one part of the story nobody disputes.

Almost none of the rest is verifiable, and one piece of it is certainly wrong:
Alexander never campaigned in the Caucasus. The traditional foundation date is a
construction, worked backwards from his death. What the archaeology and the classical sources support is a
kingdom in the Kura valley from at least the second century BC, with a capital
at Mtskheta where the Aragvi joins the Kura, ruled by a dynasty and a nobility,
and positioned — like Armenia to its south and Albania to its east — on the
route through the Caucasus and between two empires.

So the kingdom's beginning is a span rather than a date: somewhere between the
foundation the Georgians remember and the kingdom the evidence shows. What is
worth keeping from the tradition is not the chronology but the shape it
remembers — a garrison government at Mtskheta under a foreign appointee, holding
the fortresses and taxing the neighbours, replaced by a native dynasty that took
over its machinery and ran it.

The structure is the one all three Caucasian countries share. The *eristavi*
were provincial lords, the *aznauri* the noble class below them, and both held
their positions by inheritance and supplied the king's forces from their own
districts. Toumanoff's book is about that system across all three, and the
consequence is worth stating early: a king in this arrangement presides. He does
not command.
