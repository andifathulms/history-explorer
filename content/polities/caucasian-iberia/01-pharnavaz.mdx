title: Not that Iberia

The Greeks and Romans called eastern Georgia Iberia. They also called Spain
Iberia, and no connection between the two names has ever been established. The
country here is the one in the Caucasus.

The Georgians call it Kartli, and their own historiographical tradition — the
*Kartlis Tskhovreba*, assembled centuries later out of older material — gives
the kingdom a founder: Pharnavaz I, around 302 BC, who is said to have thrown
off Macedonian control after Alexander's death, established the cult of the god
Armazi at Mtskheta, and organised the country into districts under *eristavi*.

Almost none of that is verifiable and the traditional foundation date is a
construction. What the archaeology and the classical sources support is a
kingdom in the Kura valley from at least the second century BC, with a capital
at Mtskheta where the Aragvi joins the Kura, ruled by a dynasty and a nobility,
and positioned — like Armenia to its south and Albania to its east — on the
route through the Caucasus and between two empires.

So the kingdom's beginning is a span rather than a date: somewhere between the
foundation the Georgians remember and the kingdom the evidence shows.

The structure is the one all three Caucasian countries share. The *eristavi*
were provincial lords, the *aznauri* the noble class below them, and both held
their positions by inheritance and supplied the king's forces from their own
districts. Toumanoff's book is about that system across all three, and the
consequence is worth stating early: a king in this arrangement presides. He does
not command.
