By the early thirteenth century Tai chiefs across the upper Chao
Phraya basin had grown troublesome to Cambodian rule, encouraged by
the example of independent Tai states already founded further
north. The Cambodian king sent a general, Khlon Lamphong, to
restore order, but two allied Tai chiefs, Khun Bang Klang Tao and
Khun Pha Mueang, met him in pitched battle and defeated him outright.

## A king crowned in the enemy's own capital

Following their victory, the two chiefs entered Sukhothai itself,
then the northern capital of Cambodian rule in the region, and Khun
Bang Klang Tao was consecrated king there with the title Sri
Indraditya - a title that had originally belonged to his own ally,
Khun Pha Mueang, conferred by the Cambodian king himself. It was,
in effect, a kingdom founded with the enemy's own honours. The
capture so captured the Tai imagination afterward that later
generations attached to Sri Indraditya the attributes of a legendary
hero, Phra Ruang, a name later applied indiscriminately to every
king of Sukhothai who followed him.

## A war won by a prince on an elephant

Sri Indraditya spent his reign extending his new kingdom at
Cambodia's expense and at his western neighbours', a task helped
enormously by a wave of Tai refugees who fled south into Siam after
Kublai Khan's Mongol armies conquered the Nanchao kingdom of Yunnan
in 1254. An inscription records a war with the Prince of Chot, who
had attempted to seize the town of Tak; in the fighting Sri
Indraditya's third son, the young Prince Ram Khamhaeng, distinguished
himself in single combat against the Prince of Chot, both men mounted
on elephants, and forced his opponent to flee the field with his
entire army. It was a preview of the reign that would follow.
