Sri Indraditya's eldest son died young, and his second son, King
Ban Mueang, reigned only briefly before his death around 1275
brought his younger brother to the throne: Ram Khamhaeng, who over a
reign of more than forty years earned the title "the Great." Under
his direction the struggling state his father had carved out of
Cambodian territory grew into a wide and powerful kingdom. At his
death, cities and districts as far as Phrae, Nan, Luang Prabang,
Nakhon Si Thammarat, Tenasserim, Martaban and Pegu were subject or
tributary to him - a reach that carried Sukhothai's influence all
the way to the Bay of Bengal, even if, as the source notes, not
every one of these distant territories submitted to his effective
control.

## The bell of justice

Ram Khamhaeng is remembered as much for how he ruled as for how far.
He is said to have hung a bell at Sukhothai for any of his subjects
who had suffered wrong to ring, summoning the king himself to hear
the complaint in person; no one, the tradition holds, ever appealed
to him in vain. When he once wronged a weaker neighbour and was
shown to be in error, he apologized rather than seeking to punish the
man who had proven him wrong - conduct his own chroniclers took as
proof that his greatness rested on more than conquest alone.

## An alphabet of his own

Ram Khamhaeng's most lasting achievement, however, was neither
military nor judicial. Until his reign, Siam had written with
various forms of the Cambodian alphabet; Ram Khamhaeng altered and
adapted those characters to suit the sounds of the Tai language,
introducing the new script in 1283. In its essential features it is
the same alphabet still used to write Thai today, and it spread with
his own conquests across the kingdom he had built - the clearest
mark any king of Sukhothai left on the country that grew from it.
