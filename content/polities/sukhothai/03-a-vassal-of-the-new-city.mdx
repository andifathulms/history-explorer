The kingdom Ram Khamhaeng left behind did not long survive him
intact. His son and successor, King Loethai, proved unable to
defend what his father had won: the King of Pegu threw off his
allegiance and seized Tavoy and Tenasserim almost as soon as Loethai
took the throne, and an attempt to recover them in 1330 failed
outright. Closer to home, a new power was rising at Suwannaphumi, or
Ut'ong, under an energetic prince descended from the princes of
Chiang Saen; before Loethai's reign ended, this prince had already
annexed a large part of Sukhothai's own former dominions. In 1350 he
founded a new city at Ayutthaya and crowned himself King Rama
Thibodi I.

## A pious king who did not fight to recover his kingdom

Loethai's own son, Thammaraja Lutai, inherited a kingdom reduced to
little more than Sukhothai itself and a handful of nearby towns. He
made no attempt to recover what had been lost, devoting his reign
instead to temples, monasteries and Buddhist images brought from
contacts with Ceylon, to roads and canals linking his shrunken
capital with its remaining towns, and, by 1361, to becoming a
Buddhist monk himself. Later tradition remembered him as a ruler
whose "mercy and charity were as boundless as the waters of the
ocean," under whom no man in his kingdom was a slave.

## The end of independence

That peace did not survive Thammaraja Lutai's own reign. His son,
Thammaraja II, held the throne for eight years before he was forced
to accept vassal status under the newer, stronger kingdom his
grandfather's rival had founded at Ayutthaya. By the source's own
reckoning, this marked the end of the independent Tai kingdom of
Sukhothai, 132 years after Sri Indraditya's coronation in the city
his allies had won from Cambodia. Sukhothai's kings continued to
govern locally as Ayutthaya's vassals for another half-century, a
diminished afterword to the kingdom that had once reached the Bay of
Bengal and given the Tai language its own alphabet.
