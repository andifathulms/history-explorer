In 1723 the Dzungar khan took Turkestan — the old capital of the Kazakh khans, and
the place where most of them were buried — along with Tashkent and Sayram, and
reduced the Great Horde and part of the Middle to obedience.

## The flight

What followed is the event Kazakh memory keeps above all others. The greater part
of the Middle Horde retired towards Samarkand; part of the Great Horde and a small
section of the Middle went towards Khujand; the Little Horde went to Bukhara and
Khiva. Famine and want pursued the fugitives.

It was not a defeat in the field so much as the loss of the ground a pastoral
economy needs: winter pasture, the burial towns, the caravan line. Once those were
gone the hordes had to go somewhere, and everywhere they could go belonged to
somebody.

## The one effort

The disasters patched up their quarrels for a while. A general assembly decided on
a united effort to throw the enemy out of the old Kazakh country. Abu'l-Khayr,
chief of the Little Horde, was made commander-in-chief, and a white horse was
sacrificed as the pledge of mutual faith.

They advanced against the Dzungars and beat them in several encounters.

Then, afraid that the Dzungar chief would take a terrible revenge, they decided to
leave the country anyway.

That sentence is the end of the khanate. Not the defeats — they had survived
defeats for two hundred and sixty years — but a victory that nobody believed could
be held.

## Where they went

The Great Horde alone stayed behind, under the Dzungars.

The Little Horde moved west, driving the Volga Kalmyks in front of it, and settled
between the Emba and the Yaik. The Middle Horde went north as far as the Or and
the Ui, ejecting a large number of Bashkirs, who did not forget it: the dispossessed
Bashkirs and the Cossacks of the Ural were a thorn in the Kazakhs' flank for
generations afterwards.

Three hordes, on three separate frontiers, each with its own khan, its own enemies
and its own reasons to look for a patron. Within a decade all three were looking
at the same one.
