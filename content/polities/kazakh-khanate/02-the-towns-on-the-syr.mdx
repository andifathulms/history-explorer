A horde on open pasture can be beaten and re-form. What turns it into a state is
having something it cannot move, and for the Kazakhs that was the line of walled
towns along the middle Syr Darya — Sighnaq, Sauran, Otrar, Yasi — where the steppe
met the caravan roads and the grain.

## Burunduk

Kerei's son Burunduk became overchief, and Qasim, son of Janibek, was submissive
to him as his father had been. Under them the war with Abu'l-Khayr's grandson
Muhammad Shaybani ran for twenty years up and down that line.

The pattern repeats: a town changes hands, a siege is raised, a peace is made, a
daughter is married. Burunduk took Mahmud, Shaybani's brother, prisoner at Sauran
and lost him again on the road. He besieged Otrar, fought fiercely under its
walls, agreed to a peace and went home. He allied with the khan of Moghulistan
against Otrar and retired from it again. He gave a princess to Shaybani's son as
brother-in-law. Then it started over.

What that produced, over a generation, was a Kazakh presence in Turkestan that
could not be dislodged, and a reputation.

## Qasim

By 1509 Burunduk was khan *de jure* and all the authority was virtually in Qasim's
hands, and the Kazakhs could muster two hundred thousand men.

That winter Shaybani sent a force into their country from his quarters at Quruq.
It heard a report that Qasim Khan was coming and turned back — and the panic went
through the whole Uzbek army. *Nothing was attended to*, Mirza Haydar says. The
drum beat for retreat and the army reached Samarkand broken and scattered at the
end of the winter, whereupon Shaybani withdrew to Khurasan, where within months he
was killed by the Safavids.

A rumour of Qasim's approach emptying a field army is the measure of where things
had got to. Burunduk was eventually expelled and died in exile at Samarkand, and
the throne passed to Janibek's family.

## What Qasim did with it

He brought the Dasht-i Kipchak under his absolute control in a manner that no one,
except Jochi Khan himself, had ever done before. His army exceeded a thousand
thousand.

That figure is not a census; it is a historian saying that nobody had counted and
that it was the largest force between the Volga and the Tian Shan. What is
concrete is that the keys of Sayram were brought to him by its people, that he
took Taraz, and that he marched on Tashkent with an army described only as
countless, spent one night outside the citadel, and turned back — plundering
everything around it as he went.

He did not take cities. He did not need to. He had the pasture, the herds, the
men, and the ability to make a walled town's owner wonder whether it was worth
keeping.
