Mirza Haydar Dughlat, writing the *Tarikh-i-Rashidi* in Kashmir in the 1540s, gave
the Kazakhs their origin story in a paragraph, and it is one of the few foundation
accounts in Inner Asia that is neither a genealogy nor a legend but a piece of
plain political narrative.

## Two princes leave

Abu'l-Khayr, of the house of Shiban, held full power in the Dasht-i Kipchak in
the middle of the fifteenth century and had been at war with the sultans of
Jochi's line. Two of them, Kerei and Janibek, fled from him into Moghulistan.

Esen Buqa, the khan there, received them with great honour and handed over to
them Kozy-Bashi near the Chu, on the western limit of his country, where they
dwelt in peace and content. They were useful to him: they sat on the frontier he
could not garrison, and they had a quarrel with the man on the other side of it.

## The horde forms

Then Abu'l-Khayr died, and the ulus of the Uzbeks fell into confusion, and
constant strife arose among them. Most of the people joined the party of Kerei
and Janibek.

They numbered, Mirza Haydar says, about two hundred thousand persons, and they
received the name Uzbek-Kazak. The Kazak sultans began to reign in the year 870 —
1465 or 1466 — *but God knows best*.

## Why they were called that

The historian explains the name where he first uses it, and the explanation is
not flattering and not disputed. Since they had first separated themselves from
the mass of their people, and for some time had been in an indigent and wandering
state, they got the name of Kazak, which clung to them ever since.

The word means a man who has broken off — a free-lance, a masterless rider, a
fugitive from his own prince. It was a condition before it was a people. Every
steppe dynasty had produced men who lived that way for a season; what was new was
a group that did it, kept doing it, took in everyone else who did it, and turned
the word into a nation's name.

## What kind of state it was

Not an accident of pasture. It was a Chinggisid khanate with a Jochid dynasty and
a claim on the whole Kipchak, founded on the proposition that the Shibanids had
usurped it. The long war with the Uzbeks that follows for the next century is not
a border quarrel. Both sides held that the same country was theirs, and they had
been one people until Kerei and Janibek walked out of it.
