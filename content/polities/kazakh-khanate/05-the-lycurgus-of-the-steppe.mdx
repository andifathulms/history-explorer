Mirza Haydar buried the Kazakhs in the 1540s. Within twenty years they were again
the dominant power between the Syr Darya and the Irtysh, and they stayed so for a
century and a half.

## Haqnazar

Qasim's son Haqnazar put it back together. Haydar Razi calls him lord of the
Kazakhs and the Kirghiz; the ruler of Aksu and Moghulistan fell in a struggle
with him. The Uzbek court history has Abdullah Khan of Bukhara marching to the
Talas to deal with the Kazakh chiefs and finding them willing to keep their
treaty, receiving their envoys with robes of honour, and making over four towns in
Turkestan to them as a token of good will.

He appears in the Russian archives too. An envoy sent to the Nogai in 1569 reports
the Kazakh hordes of Haqnazar, Shigay and Jalim; in 1577 a Russian agent writes
that the Kazakhs under Haqnazar were at war with the Nogai but wished to be on
friendly terms with Moscow.

He was killed about 1580, after an attempt to seize Baba Sultan of Tashkent went
wrong — the intended victim had been warned, and the valley, in the court
historian's phrase, blossomed forth in tulips with the blood of the men sent to
take him.

## Shigay and Tevekkel

Shigay, who succeeded, was already very old — his father had died about 1503 and
he was still campaigning in 1582. He met Abdullah Khan at Kara Tau on the Syr
Darya, was appointed governor of Khujand, and sent his son Tevekkel off with the
Uzbek army, where the young man distinguished himself at a shooting match in the
khan's garden, firing at silver and gold balls hung on a pole.

Tevekkel took the title of khan himself in due course. The dynasty was back in the
Syr Darya towns; Turkestan, where the khans were buried, was the seat.

## Tauke

The last khan of the united Kazakhs was Tauke, and his reputation among his own
people is unusual for a nomad ruler: he is remembered not for conquest but for
law. Levchine calls him the Lycurgus and the Draco of the Kazakhs.

He introduced peace among them and ended the quarrels of their clans. His sagacity
and equity gave him great influence. He united the weak tribes with one another
and compelled the strong to respect them. He reigned at Turkestan as his ancestors
had, and in 1688 the ruler of Bukhara was still sending officers to Tashkent to
negotiate with him, which is a measure of where Kazakh authority reached.

It is to him that the division of the Kazakhs into three hordes is most reasonably
traced. He appointed three deputies to manage their affairs — Tole for the Great
Horde, Kazybek for the Middle, Aiteke for the Little. Under him these were
administrative divisions and the people remained directly subject to the khan.

## The war that undid it

Tauke spent his life fighting the Kalmyks, and it went badly.

In 1698 the Dzungar ruler Tsewang Rabtan wrote to the emperor of China setting out
his grievances against him: that Galdan had captured Tauke's son and sent him to
the Dalai Lama, that Tauke had asked him to intercede, that he had done so and
sent the son home with an escort of five hundred men — and that Tauke had put them
all to death along with a Dzungar grandee, his wife, children and people, and kept
a hundred tents. He had also waylaid a Torghut chief's son and plundered a Russian
caravan.

The war that followed was disastrous. The Kazakhs were driven out of their old
quarters and largely broken up. In Tauke's later years his authority grew weaker
and the three deputies he had appointed began to act on their own; Howorth dates
the division of the Kazakhs into three substantive hordes, each under its own
princes, from exactly this point.

In 1718 Tauke, Kaip and Abu'l-Khayr, all styling themselves khan, wrote to the
governor of Siberia offering to put themselves under Russian protection. Tauke died
about that year.
