In 919 of the Hijra — 1513 — Sultan Said Khan of Moghulistan rode out to the
Kazakh camp to ask Qasim to attack Tashkent a second time. Mirza Haydar was his
cousin and heard the story from him for the rest of his life. It is the only
extended portrait of a Kazakh khan from the inside, and almost nothing in it is
about war.

## The reception

They met at a place in Moghulistan called Jud. Qasim was nearer seventy than
sixty; the khan was under thirty and in the full vigour of youth.

Qasim begged to be excused, on the plea of age, from riding out to receive his
guest, and instead commanded all his sultans to go and kneel before him. Some of
them were fifty and sixty years old — Janish, Sabash, Mumash, Jan Haydar, Karish
and thirty or forty others, all sultans of the race of Jochi. When the two oldest
bowed the khan rose; when the rest bowed he stayed seated.

Then Qasim came forward himself, with a courtesy the khan never forgot. Whenever
he spoke of him afterwards he said Qasim was a most upright and worthy man, and
told the story again.

## What he had to give

> We are men of the desert, and here there is nothing in the way of riches or
> formalities. Our most costly possessions are our horses, our favourite food
> their flesh, our most enjoyable drink their milk and the products of it. In our
> country are no gardens or buildings. Our chief recreation is inspecting our
> herds.

So they went and looked at the herds. Qasim said he had two horses worth the
whole of the rest, and had them brought; the khan said in later life that he had
never seen such animals. Qasim said that men of the desert depend for their lives
on their horses and that he trusted no others than these two — and that since his
guest was his esteemed guest, he should take whichever seemed better and leave the
other.

The khan chose the one called Ughlan Turuq. Qasim picked out several more from the
droves and gave him those as well.

## The kumis

Then he offered a cup of kumis, saying it was one of their forms of hospitality
and that he would count it a great favour. The khan had lately renounced all
intoxicating drink and said so: how could he break his vow?

Qasim's answer is the whole scene in miniature. He had already explained that
mare's milk and its products were their favourite drink and that this was the
pleasantest of them. If his guest would not accept what he offered he was totally
at a loss to know what to give in its place in performance of the duties of
hospitality. Years must pass before such an honourable guest again entered the
house of his humble host, and now he was incapable of entertaining him. How could
he make reparation?

Then he hung his head, and sorrow appeared on his face.

The khan drank it to the dregs. They spent twenty days drinking together.

## The refusal

When the talking got round to the campaign, Qasim declined it — flatly and on
professional grounds. To attack the Shaybanids at this season would involve great
difficulties; men of the desert do not think of winter campaigns unless ordered
to; it is impossible; an expedition is not to be thought of at this time. Summer
was ending and the Kazakhs were already moving to winter quarters. He dismissed
his army and, with the utmost courtesy, sent his guest home.

Sultan Said went back to Andijan pleased. A learned man made a chronogram of it:
*Ashti-i-Kazak* — Peace with the Kazakh.

An old man on the open steppe, with more horses and more men than anyone else
between two mountain ranges, giving away the better half of what he owns, refusing
the war he has been asked for, and sending the disappointed party home flattered.
That is what the height of this khanate looked like.
