The submissions came one at a time, and the dismantling took a hundred and fifty
years, which is why it is hard to name a year in which the Kazakh khanate ended.

## Submission

Pressed between the Dzungars behind and the Bashkirs and Ural Cossacks in front,
the hordes turned to Russia. Shemyaka of the Middle Horde swore the oath to the
Empress Anne in 1732, which his own people resented enough to start disturbances;
he then led a raid on the Bashkirs in flat breach of it and afterwards sent envoys
to apologise. Patents of investiture were prepared for him in 1734 and never
delivered, because he died first. The document survives: *to our subject Shemyaka
Khan and the elders and army of the Kirghiz Kazakhs of the Middle Horde*, reciting
his recent oath and his backslidings, and offering him pardon.

Abu'l-Khayr of the Little Horde, the most important of the chiefs, spent those
years pressing the others to come in. In 1740 Abul Makhmet Khan and Ablai Sultan
and many elders of the Middle Horde took the oath at Orenburg.

They were not conquered. They were surrounded, and they signed.

## Abolition

The khanship went next, and the manner of it is worth recording.

In 1785 Baron Igelström, the new governor at Orenburg, decided to abolish the
office of khan of the Little Horde altogether and replace it with three Russian
administrators. He issued his summons for a general assembly not to the khan but
to the sultans and elders, which by itself destroyed what influence the khan had
left. When the assembly met not one sultan attended, and it was chaired by a
batyr named Syrym, an enemy of the khan's, who argued that there was no need of a
khan at all and that merit ought to weigh before birth — and demanded that
Abu'l-Khayr's family be barred from the khanship for ever. The Russians partially
agreed.

The immediate results were reported as excellent: more cattle at the frontier
fairs in 1786 and 1787, fewer Russian prisoners taken, more released, and
forty-five thousand Kazakh families wintering west of the Yaik without
depredations.

It did not hold — rival claimants, Syrym's own revolt, a restoration, a fresh
abolition. But the direction never reversed. Special tribunals called *raspravas*
were set up to handle Kazakh affairs, with salaried officials who swore allegiance
to the empress. By the 1820s Shirgazy still lived but no longer governed, and the
Little Horde was three districts under sultans-regent who were, Schuyler says,
either Russian creatures without influence or inclined to revolt themselves.
Neither they nor the annual military expeditions from Orenburg could keep order in
the steppe.

## Kenesary

The last Chinggisid rising was led by Kenesary Qasimov of the Middle Horde in the
late 1830s and early 1840s. For six years he kept the Russian authorities in
continual alarm across the Siberian steppe, with a section of the Little Horde
joining him. In 1844 he was driven into the mountains, took refuge among the Kara
Kirghiz, and was killed fighting them.

Forts followed the revolt as they always did — Turgai and Irgiz in 1847, Karabutak
in 1848, Raimsk on the lower Syr Darya in 1847 within reach of Khiva and Khoqand.
In 1869 the last of the tribal structure went: the Little Horde was divided into
the districts of Uralsk and Turgai under Russian military commanders, with only
the aul elders elected. The steppe rose again for two years over it.

## The prince with the French novel

In 1873 an American travelling to Turkestan shared part of the road with a Kazakh
named Chingis, son of the last khan of the Bukey Horde, who on his father's death
had been given the Russian title of prince. He was a good Muslim, just back from
the pilgrimage to Mecca, and on his way to spend the summer on his estates in the
government of Samara. Schuyler thought him a cultivated gentleman. He was deep in
a French novel most of the time.

Four hundred years after two princes rode out of Abu'l-Khayr's country with
nothing, and were called fugitives for it, that is where the line came to rest.
