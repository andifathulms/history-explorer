Between Tevekkel's death and Tauke's fame sit two khans who held Turkestan
for the better part of four decades and left almost nothing behind but a
string of other men's wars.

## Ishim

Tevekkel died of a wound at Tashkent in 1598, and his brother Ishim
succeeded him as over-chief of the Kazakhs. What survives of his own
reign is mostly what other people's chronicles happened to record about
him in passing: five thousand Kazakh horsemen drawn into a Bukharan
succession war in 1611, a defeated rival Tashkent khan he had killed in
1627, a Dzungar war in 1635 that cost him his own son as a prisoner and,
by most reckonings, cost him his life soon after.

The one episode that gives him a face is smaller than any of that. Around
1625 a young Chinggisid fugitive named Abulghazi - later khan of Khiva
and a historian in his own right - fled to Turkestan and lived under
Ishim's protection for three months. When the khan of Tashkent visited,
Ishim presented the boy to him with a line his own chroniclers thought
worth keeping: *never before has a prince of this house sought shelter
with us, while many of our princes have sought shelter with them.* It is
a small claim, made in passing, by a khan whose own people had gone from
refugees themselves, less than a century earlier, to a house other
refugees now measured themselves against.

## Yehangir

Ishim's son Yehangir inherited a state still fighting the same war his
father had lost his own son to. Captured by the Dzungar leader Baatur
Khungtaidshi in the early 1640s, Yehangir was eventually released and
went back to raiding the Kalmyks who held him - and in the summer of
1643, with only six hundred men against Baatur's fifty thousand, he dug
in between a line of hills, let the Dzungar army commit to the attack,
then hit it from the rear with firearms the Kalmyks had no answer for.
Ten thousand of Baatur's men were killed in the rout. A second Dzungar
campaign the following year was talked down before it started, by a
Khoshut chief who happened to be Yehangir's own friend.

He is not recorded doing much else. Levchine says only that he lived, as
his father and grandfather had, at Turkestan.

## What they held together

Neither khan is remembered the way Tauke would be. Between them,
though, they kept the Kazakh khanship continuous through four decades in
which the state that had produced it had already, within living memory,
been declared not to exist at all - and they kept it at Turkestan, the
same town, under the same institutions, for a son who would inherit both
intact enough to be called a lawgiver rather than a survivor.
