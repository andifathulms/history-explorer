Hideyoshi's most lasting achievements were not fought for. Between
1586 and 1596 his Minister of Agriculture, Nagatsuka Masaiye,
carried out a survey of the whole empire's land - the measure every
later koku assessment of a daimyo's fief, and every later Tokugawa tax
register, would be built on. It gave the regime, for the first time
since the Ōnin War had broken the old provincial administration, an
actual figure for what the country's farmland was worth and who held
it.

## A disarmament dressed as an offering

The companion measure was announced as an act of piety. Around 1587 or
1588 Hideyoshi ordered every farmer, mechanic and commoner in the
country to surrender his sword and any other weapon to a collection
point, to be melted down for the ironwork of a colossal new Buddha he
was having built in Kyoto.

The Jesuit missionary Luís Fróis, writing at the time and no admirer
of Hideyoshi's, saw the mechanism plainly and set it down in a letter
of his own:

> He is astutely planning to possess himself of all the iron in Japan,
> having ordered that all the mechanics and common people should leave
> off wearing swords and carrying any other sort of weapons... and
> bring them to a place indicated for the purpose to be converted into
> the iron work necessary in the temple. Thus the populace is
> disarmed, and he the more secure in his arbitrary dominion... Now he
> is depriving the people of their arms under the pretext of devotion
> to Religion.

No such edict, Fróis added, had ever been seen or attempted in Japan
before - not because earlier rulers lacked the will, but because none
had held enough uncontested authority over the whole country to
attempt disarming its farmers at all.

## What the two measures did together

Separately, a land register and a sword collection were administrative
housekeeping. Together they did something neither could alone: the
survey told the regime exactly what every parcel of farmland in the
country was worth and who was responsible for its taxes, while the
sword hunt made sure the people who worked that land had no weapon
left to resist the assessment with. A peasantry that had spent a
century arming itself in a country with no functioning central
government was, within a decade, both counted and disarmed by one that
had one again.

The distinction the survey drew between who held a sword and who held
a hoe hardened, over the following years, into something closer to a
caste line than an administrative convenience - the same separation of
warrior and farmer that the Tokugawa order would spend the next two
and a half centuries formally enforcing. Hideyoshi did not invent the
idea of an armed samurai class set apart from an unarmed peasantry. He
was the first ruler with the reach to make it a fact on the ground
everywhere at once, using the same instrument in both directions: one
survey to know what the country was worth, one collection to make sure
only his own soldiers could take it by force.
