The man who would eventually rule Japan more completely than any
figure since Yoritomo began life without even a settled name. His
mother, believing she had conceived by the sun itself, called him
Hiyoshi - "good sun." Others called him Ko-chiku, "small boy," and
later Saru-matsu, "monkey-pine" - a nickname that would follow him,
transformed into open insult, for the rest of his career. He enlisted
as a common soldier under yet another assumed name, and only built the
name history would remember him by, Hashiba, as an adult general,
joining two syllables borrowed from two of his own subordinate
commanders.

## Nobunaga's death answered within days

Hideyoshi was already one of Nobunaga's most capable generals,
fighting Mōri's forces in the west, when news reached him that his own
lord had been murdered by a treacherous captain, Akechi, in 1582.
Rather than continue his western campaign, Hideyoshi made rapid terms
with Mōri, marched his army back to Kyoto, and destroyed Akechi within
days - so quickly that a native proverb still records the usurper's
reign in three words: Akechi ruled three days.

## A field of rivals, and one who moved faster than the rest

Nobunaga's death had left his own territory divided among sons,
generals and rival claimants, none of whom could immediately settle who
would inherit his unfinished project of unification. Hideyoshi, having
already avenged his lord in the most visible and popular way possible,
held every advantage in the scramble that followed. When Nobunaga's
third son and his chief general, Shibata, tried to resist Hideyoshi's
own rising authority, Hideyoshi marched into Mino, defeated them, and
pursued Shibata into Echizen, where the defeated general burned his own
castle around himself, his family, and his retainers rather than
surrender. The mocking nickname "monkey" that had followed Hideyoshi
since childhood would soon apply, with equal derision, to a man sitting
in an office no one of his birth had ever been permitted to hold.
