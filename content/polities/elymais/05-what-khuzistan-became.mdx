The kingdom had no history worth the name. The country it sat in had rather a
lot of one, and almost all of it happened after 224.

## A Roman emperor's weir

In 260 the emperor Valerian was captured by Shapur I, and the Persian historians
say that during his seven years as a prisoner he was set to build a dam.

The Great Weir stands below Tustar — Shushtar, on the river the Arabs called
the Dujayl and Persians the Karun — and the Arabic writers counted it one of the
wonders of the world. It is close to a mile across. The bed of the river west of
the town was paved; the weir held the water high enough that part of the flow
could be diverted above the town into an artificial channel running east, which
irrigated the country it crossed and rejoined the river many miles downstream.
Muqaddasi says a bridge of boats ran across the top of it carrying the high road
west into Iraq.

The remains are still there.

## The hospital

Eight leagues north-west of Shushtar are the ruins the map now calls Shahabad,
and they are Gundeshapur — Junday Sabur — which was the Sasanian capital of
Khuzistan.

It is remembered for its medical school, and the shape of what happened to that
school is the useful part. The physician associated with it in the Arabic
tradition is Bukhtishu, a Christian, whose sons and grandsons after him were
personal physicians to Abbasid caliphs from al-Mansur to Harun al-Rashid — a
Christian medical dynasty from a Sasanian provincial capital, running the health
of the Islamic empire for four generations.

The town itself did poorly. It exported sugar to Khurasan and beyond, and it
made embroideries, and it grew rice; but by the tenth century Muqaddasi already
describes Gundeshapur as falling into ruin from Kurdish raiding. Ya'qub ibn
Layth the Saffarid made it his capital and died there in 878, and his tomb was
shown in the town. Mustawfi in the fourteenth century still calls it populous
and famous for its sugar cane. Today it is an almost uninhabited ruin.

## Daniel in the riverbed

And then there is Susa, which by then was called Sus: a populous medieval town
in a district full of cities, growing sugar cane and oranges, famous for raw
silk, with a fortress and a congregational mosque built on round columns.

The tomb of the prophet Daniel was there. Tradition held that the grave had been
made in the bed of the Karkhah river running past the town, with a fine mosque
on the near bank marking the spot; Mustawfi, later, describes the tomb as
standing on dry ground west of the city, and adds that in Daniel's honour nobody
would touch a fish in that river.

## The point of listing all this

Elymais held that country for three hundred and seventy years and left a coin
series, some rock reliefs, and two dead Seleucid kings.

The four centuries after it produced a dam built by a captured Roman emperor, a
medical school that supplied Baghdad with its doctors, a sugar industry
exporting east, and a river whose fish were protected because a Hebrew prophet
was supposed to be buried under it.

A polity is not the same thing as a place, and the place is usually the more
interesting of the two.
