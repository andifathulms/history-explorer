Elymais is the Greek form of Elam, and the name is three thousand years older
than the kingdom on this page.

Elam was one of the oldest states in the world: Susa on the plain and Anshan in
the highlands, writing in its own language in its own script, contemporary with
Sumer and Akkad and periodically at war with both, and finally destroyed by
Ashurbanipal in the 640s BC in a campaign the Assyrian reliefs record with
unusual relish.

This paragraph used to end by saying that the corpus did not carry Elam and that
it was one of the Ancient Near East's larger absences. It carries two Elams now:
the Middle Elamite kingdom that sacked Babylon in 1158 and took Hammurabi's stele
to Susa, and the Neo-Elamite kingdom that Ashurbanipal destroyed. The third one —
the Elam of Awan, Shimashki and the sukkalmah, which ended the Third Dynasty of
Ur in 2004 — is still not here, and the Middle Elamite record says so on its own
last page.

What is on this page is what the name came back as. After the Achaemenids, whose
winter capital was at Susa, and after Alexander and the Seleucids, the highland
country above the Susiana plain produced a kingdom again. Around 147 BC, as the
Seleucid empire on this site's neighbouring record was losing Iran to the
Parthians, a man called Kamnaskires took the royal title in Elymais.

It is the sixth and last of the vassal kingdoms this site's Parthian record
lists as the components the empire was assembled from — Persis, Elymais,
Characene, Adiabene, Media Atropatene and Armenia — and with this page all six
are in the corpus.

The kingdom lasted three hundred and seventy years, most of it as somebody's
client, and produced no narrative whatever. There is no Elymaean history and no
chronicle. What there is, is coins, some rock reliefs on outcrops in the Zagros,
scattered notices in Greek and Latin writers, and a reputation for being worth
robbing.
