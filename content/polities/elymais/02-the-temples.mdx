---
title: Worth robbing
drafted_from: wiesehofer-1996

The sanctuaries of Elymais were famous in antiquity for one reason: they had
been accumulating dedications for a very long time, in country that had been a
religious centre since the Bronze Age, and nobody had emptied them.

Antiochus III — this corpus's Seleucid record's peak ruler, the man who
recovered the east in a seven-year anabasis and was then broken by Rome at
Magnesia — needed money for the indemnity Rome had imposed. In 187 BC he
attempted to plunder a temple of Bel in Elymais and was killed there, with his
escort, by the local population. It is one of the more abrupt ends to a reign in
this corpus: a king who had marched to the Hindu Kush and back died robbing a
shrine in the Zagros because he could not pay a Roman fine.

Antiochus IV did the same thing twenty-three years later with the same result at
one remove. Polybius and the books of Maccabees have him attempting a temple in
Elymais, being driven off by the inhabitants, and dying shortly afterwards at
Gabae in 164 — and the Jewish tradition, which had every reason to want his
death explained, connected the two directly. This site's Seleucid record carries
his suppression of Jewish practice as a turning point; the sequence in which he
dies fleeing a failed temple robbery is the same fiscal desperation seen from the
other end of the empire.

Then in 139 BC Mithridates I of Parthia took Elymais and plundered the
sanctuaries himself. He was not driven off. That is this record's only turning
point, and it did two things: it made the kingdom a Parthian vassal for the
remaining three and a half centuries of its life, and it removed the thing that
had made it interesting.

There is a coding problem in all of this and the record states it. This page's
`revenue_basis` reads `land-tax`, for the irrigated Susiana plain, and the temple
treasuries are in a comment rather than a value. An accumulated hoard is a stock
and not an income; the vocabulary has `mining` for extraction and `tribute` for
transfers and `land-tax` for agriculture, and none of them describes a kingdom
whose notable asset was three centuries of other people's offerings sitting in a
building. What the treasuries actually were, in fiscal terms, was a reason for
other people to invade.
