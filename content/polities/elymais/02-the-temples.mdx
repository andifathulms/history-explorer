---
title: Worth robbing
drafted_from: wiesehofer-1996
phase: peak
---

The sanctuaries of Elymais were famous in antiquity for one reason: they had
been accumulating dedications for a very long time, in country that had been a
religious centre since the Bronze Age, and nobody had emptied them.

Antiochus III — the greatest of the Seleucids, the man who recovered the east in
a seven-year progress and was then broken by Rome at Magnesia — needed money for
the indemnity Rome had imposed. In 187 BC he attempted to plunder a temple of
Bel in Elymais and was killed there, with his escort, by the local population.
A king who had marched to the Hindu Kush and back died robbing a shrine in the
Zagros because he could not pay a Roman fine.

Antiochus IV did the same thing twenty-three years later with the same result at
one remove. Polybius and the books of Maccabees have him attempting a temple in
Elymais, being driven off by the inhabitants, and dying shortly afterwards at
Gabae in 164 — and the Jewish tradition, which had every reason to want his
death explained, connected the two directly. This is the king who had banned
Jewish practice in Judaea and provoked the Maccabean revolt, and the same fiscal
desperation is visible at both ends of his empire: a treasury short of money
looking for religious wealth to take.

Then in 139 BC Mithridates I of Parthia took Elymais and plundered the
sanctuaries himself. He was not driven off. It is the hinge of the kingdom's history and it did two
things: it made Elymais a Parthian vassal for the remaining three and a half
centuries of its life, and it removed the thing that had made it worth
attacking.

It is worth being clear about what those treasuries were, because it is easy to
mistake them for wealth. A kingdom lives on income — on what the land yields
each year, or what passes through and can be taxed. Elymais lived on the
irrigated Susiana plain like everybody else in the region. The temple hoards
were not income at all. They were a stock, accumulated over centuries by people
giving things away, sitting in a building, producing nothing.

In fiscal terms a stock like that is not an asset. It is a reason for other
people to come.
