title: Coins and rock faces

A good deal of the ancient world is known principally from its coinage —
Greco-Bactria, the Indo-Greek kingdoms, Persis, Characene. Elymais is the
thinnest case of the kind.

The Elymaean series runs from Kamnaskires' tetradrachms, which are competent
Hellenistic work with Greek legends, through three centuries of progressive
change: the portraits become frontal and schematic, the Greek legends decay into
unreadable marks and are eventually replaced by Aramaic, and an anchor appears
as a persistent symbol. The dynastic reconstruction from that is loose enough
that no greatest king can be named and no last king either.

The other evidence is on rock. At Tang-e Sarvak, on outcrops in a valley in
Khuzistan, there are reliefs of kings — an investiture scene, a reclining
banqueter, a horseman, figures in Parthian dress with Aramaic inscriptions
beside them. There are Parthian-period sanctuaries at Masjid-i Solaiman and
Bard-e Neshandeh with terraces and column bases. It is enough to establish that
there were kings who wanted to be shown as kings, and not enough to say much
more.

So: nobody knows how Elymais raised an army, or how it collected what it
collected, or how one king followed another, or how large it was, or how many
people lived in it. The only datable event in three hundred and seventy years is
somebody else arriving.

That is not a puzzle and it is not a controversy. It is the ordinary condition
of a small kingdom that lasted a long time and wrote nothing down. Most of the
past is in this state, and the reason it does not look that way is that the
places which wrote about themselves are the places anybody has heard of.

Elymais is known because it minted money, and because two Seleucid kings died
trying to take its temple treasure. In the ancient world that counts as being
well documented.
