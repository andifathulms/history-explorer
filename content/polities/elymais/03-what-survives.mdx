This corpus now has four records whose evidence is principally coinage —
Greco-Bactria, the Indo-Greek kingdoms, Persis and Characene — and this is the
fifth and the thinnest.

The Elymaean series runs from Kamnaskires' tetradrachms, which are competent
Hellenistic work with Greek legends, through three centuries of progressive
change: the portraits become frontal and schematic, the Greek legends decay into
unreadable marks and are eventually replaced by Aramaic, and an anchor appears
as a persistent symbol. The dynastic reconstruction from that is loose. This
record's `peak` and `last` ruler fields are both null and the site does not
attempt a sequence.

The other evidence is on rock. At Tang-e Sarvak, on outcrops in a valley in
Khuzistan, there are reliefs of kings — an investiture scene, a reclining
banqueter, a horseman, figures in Parthian dress with Aramaic inscriptions
beside them. There are Parthian-period sanctuaries at Masjid-i Solaiman and
Bard-e Neshandeh with terraces and column bases. It is enough to establish that
there were kings who wanted to be shown as kings, and not enough to say much
more.

So this record carries three null institution fields, two null ruler fields, no
extent, no population, and three null influence counts. It has one turning point
and it is about somebody else arriving.

That is a legitimate state for a page and the corpus should be able to hold it
without the entry reading as unfinished. The site has said this before in
different words — on the Derbend record, whose turning-point list is empty
because two centuries of a pass being taxed is not a sequence of hinges; on the
Hephthalite record, which is a polity recorded almost entirely through its
effects on other people; on the Median record, where the fields are null because
it is disputed whether there was a state to have them.

Elymais is the ordinary version of that. Not an evidential puzzle and not a
scholarly controversy — just a small kingdom that lasted a long time, wrote
nothing down, and is known because it minted money and because two Seleucid
kings died trying to take its temple treasure.
