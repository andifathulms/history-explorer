Ardashir took Elymais around 224, in the sequence this corpus's Persis record
sets out: Fars first, then Kerman, then Elymais and the Gulf coast, then
Ctesiphon and the empire. Khuzistan became a Sasanian province and one of the
most important — Shapur I settled Roman deportees there and built Gundeshapur,
which became a centre of medicine and learning, and this site's Sasanian record
describes the Christian communities that ended up in the same country.

With this page, all six of the vassal kingdoms named on this site's Parthian
record are in the corpus. That chapter describes an empire assembled out of
great noble houses and sub-kingdoms — Persis, Elymais, Characene, Adiabene,
Media Atropatene and Armenia, each with its own dynasty and its own coinage —
under a king of kings who supplied none of their administration, and it codes
the Parthian military basis `client-levy` on that account.

Reading the six together is worth doing, because they are the same object six
times. A hereditary local dynasty; its own silver with Greek legends that
gradually stop being Greek; a hundred to five hundred years of somebody's
vassalage; no surviving narrative; a page built from coins and one or two
reliefs; and an ending in the 220s at the hands of the sub-king from Fars who
took the whole thing.

They also fall on a spectrum of consequence that this corpus can now show.
Armenia became a kingdom that outlived every empire around it and has two more
records on this site. Persis produced the dynasty that ended the arrangement.
Atropatene named a country. Adiabene's royal house became Jewish and its capital
became a metropolitan see. Characene was where the ships unloaded. And Elymais
had temples somebody robbed.

That range is the argument for carrying all six rather than the two famous ones.
An empire of magnates is only legible if the magnates are on the page, and five
of these six would never appear in a narrative history of Iran at all.
