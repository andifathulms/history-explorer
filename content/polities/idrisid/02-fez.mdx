---
title: Two banks of a river

Idris II founded Fez on the river of the same name in the first decade of the
ninth century, and what made it a city rather than a fort was that it was
settled twice, by two groups of refugees, on opposite banks.

In 818 a rising in the suburb of Córdoba was crushed by the Umayyad amir
al-Hakam I, and the survivors were expelled. Some went to Alexandria and eventually took Crete;
several thousand came west to Fez and settled the right bank, which is still
called the Andalusi quarter. In 824, refugees from unrest at Kairouan, the
Aghlabid capital, settled the left bank, which is called the Qarawiyyin quarter —
the quarter of the people of Kairouan.

That is the city's whole character in its street plan. Two populations of
displaced urban Arabs, arriving within six years of each other from the two
established centres of the Islamic west, in a Berber country that had no
substantial city, under a dynasty whose claim was descent from Ali. Fez became
the first real Islamic city of the far Maghreb because two other places
produced refugees at the right moment.

The two quarters each built a congregational mosque. The one on the left bank,
the Qarawiyyin, was founded in 859, and the tradition attributes its foundation
and endowment to Fatima al-Fihri, a woman of a family that had come from
Kairouan and who is said to have spent her inheritance on it. It became the
principal centre of learning in the western Islamic world — teaching circles,
a library, a scholarly establishment that ran continuously and is running now,
which is why it is frequently described as the oldest continuously operating
institution of higher learning anywhere.

She held no office of any kind. She was a merchant's daughter with an
inheritance, in a quarter of refugees, and what she did with the money was
endow a mosque that became a teaching institution and is still one.

Every state that has governed Fez since — Idrisid, Zanata, Almoravid, Almohad,
Marinid, Wattasid, Saadi, Alaouite, French, Moroccan — has come and gone around
it. An endowment made by a private woman in 859 has outlasted eleven
sovereignties, which is a better record than any of them managed.
