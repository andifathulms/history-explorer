Idris II died in 828, and what he left was not really a state. It was a city, and
the city turned out to be the durable part.

Fez in 828 was two settlements facing each other across a river: the Andalusi
quarter on the right bank, settled by refugees expelled from Córdoba in 818, and
the Qarawiyyin quarter on the left, settled by refugees from Kairouan in 824. Two
walled towns, two Friday mosques, two populations who had arrived from opposite
ends of the Islamic west within six years of each other and who did not merge for
three hundred years.

## Why that produced a capital

Because both halves brought something the far Maghreb did not have.

The Andalusis brought the craft and commercial practice of a Umayyad city:
tanning, metalwork, textiles, and the habits of a place that had been a
Mediterranean market for a century. The Kairouanis brought the law. Kairouan was
the great centre of Maliki jurisprudence in North Africa, and the people who left
it in 824 included jurists and their libraries.

A frontier country with no urban tradition of its own acquired, in six years, a
population of skilled refugees from the two most sophisticated Muslim cities in
the western Mediterranean — because both of those cities had recently expelled
people, and there was somewhere for them to go.

## What the Idrisids actually did

Very little, and the little was decisive.

They did not build an administration. They collected no systematic taxation
anybody has recorded, kept no bureaucracy, minted only intermittently, and never
held the country outside the corridor between Fez and the Rif with any
consistency. Their army was tribal levies who came when the sharif called and
went home afterwards.

What they did was be the reason the city existed and the reason people would come
to it. A Berber Morocco with no state tradition, no city and no legal
establishment got all three inside a generation because a fugitive from a failed
rising near Mecca turned up with the right ancestry and a plausible claim to
authority, and everybody who needed somewhere to go went where he was.

## The mosque

The Qarawiyyin was founded in 859 as a neighbourhood mosque by Fatima al-Fihri, a
merchant's daughter of the Kairouani quarter, out of an inheritance.

It became the congregational mosque of its half of the city, then a teaching
institution, then the intellectual centre of the far Maghreb — a place scholars
came to from al-Andalus and from Ifriqiya, with a library and endowments and
salaried teachers, running continuously from the ninth century to the present.

That is the Idrisid peak, properly understood. Not a territorial extent, which
was never much and never stable, but a city with a law school in it, founded by
refugees and endowed by a private woman, which every subsequent dynasty in
Morocco needed to hold and none of them built.
