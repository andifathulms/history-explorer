Idris II died in 828 and divided his territory among his sons, and the Idrisid
state effectively ended there — a century and a half before the last of its
princes was removed.

What followed is not a dynasty in decline so much as a family scattered across
a country. Idrisid princes held Fez, the Rif, the Atlantic plain and towns in
the north, quarrelled, subdivided further, and were still doing it a hundred
years later. Dividing a realm among sons is the standard practice of a great many dynasties —
the Seljuks did it, the Mongols did it, the Ghurids and the Buyids did it — and it
usually takes two or three generations to destroy them. Here it happened
immediately and completely, at the first succession, with nothing in place to
arrest it.

Then the tenth century arrived and the far Maghreb became somebody else's
battlefield. The Fatimids, established in Ifriqiya from 909 and needing the
western routes, pushed west; the Umayyads of Córdoba, who declared a
caliphate of their own in 929 largely in answer to the Fatimid one, pushed south
across the strait. Neither wanted to occupy Morocco. Both
wanted the other not to have it, and both worked through Berber clients —
Miknasa, Zanata, Sanhaja — who changed sides as the subsidies changed.

The Idrisid principalities were the ground that war was fought over. They were
taken, restored, taken again by whichever side was paying that decade, and by 974 the last
of them had been removed. No single power destroyed the Idrisids. Two powers
fighting each other did it between them, over sixty years, without either of them
particularly intending to.

The city they founded did not much notice. Fez went on, was ruled by
Zanata clients and then by the Almoravids and the Almohads and the dynasties
after them, and remained the intellectual centre of the far Maghreb throughout.
The Qarawiyyin has been teaching continuously since 859, under something like a
dozen sovereignties.

The claim the dynasty left behind outlasted the state by a thousand years.
