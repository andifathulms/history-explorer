Idris II died in 828 and divided his territory among his sons, and the Idrisid
state effectively ended there — a century and a half before the date on this
record.

What followed is not a dynasty in decline so much as a family scattered across
a country. Idrisid princes held Fez, the Rif, the Atlantic plain and towns in
the north, quarrelled, subdivided further, and were still doing it a hundred
years later. This site codes appanage on several records — the Seljuks, the
Mongols, the Ghurids, the Buyids — and this is the version where it happens
immediately and completely, at the first succession, with nothing to arrest it.

Then the tenth century arrived and the far Maghreb became somebody else's
battlefield. The Fatimids, established in Ifriqiya from 909 and needing the
western routes, pushed west; the Umayyads of Cordoba, whose caliphal claim of
929 this site's Cordoba page describes as provoked by exactly that Fatimid
advance, pushed south across the strait. Neither wanted to occupy Morocco. Both
wanted the other not to have it, and both worked through Berber clients —
Miknasa, Zanata, Sanhaja — who changed sides as the subsidies changed.

The Idrisid principalities were the ground that war was fought over. They were
taken, restored, taken again by whichever side was paying that decade, and by
974 the last of them had been removed. That is why this record types the ending
`gradual absorption` and names two agents, which is unusual in this corpus: no
single power destroyed them, two powers fighting each other did it between them
over sixty years.

The city they founded did not much notice. Fez went on, was ruled by
Zanata clients and then by the Almoravids and the Almohads and the dynasties
after them, and remained the intellectual centre of the far Maghreb throughout.
The Qarawiyyin in the previous chapter has been teaching continuously since 859
under something like a dozen sovereignties.

The last chapter on this page is about the claim the dynasty left behind, which
outlasted the state by a thousand years.
