The Idrisid principalities were ground up in the tenth century between the
Fatimids and the Umayyads of Córdoba. What is less often said is where the family
went, and what one branch of it did afterwards, which is take the Umayyad
caliphate.

The Andalusi historians followed all of this closely, because it happened to
them. Al-Maqqari, compiling in the seventeenth century out of Ibn Hayyan and
others, gives the sequence.

## Losing Fez

In 917 the Fatimid general Masala ibn Habus was sent against Yahya ibn Idris,
sultan of Fez, defeated him and besieged him in his capital; he could not take it
then, came back in 921, took it, and put Yahya to death. A kinsman recovered the
city and seated himself, as al-Maqqari puts it, on the throne of his ancestors,
and was killed by a Fatimid governor two years later. In 952 the family got back
a good part of its old dominions, minus Fez.

Then the other caliphate arrived with an offer. Abu'l-Aysh Ahmad, an Idrisid
prince with the Fatimids in his territory, swore vassalage to the Umayyad caliph
Abd al-Rahman III and asked for troops. Abd al-Rahman would send none unless he
was given Tangier and Ceuta as hostages. Abu'l-Aysh refused; a Córdoban fleet
ravaged his coast until he accepted; and with those two ports in hand the caliph
extended his authority across the whole of the far Maghreb, until — in
al-Maqqari's summary — he was obeyed from Tahert to Sijilmasa.

An Idrisid asked one caliphate for protection against the other and paid for it
with the two harbours that let the protector take his country.

## Córdoba

So the family went to Córdoba, and al-Maqqari records the arrivals like a court
register.

In 943 the Idrisite chief judge of Fez came over to join the war against the
Christians and was hospitably entertained. In May 945 two more members of the
royal house entered the city, were received kindly, and went home in the autumn
loaded with presents. Then Ibn Hayyan, quoted directly:

> On Saturday the 9th of Rejeb of the year 341 … Hasan, son of Ahmed
> Al-fildhel … and Mohammed, son of ’Isa … both descendants of ’Ali Ibn Abi
> Talib, arrived in Cordova; and on Wednesday, the 26th of Rabi’-l-akhar of the
> ensuing year, ’Isa, Abu-l-’aysh, Yahya Ibn Hasan, and Hasan Ibn Mohammed, also
> belonging to the royal family of Idris. They fixed their dwelling in Cordova,
> and left a numerous posterity.

Two of them died there within a decade and were buried in the suburb cemetery,
with the chief qadi of the capital reading the funeral service over them by the
caliph's own command. Abu'l-Aysh, it is well known, was killed fighting the
Christians of the north.

That is a defeated dynasty being pensioned. It is also, on the evidence of the
next sixty years, a dynasty being kept.

## 1016

Among the Berber commanders in the Umayyad armies at the end of the tenth century
were two brothers, Ali and al-Qasim ibn Hammud, and al-Maqqari sets out their
descent at length for a reason: back through Hammud and Maymun and Ahmad and Ali
and Ubaydallah to Umar ibn Idris, son of Idris, son of Abdallah, son of Hasan,
son of Husayn, son of Ali ibn Abi Talib.

> Their ancestor was that same Idris who, in order to escape the vengeance of
> Harun Ar-rashid, the ’Abbasside, had fled from the East and taken refuge among
> the Berbers of Africa.

They rose in the Cordoban service, took a leading part in the civil war that
destroyed the Amirid regime, and were rewarded with the government of Tangier and
the north African coast. Then the whole structure came apart, and in 1016 Ali
crossed the strait — announcing that he came to avenge the murdered caliph Hisham
— beat the reigning claimant at Italica outside Seville, beheaded him, and
entered Córdoba.

He took the title *al-Nasir li-Din Allah*, defender of the faith. Two hundred and
twenty-eight years after a fugitive with no army was accepted by Berber tribes in
Morocco because of his descent, his descendant sat in Córdoba as commander of the
faithful.

## Eighteen months

Al-Maqqari's account of the reign is the reason to include it, because it does
not flatter.

Ali's first act was to discipline the Berber soldiery who had been plundering
Córdoba with impunity, and he did it by sitting at his palace gate on fixed days
to hear complaints and beheading the convicted in front of their own tribesmen. A
writer who saw it records the case of a Berber met at the gate of Amir with a
load of grapes on his saddle-bow; asked where he had got them, the man answered
*I seized them like a man*, and Ali had him beheaded on the spot and his head
paraded through the streets on the horse's back on top of the grapes.

For eighteen consecutive months, al-Maqqari writes, he persevered in the right
path. Then he learned that the Andalusians, who could not tolerate his rule, had
raised an Umayyad claimant in the east, and *he changed his conduct entirely*: he
let the Berbers go back to what they had been doing, the great houses of the
Umayyads were gutted, the rich were dispossessed and the learned insulted, and
the ruin of the city went on as fast as in its worst years.

While he was preparing to march against the rising, three Slav pages of the old
Umayyad household assassinated him in his bath and hid in a part of the palace
only they knew. That was in 1017. One historian who checked put the reign at
twenty-one months and six days. When the death became public, al-Maqqari writes,
the people of Córdoba were highly rejoiced.

His brother and then his son held the caliphal title after him, and the family
ended as one of the small kingdoms of a broken al-Andalus, with its seat at
Málaga.

## What the poet said

Al-Maqqari, who is never far from a verse, closes the reign with the court poets,
and one of them supplies the sentence the whole chapter is about. Ubada ibn Ma'
al-Sama — a Shi'i, which he notes — wrote in praise of Ali:

> Your ancestor 'Ali began in the East what another 'Ali has accomplished in the
> West. Let then all invoke the favours of God on him, and salute him; for his is
> the empire by right divine.

That is the Idrisid claim, made in Córdoba, about a man who had just been
stabbed by his own palace servants. It is also, for the two hundred and
twenty-eight years between the fugitive and the caliph, an accurate description
of what the family had been trading on the whole time.

The political afterlife was short and unhappy. The other one — a tomb in Fez and
a principle about descent — outlasted it by a thousand years.
