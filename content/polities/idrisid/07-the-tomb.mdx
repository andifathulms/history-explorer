In 1437 somebody digging in Fez found a body, uncorrupted, which was identified
as Idris II.

The dynasty had been extinct for four hundred and sixty-three years. The
discovery came at a convenient moment for the Marinid sultans, who were losing
their grip on the country and needed a legitimating asset, and the tomb was
promptly built up into a shrine — the *zawiya* of Moulay Idris — at the centre of
Fez, with a sanctuary boundary around it inside which no Jew or Christian could
enter, no armed man could pass, and any fugitive was safe from arrest.

It is still there and still working.

## Why a corpse mattered

Because sharifian descent had become the only reliable political currency in
Morocco, and a physical shrine is a considerably better asset than a genealogy.

The Idrisids had established the principle: that descent from the Prophet through
Ali and Fatima was the ground of a right to rule in the far Maghreb. What
followed them proved it. The Almoravids and the Almohads had ruled on doctrine
rather than descent and both fell. The Marinids ruled on neither and were always
vulnerable to the argument that they were only Zanata chiefs who had got lucky.

By the fifteenth century every serious claimant in Morocco either had a sharifian
pedigree or acquired one. The Saadis, who took the country in the sixteenth
century, were sharifs. The Alaouites, who took it in the seventeenth, were
sharifs, and their descendant is on the throne today. Both dynasties trace to the
Prophet, neither traces to Idris, and both of them needed the principle he had
established.

That is the Idrisid inheritance, and it is not a small one. Very few dynasties
determine the terms on which every subsequent dynasty in their country has to
argue for itself.

## The city

The other inheritance is Fez, which had a better run than the dynasty by an order
of magnitude.

The two refugee quarters merged eventually. The Qarawiyyin went on teaching. The
city was the capital of the Almoravids' Moroccan territory, then the Almohads',
then the Marinids' — who built a whole new administrative city beside it rather
than move — then the Wattasids', then a Saadi and Alaouite capital in alternation
with Marrakesh and Meknes.

It has been continuously inhabited since 789 and its medieval core is largely
intact, which very few cities of that age anywhere can say.

## What the founder would have made of it

Idris I was a fugitive from a failed Alid rising near Mecca, who arrived in a
country he had never seen with no army, no money and nothing but a name, and was
accepted by Berber tribes who had no previous reason to care about the family of
the Prophet. He was poisoned, on the usual account by an Abbasid agent, about
four years later.

Twelve hundred years afterwards the country he arrived in is ruled by a sharif,
the city his son built is one of the oldest continuously inhabited in the world,
the university endowed there is still teaching, and his own tomb is a sanctuary
in the middle of it that armed men may not enter.

Almost none of that is a consequence of anything he did as a ruler, because he
barely was one.
