In 1260, two years after Baghdad, a Mongol army under Kitbuqa moved into Syria
and took Aleppo and Damascus. The Mamluk sultanate in Cairo — ten years old,
founded by a coup, on its third sultan — marched out to meet it and won at Ayn
Jalut in the Jezreel valley.

<Figure id="ma-harod" />

The strategic circumstances were favourable: Hulegu had withdrawn most of his
force east on news of the great khan's death, and the army left in Syria was a
detachment rather than the main body. But the victory was real, it was the
first significant Mongol defeat in the west, and Baybars — who had commanded
the vanguard and murdered his way to the sultanate within months of it — spent
the next seventeen years making Syria a Mamluk province and reducing the
Crusader states to a coastal strip.

Baybars also did the thing that made the sultanate legitimate. In 1261 he
installed a survivor of the Abbasid house in Cairo as caliph, revived the
office the Mongols had extinguished three years earlier, and had himself
invested by him. The caliph had no authority, no territory and no army. He had
legitimacy, which was the one thing a Kipchak slave soldier who had killed two
sultans could not manufacture for himself.
