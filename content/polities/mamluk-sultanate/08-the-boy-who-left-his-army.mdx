At the end of 1400 the sultan of Egypt was thirteen years old and standing in
Syria at the head of a great army, trying to stop Timur. Aleppo had already been
sacked. Damascus was next.

## The name

He had been born during the rebellion of Mintash and named accordingly. After his
father Barquq beat the rebels the name was changed to Faraj — *deliverance*.

Barquq had been the strongest Mamluk sultan in decades and had handled Timur by
refusing to be handled. When Timur sent an embassy, Barquq answered in language as
haughty as he had received; he sheltered Timur's enemies, invited the expelled
sultan of Baghdad, and built a coalition on the Euphrates. It held, partly because
Timur was busy elsewhere.

Then Barquq died, more worried at the end about the rising Ottomans than about
Timur, and the coalition passed to a boy of thirteen.

## The battle

The Egyptians at first seemed to be driving the invaders back. Lane-Poole's
assessment is dry: the retreat was probably strategic.

When the Egyptians attacked they were heavily repulsed. And then the thing that
decided the campaign happened inside the Mamluk camp rather than on the field.
Faraj found that the defeat had bred sedition among his emirs, who — as
Lane-Poole puts it, with no irony — naturally desired a more experienced leader
at such a crisis.

So he withdrew in haste to Cairo, **leaving his army to its fate**.

That is the Mamluk system's central weakness stated in one movement. A sultanate
in which the ruler is made by the emirs and can be unmade by them cannot afford a
lost battle, because a lost battle is immediately a succession crisis. The
thirteen-year-old was not being cowardly; he was doing the arithmetic correctly.
Staying with a defeated army surrounded by men who wanted somebody else in his
place was more dangerous than Timur.

## Damascus

The city surrendered on terms, and was sacked, ruined and burnt anyway. All
northern Syria was cruelly devastated.

Afterwards, when Timur had finished in Anatolia and destroyed the Ottoman army at
Ankara, his envoys came to Cairo with demands. Faraj agreed to all of them: he
surrendered his prisoners, and he agreed to strike coins in the conqueror's name.

Lane-Poole adds the detail that redeems the episode slightly, and it is the kind
of thing only a numismatist notices. **No such coins have ever been found.** Timur
never entered Egypt and never controlled it. He died in February 1405 while Faraj
was raising yet another army to resist whatever came next.

The submission, in other words, was made and then quietly not performed — which
is exactly what a state does when it has agreed to something it cannot survive
refusing and cannot bear to carry out.

## What it cost

Faraj had lost his credit by these proceedings.

A struggle for power among the leading mamluks followed, in which the sultan was
treated with contumely and beaten in the field, and ended in his sudden
disappearance in 1405.

The material damage was real — Damascus burnt, northern Syria wrecked, the Syrian
tax base broken for a generation — but it was survivable, and the sultanate
survived it by another century. What did not recover was the thing Barquq had
briefly possessed: a ruler whose emirs could not remove him in the middle of a
war. Every crisis for the next hundred years would be conducted by men who had
watched a sultan ride away from his own army and had learned the lesson from the
other side.
