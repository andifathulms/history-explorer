title: Al-Nasir Muhammad

Qalawun's son al-Nasir Muhammad was made sultan three times — as a boy in 1293,
again in 1299, and finally from 1310 to 1341 — and the third reign is the
sultanate at its height. Thirty-one years without a deposition is by itself
remarkable in this polity's record.

He got there by breaking the men who had twice removed him. The senior emirs of
Baybars' and Qalawun's households were killed, exiled or bought, and al-Nasir
then declined to build a comparable faction of his own: he kept his purchased
mamluks numerous but junior, promoted through marriage and money rather than
through household seniority, and made himself the only fixed point in the system.
Irwin's account of how he did it is the best argument in the literature that the
Mamluk polity had no constitution — only a distribution of force that a
sufficiently ruthless man could rearrange.

The instrument was fiscal. The rawk of 1315, a cadastral survey and general
redistribution of iqta' across Egypt, took land out of the hands of the great
households and reassigned it — enlarging the sultan's own share to something
near a third of the whole and cutting the emirs' independent revenue. A survey
is not a dramatic event and it did more to his rivals than any of his campaigns.

What the money bought is still standing. His reign is the great age of Mamluk
building: the mosque in the citadel, the enormous complexes along the Bayn
al-Qasrayn, the aqueducts, the hippodromes, an expansion of Cairo that made it
plausibly the largest city west of China. He married into the Ilkhanid and
Golden Horde houses, received Rasulid and Ethiopian embassies, and sent the
kiswa to Mecca every year.

The weakness was the one he had designed. By refusing to let any faction grow,
he left none capable of governing after him: twelve of his sons and grandsons
held the title in the forty-one years after his death, most of them children,
while the emirs who had been kept junior fought over which of them would hold
the child.
