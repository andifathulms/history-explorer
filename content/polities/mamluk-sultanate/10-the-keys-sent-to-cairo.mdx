The Mamluk sultanate was killed by an Ottoman army in 1516, and the sixty years
before that are the record of Cairo watching it come and being unable to do
anything about the frontier where it would arrive.

## The thorn

Northern Syria and the Taurus marches were held by Turkman principalities that
were technically Egyptian vassals — the White Sheep, the Dhu'l-Qadr of Elbistan,
Karaman — and were in practice whatever their own succession disputes made them.

Lane-Poole is precise about why this mattered. The trouble was not the chronic
insubordination of the vassals in itself. It was that every disturbance among
them furnished a pretext for intervention by the sultanate's most dangerous
neighbour, whose power was about to be crowned by the conquest of Constantinople.

Successive sultans tried every available method. Al-Mu'ayyad and his son reduced
the border states to temporary submission more than once. Barsbay went to war with
the White Sheep in 1433 and besieged Amid without success. Jaqmaq took the other
approach entirely — cultivated the border chieftains, married their daughters,
and received their homage at least in form. Inal continued that conciliatory line,
kept a friendly if nominal overlordship, and had to send an army in 1456 to
recover Tarsus and Adana from the prince of Karaman anyway.

## What vassalage had become

Then Uzun Hasan of the White Sheep found the method that exposed the whole
arrangement.

He captured Egyptian cities and fortresses — Kharput, Karkar — and then sent the
keys to Cairo, on the theory that this made it all correct.

Lane-Poole's sentence ends with an exclamation mark, and it deserves one. A vassal
was taking his overlord's cities by force and presenting the conquest as an act of
homage, and the overlord had no answer, because the alternative to accepting the
keys was a war Cairo could not be sure of winning. Uzun Hasan went on sending
presents of camels, coats of mail and Circassian slaves for years.

## The neighbour stops pretending

Egypt and the Ottomans had been formally friendly. Inal decorated Cairo for
several days in 1453 when Mehmed II's embassy brought news that Constantinople
had fallen.

The signs that this was over came as protocol before they came as war. An Ottoman
embassy in 1464 pointedly disregarded some of the customary etiquette — which in
this world is a message, sent deliberately, and read correctly.

Then the two states took opposite sides over the succession to Karaman and nearly
came to blows. Then, in the dispute between two brothers for Dhu'l-Qadr — a state
technically tributary to Egypt — the Porte secretly supported Shah Suwar, the
candidate Cairo opposed. The mamluks were beaten with heavy loss at Ayntab, and
beaten again near the Ceyhan.

Egypt eventually won that one. Deprived of Turkish support, the rebel had to
surrender, was brought to Cairo and hanged. But Lane-Poole's conclusion is the
point of the episode: the course of the war had shown the danger of Ottoman
intervention.

## Winning the argument, losing the position

Qaytbay reigned longer than any other Circassian sultan and was, by every account,
one of the capable ones. His buildings are still the finest things in Cairo. He
held the frontier, hanged the pretender, kept the vassals nominally his.

And at the end of it the northern march was a place where an Ottoman sultan could
choose a candidate, back him quietly, beat an Egyptian army twice through a proxy,
and lose nothing when the proxy failed.

That is a different kind of decline from a lost battle or an empty treasury. The
sultanate could still win engagements on that frontier for another forty years. It
could no longer make the frontier mean anything, because the state on the other
side of it had worked out that Egyptian suzerainty in the Taurus was a form of
words — and had discovered, at no cost, exactly how much force it took to make
Cairo march.
