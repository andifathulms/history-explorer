---
title: Baybars builds the machine
drafted_from: irwin-1986
phase: expansion
---

Baybars had commanded the vanguard at Ayn Jalut, murdered the sultan on the way
home, and ruled for seventeen years. What he built in them is the reason the
sultanate lasted another two and a half centuries after him, and almost none of
it was a battle.

He quadrupled the standing cavalry, from something like ten thousand to forty,
by buying on a scale no previous ruler in the region had attempted — the Kipchak
steppe was being emptied by the Mongols, and the slave markets of the Black Sea
were full. He rebuilt the Syrian citadels the Mongols had slighted and garrisoned
them. He laid out the barid, a relay post system with fixed stages between Cairo
and Damascus that carried a message in four days, and supplemented it with
pigeons; a sultan in Cairo learned of a raid on the Euphrates within a week, and
this, more than any single victory, is what let one government hold two countries
separated by Sinai.

The diplomacy was of the same kind. Baybars allied with Berke of the Golden
Horde — a Muslim Chinggisid at war with his Ilkhanid cousins — which secured the
Black Sea slave route that the army depended on, and negotiated with Byzantium
for passage through the straits to keep it open. He made the Nubian kingdoms
tributary and took the Red Sea ports. The state's inputs were as deliberately
engineered as its army.

Against the Franks he was methodical rather than dramatic. Caesarea and Arsuf in
1265, Safad in 1266, Jaffa and Antioch in 1268, Krak des Chevaliers in 1271 —
taken one at a time, and where the position could not be held it was demolished
so that no fleet could reoccupy it. Antioch had been Christian-ruled for a
hundred and seventy years and was sacked with a thoroughness that shocked even
sympathetic chroniclers. The kingdom of Jerusalem was reduced to a coastal strip
before he died in 1277, reportedly by drinking poison he had prepared for
somebody else.
