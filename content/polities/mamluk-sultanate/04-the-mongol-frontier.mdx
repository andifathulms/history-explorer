Ayn Jalut did not settle anything. For sixty years the Ilkhanate raided into
Syria and the Mamluks raided back, and the sultanate's border ran along the
Euphrates with client Turkmen and Armenian principalities in between, changing
sides according to who had campaigned most recently.

The Mamluks lost the largest of those battles. At Wadi al-Khazandar near Homs
in December 1299, Ghazan — an Ilkhan who had converted to Islam four years
earlier, and who therefore arrived preaching against them rather than at them —
broke the Egyptian army and took Damascus. The citadel held out; the Ilkhanid
occupation lasted about a hundred days and dissolved when Ghazan withdrew, but
the shock in Cairo was severe and the sultanate's claim to be Islam's shield
had been answered by a Muslim.

The rematch at Marj al-Suffar south of Damascus in April 1303 went the other
way and settled it. There were no further serious Ilkhanid invasions, and in
1322 the two states signed a peace. Sixty-two years after Ayn Jalut, the
Mamluks and the Mongols recognised each other's frontiers, exchanged embassies,
and let the merchants through.

The Ilkhanate dissolved into fragments within fifteen years of that treaty, and
the Mamluks outlived it by nearly two centuries. Both things were true at once:
the sultanate's founding claim was that it was the state that stopped the
Mongols, and the way the Mongol wars actually ended was a negotiated border
between two Muslim powers who had run out of reasons.
