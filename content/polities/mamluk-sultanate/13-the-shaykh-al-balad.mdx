The sultanate ended in 1517 and the system did not.

Selim took Egypt, hanged the last sultan at the Zuwayla gate, and installed as
his first governor the Mamluk commander whose defection had helped him win.
Egypt became a province administered from Constantinople and separated from
Syria and Arabia beside it.

But the mamluks were still there — still being bought, still being trained,
still holding the land grants and the offices. Lane-Poole's sentence is the one
to keep: the power of the mamluks was not extinguished.

## The slow reversal

As the decades passed, the authority of the Turkish pasha and his janissaries
shrank, and the strength of the mamluk households grew back into the space.

<Figure id="ma-murad" />

Their chief emir acquired a title, and the title is almost comic given what the
office actually was: *shaykh al-balad*, the mayor. The man who really governed
Egypt on behalf of a sultan in another country was formally the head of the
town.

By the eighteenth century the mayor was the government. In 1768 Ali Bey
expelled the Ottoman pasha outright, declared Egypt independent, took part of
Arabia and tried to annex Syria — which is exactly the programme of every
Egyptian ruler since Ibn Tulun, attempted eight hundred and eighty years later
by a purchased soldier from the Caucasus.

He was betrayed, beaten and killed in 1772 by his own favourite general, whom
he had raised. That is also the programme.

<Figure id="ma-alibey" />

## How it actually ended

The mamluk households went on fighting each other over Egypt, more or less
subject to the Porte, until Napoleon landed in 1798 and beat them outside
Cairo, turning Egypt into a French province for three years. The British ended
that; the Ottoman authority was restored on paper; and the mamluks and the
pasha resumed their quarrel where they had left it.

<Figure id="ma-pyramids" />

It was finished by Muhammad Ali, who expelled the pasha in 1805 and massacred
the leading mamluks in 1805 and again in 1811, and founded the dynasty that
ruled Egypt until the twentieth century.

<Figure id="ma-massacre" />

## Five hundred and sixty-one years

Count it from the Bahriyya killing the last Ayyubid sultan in 1250 to the
citadel massacre of 1811 and you get five hundred and sixty-one years in which
Egypt was governed, or effectively governed, by men bought as children in the
Black Sea or the Caucasus, trained as cavalry, freed, and made into an
aristocracy that renewed itself by purchase rather than by birth.

Two hundred and ninety-four of those years came after the sultanate had been
formally abolished by a foreign empire, which is the part worth sitting with.
The Ottomans conquered the state. They did not touch the institution, because
the institution was how Egypt worked, and for the next three centuries it went
on working that way underneath a governor sent from somewhere else.
