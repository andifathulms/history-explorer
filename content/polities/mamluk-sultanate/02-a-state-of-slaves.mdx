---
title: A state that would not become a dynasty
drafted_from: petry-1998
phase: peak
---

The Mamluk system worked like this. Boys were bought on the steppe or in the
Caucasus, brought to Cairo, converted, trained for years in barracks, freed on
completion, and attached for life to the household of the man who had bought
them. Advancement ran through that household. A sultan's sons, born free and
Muslim, were not mamluks and generally could not inherit his position; power went
to another purchased soldier.

That last feature is the unusual one. Almost every polity in this corpus is a
dynasty, and its recurring failure is the succession — this site has recorded
fragmentation on inheritance for the Mongols, the Seljuks, the Carolingians and
the Ayyubids. The Mamluks removed heredity from the equation and got something
else instead: violent, frequent, factional turnover at the top, with sultans
lasting months, alongside remarkable institutional stability underneath, because
the barracks, the households and the administration continued regardless of who
was currently sultan.

They held Egypt and Syria for two hundred and sixty-seven years, stopped the
Mongols, ended the Crusader states, and kept a caliph in Cairo whose only
function was to make the arrangement lawful.

It is the one polity here that solved the problem this site keeps returning to —
that an army which can make a ruler will eventually make one it prefers — by
conceding the point entirely and building the state around it.
