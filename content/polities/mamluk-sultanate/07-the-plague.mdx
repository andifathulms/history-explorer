title: The plague, and after it the Circassians
drafted_from: petry-1998

The Black Death reached Alexandria in the autumn of 1347 and Cairo in 1348. It
is impossible to give a cited figure here — no source consulted publishes one
this site would print — but the qualitative record is unambiguous: villages in
the Delta abandoned, canals not cleared, the sugar plantations short of hands,
and the plague returning roughly every decade for the rest of the sultanate's
life rather than passing once.

The fiscal consequence ran straight through the state's design. Mamluk revenue
was iqta' revenue, iqta' revenue was agricultural, and agricultural revenue was
labour. When the labour died the assignments were worth a fraction of their
assessed value, and an emir's ability to maintain the mamluks his rank required
fell with them. The army contracted. So did the number of men any household
could buy, at exactly the point when the households were the only durable
institutions left.

What followed was two generations of factional war among the descendants of
al-Nasir Muhammad's officers, with sultans installed as children and removed as
adolescents. A regent, Yalbugha al-Umari, tried to restore the training regime
and was murdered by his own mamluks in 1366. Meanwhile the awlad al-nas, the
freeborn sons of mamluks who were barred from the top by the system their
fathers had joined, were being promoted into it by sultans who trusted family
over faction — the one reform that would have made this a dynasty, attempted
repeatedly and never carried.

In 1382 Barquq deposed the last Qalawunid child and took the sultanate himself.
He was Circassian, bought from the Caucasus rather than the Kipchak steppe —
that route had closed as the Golden Horde broke up — and he recruited his
successors from the same place. Historians conventionally call everything after
him the Burji sultanate. The system did not change; the men in it came from
somewhere else, and the fiction of a Qalawunid line was dropped.

Then Timur. In 1400 he took Aleppo and Damascus, sacked both, deported the
craftsmen of Damascus to Samarkand, and left. The sultanate did not fall, and
Syria did not recover its position for the rest of the century.
