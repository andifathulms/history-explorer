---
title: The monopoly, and then the Cape
drafted_from: petry-1998
phase: contraction
---

Barsbay, sultan from 1422 to 1438, responded to a shrinking agricultural revenue
by taking the trade revenue directly. He forced the Indian Ocean traffic away
from Aden and through Jeddah, where he could tax it; he then made the state the
sole buyer and seller of pepper and other spices at Alexandria, fixing the price
the Venetians paid. He occupied the Hejaz to enforce it, raided Cyprus in 1426
and captured its king, and struck gold coin again from the ransom.

It worked, in the short term, and it damaged the thing it taxed. The Karimi
syndicate that had carried the trade for two centuries did not survive the state
becoming its competitor. European buyers, facing a monopoly price at Alexandria,
had a standing motive to find another way to the pepper, and the Portuguese
crown was already looking.

Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498. Within a decade Portuguese squadrons were
in the Indian Ocean intercepting the Red Sea traffic at its source. Qansuh
al-Ghawri built a fleet, had timber hauled overland to Suez because Egypt has
none, sent it to India in alliance with Gujarat, and lost it at Diu in 1509 to
ships and guns his shipwrights could not match. The spice revenue never
recovered, and the state was in permanent fiscal crisis for its last twenty
years — debasing coin, confiscating waqf endowments, and taxing the same
merchants it had bankrupted.

The military problem was the same problem. Firearms existed and the Mamluks knew
it; al-Ghawri raised an arquebus corps and it was despised, because the mounted
archer trained from boyhood was the whole identity of the caste and a gun could
be handed to a peasant in a fortnight. Historians have argued about how much
this mattered ever since. What is not in dispute is that the sultanate met the
Ottomans in 1516 with a cavalry army and lost to artillery.
