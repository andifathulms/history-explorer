Qalawun was another of al-Salih Ayyub's purchases, an emir of Baybars'
generation who took the sultanate in 1279 and held it eleven years, and he is
the nearest thing the polity has to a founder of a house: his descendants held
the title, with interruptions, for a century. He beat the Ilkhanids at Homs in
1281, signed commercial treaties with Genoa, Aragon and Byzantium, and took
Tripoli in 1289. He died in 1290 on the march to Acre.

<Figure id="ma-qalawun" />

His son al-Ashraf Khalil finished it. Acre fell in May 1291 after six weeks;
Tyre, Sidon, Beirut and Athlit were abandoned within months, and the Frankish
presence on the Syrian mainland ended after a hundred and ninety-two years.

<Figure id="ma-papety" />

What followed is the part that mattered to the polity. The Mamluks did not
garrison the coast — they destroyed it. Harbours were blocked, walls thrown
down, and the ports left ruined on the explicit reasoning that a rebuilt
Frankish landing would be worse than an empty shore. Coastal Syria was still
visibly derelict two centuries later. It was a defensive judgement made by a
land power against a sea power it could not meet at sea, and it cost the Syrian
littoral its trade for the rest of the Middle Ages, which is one reason the
Egyptian route through Alexandria mattered as much as it later did.

The victory also removed the sultanate's public justification. A state built on
jihad against Franks and Mongols had disposed of the first and would shortly
come to terms with the second, and the emirs of the fourteenth century had to
find something else to fight about. They found each other.
