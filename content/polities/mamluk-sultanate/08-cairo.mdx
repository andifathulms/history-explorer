---
title: What the sultanate paid for
drafted_from: petry-1998

The politics were violent and the city was magnificent, and a page that records
only the first has described the sultanate the way its chroniclers' index
entries do rather than the way it looked.

Cairo under the Mamluks was the largest city in the Mediterranean world and
probably in Islam, and it was rebuilt continuously for two centuries by men who
could not pass their wealth to their sons. That is not incidental. A mamluk's
iqta' reverted to the state when he died and his children were outside the
system, so the reliable way to convert a career into something durable was the
waqf: an endowed mosque, madrasa, hospital or tomb, legally inalienable, with a
salaried staff that could include one's own descendants. The peculiarity of the
succession rule is therefore visible in the skyline. Qalawun's hospital complex,
Sultan Hasan's madrasa, Barquq's and Barsbay's and Qaytbay's funerary
foundations — the architecture that still defines historic Cairo is the output
of a ruling class working around its own childlessness.

The money came through Egypt because the alternatives had been closed. With the
Syrian coast deliberately ruined and the Persian Gulf route disrupted, the
Indian Ocean spice trade reached Europe through the Red Sea, and the Karimi
merchants who ran it — a syndicate of long-distance traders operating between
Aden, Jeddah and Alexandria — were rich enough to lend to sultans. Venetian and
Genoese factors bought at Alexandria under treaty. Sugar was refined and
exported. The customs on all of it were the sultanate's second revenue.

The scholarship was of a piece with the wealth. Cairo took in what Baghdad had
lost: Ibn Khaldun taught and judged here and wrote the Muqaddima's final version
here; al-Maqrizi wrote the topography of the city and a treatise on its
famines; Ibn Hajar and al-Suyuti made it the centre of hadith study and of
almost everything else. A Turkish-speaking military caste that did not
intermarry with the population it ruled endowed, in Arabic, the largest
concentration of religious learning in the Sunni world.
