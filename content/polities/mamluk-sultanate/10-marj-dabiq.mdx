Selim I had beaten the Safavids at Chaldiran in 1514 with field artillery and
janissary firearms, and the Mamluk position — a neutral third power between two
larger states, holding the Syrian corridor between them — stopped being
available. Qansuh al-Ghawri marched north in 1516, whether to fight the Ottomans
or to mediate is disputed by the sources.

At Marj Dabiq north of Aleppo on 24 August the Ottoman guns broke the Mamluk
centre, the governor of Aleppo changed sides, and al-Ghawri died on the field —
of a stroke, by most accounts, at about seventy-five. Syria was Ottoman within
weeks. Tuman Bay II was raised in Cairo, refused Selim's terms, and fought again
at Raydaniyya outside the city in January 1517 with entrenched cannon he had had
no time to learn to use. Cairo was taken after street fighting. Tuman Bay was
hanged at the Bab Zuwayla in April.

Two things went to Istanbul. One was the last Cairo Abbasid caliph, al-Mutawakkil
III, taken north and eventually returned; the Ottoman claim to have received the
caliphate from him is a much later story, constructed in the eighteenth century
when it became useful. The other was the guardianship of Mecca and Medina, which
the sharif of Mecca transferred to Selim, and which the Ottomans did put at the
centre of their self-description for the next four hundred years.

The mamluk households were not abolished. Egypt was governed as an Ottoman
province by a pasha from Istanbul alongside a mamluk beylicate that kept buying
boys from the Caucasus, training them in Cairo, and running the countryside; by
the eighteenth century the beys were the effective government again, and it took
Muhammad Ali's massacre of them in 1811 to end the system. The sultanate stopped
in 1517. The institution it was built on did not.
