Akbar came to the throne at thirteen in 1556 and spent fifty years building an
apparatus that solved, for about a century, the problem every state in this
part of the world had failed to solve.

## The problem

A ruler pays his officers with assignments of land revenue because he has no
other way to pay them. Within three generations the officers' families own the
land, the assignments have become hereditary, and the men who were supposed to
serve the crown have their own resources, their own dependants and their own
opinions.

The Delhi Sultanate ran into it. The Seljuks, the Mamluks and the Ottomans all
ran into it. It is the most reliable failure mode in pre-modern government.

## The answer

Akbar's *mansabdari* system put every officer of the empire — Muslim, Hindu,
Persian émigré, Rajput prince — into a single numerical hierarchy.

A *mansab* was a rank expressed as two numbers: the *zat*, which fixed personal
status and pay, and the *sawar*, which fixed how many cavalrymen the holder had
to maintain. Everything followed from the rank: salary, precedence, the size of
the contingent, the position in the hall.

The rank was granted by the emperor, held at his pleasure, and not heritable.
When a *mansabdar* died his assignment reverted to the crown and his estate was
audited for what he owed it.

And the assignments — *jagirs*, districts whose revenue paid the salary — were
rotated. A holder kept one for a few years and was then moved somewhere else, so
that no family could put down roots in a province, build a local following, or
confuse the revenue of a district with the ownership of it.

To make the rotation possible you have to know what every district is worth.
Which is why the other half of the system is the survey.

## The survey

Todar Mal's revenue settlement measured the cultivated land of the empire's core
provinces with a standard unit, classified it by what it grew and how reliably,
averaged the yields and the prices over ten years, and fixed the state's demand
in cash as a share of that average.

That is a permanent, written, checkable assessment covering millions of
cultivators, produced by a sixteenth-century government. Abu'l Fazl's *A'in-i
Akbari* prints the results province by province, along with the ranks, the
salaries, the imperial workshops, the price of everything the household bought,
and the regulations for branding cavalry horses so that a *mansabdar* could not
hire the same animals twice for two musters.

## The other settlement

The administrative machinery would not have held a majority-Hindu empire on its
own, and Akbar knew it.

He abolished the *jizya* on non-Muslims in 1564, and the pilgrimage tax before
that. He married Rajput princesses and did not require them to convert. He put
Rajput rulers into the *mansabdari* at high rank, commanding imperial armies,
including against other Rajputs — Man Singh of Amber led Mughal forces for
decades.

By the end of the reign something over a fifth of the senior nobility was Hindu.
That is a Muslim dynasty governing India through a nobility a substantial part
of which was not Muslim, by design, as policy.

The religious experiments of his later years — the debates at the Ibadat Khana
where Jesuits, Jains, Zoroastrians and Hindus argued in front of him, and the
*din-i ilahi*, a small circle of personal discipleship the sources describe
variously — are usually treated as the interesting part. They mattered far less
than the tax exemption and the marriages, which were structural.

## What it produced

By 1600 the Mughal empire had perhaps a hundred million subjects, the largest
revenue of any state on earth, and an administrative record so detailed that
economic historians still work from it.

It also had a rule about land that would break when there was no more land to
assign, and a settlement about religion that depended on the emperor of the day
wanting to keep it.
