Akbar's arrangement — the *jizya* abolished, Rajput houses married into the
imperial family, a nobility that was substantially Hindu, and a state that did not
identify itself with one community against the others — held for roughly a
century under Jahangir and Shah Jahan.

Aurangzeb reversed much of it. He reimposed the *jizya* in 1679, patronised a
stricter Sunni establishment, and spent the last twenty-five years of a
forty-nine year reign campaigning in the Deccan against the Marathas and the
Deccan sultanates. The empire reached its greatest extent under him and was
insolvent when he died in 1707.

<Figure id="mg-aurangzeb" />

The two facts are connected and the causation is argued about. The Deccan wars
consumed revenue and kept the emperor and the army permanently a thousand miles
from the capital; the *mansabdari* system ran short of assignable land to pay an
expanded nobility; the Marathas were never defeated so much as absorbed as a
permanent expense. How much the religious policy contributed is genuinely
contested, and the honest answer is that the fiscal argument explains more of it
than the confessional one does.

What followed was rapid. Within fifty years the empire was a set of effectively
independent provinces — Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad — whose governors remitted
nothing and whose emperor was a figure of ceremony in Delhi.

That is the same shape the Abbasid caliphate took under the Buyids, four
hundred years earlier and two thousand miles west: an imperial office retaining
its legitimacy and its ceremony long after it had stopped governing, useful to
everyone who took power precisely because it was powerless and could not
object. The East India Company ruled Bengal in the emperor's name from 1765.
