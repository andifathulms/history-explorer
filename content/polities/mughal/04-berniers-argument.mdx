François Bernier spent twelve years in India, most of them attached to a senior
noble of Aurangzeb's court, and went home in 1668 with a theory about why the
country was poor. The theory was wrong in an interesting way and it shaped
European thinking about Asia for the next two hundred years.

## What he saw

He saw the *jagir* system from the inside, and what he saw was real.

An officer holds a district for a few years, is paid out of its revenue, and
knows he will be moved. He therefore has no reason whatever to invest in it: not
in the canals, not in the wells, not in anything that pays back over a decade.
What he has every reason to do is extract as much as the district will bear
before he is transferred.

Bernier's description of the consequence is unsparing:

> a tyranny often so excessive as to deprive the peasant and artisan of the
> necessaries of life, and leave them to die of misery and exhaustion... a
> tyranny, in fine, that drives the cultivator of the soil from his wretched home
> to some neighbouring state, in hopes of finding milder treatment, or to the
> army.

And the mechanism underneath it:

> As the ground is seldom tilled otherwise than by compulsion, and as no person
> is found willing and able to repair the ditches and canals for the conveyance
> of water, it happens that the whole country is badly cultivated, and a great
> part rendered unproductive from the want of irrigation.

He puts the peasant's reasoning in the peasant's mouth: *Why should I toil for a
tyrant who may come to-morrow and lay his rapacious hands upon all I possess?*

He also notes, precisely, that the emperor could not stop it even if he wanted
to, *in provinces not contiguous to his capital*.

## The theory he built on it

Bernier's explanation was that the king owned all the land — that there was no
private property in the soil at all, and therefore none of the security that
makes people improve things.

That argument travelled. Montesquieu took it, and it became "oriental
despotism": a whole civilisation explained by the absence of property, standing
in permanent contrast to a Europe defined by having it. Marx took a version of
it for the Asiatic mode of production. It was still doing service in the
nineteenth century as a justification for governing India, on the grounds that a
people who had never had property rights could not be expected to manage
themselves.

## Why it is wrong

Every historian of Mughal revenue since has attacked it, and the attack is
straightforward: there was extensive private property in land, transacted,
mortgaged, inherited and litigated over.

The *zamindars* held hereditary rights in the revenue of their localities and
sold them. Peasants held heritable occupancy rights that the courts recognised.
The state's claim was on a share of the produce, which is a tax, and a heavy tax
is not the same thing as the absence of ownership.

What Bernier had actually observed was narrower and more accurate than the
theory he wrapped it in: a *transferable revenue assignment* creates no incentive
to invest, and by the 1660s the empire was running out of assignments to give
out.

## Which is the real problem

Akbar's system worked because the number of *mansabdars* was matched to the
amount of assignable revenue. Aurangzeb doubled the nobility — partly by
absorbing the Deccan sultanates' officers, partly to buy Maratha and other
support — while the revenue did not double.

So *jagirs* were over-assessed on paper against what they actually yielded,
holders received assignments that could not pay their obligations, transfers
became more frequent to spread the shortfall, and the incentive to strip a
district before moving on became irresistible.

Contemporary Mughal officials wrote about this as the *be-jagiri* crisis — the
shortage of assignments — and it is the mechanism by which the most
sophisticated revenue system of the seventeenth century turned into the thing
Bernier described.

He was in the right country, watching the right process, and reached for an
explanation about the nature of Asia when the answer was an accounting problem.
