Babur took northern India in 1526 and then wrote down what he thought of it,
and no conqueror anywhere has left a less flattering account of his own prize.

## The complaint

The passage is famous and it is worth having in full, because the specificity
is the point:

> Hindustan is a country of few charms. Its people have no good looks; of social
> intercourse, paying and receiving visits there is none; of genius and capacity
> none; of manners none; in handicraft and work there is no form or symmetry,
> method or quality; there are no good horses, no good dogs, no grapes,
> musk-melons or first-rate fruits, no ice or cold water, no good bread or cooked
> food in the bazars, no hot-baths, no colleges, no candles, torches or
> candlesticks.

He then explains the last item at length, and the explanation is the most
revealing thing in it. Instead of candles they have a *great dirty gang* called
lamp-men, who hold a wooden tripod with a thick wick in one hand and a gourd of
oil in the other, and dribble oil onto the wick as it needs it. Great men keep
one or two hundred of them. If a noble has work at night, these men come and
stand close and hold the light.

A ruler from Kabul looks at that and sees the absence of candlesticks. What he
is describing is a country where labour is so abundant that nobody bothered
inventing the candlestick.

## What he liked

He is fair about the compensations and there are exactly two.

> Pleasant things of Hindustan are that it is a large country and has masses of
> gold and silver.

And the monsoon air, which he found remarkably fine and unsurpassed for
healthiness — with the qualification that it ruins everything. A Transoxanian
bow taken through the rains cannot be drawn afterwards; *not only the bow,
everything is affected, armour, book, cloth, and utensils all; a house even*.

A large country with masses of gold and silver. That is the whole case for the
conquest, stated by the man who made it, in eleven words.

## Why the complaint matters

Because it is the founding document of a dynasty that spent the next two
centuries becoming Indian, and it establishes what they started from.

Babur is homesick throughout. He writes about the melons of Ferghana, the
running water in Kabul gardens, the ice. His first substantial act in India was
to lay out a garden — a *charbagh*, quartered by water channels — at Agra,
because the absence of running water in the residences is one of his listed
complaints, and he fixed it.

That garden is the ancestor of every Mughal garden afterwards, including the one
the Taj Mahal stands in. A dynasty's most recognisable architectural form began
as a Central Asian émigré's attempt to make the place bearable.

## What the conquest actually was

Panipat, in April 1526, was not a large battle by Indian standards and Babur's
account of it is precise about the method: a fortified centre of carts lashed
together with gaps for the guns, flanking cavalry to wheel around and take the
enemy from behind, and matchlockmen and artillery under an Ottoman gunner.

Ibrahim Lodi's much larger army was funnelled into the front of it and could not
deploy. He was killed on the field.

Babur then had to do it again the following year at Khanwa against the Rajput
confederation under Rana Sanga, which was the harder fight and the one that
actually secured the conquest.

He died in 1530, four years after Panipat, having spent almost all of them
campaigning, and was buried at Agra — and then, on his own instructions, moved
to Kabul, to a garden on a hillside, which is where he still is.
