Shah Jahan fell seriously ill in September 1657, past sixty-four and
suffering from a disorder Bernier calls too disgraceful to describe.
The illness itself mattered less than what it triggered: all four of
his sons - Dara Shikoh at Delhi and Agra, Shah Shuja in Bengal,
Aurangzeb in the Deccan, Murad Bakhsh in Gujarat - began raising
armies before anyone could confirm whether their father was even
still alive. Shah Jahan recovered enough to send each of them letters
ordering them home, but Shuja's own courtiers told him the emperor's
recovery was a fiction, and Aurangzeb, distrustful of his eldest
brother Dara's hold on the ailing king, kept his own army moving north
regardless. Bernier, who had arrived in India the previous year and
would spend most of the next decade attached to a noble at Aurangzeb's
own court, watched the whole war unfold at close range.

## Two battles, one winner

Aurangzeb and Murad Bakhsh combined their forces and crushed an
imperial army under Dara's own general, Jaswant Singh, at a river
crossing near Dharmat in April 1658 - a defeat sealed when the
imperial commander Kasim Khan abandoned the field, leaving the Rajput
contingent that had fought loyally to be cut down almost to a man.
Dara met the brothers himself the following month at Samugarh, and by
Bernier's own account fought with real courage - riding an elephant
into the thick of the fighting, rallying broken units back into line,
twice driving Aurangzeb's own wing from the field. But the battle
turned on treachery rather than force: Khalilullah Khan, commanding
thirty thousand of Dara's own troops on the right wing, held his
division back from the fighting entirely, and without it Dara's
advantage evaporated. He fled the field, and his army, which had been
winning when he left it, dissolved behind him.

## A trap sprung on its own builder

Shah Jahan, still confined to the fortress at Agra, tried to play
Aurangzeb against his own son the way he had once played his sons
against each other - sending secret messages of support to whichever
side seemed useful in the moment, while publicly professing loyalty
to none of them. Aurangzeb, who knew exactly what his father was
doing, refused to enter the fortress himself; instead he sent his own
son, Sultan Muhammad, ostensibly to deliver a message, who turned on
the gate guards the moment he was inside and took the fortress by
surprise. Shah Jahan, who had spent weeks trying to out-scheme his
son, discovered that he had walked into the same kind of trap he had
set for others - a prisoner in his own citadel, with Aurangzeb now
holding both the fortress and, in practice, the throne.

## A charge that settled the question in advance

Dara fled first toward Sindh and then east, betrayed in the end by a
local chieftain, Malik Jiwan, whose life Dara had once spared and who
delivered him to Aurangzeb's forces in chains. Bernier records the
charge that condemned him: not treason alone, but apostasy - that he
"had long since ceased to be a Musulman, and become a Kafer," a
judgment secured, Khafi Khan's own account adds, by a legal opinion
from the court's own jurists that he had "apostatised from the law,
had vilified religion, and had allied himself with heresy and
infidelity." It was the same charge, in miniature, that Aurangzeb's
later religious policy would apply to the empire at large. Dara was
executed in his cell in 1659, and by the account of witnesses the
translator's own notes preserve, his head was embalmed, sealed in a
box, and delivered to Shah Jahan in Aurangzeb's name. The old emperor,
still imprisoned at Agra, opened it expecting a gesture of
reconciliation from his usurping son and found his own son's face
instead.

## What died at Samugarh

Dara Shikoh had spent the years before the war as governor of
Benares commissioning a Persian translation of the Upanishads, made
by a staff of Benares pandits and titled the Sirr-i-Akbar - the
Secret of Secrets - a scholarly project that later found its way, by
a long chain of translation, into European hands entirely. None of
that scholarship survived him into power. Murad Bakhsh, who had
fought beside Aurangzeb at both battles, was imprisoned and then
executed in turn: unwilling to poison a member of the royal family
whose death might later be doubted, Aurangzeb had the sons of a man
Murad had murdered years earlier in Gujarat brought to court to
demand his head, and used their claim of blood as the form of law
for an execution he had already decided on. Shah Shuja fled to
Arakan and, by the account Bernier's own translator records, was
drowned there with his entire family by the local raja's own order
in 1660.
Aurangzeb held the throne he had taken by 1658 for the next
forty-nine years, ruling a father he kept confined at Agra until Shah
Jahan's own death in 1666 - a reign that began, before any campaign
or any law, with a brother's head arriving in a box addressed to a
prisoner.
