The Mughal empire stopped governing around 1720 and was not abolished until
1858, and the hundred and thirty-eight years in between are the most instructive
part of its history.

## The useful shell

By the 1750s the emperor at Delhi had no army, no revenue and no province. What
he had was a signature that everybody wanted.

The Nawabs of Bengal, Awadh and the Carnatic, the Nizam of Hyderabad, the
Marathas, the French and the English all governed in his name and remitted him
nothing. Every one of them held their position by an imperial grant, and every
one of them found it cheaper to obtain the grant than to argue about whether it
was necessary.

In 1765 the East India Company took the *diwani* of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa —
the right to collect the revenue of the richest provinces in India — from Shah
Alam II, by a treaty, in return for an annual payment. A trading company
became the tax authority of thirty million people by receiving an office from an
emperor who could not have enforced anything against it.

<Figure id="mg-diwani" />

Company coins were struck in the emperor's name until 1835. Company documents
ran in his name for decades after nobody pretended he ruled.

## Why the shell was worth having

Because legitimacy is a real asset and the alternative was expensive.

A Maratha general or a Company official who governed in the emperor's name was
doing something the local revenue officials, the *zamindars* and the courts
already knew how to recognise. Somebody governing in his own name would have had
to explain himself to all of them, in every district, and enforce the
explanation.

That is the same arrangement the Abbasid caliphate had in Baghdad after the
Buyids, four hundred years earlier and two thousand miles west: an office
retaining its ceremony long after it had stopped governing, useful to everyone
who took power precisely because it was powerless and could not object.

## 1857

The end came because somebody took the fiction seriously.

When the sepoy regiments mutinied in May 1857 they marched to Delhi and
proclaimed Bahadur Shah Zafar — an eighty-two-year-old poet who had been a
British pensioner in his own palace, whose authority ran to the Red Fort and not
beyond it — as emperor of Hindustan.

He did not want it. He had no forces, no money and no illusions. But the rebels
needed a name that everyone from Bengal to the Punjab already accepted, and
there was only one, which is a measure of how much residual value the office
still had after a century of meaning nothing.

The British retook Delhi in September, shot his sons, tried him for treason
against a crown he had never sworn to, and exiled him to Rangoon, where he died
in 1862. The Company was abolished in the same settlement and India passed to
the Crown; Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India in 1877, taking the title
the Mughals had held.

<Figure id="mg-zafar" />

Bahadur Shah wrote in Urdu, and some of the ghazals attributed to him are still
sung.

## What is actually inherited

The buildings, most obviously — the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort, Fatehpur Sikri,
Humayun's tomb, the Lahore fort — and a style that regional courts across the
subcontinent copied for two centuries.

<Figure id="mg-taj" />

The administrative geography. The Mughal *subas* are the ancestors of the
provinces the British administered and, in modified form, of several modern
Indian states. The revenue settlements the British made in the nineteenth
century were built on Mughal registers and Mughal categories, which is why the
land records of large parts of India still carry terms from Akbar's survey.

The language, which the previous empire started and this one carried: Urdu
acquired its literature at the Mughal and successor courts, and Persian remained
the language of government until 1837.

And an argument that has not stopped. Whether the empire was an Indian state
that happened to be ruled by Muslims or a Muslim state ruling Indians is a
question about the seventeenth century that is fought over in the twenty-first,
in politics rather than in journals, by people for whom Akbar's *jizya*
abolition and Aurangzeb's reimposition of it are positions rather than events.

That is what happens to an empire that lasted long enough to be everybody's
ancestor.
