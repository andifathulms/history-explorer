Long before they had a monarchy, the people later remembered as Toltecs
were exiles on the move - driven, tradition holds, from a homeland
called Huehue Tlapallan, and led for generations by rebel princes and a
council of seven chiefs who answered, in matters of real importance, to
an aged prophet named Hueman. They settled for a time at Tulancingo,
building a single house large enough to shelter the whole community
while they sent out smaller parties to found satellite settlements
nearby, as they had done at every previous stop on their long migration.

## A capital taken from its own earlier inhabitants

On Hueman's own counsel, the wanderers moved on to a river valley
already held by an Otomí settlement, and there founded the city of
Tollan. Colonial-era chronicles pass in near silence over how the land
actually changed hands - it strains belief that the valley's own
previous inhabitants yielded it without a fight, yet no account of such
a struggle survives, an omission historians attribute to native annalists
simply choosing not to record it. What the sources agree on is what came
next: six years spent building the new city and cultivating the
surrounding land, under a government that was, in its own structure,
a theocratic republic - each of the seven chiefs commanding his own
people in war and peace, but all of them yielding, in matters of
national importance, to the prophet's own spiritual authority.

## A republic that voted itself out of existence

In the seventh year of peace and prosperity at Tollan, the chiefs
convened an assembly of the heads of every family and the community's
leading men. Their proposal was to abolish the collective government
they themselves led, fearing that a commonwealth answering to so many
independent chieftains would eventually tear itself apart, and to
replace it with a single monarch chosen by election - offering, in the
same breath, to surrender their own standing as the price of stability.
The assembly agreed.
