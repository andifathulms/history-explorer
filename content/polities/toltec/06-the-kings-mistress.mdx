A Toltec noble named Papantzin came to Huemac II's own court one day
with his daughter Xochitl, bringing the king a new sweet syrup made
from maguey sap that Papantzin himself had invented. The king was
pleased enough with the gift, and with the young woman who brought it,
to ask that she return with more of it - alone, save for a single
attendant. Papantzin, proud of the honour shown his family, suspected
nothing. Xochitl resisted the king's advances when she came, but was
compelled by threats to submit, and was kept afterward, cut off from
her own family, in a guarded palace near the capital under the pretext
that she was being groomed for a great marriage.

## A secret that produced an heir

The affair lasted three years in secret, during which, in 1002, Xochitl
bore Huemac a son, named at birth Meconetzin - "child of the maguey" -
and known later as Acxitl. Papantzin eventually learned the truth by
disguising himself and visiting the palace directly; his anger was
calmed only by Huemac's promise that the boy would be named heir to the
throne, since the queen had given the king only daughters. The
Tezcatlipoca priesthood, no friend to a king of Quetzalcoatl's own
religious line, spread the scandal as widely as it could, and Toltec
faith in a monarch reputed for his own piety was badly shaken.

## A succession the highest-ranking princes would not accept quietly

After the queen's death, Xochitl and her son were brought openly into
the royal palace, and some accounts hold she was made Huemac's own
legitimate wife by formal marriage. None of this settled the deeper
problem: naming Acxitl heir meant disinheriting princes of higher rank
who had expected the succession for themselves, and their resentment,
compounded by the scandal's own damage to the king's reputation, left
the throne of Tollan resting on foundations considerably less secure
than the ones Huemac had inherited.
