Rather than raise one of their own seven chiefs to the new throne,
Tollan's assembly sent an embassy laden with gifts to the distant court
of Huehue Tlapallan, the homeland the Toltecs themselves had been exiled
from generations earlier, asking for a prince of that court's own royal
line to come and reign over them. The mission succeeded: a young son of
the Tlapallan king, still largely unknown in his own country, was
brought back and crowned at Tollan under the name Chalchiuh Tlatonac,
"shining precious stone" - his personal charm and intelligence, the
chronicles say, winning the new subjects over almost immediately.

## A throne no one could hold forever

One of the new king's first acts, together with his own counsellors, was
to set a limit on his own office that no later Toltec ruler would be
free to ignore: no king was to reign more than fifty-two years - one
full turn of the Mesoamerican calendar round - after which he was
required to abdicate in favour of his eldest son, continuing afterward
only as an adviser rather than a ruler. Should a king die before his own
fifty-two years were up, the unexpired term was to be governed by
magistrates chosen by the people rather than left to an underage or
unready heir.

## A capital that outgrew the plan that founded it

Tollan's own transformation from a republic of exiles into a monarchy
with a written rule of succession happened, by the chronology Bancroft's
own sources suggest, sometime in the first quarter of the eighth
century, between roughly 710 and 720. What began as a modest new
settlement on contested Otomí land would, within less than two
centuries, become the city every later Mesoamerican civilization - the
Aztecs prominent among them - looked back on as the source of
legitimate kingship itself.
