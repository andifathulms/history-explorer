title: A king who would not shed blood

Quetzalcoatl's own religious reforms, popular as they were among much
of the Toltec population, made him powerful enemies from the very start
of his reign. Human sacrifice, practiced since long before Tollan
existed and adopted widely across the Toltec world, he prohibited
outright within his own capital's temples - a direct affront to the
established priesthoods of rival cities, who saw a king who was also
their own religion's supreme pontiff turning his combined authority
against the rites that had always underpinned it. Toltec nobles, too,
grew restive under a degree of priestly restraint their counterparts in
other cities did not have to answer to.

## An opposition led by his own rival priest

The dissident faction at Tollan coalesced around a figure the chronicles
call Huemac, also known by the name Tezcatlipoca - a rival high priest
with, the sources suggest, a plausible claim of his own to the throne,
and the backing of allied monarchs uneasy at how thoroughly Tollan's own
prestige had come to eclipse theirs. Colonial-era accounts remember the
struggle between the two men and their factions in the language of
sorcerers' tricks and supernatural contests, but the underlying conflict
was concrete enough: whether human sacrifice would remain central to
Toltec religion, and whether Tollan's own primacy would continue
unchallenged.

## A throne surrendered rather than defended by force

After roughly two decades on the throne, and with his own more warlike
supporters urging him to fight for his position, Quetzalcoatl chose
instead to give it up. Rather than preside over a restoration of the
sacrifices he had banned, he left Tollan by his own choice, staying
briefly at Quauhtitlan before crossing east to the plateau of
Huitzilapan in 895. Huemac succeeded him immediately as ruler of Tollan
- and the faction Quetzalcoatl had spent a reign trying to suppress
inherited the city his own reforms had failed to make permanent.
