By the close of the tenth century Tollan stood, in art and learning, at
the head of the whole Toltec world, though the older political
precedence of Culhuacan lingered in formal terms even as its practical
weight faded. Sources give no data precise enough to draw a firm
boundary around the domain the allied Toltec monarchs actually
controlled at this height, but its reach is generally reckoned to have
matched, and in some directions exceeded, what the Aztec empire would
later hold - stretching into Michoacán and a wide stretch of the
north-west that no later Nahua power would fully master either.

## A king who inherited a rival's name

The throne passed in 994 to a second king remembered as Huemac -
distinguished from the priest-rival of Quetzalcoatl's own generation by
the numeral historians attach to him, Huemac II, also called
Tecpancaltzin. Unlike his namesake, this Huemac belonged to
Quetzalcoatl's own religious faction rather than opposing it, restraining
the practice of human sacrifice in Tollan's temples much as his
predecessor had, and by some accounts even taking Quetzalcoatl's own
name as part of his royal titles - a fact that has left later
chroniclers genuinely unable to say, in places, whether a given episode
belongs to this reign or to the earlier one.

## A promising start weighed down by old grievances

Huemac II's first year on the throne won him, by every account, the
same kind of popular trust his more famous predecessor had once
enjoyed: careful in his religious duties, sparing with his words, prompt
and impartial in dispensing justice to subjects of every rank. But the
old rivalry between Quetzalcoatl's followers and the partisans of the
bloodier Tezcatlipoca cult, reduced to a minority but never
extinguished, had not gone away - and its adherents, unable to match
Huemac's own popularity directly, set about looking for a way to bring
him down by more indirect means.
