John II Komnenos, Alexios's son, ruled for twenty-four years and left
almost no record of the scandal, treachery or self-indulgence that
fills the reigns on either side of his. Even hostile Latin writers
conceded his virtue, and his own subjects nicknamed him first
Maurojoannes - dark John, for his complexion - and then, as his
government won them over, Kalojoannes: John the Good.

## A friend found outside his own family

John came to the throne to find the plot that would define his
reign's one real danger already forming around him: his mother
Irene, who had never wanted him to succeed, and his own elder
sister Anna Komnene - the future historian - conspiring to put
Anna's husband on the throne instead. John survived it by trusting
almost no one born into his own class. His closest advisor and
eventual prime minister was Axouchos, a Turkish boy captured as a
child at the fall of Nicaea in 1097 and raised alongside John in the
palace; the two became genuinely close friends, and John made him
the most powerful minister in the empire once he was old enough to
rule - a scandal to the aristocracy that says as much about their own
corruption as about John's judgment.

## A sister who wanted him blinded

Anna's own conspiracy, organized to put her husband Nikephoros
Bryennios on the throne in John's place, required either John's
murder or his blinding to disqualify him from rule. When Bryennios
balked at the plan, Anna reportedly denounced his hesitation as
womanish weakness, contrasting it with what she called her own manly
resolve. The plot was discovered and John, characteristically,
declined to execute his own sister or brother-in-law - he confiscated
Anna's property instead, a mercy remarkable enough that later writers
recorded it as unusual for a Byzantine court used to blinding its
rivals as a matter of course.

## A reign of steady, unglamorous recovery

John spent his twenty-four years pushing the recovery Alexios had
begun further outward - campaigns against the Pechenegs, the
Hungarians and the Serbs on the European frontiers, and a long series
of expeditions into Anatolia and northern Syria that recovered
Byzantine authority over Cilicia and forced the Latin principality of
Antioch to acknowledge Byzantine overlordship, the same campaign that
carried off the Rubenid prince Levon to Constantinople in 1137. None
of it was as dramatic as Manzikert or as celebrated as Basil II's
conquest of Bulgaria, and that is precisely why it is easy to skip:
John's whole method was patient, well-financed, unspectacular
soldiering rather than the kind of single decisive battle histories
remember.

## A wound from his own quiver

John died camped near Anazarbos in Cilicia in April 1143, preparing a
spring campaign into Syria. Hunting a wild boar on Mount Taurus, he
was gored, and in the struggle a poisoned arrow from his own quiver
scratched his hand. He ignored the wound until his arm had swollen
past the point where amputation could still save him, and spent his
final days arranging a peaceful transfer of power to his youngest
son, Manuel, whose talents he judged superior to his elder surviving
brother's. He died, by every account, as calmly as he had ruled.
