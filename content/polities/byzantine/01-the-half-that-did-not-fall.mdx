The two halves of the Roman empire were divided in 395 between two boys and
faced the same century. By 476 the western administration had ceased to exist and
the eastern one had not lost a province.

The difference is not that the east was better governed, and the contemporaries
who thought it was virtue were wrong. It is mostly geography and money.

## Four reasons

The western frontier is the Rhine and the upper Danube, running through open
country for well over a thousand kilometres with organised peoples on the far
side of all of it. The eastern land frontier in Europe is the lower Danube and
then, once past it, an attacker faces the Bosphorus. The Persian frontier in the
east was long but faced a state that wanted the same things Rome did and could
be negotiated with; this corpus's Sasanian record spent most of the fifth
century at peace with Constantinople, largely because both were dealing with
people coming out of the steppe.

The east was richer. Egypt, Syria and Asia Minor were the empire's most
productive provinces and the ones least disturbed in the third century. When the
western half lost Africa to the Vandals in 439 it lost its grain and its tax base
at once, and the army it could no longer pay was the army it needed to get them
back.

The east could buy its way out. Constantinople paid the Huns enormous annual
subsidies through the 440s, and paid Goths, Bulgars and Persians at various
points afterwards, and the western half is often criticised for the same policy
when it could afford it less. A rich state that pays is not weaker than a poor
state that fights; it is a rich state.

And the east had one city that could not be taken. Constantine's capital sat on a
peninsula with water on two sides, and in 413 the prefect Anthemius built the
land walls — a triple line with a moat, four and a half miles long — which were
not breached by an enemy for a thousand and forty years.

## What the west's fall was

It is worth being precise, because this record's neighbour on the site is the
Carolingian attempt to have it back.

The western provinces were not conquered in a campaign. Groups already inside the
empire, mostly serving in its armies under their own commanders on the model the
Roman record's last turning point describes, converted their commands into
kingdoms as the central government's ability to pay and to reassign them
disappeared. Britain fell out of the system around 410 and out of the coin
economy within a decade. Africa went in 439. Gaul and Spain were held by treaty
arrangements that became kingdoms without anybody declaring it. In 476 Odoacer
deposed the last western emperor and sent the regalia east with a note saying the
west did not need one.

The eastern government's view of all this, for the following century, was that
the western provinces were temporarily under improper management. The next
chapter is about the emperor who acted on it.
