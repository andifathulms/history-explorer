The word Byzantine was applied to this empire by a German scholar in 1557, a
century after it had ceased to exist. Its inhabitants called their state the
*Basileia Rhomaion*, the empire of the Romans, and called themselves *Rhomaioi*,
Romans, until the end. Their neighbours agreed: the Arabs called them *Rum*, and
the Seljuk sultanate in Anatolia took its name from the Roman ground it sat
on.

The renaming was not neutral. It let western European scholarship treat the
medieval Roman empire as a separate and lesser thing — oriental, static,
theocratic, in permanent decline for a thousand years — and reserve the Roman
inheritance for the Latin west. Gibbon's account, which shaped English usage for
two centuries, is the most influential version.

<Figure id="great-palace-mosaic" />

What the state actually did between 600 and 1025 was survive the loss of half
its territory, reorganise its army and taxation from the ground up, hold
Constantinople through two Arab sieges that would have ended it, convert the
Slavs and the Rus and give them an alphabet, and then reconquer Anatolia,
Bulgaria and northern Syria under a line of soldier-emperors. A polity in
continuous decline does not do that.

The Cyrillic alphabet comes out of that missionary work: a script devised for a
language that did not have one, transmitted outward deliberately, and still in
use by hundreds of millions of people who have never given the empire that sent
it a thought.
