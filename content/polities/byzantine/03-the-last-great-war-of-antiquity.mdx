Between 602 and 628 the eastern Roman empire and Sasanian Iran fought a war that
neither could afford and that destroyed both as they had existed.

The Persians took Syria in 613, Jerusalem in 614 — carrying off the relic of the
True Cross — Egypt by 619, and most of Anatolia. In 626 a Persian army stood on
the Asian shore opposite Constantinople while an Avar and Slav host assaulted the
land walls from Europe, and the city held.

Heraclius then did something no Roman emperor had done for centuries: he took
the field himself, went around the Persian armies rather than through them, and
campaigned into Iran, destroying the fire temple at Ganzak and beating a Persian
army at Nineveh in 627. Khusrau II was overthrown and killed by his own nobles,
the new government made peace, and in 630 Heraclius carried the Cross back into
Jerusalem in procession.

## A peak that was not the largest or the strongest

It is not the empire's largest extent, which was Justinian's, a century
earlier. It is not the empire's most effective moment,
which most accounts put three and a half centuries later under Basil II.

It is the last moment at which the two great powers of late antiquity were the
only powers that mattered, and the last Roman victory of the ancient world.
What follows is a different kind of history altogether.

It is worth saying plainly that a thousand-year state does not have one peak.
The empire of the tenth century, described five chapters on, was in most
respects more successful than this one — and it arrived two centuries into what
any account of the seventh and eighth centuries has to call decline. Any single
curve drawn through this much time will be wrong somewhere.

## Eight years

The war ended in 628 and the Arab armies took Syria in 636.

The usual observation is that both empires were exhausted, which is true and is
not sufficient — the Sasanian state collapsed entirely and this one did not, and
both were equally exhausted. What can be said with more confidence is narrower.
Twenty-six years of war had wrecked the fiscal apparatus of Syria and Egypt and
had moved the population of both through several changes of government. The
Monophysite Christians of Syria and Egypt had spent a century being coerced over
doctrine by Constantinople, most recently by Heraclius's own attempt to settle
it, and had no particular reason to prefer him. The field armies had been
fighting continuously for a generation.

And nobody in either empire had any idea what was coming, because there was
nothing in the previous four hundred years to suggest that anything could come
out of Arabia.

What happened next was the Arab conquest, and the empire had nothing left to
meet it with.
