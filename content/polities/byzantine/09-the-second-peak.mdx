Most empires have one best century. The eastern Roman empire had two, four
hundred years apart, with a catastrophe in between — and the second is the more
remarkable, because it came after the loss of Syria, Egypt and North Africa, and
after three centuries of holding a mountain frontier against raids it could not
stop.

## What happened in it

From the 860s the empire stopped defending and started taking.

The Taurus frontier that had held for three hundred years was pushed south. Crete
was retaken in 961 after a hundred and thirty years in Arab hands, and the Aegean
stopped being raided. Cilicia went in 965, and Antioch — lost in 638 — in 969,
with Byzantine armies operating in northern Syria and into the Jazira; the
Hamdanids of Aleppo spent those decades on the other side of it. The eastern
frontier reached the Euphrates and passed it.

Basil II reigned from 976 to 1025. He spent the first thirteen years suppressing
two enormous revolts by the Anatolian military families, and the next thirty
destroying the Bulgarian empire — a campaign of attrition rather than of battles,
conducted almost annually, that ended with Bulgaria annexed and the Danube
frontier restored for the first time since the sixth century. Armenia and Georgia
were absorbed or made client; Bagratid Armenia ended against this empire in 1045.

At his death the empire ran from the Danube to the Euphrates and from southern
Italy to the Caucasus, with a full treasury and no serious enemy on any frontier.

## Why it did not last

Fifty years later Anatolia was gone, and the standard explanations are internal.

The tenth-century laws against the *dynatoi* had failed. The great Anatolian
families had absorbed the smallholdings the theme system depended on, and the
soldiers those holdings produced were increasingly replaced by mercenaries paid
in coin. Basil's successors then cut the army to save money, in a period with no
visible threat, and let the frontier defences in Armenia go when they annexed the
buffer kingdoms that had been holding them.

Manzikert in 1071 is the date everyone knows, and the battle is not what lost
Anatolia. Alp Arslan captured the emperor, treated him well and released him on
terms; he wanted Syria and Egypt, and had come north because he was being raided.
What lost Anatolia was the civil war the defeat set off, in which rival Byzantine
claimants hired Turkish troops and paid them in land. By 1081 the plateau that
had supplied the empire's soldiers and taxes for four hundred years was held by
people who had been invited in.

An army that can make a ruler eventually makes one it prefers. The variation here
is that the army was somebody else's.
