title: The more astute had another reason
drafted_from: alexiad-dawes
phase: contraction
---

Anna Komnene wrote her father's life half a century after the First Crusade
reached Constantinople, and she is the only person who described that arrival
from inside the city. She is a partisan, a princess who had lost a throne, and
writing to defend a reign. She is also the only witness who says what the empire
thought was happening.

Her account of why they came is two sentences and it contains the whole
subsequent history of Byzantine-Latin relations. The simpler-minded, she says,
were urged on by a genuine desire to worship at the Sepulchre and see the holy
places. *But the more astute, especially men like Bohemond and those of like
mind, had another secret reason, namely, the hope that while on their travels
they might by some means be able to seize the capital itself, looking upon this
as a kind of corollary.*

That is written after 1204, by someone who had known Bohemond personally, in a
book about the years before it. It is not evidence that Bohemond intended to take
Constantinople in 1096. It is evidence of what a member of the imperial family
had concluded by the time she wrote, and of the assumption the government worked
on from the first day — which is why what the government actually did looks the
way it does.

What it did was manage them like weather. Officers were sent ahead to mark the
route and then follow the columns out of sight, with orders to come out and
skirmish if the Franks began foraging; and each detachment was given men who
spoke Latin, so that disputes could be settled before they became incidents. Anna
describes Peter the Hermit crossing first with numbers she gives as eighty
thousand foot and a hundred thousand horse, being advised by the emperor to wait
for the great lords, refusing because he trusted his own multitude, and being
destroyed. Godfrey refused for weeks to swear the oath of fealty and had to be
argued into it by his own colleagues, and then compelled.

Her general verdict on the Franks is the one every Byzantine writer after her
borrowed, and it is not stupid: *the Frankish race is always very hot-headed and
eager, but when once it has espoused a cause, it is uncontrollable.* She admires
the courage and describes the cruelty — the Normans who detached themselves and
devastated the country round Nicaea and did things to children she records in
detail — in the same paragraph.

The empire got what it wanted out of the crusade in the short run: Nicaea back,
the western Anatolian coast recovered, an enemy diverted. It also acquired, in the
same transaction, a permanent western belief that the Greeks were treacherous and
a permanent Greek belief that the Latins had come to take the city. Anna states
the second one as established fact in the middle of describing an alliance, which
is how far the relationship had travelled by the time she picked up her pen.
