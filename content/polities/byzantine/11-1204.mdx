The Fourth Crusade set out for Egypt, could not pay the Venetians for the fleet
it had commissioned, worked off part of the debt by taking a Christian city on
the Dalmatian coast for Venice, and then accepted an offer from a Byzantine
claimant who promised money and troops if they restored his father. They restored
him, he could not pay, the city rioted and killed him, and in April 1204 the
crusaders stormed Constantinople and sacked it for three days.

The city had not been taken by an enemy in eight hundred and seventy-four years.
The bronze horses now at San Marco came out of it, along with most of the
relics of western Europe and an unrecoverable quantity of manuscripts and
metalwork.

## What broke

The empire, into pieces. A Latin empire in Constantinople. A Greek government at
Nicaea, which retook the city in 1261. A second Greek state at Trebizond on the
Black Sea, which outlasted everything and fell in 1461. A despotate in Epirus.

What the restoration of 1261 did not restore is the part that matters here. The population did not
come back; fourteenth-century travellers describe fields and orchards inside the
walls. The treasury did not. And the commercial position did not, because the
concessions that Venice and then Genoa had been extracting since the 1080s in
exchange for naval help had by the fourteenth century given the Italian
republics the customs revenue of the straits.

The empire had lived on its land tax and the customs of the straits. By the
1340s a Byzantine emperor's customs revenue from
the Bosphorus was reportedly a small fraction of the Genoese colony's on the
opposite shore of the same waterway, and the empire fought a naval war with
Genoa over it and lost.

## The last choice

The civil wars of 1341 to 1354 are the last point at which the empire had a
choice about anything.

Two of them, fought over the regency for a nine-year-old emperor. Both sides
hired Turkish troops. One of them, John VI Kantakouzenos, married a daughter to
the Ottoman emir Orhan and ferried Ottoman soldiers into Europe to fight for him;
in 1354 an earthquake destroyed the walls of Gallipoli and the Ottomans, already
there, kept it. Serbia took Macedonia, Thessaly and Epirus while the empire was
occupied with itself, and Stefan Dušan had himself crowned emperor of the Serbs
and Greeks.

At the end of it the empire was Constantinople, a strip of Thrace, Thessalonica
and part of the Peloponnese, with an Ottoman state in Europe that it had brought
across.

The rest is the last chapter, and it takes ninety-nine years.
