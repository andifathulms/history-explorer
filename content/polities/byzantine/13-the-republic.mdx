Roughly half the emperors who reigned between 395 and 1453 were deposed. What
you make of that number depends entirely on what you think the empire was.

## The old picture

The standard account of Byzantine government, for most of the last two hundred
years, is a sacral autocracy: an emperor chosen by God, ruling without
constitutional limit, surrounded by a court ceremonial designed to make him
appear more than human, in a state where church and throne were fused and
politics consisted of palace intrigue. On that reading the depositions are
evidence of instability — a system that did not work.

## Kaldellis's argument

His case is that this gets the constitution backwards. The eastern Roman empire
was a *res publica* — the word is in the sources, in Greek as *politeia* — whose
emperor held power on behalf of the people and could lose it. The acclamation by
senate, army and people at every accession was not decoration but the
constitutive act. The emperor was not above the law in the sources' own account
of him.

And the depositions are not evidence of a broken system. They are what a
constitution without a formal removal mechanism looks like when it removes
somebody, and they follow a recognisable pattern: the emperor loses a war or
debases the coinage or raises taxes, the population of the capital riots, the
army or the senate acts, and a successor is acclaimed.

On this reading, half the emperors being deposed is not chaos. It is a republic
with an unusually violent recall procedure.

## What is not in dispute

The argument is live, and Kaldellis has serious critics. But the disagreement is
narrower than it looks, and it is worth being exact about where it falls.

Nobody disputes that every accession involved an acclamation, that the ceremony
was recorded and described in detail, that usurpers went out of their way to
obtain one, or that the sources treat an unacclaimed emperor as lacking
something. What is disputed is whether the acclamation had teeth — whether it was
a constitutional act or a ritual that had outlived its content.

That distinction matters here more than it might elsewhere, because the
alternative reading has a history of its own. The picture of Byzantium as an
oriental theocracy in permanent decline was built largely by western scholarship
that had reasons to want the Roman inheritance for the Latin west, and it is the
same scholarship that produced the name "Byzantine" for a state whose inhabitants
called themselves Romans until the day the walls came down.
