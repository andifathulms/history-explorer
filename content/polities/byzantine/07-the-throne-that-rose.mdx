---
title: The throne that rose
drafted_from: liudprand-wright

A north Italian bishop went to Constantinople twice, twenty years apart, and
wrote up both journeys for western readers. The first account is admiring. The
second is one of the most sustained insults in medieval literature. Together they
are the best description there is of how the empire used a room.

In 949 Liudprand was received by Constantine VII. In front of the throne stood a
gilt bronze tree, its branches full of gilt bronze birds, each of which sang the
cry of its own species. The throne itself was guarded by lions of bronze, or of
wood plated with gold, which beat the ground with their tails and roared with
open mouths and moving tongues. He was brought in on the shoulders of two eunuchs.
As he approached, the lions roared and the birds began to sing. He made obeisance
three times with his face on the floor, and when he lifted his head the emperor —
who had been sitting at a moderate height a moment earlier — had changed his
clothes and was sitting level with the ceiling. Liudprand could not work out how,
and guesses at a device like the one used for lifting the beam of a wine press.
The emperor did not speak to him; the distance, he explains, would have made
conversation unseemly, so a secretary asked after his master's health on the
emperor's behalf.

And then the line that gives the whole apparatus away. *I was neither terrified
nor surprised, for I had previously made enquiry about all these things from
people who were well acquainted with them.*

That is what the machinery was for and what it was worth. An audience at
Constantinople was a designed experience, and the design was well enough known
that a competent envoy could be briefed on it in advance and sit through it
unmoved — while still, twenty years later, describing every part of it in order.

The second embassy, in 968, was to Nikephoros Phokas on behalf of Otto I, and it
failed. Liudprand's account of it is an act of revenge. He is kept waiting,
lodged badly, lectured, and outranked by Bulgarian envoys; the emperor, he
reports, told him that the insult was not to Bishop Liudprand personally but to
his master. His indignation, he writes, was appeased by a handsome present: the
sacred emperor sent him one of his most delicate dishes, a fat goat that he had
eaten from himself, richly stuffed with garlic, onions and leeks and swimming in
fish sauce. *I wish, sires, that you could have had it on your table. The sight of
it, I am sure, would have banished any incredulity you have felt concerning the
sacred emperor's luxurious ways.*

He was made to dine there again eight days later, in ill health, and cross-
examined in front of the patriarch about which church councils the west
recognised.

Both visits describe the same institution and neither is neutral. In 949 he was
bringing a request and the ceremonial was marvellous; in 968 he was refused and it
was absurd. What survives the change of tone is the thing itself — a court that
had turned the reception of foreigners into a repeatable performance with
mechanical effects, a graded seating order that told every envoy what his ruler
was worth, and enough confidence to let a resentful bishop go home and write
whatever he liked about the goat.
