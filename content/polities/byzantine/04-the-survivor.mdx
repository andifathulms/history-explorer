---
title: The empire that lost half of itself and continued
drafted_from: treadgold-1997
phase: contraction
---

In 630 Heraclius returned the True Cross to Jerusalem, having just won a
twenty-six year war against Persia that had reached the walls of Constantinople.
Within twelve years Syria, Palestine, Egypt and North Africa were gone.

The losses were catastrophic in a way the numbers understate. Egypt was the grain
supply; Syria and Egypt together were the richest provinces and the demographic
core; and the Monophysite Christian populations of both had spent a century being
persecuted by Constantinople over doctrine and did not, on the whole, resist the
change of ruler. What remained was Anatolia, the Aegean, and a Balkan frontier
that Slavs and Bulgars were crossing at will.

The empire that emerged is a different thing from the one that entered. It was
smaller, poorer, Greek rather than Latin in administration, organised into
military districts — *themes* — where soldiers held land in exchange for service
because there was no longer cash to pay them, and permanently defensive on a
frontier that ran through central Anatolia for three hundred years.

It also did not fall, which at the time was the surprising outcome. Arab armies
besieged Constantinople in 674–678 and again in 717–718, and both failed. Had
either succeeded there would have been no Christian power in the eastern
Mediterranean, and the Balkans and Italy would have faced the caliphate directly.
The Byzantine survival is the single most consequential thing that did not happen
in this period.
