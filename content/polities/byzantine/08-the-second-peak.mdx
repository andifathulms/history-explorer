This chapter is tagged `contraction` and it describes the most successful period
in this record's thousand and fifty-eight years. The tag is wrong and it is the
only tag available, and that is worth explaining before anything else.

This site's chapters run on an arc — formation, expansion, peak, contraction,
end — which the build enforces as a sequence that cannot run backwards. It has
one peak. Hard rule 10 says the arc is a way of reading rather than a shape every
polity had, and this record is where that concession is needed: the eastern Roman
empire has two peaks, four hundred years apart, with a catastrophe between them,
and the third chapter of this record already took the tag.

So the tenth-century recovery is filed inside the contraction, and the reader
should know that the file name is a limitation of the model rather than a
judgement about the century.

## What happened in it

From the 860s the empire stopped defending and started taking.

The Taurus frontier that had held for three centuries was pushed south. Crete was
retaken in 961 after a hundred and thirty years in Arab hands and the Aegean
stopped being raided. Cilicia went in 965, Antioch — lost in 638 — in 969, and
Byzantine armies operated in northern Syria and into the Jazira; this corpus's
Hamdanid record is largely about being on the other side of that. The eastern
frontier reached the Euphrates and beyond.

Basil II reigned from 976 to 1025, spent the first thirteen years suppressing two
enormous revolts by the Anatolian military families, and the next thirty
destroying the Bulgarian empire — a campaign of attrition rather than of battles,
conducted almost annually, that ended with Bulgaria annexed and the Danube
frontier restored for the first time since the sixth century. Armenia and Georgia
were absorbed or made client; this site's Bagratid Armenia record ends against
this empire in 1045.

At his death the empire ran from the Danube to the Euphrates and from southern
Italy to the Caucasus, with a full treasury and no serious enemy on any frontier.

## Why it did not last

Fifty years later Anatolia was gone, and the standard explanations are internal.

The tenth-century laws against the *dynatoi* had failed: the great Anatolian
families had absorbed the smallholdings that the theme system depended on, and
the soldiers those holdings had produced were increasingly replaced by
mercenaries paid in coin. Basil's successors then cut the army to save money, in
a period with no visible threat, and let the frontier defences in Armenia go when
they annexed the buffer kingdoms that had held them.

This record's fourth turning point is Manzikert in 1071, and the point of the
entry is that the battle is not what lost Anatolia. Alp Arslan captured the
emperor, treated him well and released him on terms; he wanted Syria and Egypt
and had come north because he was being raided. What lost Anatolia was the civil
war the defeat set off, in which rival Byzantine claimants hired Turkish troops
and paid them in land, and by 1081 the plateau that had supplied this record's
soldiers and taxes for four hundred years was held by people who had been
invited in.

An army that can make a ruler eventually makes one it prefers, and this site's
Roman record and half its Islamic records say the same thing. Here the variation
is that the army was somebody else's.
