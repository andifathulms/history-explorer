Around 726 the emperor Leo III began removing religious images from public
display, and for the next hundred and seventeen years, with a gap, the official
position of the eastern Roman state was that representing Christ and the saints
in painting was idolatry.

The sources for it are almost all written by the winners after 843, which means
the standard narrative — a persecuting emperor, smashed icons, martyred monks —
comes from people with an interest in it having been that. What is reasonably
clear is that images were removed from churches and replaced with crosses and
ornament, that the policy had support in the army and in Anatolia, that it was
reversed in 787, reimposed in 815 and finally abandoned in 843, and that the
emperors who held it were among the most militarily successful of the period.

## Why an empire would do this

Several arguments are on the table and none of them is settled.

The theological one is that the second commandment means what it says and that a
church which had spent three centuries arguing about the nature of Christ had
never resolved what it meant to paint him. The political one is that the emperors
who took the position had just watched God hand Syria, Palestine, Egypt and North
Africa to an army whose own position on images was unambiguous, and drew a
conclusion. The institutional one is that icons and the monasteries that held
them were a source of authority the emperor did not control.

The last of those is the one worth dwelling on. An emperor whose right to rule
comes from God has an obvious interest in being the person who says what God
wants. Iconoclasm is the last occasion on which a Byzantine emperor tried to
settle a doctrinal question by imperial decree, and it is the occasion on which
that failed permanently.

## 843

The restoration was a council, a decree and a procession, and the settlement held
for the remaining six hundred years. The Sunday it happened is still kept in the
Orthodox calendar as the Triumph of Orthodoxy.

What was settled that day was not really the images. It was the shape of Orthodox practice was fixed there — a church defined by
its liturgy and its images, in which doctrine is settled by councils and not by
emperors — and that this is the church the missions of the following generation
carried out of the empire.

Everything the empire afterwards exported — the liturgy, the alphabet, the
church order — was fixed in that settlement. The next chapter is about where it
went.

## What the emperors kept

It would be wrong to leave this as a defeat for the throne.

The emperor went on appointing and deposing patriarchs, summoning councils,
legislating on church property and being crowned by the patriarch in Hagia
Sophia. What he could not do after 843 was define doctrine. That line — between
controlling the church and deciding what it taught — is where the Byzantine
emperor differed from the Sasanian king of kings and from the caliph, both of
whom claimed the second as well as the first.
