Constantinople fell on 29 May 1453 and within a few months its conqueror had
reinstalled the institution that had defined it, because he needed the city to
work.

Mehmed II wanted Constantinople repopulated. It had been emptying for a century
before he took it and the sack had finished the job, and a capital of empty
streets was no use to him. So he gave the Christians permission to follow the
customs of their churches — and, learning that they had no patriarch, ordered
them to choose one.

He promised in advance to accept whoever they chose, and that the man should
enjoy very nearly the same privileges as his predecessors.

## The scene at the seraglio

A local synod met and elected George Scholarios, who took the name Gennadios.
He was a monk, a scholar, and the leading opponent of the union with Rome that
the last emperors had negotiated in order to get western help.

The sultan received him at the palace and with his own hands gave him a pastoral
cross of silver and gold, and said:

> Be patriarch and be at peace. Count upon our friendship as long as thou
> desirest it, and thou shalt enjoy all the privileges of thy predecessors.

Then he had him mounted on a richly caparisoned horse and conducted in
procession to the Church of the Holy Apostles, which he gave him as the seat of
the patriarchate, as it had formerly been.

Afterwards, according to a contemporary Greek historian who wrote for Mehmed, the
sultan went on seeing him — discussing questions about Christianity, urging him
to speak freely, visiting him, and bringing along the learned men he had
collected at his court.

## What was actually being set up

Not toleration in the modern sense, and not a courtesy. An administration.

The patriarch became the head of the Orthodox population of the empire, with
authority over its law, its courts, its marriages, its taxes on itself and its
own internal discipline — an arrangement the Ottomans extended to other
confessions and which lasted, in one form or another, into the twentieth
century. The office survived the state that created it and then survived the
state that destroyed the state that created it.

That is the largest single thing Byzantium left behind. Not the claim to Rome,
which the sultan took for himself, and not the territory, which was gone. An
institution: the church of the empire, continuing inside the empire that had
conquered it, with its rank confirmed by the conqueror because a conqueror who
wants a working city needs somebody the population will obey.

## The building

There is a coda in a footnote, and it is the whole of the Ottoman century in one
sentence.

The Church of the Holy Apostles — the burial church of the emperors, where
Constantine and Justinian lay — was handed to Gennadios with ceremony as his
official residence.

It was subsequently taken back from the Greeks, demolished, and replaced by a
mosque built in honour of the conqueror.

The patriarchate moved, and moved again, and is still in the city. The church
that was given to it lasted about a decade.
