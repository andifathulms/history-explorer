The state that came out of the seventh century paid its army in land because it
no longer had the cash to pay it in coin.

The field armies that retreated out of Syria and Mesopotamia were quartered on
the Anatolian provinces they fell back into, and the districts they occupied
took the armies' names. *Thema* originally means a body of troops; within a
century it means the province the troops are in, under a general who is also the
civil governor. Soldiers held land, were entered on a register, served from it,
and passed the obligation with the land.

Haldon's argument, and the reason this record's `military_basis` is coded from
him, is that nobody designed this. There is no reform, no edict, no emperor's
name attached. It is what an administrative system does when it is retreating and
insolvent, rationalised afterwards into a structure.

## The same instrument, five times over

This corpus codes `land-grant` on a great many records and the Byzantine themes
are the earliest of the medieval versions.

The Buyid and Seljuk *iqta*, the Ottoman *timar*, the Byzantine *pronoia* on the
Nicaean record, the Mamluk system — all of them assign the revenue of land to a
soldier in return for service, and all of them arrive at it independently from
the same problem: a state that must field cavalry and cannot pay them in money.
This site's Nicaean record says the pronoia was reached independently in a Greek
state, and this chapter is where the Greek state reached it the first time.

The failure mode is also the same everywhere, and it is visible here from the
tenth century: land held for service becomes land held, the obligation attenuates,
and the great families accumulate the holdings of the smallholders whose service
the system depended on. A long series of tenth-century laws tried to stop the
*dynatoi* — the powerful — from buying up soldiers' land, and did not.

## What else was rebuilt

The tax register survived, and it is the reason the state did.

Everything else about the empire changed between 600 and 700 — the language of
administration went from Latin to Greek, the cities of Anatolia contracted into
hilltop fortresses, the coinage shrank, the senate faded, the consulship ended,
the old provincial structure dissolved — and the land tax went on being assessed
on registered holdings by a central bureau, in coin, without interruption. When
the empire needed to raise armies in the eighth century it could find out who
owed what.

This record's `revenue_basis` reads `land-tax, trade-toll`, and the second value
is the other thing a shrunken empire had: a capital on the straits, taking ten
per cent on everything that passed. A state reduced to Anatolia and the Aegean
still controlled the only sea route between the Mediterranean and the Black Sea.

## Two sieges

The Arabs besieged Constantinople in 674–678 and again in 717–718, and this
record's second turning point is the failure of the second.

Both failed on the walls, on the fleet and on the incendiary weapon the sources
call Greek fire, whose composition is not known and which was a state secret
successfully kept. The 718 siege ended with the Umayyad fleet largely destroyed
by storms and a Bulgar attack on the besieging camp, and this corpus's Umayyad
record dates the caliphate's turn away from Constantinople to the same year.

The Byzantine survival is the single most consequential thing in this period that
did not happen, and the existing fourth chapter of this record says so.
