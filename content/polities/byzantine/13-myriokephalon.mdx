Manuel I Komnenos inherited an empire near the height of its
Komnenian recovery and spent thirty-seven years spending that
inheritance on an ambition his father had never shared: restoring
Byzantine primacy over the whole eastern Mediterranean, Latin
crusader states included, rather than simply defending what
Alexios and John had rebuilt.

## A court that impressed and appalled its own guests

Manuel's reign was the Komnenian dynasty at its most theatrical.
He negotiated commercial treaties with Venice, Pisa and Genoa that
opened Constantinople's markets to Italian merchants on generous
terms; he intervened in Antioch and forced its Latin prince to
acknowledge him as overlord in a public ceremony; he fought a war
against the Norman kingdom of Sicily after Roger II's fleet sacked
Corinth and Thebes and carried off the city's silk-weavers as
captive labor for a new industry in Palermo. Manuel personally led
armies in Asia Minor, the Balkans and Egypt, married a Frankish
princess, Maria of Antioch, as his second wife, and ran a court that
Latin visitors described as the most magnificent in Christendom -
while Byzantine writers closer to home worried, with some
justification, that a middle class capable of restraining an
emperor's ambitions had stopped existing in the empire he ruled.

## An army committed to a narrow valley

Manuel's Anatolian ambitions ran directly against the Sultanate of
Rum, and in September 1176 he marched a large, heavily provisioned
army - three thousand supply wagons among the baggage train alone -
toward Konya itself, intending to end the sultanate as a threat for
good. Sultan Kilij Arslan II offered peace on the terms of an earlier
treaty as the army approached a ruined fortress called Myriokephalon;
Manuel refused to answer until he reached Konya and pressed on into
the long, narrow pass of Tzybritze without sending cavalry ahead to
clear the heights - a precaution the fate of the failed Second
Crusade's own armies, a generation earlier, should have taught him
to take.

## A column strangled in its own baggage train

The Turks had watched the whole approach from ambush along both
sides of the pass. Once the Byzantine baggage train reached the
valley's midpoint, they struck the front and rear simultaneously.
The right wing, commanded by the empress's own brother Baldwin, was
cut off from the rest of the army by the wagon train and annihilated
where it stood; the Turks then dragged the captured wagons across the
road to seal it and turned on the central division, where Manuel
commanded in person, surrounded by his guard. The valley was too
narrow for the heavy Byzantine cavalry - an arm that could have swept
the lighter Turkish horsemen aside on open ground - to form any kind
of line. Wagons, dead cattle, infantry and cavalry were crushed
together in a single choked mass while fresh Turkish squadrons kept
arriving from side ravines to renew the attack on both flanks at
once.

## A peace, and a mind that never recovered

The army survived the disaster rather than being destroyed outright -
the vanguard held the high ground at the pass's exit and Manuel
himself escaped the trap - but the losses and the humiliation ended
any Byzantine ambition of conquering Rum outright. Manuel negotiated
a peace with Kilij Arslan on terms neither side found fully
satisfying and never again attempted an offensive war on that scale.
Contemporaries agreed that his body recovered from Myriokephalon far
faster than his mind did: he grew withdrawn and melancholy in his
final years, and died in 1180 having spent the last of the
Komnenian dynasty's confidence in a single afternoon in a mountain
pass south of Konya.
