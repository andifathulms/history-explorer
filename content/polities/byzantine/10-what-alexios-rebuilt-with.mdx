Alexios I Komnenos took the throne in 1081 over an empire that had lost most
of what the second peak had won. Anatolia, the plateau that had produced four
centuries of soldiers and tax, was in the hands of Turkish beys who had been
invited in during the civil wars after Manzikert. Norman adventurers under
Robert Guiscard were crossing from Italy into the Balkans the same year.
Pechenegs were raiding south of the Danube. The treasury was empty enough
that Alexios melted down church plate to pay an army, and the coinage he
inherited had been debased for a generation of rival claimants each paying
troops with money the state did not have.

What he had to rebuild with was not soldiers or silver. It was land, and the
authority to assign its revenue to whoever would fight for it.

## Pronoia

The instrument Alexios and his successors relied on, developed and expanded
across the twelfth century, granted a named individual the tax revenue of a
specified piece of land - not the land itself, and not the peasants working
it as personal property, but the state's own claim on what it would
otherwise have collected - in exchange for military service, usually as a
cavalryman equipped and mounted at the grantee's own expense. The grant was
not hereditary in principle: it reverted to the state at the holder's death
and could be reassigned, which in theory kept the emperor able to reward
loyalty and withhold it from families who had none to offer.

In practice the distinction between a *pronoia* and inherited property blurred
within a few generations, exactly as it had with the theme lands three
centuries earlier and would again with the Ottoman *timar* two centuries
later - the same instrument arriving at the same failure mode each time it
was tried, because a family holding the same revenue for two generations
does not experience it as temporary even when the law says it is.

## What it bought, while it worked

For about a century it worked well enough to matter. Alexios used the
system, combined with a stabilised gold coinage - the *hyperpyron*,
introduced around 1092 after decades of debasement - and a network of
marriages binding the great Anatolian and Balkan families to his own house,
to assemble an army capable of holding what remained of the empire and
recovering some of what had been lost. Nicaea and the western Anatolian
coast came back within the reign, with crusader help that the next chapter
takes up in full. His son and grandson, John II and Manuel I, extended the
same methods for another sixty years, campaigning in Anatolia, the Balkans
and northern Syria with armies raised substantially on this basis.

## What it cost

The court that pronoia grants and dynastic marriage produced was not the
old Constantinopolitan bureaucracy the empire had run on before Manzikert.
It was a network of related noble families - Komnenoi, Doukai, Angeloi,
Palaiologoi, the names that fill the rest of Byzantine history - holding
military command and provincial revenue as something close to a family
business, with the emperor as the senior partner rather than an
administrator standing above them all.

That arrangement rebuilt an army out of an empire that had none left to
speak of, and it is why the twelfth century, rather than the years right
after Manzikert, is when Byzantium last mattered as a first-rank power. It
also planted the same aristocratic entrenchment the tenth-century laws
against the *dynatoi* had failed to stop, in a more concentrated and more
overtly dynastic form, and it is the family politics of exactly this class
that produces the civil wars and coups that run through most of what
follows.
