In 863 two brothers from Thessalonica, Constantine — later Cyril — and
Methodius, were sent by the emperor to Moravia at its prince's request. Before
going, Constantine devised an alphabet for the Slavonic language, which had none,
and began translating the liturgy and the scriptures into it.

The Moravian mission failed politically, driven out by Frankish clergy within a
generation. The alphabet did not. Their pupils took it to Bulgaria, where it was
adapted into the script now called Cyrillic; the Bulgarian church took it to
Serbia and to the Rus; and it is written today by something over two hundred and
fifty million people across a dozen countries.

## The clearest case on the site

Most claims that one alphabet descends from another have to be argued through
transitional inscriptions and contested datings. This one does not. The parent
script is Greek; the derivation is documented, named and dated; the transmission
was direct, by men an emperor sent; the language had no script before; and the
result is in daily use by a quarter of a billion people.

A named man devised a named script for a named mission in a known year, and it
is still being written.

## And the religion

The other export was the church itself, and it went to populations that had not
held it rather than being adopted at home.

The Bulgarian conversion in 864 is the political case: Boris played
Constantinople against Rome for several years, took baptism from
Constantinople, and got a church with its own archbishop and a liturgy in a
language his subjects spoke, which is a better offer than Latin. The Rus
conversion under Vladimir around 988 is the consequential one, and the story in
the Russian Primary Chronicle — envoys sent to compare religions, who report from
Hagia Sophia that they did not know whether they were in heaven or on earth — is
a legend about a real building that was, and is, exactly as overwhelming as they
said.

## What it bought

Not much, in the short term, which is worth saying on a site that counts these
things.

Bulgaria fought the empire repeatedly after its conversion and a Bulgarian tsar
was besieging Constantinople within sixty years of it. The Rus raided the city
four times. A shared religion did not produce a shared politics, and the next
chapter is about an emperor who spent thirty years destroying the Bulgarian state
his predecessors had converted.

What it did produce is the thing the coded fields register: a religious and
literary culture across eastern Europe that outlived the empire that exported it
by five hundred years and counting. The empire's most durable single export was
made by two missionaries whose mission failed.
