George Rákóczi succeeded Bethlem and governed successfully for years
before overreaching in pursuit of a crown outside his own borders,
allying with Sweden against Poland in the hope of adding the Polish
throne to his own. The gamble failed disastrously: Poles, Tartars and
Austrian troops sent by Emperor Leopold overwhelmed his forces, and the
Ottomans, furious at a vassal making war without their leave, moved to
depose him outright. Leopold, bound by treaty to help him, instead
abandoned Rákóczi entirely and let an Ottoman army destroy him in battle
near Kolozsvár.

## A throne handed from puppet to puppet

What followed was a decade of Ottoman-managed succession. The Turks
installed two short-lived princes in turn, then backed the ambitious
John Kemény, who seized power after Rákóczi's own underage son converted
to Catholicism and alienated the country's Protestant majority. Kemény's
appeal to Vienna for help against the Ottomans backfired: the German
troops Leopold sent proved as unwelcome to the Transylvanians as the
Ottomans themselves, and Kemény was eventually defeated and killed. The
Porte then settled on Michael Apafi, a compliant choice who would govern
- nominally as sovereign prince, in practice as an acknowledged Austrian
dependency in all but name - for the rest of his life.

<Figure id="tr-apafi" />

## A war neither Vienna nor Transylvania could fully control

Through Apafi's long reign Transylvania became less a participant in
its own affairs than a territory contested between Habsburg and Ottoman
armies each pursuing goals of their own. Austrian forces occupied it in
substance well before any treaty formally recognised the fact, and the
Transylvanian estates found their objections to foreign garrisons
ignored by both sides in turn.

## A settlement made over the principality's own head

The peace concluded between Austria and the Ottomans in November 1691
formalised what years of occupation had already made a practical
reality: Hungary, Slavonia and Transylvania were declared hereditary
possessions of the House of Austria, in a treaty this account calls the
most consequential in the whole history of the Habsburg dynasty. No
Transylvanian delegate held a genuine seat at the negotiations that
ended a century and a half of princely rule under alternating Ottoman
and Habsburg protection. The principality that an infant king's cradle
had first defined in 1541 ended in the same way it began - as a
territory disposed of by treaty between larger powers, with its own
voice heard by neither.
