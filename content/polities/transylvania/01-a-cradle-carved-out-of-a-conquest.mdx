Queen Isabella and her infant son John Sigismund were besieged in the
citadel of Buda in 1541 when Suleiman the Magnificent's own army,
having entered the city under pretence of a friendly visit, suddenly
seized every strongpoint and disarmed the garrison. Isabella's appeals
to have her friends released and her capital restored met a blunt
answer: she and her son's guardians would renounce all claim to the
wider Hungarian throne, and in exchange the infant would be invested
with the principality of Transylvania as a tributary of the Ottoman
Porte.

<Figure id="tr-isabella" />

## A regency inherited before it could be governed

Isabella, more attached to her son than to any crown, accepted without
resistance and set out for a territory the Transylvanian nobles at
first refused to let her enter, fearing Ottoman reprisal for sheltering
her. She had to call on Cardinal Martinuzzi - the same adviser she
feared and distrusted - to force her own entry into the principality she
now nominally ruled.

## A murdered regent and a temporary surrender

Martinuzzi's own ambition eventually proved fatal to him: suspected of
secretly favouring Ferdinand of Habsburg's rival claim, he was
assassinated by imperial agents, and shortly afterward Isabella was
pressured into a formal ceremony at Kolozsvár surrendering the crown
regalia to Ferdinand's representative outright - her young son reaching
for the glittering symbols of an authority he was too young to
understand he had just lost.

<Figure id="tr-martinuzzi" />

## Restored by the very army that had first confined her

The surrender did not last. Suleiman, furious that an effort had been
made to strip John Sigismund of the province he had personally
invested him with, marched two armies toward Hungary, and Isabella
hurried to rejoin the advancing Ottoman forces rather than remain under
Habsburg protection. The Austrian commander who had accepted her earlier
surrender found himself abandoned by a mutinous garrison and a hostile
population, and was driven out of the territory entirely. Mother and
son returned to govern the principality the Ottomans had created for
them in the first place - a reminder, from the very beginning, of whose
protection Transylvania's own independence actually rested on.
