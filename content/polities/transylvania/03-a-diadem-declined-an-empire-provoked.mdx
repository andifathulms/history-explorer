Stephen Bocskai drove the Austrians out of Transylvania in 1605 with
Ottoman men and money behind him, and returned to Hungary in triumph to
find the sultan's own army waiting to honour him on the plains of
Rákos. Sultan Ahmed sent him a club, a sabre and a standard; the grand
vizier placed on his head a diadem once worn by the despots of Serbia
and proclaimed him king of Hungary and prince of Transylvania in the
same breath.

## A crown too dangerous to accept

Bocskai judged the gift more dangerous than useful. Accepting Ottoman
coronation as king would have made him visibly dependent on the Porte
for a title his own country's nobility alone had the right to grant, so
he declined the kingship outright, treating the sultan's gesture as
nothing more than a token of goodwill. It was a small act of
self-restraint with a large consequence: Transylvania's princes
continued to derive their legitimacy from their own estates rather than
from an outright Ottoman coronation, even while depending on Ottoman
protection in every practical sense.

## A widow's kingdom passed to an able soldier

Bocskai's own line did not long survive him, and the principality passed
eventually to Gabriel Bethlem, a soldier and statesman whose right to
the throne earlier rivals had already tacitly recognised before his
formal election. Bethlem proved the most capable prince Transylvania
would produce - rebuilding its finances, its army and its diplomatic
standing across a Europe increasingly consumed by religious war.

## A Transylvanian army decides a German battle

When Bohemia's Protestant nobility deposed their Habsburg king and
offered the crown to the Elector Palatine Frederick, Bethlem answered
the Hungarian Protestants' own call for support by invading Hungary,
seizing Pressburg by treachery, and capturing the royal regalia
outright - forcing Ferdinand II to fight a war on two fronts at once.
The consequences reached Bohemia itself: distracted by Bethlem's own
campaign, Frederick's forces were routed at the White Mountain outside
Prague, losing five thousand men, a hundred standards and all their
artillery in barely an hour. Bethlem's own war against Austria
continued for years afterward, forcing repeated truces on an emperor
who could never fully turn his attention away from Transylvania's
eastern flank - proof that a principality built from an infant's
consolation prize had grown capable of shaping the outcome of wars far
larger than its own.
