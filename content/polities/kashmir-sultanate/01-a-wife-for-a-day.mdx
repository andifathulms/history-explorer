Islam entered the Kashmir valley through an adventurer rather than a
conquest. Shah Mirza arrived from Swat in 1315 and entered the service of
Sinha Deva, a local chieftain who then ruled the valley. Sinha Deva was
overthrown and killed by his own minister, Rainchan, a Tibetan who accepted
Islam — probably at Shah Mirza's own suggestion — and made his adviser
tutor to his children, trusting him with exactly the kind of long-term
access to the succession that later proved fatal to his own family's hold
on the throne.

## A widow's mistake

When Rainchan died, a scion of the old royal house, Udayana Deva, returned
from exile, married Rainchan's widow Kota Devi, and took the throne
himself. He reigned fifteen years. On his death, the widowed queen again
turned to Shah Mirza — by now a minister of long and trusted standing — and
asked him to place her own son on the throne. It was, after decades of
faithful service, one request too many for a man who had already built a
faction of his own inside the court and had no further interest in
remaining a kingmaker rather than a king.

## What happened when he refused

The accounts of what followed disagree on the sequence but not the
outcome. One version has Shah Mirza proposing marriage to the widowed
queen, who killed herself rather than accept; the more probable account,
Haig judges, is that when he hesitated to obey her command outright, she
raised an army against him — and lost. Shah Mirza forcibly married her,
imprisoned her within twenty-four hours of the wedding, and ascended the
throne in 1346 under the title Shams-ud-din Shah. Decades of patient
service, three successive rulers outlasted, and a queen's own miscalculated
appeal had converted a foreign adventurer's influence into an outright
dynasty.

## A gentler hand than his predecessors

Whatever the manner of his accession, Shah Mir governed with a restraint
the Hindu kings before him had rarely shown. Where they had left their
subjects, by Haig's account, "nothing beyond a bare subsistence," Shah Mir
abolished the arbitrary taxes and cruel collection methods of the old
regime and fixed the crown's share of the land's produce at a flat
one-sixth — a founder who had taken his throne by force and then governed,
for the three years remaining to him, as though force were not the only
tool available to a king.
