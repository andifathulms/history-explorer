Zain-ul-Abidin's death in the winter of 1470 did not just end a reign; it
ended, in every way that mattered, the rule of the family he belonged to.
His own account of the Chakk clan was that they were already a menace to
public order serious enough that he had expelled them from the valley
outright. Under the feebler kings who followed him they drifted back, and
for the better part of a century Kashmir's throne belonged, in substance,
to whichever Chakk chieftain could out-manoeuvre his own kinsmen for the
year.

## A century of puppets

The pattern set in almost immediately. Zain-ul-Abidin's own sons fought
each other for the throne before and after his death; his grandson Hasan
Shah divided the tutoring of his two young heirs between rival factions
as though impartiality between them were a policy rather than an
abdication of one, and watched the resulting rivalry consume the court
around him. A ten-year Mughal occupation under Mirza Haidar Dughlat came
and went in the 1540s, ended by a night attack that killed its own
commander by the hands of his own men. Through all of it, the office of
kingmaker passed between Chakk chieftains — Kaji Chakk, then Ghazi Khan —
who found it more useful for decades to rule through a succession of
puppet Shah Miri princes than to wear the crown themselves.

## Ghazi Khan stops pretending

Ghazi Khan was the one who ended the pretence. Having already crushed
rebellions, executed his own conspiring son, and deposed one puppet king
after another, he dethroned and imprisoned the reigning Habib Shah in
1561 and took the throne in his own name as Ghazi Shah. The house Shah
Mirza had founded in 1346 had held the crown for two hundred and fifteen
years, but for the last ninety of them, since Zain-ul-Abidin's death, it
had held no real authority at all — Ghazi Shah's coronation only made
official what the Chakk clan's chieftains had already been for two
generations.

## A leper's throne, and the family quarrels resume

Ghazi Shah's own reign closed in the same pattern it had exploited: a
military failure in Tibet cost him his son's loyalty, and leprosy cost
him his fingers and then his sight. He abdicated to his half-brother
Husain Shah in 1563-64, tried unsuccessfully to reclaim the throne
when he disagreed with a decision of his successor's, and died,
Haig records, of grief after his own son was blinded for conspiring
against the new king. Husain Shah's own reign ran through further
rebellions, a religious dispute over a wounded qazi that drew in one of
Akbar's own envoys, and ended in 1569-70 when his brother Ali Shah raised
troops against him while he lay weakened by dysentery and took the
crown without a fight. The clan that had spent a century making and
unmaking Shah Miri princes had, by unmaking itself with the same
regularity, made its own hold on the throne no steadier than the one
it replaced.
