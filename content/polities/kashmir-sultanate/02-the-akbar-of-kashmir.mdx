Zain-ul-Abidin took the throne in June 1420 and held it for fifty years —
long enough, and governed well enough, that Haig reaches for the same
comparison every historian of Kashmir eventually makes: he was, in
substance if not in scale, the Akbar of Kashmir, a full century before the
Mughal emperor whose name attached to the comparison was even born.

## What his father had broken, he mended

His own father, Sikandar, had earned the epithet "the Iconoclast" for a
reign of religious severity that left Kashmir with no more than eleven
Brahman families still openly practising their faith. Zain-ul-Abidin
recalled the exiles, let those who had only feigned conversion to Islam
return openly to their old religion, released prisoners held under his
father's harsher laws, and abolished the jizya, the poll tax on
non-Muslims, outright. He restored temples his predecessors had left in
ruin, and — in the one point of genuine continuity with his father's
reign — admitted learned Hindus and Brahmans to his own court on the same
terms as Muslim scholars, a taste for mixed company Sikandar had shared
even while persecuting the community it was drawn from.

## Roads, water, and a currency worth trusting

His public works reached further than piety. He founded a new city,
bridged rivers, and built irrigation channels that carried water to nearly
every village in the valley — work he had convicted criminals perform in
place of the execution his predecessors' penal codes would otherwise have
demanded. He made village communities collectively responsible for crimes
committed on their land, restored a currency his predecessors had debased
by melting down idols of inconsistent purity into coin, and financed most
of his government from the produce of his own royal mines rather than
squeezing his subjects for it.

## A scholar-king with an unlikely correspondence

He was fluent in Persian, Hindi and Tibetan on top of his own language,
had the Mahabharata and the Rajatarangini — the only work in Sanskrit
generally regarded as genuine history — translated into Persian, and kept
up friendly correspondence with a roster of contemporaries that crossed
every political and religious line in reach: Bahlul Lodi of Delhi, Mahmud
Begada of Gujarat, the Mamluk sultans of Egypt, the Sharif of Mecca, and a
Hindu raja of Gwalior with whom he shared nothing but a love of music.

## What tolerance did not extend to his own sons

None of this serenity survived intact into his own family. Three sons —
Adam Khan, Haji Khan and Bahram Khan — spent his last years locked in
rivalry violent enough that the one occasion Haig records him abandoning
his own character was directed at them: defeating a rebellious Haji Khan
in the field, he massacred his prisoners and built a column of their
skulls, a cruelty foreign to everything else known about him. He refused
his counsellors' pleas to abdicate in favour of one son while the rivalry
still raged, and only settled the question by outliving it: when he died
in the winter of 1470, Haji Khan, restored to favour after his own period
of exile and rebellion, ascended the throne without opposition as Haidar
Shah — the one clean succession his fifty-year reign, for all its
enlightenment, was never quite able to guarantee in advance.
