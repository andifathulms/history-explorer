Sikandar came to the throne as a child in 1394, placed there by his
own mother Sura after her husband Qutb-ud-din's death - and secured on
it, in the account Haig records, by her own hand: she had her
daughter and son-in-law put to death to remove any rival claim. A
Hindu courtier, Rai Madari, is said to have poisoned Sikandar's
brother Haibat Khan at her instigation as well, though the plot
provoked the young king's own fury rather than his gratitude; Rai
Madari fled into a command in Little Tibet, rebelled there once he had
occupied it, and was captured and driven to suicide in prison when
Sikandar marched against him.

## A conqueror who never arrived

In 1398 Timur, then at Delhi and preparing to withdraw along the
Himalayan foothills, sent envoys to Sikandar, who received them
courteously and set out to wait on the conqueror in person. His own
rapacious courtiers, without Timur's knowledge, demanded thirty
thousand horses and a hundred thousand gold dirhams from the Kashmiri
embassy - a sum well beyond what Sikandar had brought as tribute, and
he turned back toward Srinagar rather than arrive empty-handed. Timur,
learning of his own officers' extortion, sent word inviting Sikandar
to meet him on the Indus without fear of further demands; Sikandar set
out a second time, but by the time he reached Baramulla, Timur had
already abandoned the Indian frontier for Samarkand, and the meeting
that might have defined his reign never happened.

## A kingdom's religion remade by decree

What did define his reign was a turn no earlier Muslim king of
Kashmir had taken. His predecessors, Haig notes, had been careless of
their subjects' religion and free of persecuting zeal; Sikandar,
surrounded by scholars drawn from Persia, Arabia and Mesopotamia and
driven on, Haig suggests, by their own exhortations, destroyed the
valley's most famous Hindu temples and melted their idols into coin.
His minister Sinha Bhat, a converted Brahman, sustained the campaign
with a convert's own zeal, and after banning the burning of widows
along with a range of other Hindu rites, Sikandar finally gave the
valley's Brahmans a choice between Islam and exile. Some fled; some
took their own lives rather than abandon either faith or home; many
more converted. The scale of what followed is still legible in the
numbers Haig cites for his own time: barely five hundred Hindus in
every ten thousand people in a valley that had been overwhelmingly
Hindu before Sikandar's reign began. He died in 1416, remembered by
the title his own zeal had earned him - Butshikan, the Iconoclast.

## A troubled heir, and the throne that passed him by

His eldest son, 'Ali Shah, inherited a kingdom that did not stay
settled. Wanting to make the pilgrimage to Mecca, 'Ali Shah named his
own brother Shahi Khan regent and left the country - only to be talked
out of the journey by his own father-in-law before he had gone far,
and to return and drive Shahi Khan from the throne by force instead.
Shahi Khan took refuge with Jasrat, chief of the Khokar tribe, and
when 'Ali Shah marched against them he exhausted his own army on a
forced march through the hills, where Jasrat's forces caught it
unprepared and destroyed it. 'Ali Shah was never heard from again.
Shahi Khan returned to Srinagar in June 1420 and took the throne under
the name his own reign would make famous: Zain-ul-Abidin.
