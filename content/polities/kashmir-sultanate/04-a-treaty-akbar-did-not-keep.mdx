Ali Shah's son Yusuf Shah inherited a throne in 1579 that was, by then,
barely his to keep. Within his first years he put down a rebellion led by
his own uncle, watched a Sayyid noble usurp the crown outright and then
lose it in a counter-rebellion, and was finally driven out himself when
his cousin Lohar Chakk seized the throne in his place.

## A throne recovered with borrowed authority

Yusuf did not try to retake Kashmir with an army of his own. He travelled
to Akbar's court at Fathpur-Sikri in January 1580 and asked for the
emperor's help. He left with an imperial order directing Mughal officers
in the Punjab to back him — and before that force ever had to march, word
that Akbar was prepared to aid him was enough on its own to bring
Kashmir's nobles back to Yusuf's side. He returned alone, defeated and
captured Lohar Chakk at Sopur that November, and had his throne back
without a single Mughal soldier crossing the passes. It was a debt he
could not fully repay: he had recovered the crown through Akbar's
reputation rather than Akbar's army, which let him tell himself he ruled
as an independent king, while the court that had lent him that
reputation took a very different view.

## The homage he kept failing to pay in person

For years afterward, Yusuf managed the relationship at arm's length,
sending sons and envoys to Akbar's court in his own place rather than
appearing himself, and the absence was noticed each time. When his elder
son fled the imperial camp fearing for his life, and Yusuf's own nobles
talked him out of going in person to smooth things over, Akbar stopped
treating him as anything other than a defiant vassal. An army under Raja
Bhagwan Das marched on Kashmir; Yusuf held the mountain passes against
it long enough that the raja, unwilling to risk a winter campaign in the
hills, agreed to a peace in exchange for Yusuf's promise to present
himself at court.

## A promise kept, and a promise broken

Yusuf kept his side of it. He appeared before Akbar on 7 April 1586 to
fulfil the terms his own general had negotiated on his behalf — and Akbar
refused to honour the treaty Bhagwan Das had made, detaining Yusuf as a
prisoner the moment he arrived. Bhagwan Das, whose word had been given in
good faith and broken by his own emperor, took his own life over the
dishonour. Yusuf's elder son Yaqub stayed behind in Kashmir and held out
against the imperial administration for more than two years of guerrilla
resistance before finally submitting to Akbar in person in August 1589.

## What became of the man himself

Haig calls Akbar's treatment of Yusuf one of the chief blots on his
reputation. After a year in captivity Yusuf was released with a modest
fief in Bihar and command of five hundred horse — a rank he never rose
above, serving under Man Singh in Bengal, Orissa and Chota Nagpur in the
years that followed, the last king of an independent Kashmir living out
his life as one more officer in the army of the empire that had ended it.
