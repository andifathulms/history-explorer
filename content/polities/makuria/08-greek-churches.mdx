In 1813 John Lewis Burckhardt went up the Nubian Nile disguised as a poor Syrian
trader, on very little money, writing down the stages and the prices. He passed
through the middle of what had been this kingdom, and he did not know it.

He mentions the remains four or five times, always in passing, always on his way
to somewhere else, and the mentions are among the better evidence for how much
of it there had been.

At Ibrim — the cliff fortress the kingdom's own archive would eventually be dug
out of — he found inside the walls *the remains of two public buildings, probably
Greek churches*, built in the same neat cut stone as the ancient wall, which he
took for late Roman work. Five and a half hours out of another stage he noted, on
the hills, *the ruins of several Greek churches*. That is the entire entry. He
does not stop.

And at Meharraka he came on an Egyptian temple with a portico of fourteen
columns, its southern wall lying on the ground in layers as though it had gone
over all at once. On the walls were *several paintings of Greek saints*, and
Greek inscriptions written in red ink, of which he could clearly read one.

## What he was looking at

Not Greek churches. Nubian ones.

Makuria built in a Byzantine idiom because its church came from Alexandria and
its books were in Greek, and to a traveller in 1813 the results looked Greek.
What Burckhardt rode past was the parish architecture of a Christian civilisation
that had held that river for seven hundred years and had been gone for four
hundred and fifty — standing on the hills in numbers casual enough that he lists
them the way one lists milestones, without curiosity, because he had no reason to
think they were anything but a provincial echo of Byzantium.

The temple at Meharraka is the whole history of the middle Nile in one building.
Pharaonic columns; a Nubian congregation's saints painted over them; Greek
graffiti in red ink; derelict in a Muslim country; recorded by a Swiss
Protestant travelling as a Muslim, who was interested in none of it.

## What was under the sand

The kingdom was recovered, almost entirely, in about fifteen years, and only
because it was about to be drowned.

The Aswan High Dam was going to flood two hundred and fifty miles of the Nile
valley, and in the 1960s an international salvage campaign went in ahead of the
water. At Faras a Polish team dug into a mound and found a cathedral, and under
the plaster of its walls a hundred and sixty-nine paintings — bishops named in
Greek and Old Nubian, saints, the three Hebrews in the furnace, and Nubian kings
and queen mothers with Christ standing behind them and a hand on the royal
shoulder. The paintings were lifted and divided between Warsaw and Khartoum.

At Qasr Ibrim, which stood high enough to become an island in the reservoir
rather than disappear under it, excavators found the kingdom's paperwork: land
sales, loans, accounts and private letters in Old Nubian, on parchment and paper,
much of it still unpublished.

Everything anybody knows about how Makuria actually worked — the money, the
contracts, the women buying land, the exchange rates — came out of that fortress,
and it came out because a dam was being built.

## The order of the recovery

It is worth stating the sequence plainly, because it is unusual.

A kingdom that beat two Arab armies, made a treaty that lasted six centuries, and
wrote its own language in its own script was, until the middle of the twentieth
century, a footnote in Arabic geography and a scatter of ruins that a passing
European catalogued as Greek. Then a reservoir forced the excavation, and the
cathedral and the archive came out of the ground in a rush, a decade before the
water.

Faras itself is under the lake now.
