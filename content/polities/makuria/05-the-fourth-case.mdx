The Makurian throne passed to the king's sister's son.

It is in the Arabic accounts, it is in the Nubian material, and it is visible in
the way the royal women are titled: the king's mother and the king's sister hold
formal position, carry their own styles, and appear in the wall paintings at
Faras standing beside the kings under the protection of the Virgin.

## Why a kingdom would do that

Al-Bakri, writing about Ghana in the eleventh century, gives the reason he was
given by people who practised it, and it is disarmingly practical: a man can be
certain that his sister's child is of his own blood, and cannot be certain of his
wife's.

That is a serious answer to a real problem. Every succession system has to
decide what it is actually tracking, and a system that tracks descent through
women is tracking something observable. The cost is that a king does not hand the
throne to his own son, which requires a court to accept that the ruler's children
are not the ruler's heirs — an arrangement most dynasties find intolerable and
some manage for centuries.

## Where else it turns up

Four times, on three continents, over about two and a half thousand years, with
no possibility of contact between the earliest case and the others.

**Elam**, in the third and second millennia BC, where the throne passed from
brother to brother and then to a sister's son.

**Ghana**, before 1068, where al-Bakri states the rule outright and supplies the
reasoning above.

**Makuria**, from the seventh century to the fourteenth.

**Mali**, in 1352, where Ibn Battuta records inheritance through the sister's son
as a general practice and disapproves of it at some length, and where the royal
succession shows the same mixed shape.

And later, in the Akan forest, the Asante, whose ruler succeeded through the
matriclan with the queen mother nominating from the eligible line and a council
of chiefs confirming or refusing — the best-documented version of the
arrangement anywhere, because it survived into a period when people wrote it down
in detail.

## What it did not mean

It did not mean the succession was unstable.

That is worth separating out, because an arrangement this unfamiliar invites the
assumption that it must have been fragile. Makuria's rule was known, followed,
and produced a kingdom that lasted seven hundred years on a frontier with the
Islamic world, which is longer than almost any European dynasty of the same
period managed with primogeniture.

What it did eventually mean was the opposite of instability, and much worse.
Arab men married Nubian women, and under Nubian rules their sons were Nubian
heirs — so the ruling families of the kingdom became Muslim by inheritance,
generation by generation, without a single battle being fought about it. The rule
that had kept the throne secure for seven centuries is one of the mechanisms by
which the kingdom stopped being Christian.
