This record's `descendant_scripts` count is 1 and the item is Old Nubian, which
makes it one of a handful of non-zero entries on that axis in the corpus and
puts it in the same structural position as the Aksumite record four hundred
kilometres east.

## What was done

The Nubians took the Coptic alphabet — Greek letters, with a few signs from
demotic Egyptian, used to write the Egyptian language — and added three more
letters borrowed from Meroitic, the script of the kingdom that had ruled this
stretch of river before them and whose language is still undeciphered.

The three additions cover sounds Coptic had no signs for. The result writes Old
Nubian, a Nilo-Saharan language that neither Greek nor Coptic nor Meroitic had
ever been used for.

Coding rule 3 counts an existing script extended with new letters or conventions
to write a language it did not previously write, and counts it on the record of
the polity whose system was adapted.

## Which is usually somebody else

That last clause has sent four claims in this corpus to a page other than the one
that did the work. Hittite cuneiform is counted on the Old Babylonian record.
Elamite cuneiform on a Mesopotamian one. Urartian cuneiform on the Neo-Assyrian
record, which is the empire Urartu built its state to resist. And Cyrillic on the
Byzantine record, although the workshop was at Preslav — the First Bulgarian
record calls it the most counter-intuitive zero on this site.

This record is not one of those, for the same reason the Aksumite record is not.
The parent script here is Coptic, and no Coptic-era Egyptian polity is in this
corpus to receive the count. And what happened in Nubia was not a borrowing of
somebody's system for local use — it was a modification of the system, adding
letters that did not exist in it, from a third script.

So the count stays where the work was done. Two African records, both on the same
principle, both arrived at independently: Aksum vocalising a South Arabian
consonantal alphabet into a syllabary, and Makuria extending a Greek-derived one
with Meroitic signs.

## What it was used for

Ordinary business, which is the unusual part.

Most of the corpus's script claims are about liturgy, monumental inscription or
royal display. Old Nubian's surviving corpus is a fortress archive of land sales,
loans, accounts and letters — people writing to each other about money. Ruffini's
book is built on it and its finding, quoted in this record's `revenue_basis`
comment, is a monetised economy with exchange rates tied to the wider world.

A script invented for a language nobody else wrote, used mainly for conveyancing,
in a kingdom whose international reputation was archery and a treaty.

## And when it stopped

With the kingdom, more or less. Old Nubian documents thin out through the
fourteenth century and the language was eventually displaced by Arabic in the
Nile valley, though Nubian languages are still spoken.

The script did not descend into anything. It has no children of its own, which is
why the axis this chapter is about has an entry on this page and would have
nothing to say about the next four hundred years.
