The Nubians took the Coptic alphabet — Greek letters, with a few signs from
demotic Egyptian, used for writing the Egyptian language — and added three more
letters borrowed from Meroitic, the script of the kingdom that had ruled this
stretch of river before them and whose language is still undeciphered.

The three additions cover sounds Coptic had no signs for. The result writes Old
Nubian, a Nilo-Saharan language that neither Greek nor Coptic nor Meroitic had
ever been used for.

That is a rarer act than borrowing a script. Most peoples who take up somebody
else's writing take it as it stands and make do with the fit; the Hittites wrote
their language in Mesopotamian cuneiform, the Urartians in Assyrian cuneiform,
and the Slavs in letters adapted from Greek at Preslav. Nubia's scribes changed
the system instead of accommodating themselves to it, and they raided a third,
dying script to do it.

Aksum, four hundred miles east across the desert, did the same kind of thing in
the same centuries, taking a South Arabian consonantal alphabet and rebuilding it
into a syllabary with vowels. They are the two places on the continent where a
writing system was reworked rather than adopted, and as far as anybody can tell
neither knew what the other was doing.

## What it was used for

Ordinary business, which is the unusual part.

Most surviving early scripts are known from liturgy, monumental inscription or
royal display. The Old Nubian corpus is a fortress archive: land sales, loans,
accounts and letters — people writing to each other about money. Ruffini's book is
built on it, and what it shows is a monetised economy with exchange rates tied to
the wider world.

A script invented for a language nobody else wrote, used mainly for conveyancing,
in a kingdom whose international reputation rested on archery and a treaty.

## And when it stopped

With the kingdom, more or less. Old Nubian documents thin out through the
fourteenth century, and the language was eventually displaced by Arabic in the
Nile valley — though Nubian languages are still spoken there.

The script had no children. Nobody adapted it further, nobody carried it
anywhere, and when it stopped being written the three Meroitic letters went out
of use for good, having outlived the kingdom that invented them by more than a
thousand years.
