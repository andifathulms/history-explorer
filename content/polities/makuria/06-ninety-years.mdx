In 1276 a Mamluk expedition beat the Nubian army, took Dongola, and installed a
king who agreed to pay tribute. That is the end of the baqt, which had been an
exchange between two parties, and the beginning of something else.

From Cairo it was one item of business among several. Baybars made the Nubian
kingdoms tributary and took the Red Sea ports in the same years he was
negotiating with the Golden Horde and with Byzantium over the straits, and the
Mamluk chronicles give it a clause.

From Dongola it was the end of a six-hundred-year arrangement.

## Ninety years

What followed was not a conquest and was worse.

Mamluk expeditions came repeatedly, installing and deposing kings; Makurian
claimants went to Cairo to be backed; the succession through the sister's son was fought over by candidates with Egyptian sponsors. In 1317 the
audience hall of the royal palace at Dongola was converted into a mosque and an
inscription was set up recording it — the building and the inscription both
survive — and there was a Muslim on the Makurian throne.

Underneath that, two slower things. Arab groups had been moving into the Nile
valley and the grasslands beside it for generations, marrying into local
families; and because Makurian descent ran through the mother, the sons of Arab
fathers and Nubian mothers were Nubian heirs. The rule that had kept the throne
secure for seven centuries is one of the mechanisms by which the kingdom's
ruling class became Muslim without anybody conquering it.

The church failed for a reason of its own. Nubian bishops were consecrated in
Alexandria, and the Coptic patriarchate under Mamluk rule was under pressure and
increasingly unable to supply them. The same connection had kept Ethiopian
Christianity alive for a thousand years and would keep it alive for six hundred
more; here is where it broke.

A kingdom losing its clergy from one direction and its ruling class from the
other has perhaps two generations left, and it had about three.
