The Arab armies took Egypt in 641 and kept going. They went west along the coast
and reached the Atlantic; they went east and reached the Indus. They went south
up the Nile twice and stopped.

The first expedition, in 642, was beaten. The second, under Abdallah ibn Sa'd in
651, besieged Dongola, brought up a catapult, damaged the cathedral, and made
terms rather than take the city.

What stopped them, in every Arab account, was the archery. The Nubians are called
*rumat al-hadaq* — the pupil-smiters — and the accounts describe an enemy that
would not close, shot from a distance with astonishing accuracy, and specifically
aimed at the eyes. One tradition has a hundred and fifty Arab soldiers blinded in
a single engagement.

It was a levy, not a paid force — farmers with bows, called up — and it beat two
Arab armies in ten years, which almost nothing else between Spain and the Punjab
managed.

## The baqt

The agreement that followed may be the most durable single treaty of the
medieval world.

It was not tribute. Both sides gave. Nubia sent a fixed annual number of slaves
to Egypt; Egypt sent back grain, cloth, wine and horses. Merchants had free
passage in both directions. Muslims could travel in Nubia but not settle, and
Nubia guaranteed the upkeep of a mosque at Dongola for Muslim travellers.

It held, with interruptions and quarrels, for about six hundred years.

Six hundred years is an extraordinary span for a negotiated arrangement. The
Peace of Augsburg lasted sixty-three. The treaty between Hatti and Egypt after
Kadesh — the earliest parity treaty that survives, in two languages — governed
relations for about seventy. Rome's client kingship over the Nabataeans ran a
hundred and sixty-nine. The baqt outlasted all of them several times over, on a
frontier between two religions that were at war with each other almost
everywhere else they met.

## Why it worked

Because both parties wanted the same thing, which was for nothing to happen.

Egypt had no interest in occupying a narrow floodplain between deserts, five
hundred kilometres of cataracts upstream, defended by archers. Nubia had no
interest in provoking a power that could send an army whenever it decided the
cost was worth paying. The baqt priced the arrangement and left it alone, and the
frontier at the third cataract became one of the quietest in the medieval world.

That is a kind of achievement history is bad at recording, because it consists
of things not happening. Six centuries of a quiet frontier produce no battles to
name, no treaties to renegotiate and no chroniclers, and the result is that a
kingdom which solved a problem everybody else on that religious border failed to
solve is known mainly for the ten years in which it was fighting.

## Three kingdoms

Christian Nubia was three kingdoms: Nobatia in the north, below the second
cataract; Makuria in the middle, from Dongola; and Alodia in the south, beyond
the confluence of the two Niles.

Makuria absorbed Nobatia in the seventh century and outlived Alodia. It is the
one the baqt was made with, the one the Arab geographers wrote about, and the one
whose archive was found — which is why it is the Nubian kingdom anybody can say
anything about.

Upstream and much earlier there had been Kush, which ruled Egypt as its
twenty-fifth dynasty and then held Meroë for a thousand years, writing a script
that can be pronounced and not understood. Three centuries separate the end of
Meroë from the first mention of these kingdoms, and nobody can fill them.
