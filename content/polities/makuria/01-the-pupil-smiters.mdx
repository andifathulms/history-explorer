The Arab armies took Egypt in 641 and kept going. They went west along the coast
and reached the Atlantic; they went east and reached the Indus. They went south
up the Nile twice and stopped.

The first expedition, in 642, was beaten. The second, under Abdallah ibn Sa'd in
651, besieged Dongola, brought up a catapult, damaged the cathedral, and made
terms rather than take the city.

What stopped them, in every Arab account, was the archery. The Nubians are called
*rumat al-hadaq* — the pupil-smiters — and the accounts describe an enemy that
would not close, shot from a distance with astonishing accuracy, and specifically
aimed at the eyes. One tradition has a hundred and fifty Arab soldiers blinded in
a single engagement.

This record's `military_basis` reads `conscript` on that. It was a levy, not a
paid force, and it beat two Arab armies in ten years, which almost nothing else
between Spain and the Punjab managed.

## The baqt

The agreement that followed is this record's first turning point and it is the
most durable single arrangement in this corpus.

It was not tribute. Both sides gave. Nubia sent a fixed annual number of slaves
to Egypt; Egypt sent back grain, cloth, wine and horses. Merchants had free
passage in both directions. Muslims could travel in Nubia but not settle, and
Nubia guaranteed the upkeep of a mosque at Dongola for Muslim travellers.

It held, with interruptions and quarrels, for about six hundred years.

Nothing else on this site is a single agreement lasting that long. The Roman
record's longest arrangement is the client kingship the Nabataean record
describes at a hundred and sixty-nine years. The Peace of Augsburg lasted
sixty-three. The Hittite treaty with Egypt, which this corpus carries on both
records as the earliest surviving parity treaty in two languages, governed
relations for about seventy.

## Why it worked

Because both parties wanted the same thing, which was for nothing to happen.

Egypt had no interest in occupying a narrow floodplain between deserts, five
hundred kilometres of cataracts upstream, defended by archers. Nubia had no
interest in provoking a power that could send an army whenever it decided the
cost was worth paying. The baqt priced the arrangement and left it alone, and the
frontier at the third cataract became one of the quietest in the medieval world.

That is a kind of political achievement this site's fields cannot register at
all. There is no measure for *nothing happened here for six centuries*, and this
record's `turning_points` list has three entries across seven hundred and
fourteen years, two of which are the beginning and the end.

Coding rule 4.3 says a polity with no turning points is complete, and that the
corresponding warning is not to pad. This record is close to that condition on
purpose.

## Three kingdoms, one record

Christian Nubia was three kingdoms: Nobatia in the north, Makuria in the middle,
Alodia in the south beyond the confluence. Makuria absorbed Nobatia in the
seventh century and outlived Alodia; this record carries Makuria, which is the
one the baqt was made with and the one the sources are about.

Alodia lasted until about 1504, a hundred and forty years past this record's
ending, and it is not carried here. Neither is Kush, the kingdom that ruled Egypt
as its twenty-fifth dynasty and then held Meroë for a thousand years upstream of
all of this, and which this corpus's Egyptian records point at four times.

Two absences on one stretch of river, and both are on the region's list.
