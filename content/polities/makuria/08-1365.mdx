In 1365 the Makurian court left Dongola for Gebel Adda, two hundred and fifty
kilometres downstream, and that is where the kingdom's record stops.

There is no last battle. There is no last king anybody can name with confidence
and no account of the move. What there is, is a court that had sat at Dongola for
seven hundred years abandoning it for a fortress much further north, closer to
Egypt and further from the country it had governed — which is what a government
does when it has stopped being able to hold the middle of its own territory and
would rather be near the border it can still be protected on.

## Nobody conquered it

That needs saying because the alternative is the assumption a reader will
otherwise make.

Egypt never annexed Makuria and never administered the middle Nile. There was no
campaign of conquest, no treaty of cession, no governor appointed. What there was
was ninety years of intervention by a neighbour that mostly wanted the frontier
quiet, a demographic transformation running underneath it that no army could have
produced, and a church that ran out of bishops.

The Mamluks are the proximate cause and they are not a sufficient one. They
installed and deposed kings, backed claimants and converted a palace hall into a
mosque; they did not take the country, because taking the country was never worth
what it would have cost, which had been the whole basis of the six-hundred-year
peace.

## Seven hundred and fourteen years

Makuria held a stretch of river between two deserts from the sixth century to the
fourteenth. In that time it beat two Arab armies, negotiated a treaty that
outlasted every other agreement of the medieval world, built an irrigated economy
on a waterwheel, ran a monetised land market with written contracts, invented a
script for its own language, painted a cathedral at Faras with a hundred and
sixty-nine images, and produced kings who could ordain priests.

Almost none of that was known before the 1960s, when the Aswan dam forced the
excavation of a stretch of Nubia that was about to be flooded, and archaeologists
dug the Faras cathedral out from under a mound of sand and found the paintings
intact underneath the plaster.

The kingdom's own archive came out of a cliff fortress at Qasr Ibrim, which is
now an island in the reservoir.
