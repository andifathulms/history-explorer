What is known about Makuria from outside is a treaty, some archery, and a
handful of embassies. What is known from inside was dug out of a fortress at
Qasr Ibrim on a cliff above the Nile, and it is a different kingdom.

The Qasr Ibrim documents are Old Nubian, on parchment and paper, much of it still
unpublished. They are land sales, loans, accounts, letters and legal
instruments — the ordinary paperwork of people arranging their affairs — and
Ruffini's book, which this record's institutions are coded from, is the first
attempt to build a society out of them.

## What they show

A monetised economy. Prices in gold and in cloth measures, with exchange rates
that track the wider medieval world's. Land bought and sold by named individuals
with witnesses and dated formulae. Loans with terms. A legal vocabulary
descending partly from Greco-Roman practice — the documents use Byzantine
formulae five hundred years after Byzantium stopped ruling anything nearby — and
partly from something local that has no obvious parallel.

Women appear as parties to transactions in their own right, buying and selling
land.

That last is not an aside on a record whose `succession_rule` is null because
the throne passed to the king's sister's son. A kingdom in which property runs
through women in the documents and the crown runs through women in the titulature
is describing one arrangement twice, and this site can code neither half.

## `land-tax, trade-toll`

The revenue field reads those two, and the first of them rests on a machine.

The *saqia* — the ox-driven waterwheel, lifting Nile water into channels above
the flood line — arrives in Nubia in this period and is what makes the middle
Nile support a kingdom rather than a string of villages. A narrow floodplain
between two deserts becomes farmable above the reach of the annual inundation,
and Welsby's excavation record shows settlement expanding onto exactly the ground
the saqia makes usable.

The second value is the river itself: everything moving between Egypt and the
African interior passed through, and the baqt guaranteed merchants free passage
in both directions, which is a customs regime written into a peace treaty.

## The paintings

One more body of evidence, because it is how the legitimation field is coded.

The cathedral at Faras, excavated ahead of the Aswan dam in the 1960s and now
divided between Warsaw and Khartoum, had a hundred and sixty-nine wall paintings
under the plaster. Bishops, saints, the Nativity, the three Hebrews in the
furnace — and portraits of Nubian kings and queen mothers with Christ or the
Virgin standing behind them, a hand on the royal shoulder.

That is `divine-sanction` painted on a wall. And Ibn Selim al-Aswani, a Fatimid
envoy who reached Dongola in the 970s and wrote about it, reports that the
Makurian king could ordain priests — a lay ruler holding a sacramental power that
no Christian monarch this corpus carries in Europe or Byzantium claimed.
