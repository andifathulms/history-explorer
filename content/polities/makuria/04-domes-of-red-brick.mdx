A thirteenth-century Egyptian survey of Christian buildings has a Nubian chapter
appended to it, and it is the fullest outside description of the kingdom that
anybody wrote. The compiler does not say where he got it, and the material
arrives mixed with folklore, which is a problem worth keeping in view rather than
solving.

## Dongola

> City of Dongola. Here is the throne of the king. It is a large city on the
> banks of the blessed Nile, and contains many churches and large houses and wide
> streets. The king's house is lofty, with several domes built of red brick, and
> resembles the buildings in Al-'Iraq; and this novelty was introduced by
> Raphael, who was king of Nubia in the year 392 of the Arabs.

A dated architectural innovation, attributed to a named king, in the year 1002.
That is a specific piece of information about a building programme in a kingdom
whose own records are almost entirely lost, and it says something precise: the
Makurian court took its palace architecture from Iraq, not from Egypt and not
from Byzantium.

Downriver at Ibrim, the residence of the man the text calls the Lord of the
Mountain, there was a walled town with a large and beautiful church of the Virgin,
finely planned, with a high dome, and on the dome a large cross. Evetts's
co-editor adds the observation that makes it matter: in Egypt, even in the
nineteenth century, Copts ventured to put a cross on a cupola only in remote and
desert places.

## The search at the frontier

Near the cataract was a town where the kingdom's border control was conducted.

> No one is allowed to pass by the inhabitants of this place, without being
> searched, even if he be a king; and if any one pushes on and refuses to be
> searched, he is put to death.

And then a sentence that sits awkwardly beside everything else known about this
kingdom:

> The people carry on their trade in kind; and selling and buying among them is
> done by exchange; thus they exchange woven stuffs and slaves; and all that is
> bought and sold is exchanged.

## The contradiction

That is worth stopping on, because the documents the Nubians themselves left say
something different.

The Old Nubian legal papers from Qasr Ibrim record sales, loans and land
transfers valued in gold and in standard measures, with prices, witnesses and
dates — the paperwork of people who had a unit of account and used it. An
Egyptian writing about the same kingdom in the same century says they barter.

Both can be true at once, and the likeliest reading is that they are describing
different things. A frontier town where foreign merchants are searched and then
trade woven cloth against slaves is not the same institution as a landowner at
Ibrim writing a deed valued in gold. The visitor saw the border; the documents
come from the interior.

It is also possible that the compiler, who never says he went there, was simply
told wrong. He also reports, on the same page, that the Nubians trace furrows in
a field, lay the seed at the edge with a supply of beer beside it, walk away, and
come back to find the sowing done and the beer drunk — and that they say this is
performed by beings of a different order from ourselves.

## How it is supposed to have started

The conversion story he gives belongs to the same register and is worth having
for what it assumes.

The Nubians formerly worshipped the stars. The first of them converted was
Bahriya, the king's sister's son, *who was learned in the science of the sphere,
and was wise and skilful*; when he came over, all the blacks of Nubia followed
him, and he built many churches and monasteries throughout the land, some away
from the river and some on its banks.

An astronomer nephew of the king converts and the country follows. Nothing in the
Byzantine or Coptic accounts of the sixth-century missions says anything of the
kind. What the story preserves is what Nubians, or people who had talked to
Nubians, were saying six hundred years later about how their church began: not
that foreigners brought it, but that it was worked out by the cleverest man at
court.
