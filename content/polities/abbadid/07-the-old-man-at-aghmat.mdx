Al-Mu'tamid died at Aghmat, below the Atlas, in 1095, four years after being
shipped out of the city he had ruled for twenty-seven.

Al-Maqqari records what happened at the graveside, and it is the detail that has
kept the story alive for nine hundred years. When the funeral service was read,
*the prayer of the stranger was chanted, as if he had been an adventurer* —
the rite used for a man with no connections and no standing in the place, said
over a king, without regard, al-Maqqari writes,

> either to the nobility of his birth, or to the extension of his empire, or the
> splendour and magnificence of his court; or to his having ruled over Seville
> and its districts, Cordova and its Az-zahrá. Such, however, are the ways of
> this world.

## What he wrote in prison

The poems from Aghmat are the most admired he produced and among the most
admired in Arabic, and they were written by a man in chains with his daughters
spinning for a living.

One fragment al-Maqqari preserves is addressed to the fetters themselves:

> Chains! do ye not know that I am a Moslem?
> I will watch at night, until ye are moved to pity.

That is not a great king's complaint about injustice. It is a man pointing out
to a piece of ironmongery that it has made a category error, which is either the
last resort of somebody with nothing else to argue with or a joke, and is
probably both.

## Five hundred years later

In 1601 al-Maqqari went to look for the grave.

He was at Marrakesh, went out to Aghmat, and found that nobody could tell him
where the tomb was. He and his companions asked such of the inhabitants as they
met, without success, until

> at last an old man, bent with age, showed us the place, saying, "Here lies a
> king of Andalus, and by his side she whom his heart loved tenderly."

They recognised it from a description in verse written by another visitor two
and a half centuries earlier — Ibn al-Khatib, vizier of Granada, who had made the
same journey for the same reason — and stood there for some time, al-Maqqari
says, assailed by fear and thought.

The woman beside him was Rumaykiyya, the mother of his children, who had died at
Aghmat before he did.

## Why anybody kept going

Because al-Mu'tamid became the standing image of what happened to al-Andalus, and
he became it almost immediately.

A poet-king, in the richest and most cultivated court in the western
Mediterranean, who made a choice between two humiliations and chose the one he
thought God preferred; who was proved right about the Christians and wrong about
the Muslims; and who ended in a village in the Atlas, buried as a stranger, with
his wife beside him and his children spinning wool.

Andalusi exiles told that story to each other for five hundred years, and then
for another four hundred after there was no al-Andalus left to be exiled from.
Al-Maqqari, writing in Cairo in the 1620s about a country his own family had been
driven out of, went to stand at the grave first.
