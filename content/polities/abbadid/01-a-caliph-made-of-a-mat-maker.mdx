title: A caliph made of a mat-maker

This site's Cordoba page ends with the caliphate formally abolished in 1031 and
al-Andalus divided into the *taifa* kingdoms — "Seville, Zaragoza, Toledo,
Granada and a couple of dozen others". That sentence has been the corpus's
whole account of a period that lasted eighty years. This is the largest of
them.

The taifas were what happened when a centralised state disintegrated in a
country full of cities. Some were taken by Berber commanders of the caliphal
army, some by Andalusi notables, some by the *saqaliba* — the slave-soldiers of
European origin this site's Cordoba page describes — and some, like Seville, by
their own judges. Abu al-Qasim Muhammad ibn Abbad was the qadi of the city and
its most respected citizen, and in 1023 he was the man in the room when it
needed a government.

He had no army, so he bought one: Berbers, Arabs, Christians, foreigners,
slaves from Nubia and the Sudan, recruited and paid, which is what a rich city
with no military tradition does and is why the `military_basis` on this record
reads mercenary.

And he had no legitimacy, so he manufactured some. The caliph Hisham II had
disappeared in the collapse and was probably dead; nobody knew. Around 1035 Ibn
Abbad produced a man he said was Hisham — by trade a mat-maker, who resembled
him — installed him in Seville, and governed in his name. Several other taifas
recognised the fiction and joined the coalition it gave them a lawful head for.
It let Seville act as the legitimate successor of the caliphate against Berber
rivals who could only claim to have taken things.

This corpus has spent a great many pages on legitimacy: caliphal diplomas,
descent from Ali, mahdis, Sasanian titulature, the mandate of heaven. This is
the same requirement met at the cheapest available price, by a judge, with a
lookalike — and it worked well enough that the neighbours signed on.
