---
title: A caliph made of a mat-maker
drafted_from: kennedy-1996

The caliphate of Córdoba was formally abolished in 1031 and al-Andalus broke
into the *taifa* kingdoms — Seville, Zaragoza, Toledo, Granada and a couple of
dozen others, most of them a city and its hinterland. Seville became the largest
of them.

The taifas were what happened when a centralised state disintegrated in a
country full of cities. Some were taken by Berber commanders of the caliphal
army, some by Andalusi notables, some by the *saqaliba* — the slave-soldiers of European
origin the caliphate had bought and armed — and some, like Seville, by their own
judges. Abu al-Qasim Muhammad ibn Abbad was the qadi of the city and
its most respected citizen, and in 1023 he was the man in the room when it
needed a government.

He had no army, so he bought one: Berbers, Arabs, Christians, foreigners, slaves
from Nubia and the Sudan, recruited and paid. That is what a rich city with no
military tradition of its own does, and it is a workable arrangement for exactly
as long as the money holds.

And he had no legitimacy, so he manufactured some. The caliph Hisham II had
disappeared in the collapse and was probably dead; nobody knew. Around 1035 Ibn
Abbad produced a man he said was Hisham — by trade a mat-maker, who resembled
him — installed him in Seville, and governed in his name. Several other taifas
recognised the fiction and joined the coalition it gave them a lawful head for.
It let Seville act as the legitimate successor of the caliphate against Berber
rivals who could only claim to have taken things.

Rulers everywhere spent enormous effort on legitimacy — caliphal diplomas,
descent from Ali, manufactured mahdis, borrowed imperial titles. This is the same
requirement met at the cheapest available price, by a judge, with a lookalike, in
a city that needed a government by Thursday. And it worked well enough that
several of the neighbours signed on.
