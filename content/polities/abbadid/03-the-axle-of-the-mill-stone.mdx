The man who made Seville the largest of the taifas was not the poet-king. It was
his father, and the Andalusi writers found him difficult to describe without
reaching for metaphor.

Ibn Khallikan quotes Ibn Bassam, who was writing within a generation, and the
passage is the fullest character sketch anybody produced of a taifa ruler:

> Then the authority passed to Abbad, in the year 433; he at first bore the
> surname of Fakhr ad-Dawlat, glory of the empire, and afterwards that of
> al-Mu'tadid. He was the axle of the mill-stone of civil discord and the utmost
> limit of affliction: think of a man whom neither strong nor weak could
> withstand, from whom neither the near nor the distant could escape; a mighty
> prince who consolidated the power which had been shaken; a lion who, whilst
> crouching, devoured the fawn; a precipitate politician against whom even the
> astucious had to be on their guard, and a dastard of whom the bravest warriors
> stood in awe; misguided, he followed the right path; consolidating the state,
> he cut away and spared not; he assaulted, and the people were hostile to him.

Then, having said all that, Ibn Bassam adds the rest of it: a handsome face, a
colossal stature, a liberal hand, presence of mind, and — before ambition led him
to power — a close and acute reading in literature, done without serious study,
without reading far, and without any interest in collecting books. He composed
verse of his own that men of letters thought worth copying out. He had, it is
said, about twenty sons and as many daughters.

## What the family was

Ibn Khallikan begins where these things are supposed to begin, with descent, and
the descent is worth having because of where it goes.

The Abbadids were Lakhmids, and traced themselves to al-Nu'man ibn al-Mundhir,
the last king of al-Hira — the Arab client kingdom on the Euphrates that the
Sasanians abolished four centuries before the family reached Spain. A poet made
the obvious compliment:

> The sons of Abbad, the progeny of the Mundhirs — there is an origin! — have
> added fresh lustre to the renown of their ancestors.

The first of them in Spain was a man from al-Arish on the edge of the Sinai
desert, who settled in a village outside Seville. The first to emerge from
obscurity was a qadi: Muhammad ibn Isma'il, who held the judgeship of Seville
and administered it, Ibn Khallikan says, with such justice and moderation that he
drew on him the attention of every eye and the love of every heart.

Then the city was besieged by a tyrannical prince, and the chief men came to the
judge and said: *Arise then with us, and let us go forth against him; we will
give thee the sovereignty over us.* He accepted. The besieger, who was drunk,
mounted to meet them and was killed. That is how a judgeship became a kingdom.

## The son

Al-Mu'tadid ruled Seville from 1042 and spent twenty-seven years swallowing the
smaller taifas of the west. Ibn Bassam's verdict — *consolidating the state, he
cut away and spared not* — is the polite version; the Andalusi tradition
remembered him for what he did to the rulers he displaced.

His own son wrote a poem about him, and it is not about any of that:

> A generous prince, bestowing thousands before the request is made, and who
> offers excuses, thinking his gifts too small. His hand is kissed by every
> mighty man, and were it not for its moisture — its liberality — we should say
> it is the sacred stone of Mecca's temple.

The son was al-Mu'tamid, and the fact that the poem exists is most of what the
taifa century was about. A king who took his neighbours' cities one at a time
kept a court at which his heir wrote verse good enough to be anthologised, about
him.

## The omen

Ibn Khallikan ends the reign with a scene.

Al-Mu'tadid was attacked by a quinsy. Feeling death coming, he had a singer
brought in, intending to take an omen from the first words of whatever she sang.
She began:

> We kill time, knowing that it will kill us; mix then the wine, young girl, with
> the water of the cloud and give us to drink.

He drew a bad omen from it, and survived five days. He died at Seville on Monday
28 March 1069 and was buried the next day.

Seventeen years later his son, holding the largest kingdom in al-Andalus, wrote
to Morocco for help against Castile.
