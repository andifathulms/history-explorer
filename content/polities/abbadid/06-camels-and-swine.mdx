Al-Mu'tamid, Abd Allah of Granada and al-Mutawakkil of Badajoz sent to Morocco
for the Almoravids — a Saharan movement holding both ends of the West African
gold road, invited across the strait by kings who wanted a battle won and not a
government replaced.

Al-Maqqari preserves the argument that went on inside al-Andalus about it, and
al-Mu'tamid's answer to it is the reason this chapter has the title it does.

His fellow rulers warned him what he was importing. *A kingdom without heirs and
one long sword do not find room in the same scabbard*, they told him. His reply
became a proverb in Spain:

> Better be a camel driver than a driver of pigs

— meaning, al-Maqqari explains, that he would rather be Yusuf's prisoner and
guard his camels in the desert than become the captive of Alfonso and keep his
swine in Castile.

He also set out his reasoning, which is more careful than the epigram suggests.
His position, he said, was of two sorts, one of doubt and one of certainty.
Whether Yusuf or Alfonso would keep his promises was doubtful either way. What
was certain was that leaning on Yusuf was an act agreeable to God and leaning on
Alfonso was not. Given a choice between two uncertainties, he took the one that
was not also a sin.

They got the battle. Yusuf ibn Tashfin crossed, beat Alfonso at Sagrajas near
Badajoz in 1086, and went home. Then he came back, and in 1090 and 1091 he
deposed the taifa kings one after another — with legal opinions from the
jurists of al-Andalus and from the east holding that rulers who paid tribute to
Christians out of taxes the law did not permit had forfeited their authority.
The Andalusi jurists signed. It is worth pausing on that: the religious
establishment of the taifas certified the removal of the taifa kings.

Seville was besieged and taken in September 1091. Al-Mu'tamid was shipped to
Morocco and confined at Aghmat below the Atlas, where he lived four more years
in poverty with his family, and wrote — the poems from Aghmat are the most
admired he produced, and among the most admired in the language. His daughters
are reported to have spun for a living. He died there in 1095 and is buried
there.

## The shape of it

The state was destroyed by the army it had itself imported, on a legal argument
its own scholars endorsed, after a battle fought on its behalf and won.

Very few polities end that way. It is not a miscalculation about whether the
Almoravids would win — they won — but about what winning would leave behind. A
movement that crosses a strait to fight somebody else's war and finds that the
people who invited it are richer, more sophisticated and militarily useless has
no particular reason to go home twice.

The judgement on al-Mu'tamid is still contested. Al-Mu'tamid is either the man who saved
al-Andalus from Castile for a century and a half, or the man who ended
Andalusi self-rule to avoid paying a tribute. Both readings were current within a
generation and both are current now, and there is no obvious way to settle it:
the counterfactual in which he pays Alfonso for another twenty years is
unavailable.
