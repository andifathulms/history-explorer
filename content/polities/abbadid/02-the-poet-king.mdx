---
title: The poet, and the men he bought off
drafted_from: kennedy-1996

Seville spent the middle of the eleventh century eating its neighbours. Al-
Mu'tadid, who ruled from 1042, was a poet, a competent administrator and
extremely violent: he annexed Arcos, Morón, Ronda and much of the Algarve, and
in 1053 dealt with a group of Berber taifa chiefs by inviting them to a
bathhouse and having the hot room sealed. By 1069 the Abbadids held most of
western al-Andalus.

His son al-Mu'tamid is the figure the period is remembered by, and he is
remembered for the right reasons and usually without the second half.

He was the finest poet among the taifa kings, in a generation where being a
poet was a qualification for the throne rather than a hobby beside it. His
court at Seville is the high point of Andalusi letters. His friendship with
Ibn Ammar, a poet of no family whom he made his chief minister, is one of the
famous relationships in Arabic literature — and he had him killed with a
hatchet, in person, after Ibn Ammar rebelled and was captured. His wife
I'timad, whom he had bought as a slave, is in half the anthologies.

The second half is the tribute. By the 1060s the taifa kings were paying
*parias* to the Christian kingdoms of the north: annual sums, in gold, in
exchange for not being attacked, and often in exchange for help against other
taifas. Seville paid Castile. So did Toledo, Zaragoza and Badajoz. The money
came out of an economy still running on Cordoba's inheritance, and it went into
the treasuries of Castile, Leon and Aragon, where it paid for the castles and
the cavalry that were used to demand more of it the following year.

That is the taifa system in one sentence, and it is why the period gets treated
as decadence. The judgement is unfair to the culture and exactly right about
the arithmetic: thirty small states, each individually rational in paying not
to be the one attacked, collectively financing the power that would take all of
them.

Al-Mu'tamid understood it. He is reported to have said, when his son argued
against calling in the Almoravids, that he would rather pasture camels for the
Berbers than herd swine for the Castilians. The next chapter is what that cost.
