title: What the small courts made

The taifa century is the most productive period in the history of al-Andalus,
and the reason is the fragmentation the previous chapters treat as a
catastrophe.

Thirty small courts competing for prestige is a better market for a poet than
one large one. Cordoba's scholars, secretaries and singers dispersed after 1031
into Seville, Zaragoza, Toledo, Granada, Almería, Badajoz and the rest, and
every taifa king needed a chancery, a library and a court poet in order to look
like a king. Al-Mu'tamid of Seville, in the chapter before this, was one of the
better poets of the century and employed several of the others.

Three things came out of it that outlived every one of those states.

Ibn Hazm of Cordoba wrote the *Tawq al-Hamama*, the Ring of the Dove, in the
1020s: a treatise on love, its symptoms and its accidents, illustrated from
people he knew and from his own life, in a register no other Arabic prose of
the period uses. He also wrote the *Fisal*, a comparative critique of religions
and sects — Jewish, Christian, Zoroastrian, Muslim — which is one of the
earliest works of comparative religion anywhere and is argued from texts rather
than from abuse.

The *muwashshah* and the *zajal* — strophic poems in classical Arabic and in
colloquial Andalusi Arabic respectively, with a closing couplet often in
Romance or in mixed Romance and Arabic. Those closing lines, the *kharjas*, are
the earliest substantial evidence of Ibero-Romance vernacular poetry that
survives anywhere, preserved because Arabic and Hebrew poets quoted them. The
form was invented in al-Andalus and spread east across the whole Arabic-writing
world.

And Toledo. When Alfonso VI took the city in 1085 — the event two chapters
earlier that panicked al-Mu'tamid into sending for the Almoravids — he took a
working Arabic library and a population of Arabic-reading Christians and Jews
with it. Within two generations Toledo was the place where Latin Europe went to
read Arabic. Gerard of Cremona and the translators around him rendered Ptolemy's *Almagest*,
Euclid, Galen, al-Khwarizmi, Ibn Sina's *Canon* and Ibn Rushd's commentaries out
of Arabic and into Latin.

That is the second leg of a journey that had begun four hundred years earlier.
Greek science was translated into Arabic at Baghdad across two centuries on
caliphal money; Toledo is where a large part of it was translated out again,
because a taifa capital with a library changed hands and the new owners did not
burn it.

It is worth stating what that means about the political history. The taifa
period is conventionally treated as a decline — the caliphate broken, al-Andalus
fragmented, the Christian kingdoms advancing, and eighty years of small courts
paying protection money until the Almoravids arrived to end the humiliation.

All of that is true, and the same eighty years produced more poetry, more
science and more transmission westward than the caliphate had in its whole
existence. A dozen courts competing for the same limited supply of scholars will
outbid one court every time, and the reason is not that fragmentation is good for
learning but that patronage is, and fragmentation multiplies patrons.
