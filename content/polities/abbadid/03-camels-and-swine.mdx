Alfonso VI took Toledo in 1085. It was the largest of the taifa capitals, an
old Visigothic royal city, and its fall meant the arrangement described in the
previous chapter had stopped being protection and become instalments.

Al-Mu'tamid, Abd Allah of Granada and al-Mutawakkil of Badajoz sent to Morocco
for the Almoravids. This site's Almoravid page has the other side of it: a
Saharan movement holding both ends of the gold road, invited across the strait
by kings who wanted a battle won and not a government replaced.

They got the battle. Yusuf ibn Tashfin crossed, beat Alfonso at Sagrajas near
Badajoz in 1086, and went home. Then he came back, and in 1090 and 1091 he
deposed the taifa kings one after another — with legal opinions from the
jurists of al-Andalus and from the east holding that rulers who paid tribute to
Christians out of taxes the law did not permit had forfeited their authority.
The Andalusi jurists signed. It is worth pausing on that: the religious
establishment of the taifas certified the removal of the taifa kings.

Seville was besieged and taken in September 1091. Al-Mu'tamid was shipped to
Morocco and confined at Aghmat below the Atlas, where he lived four more years
in poverty with his family, and wrote — the poems from Aghmat are the most
admired he produced, and among the most admired in the language. His daughters
are reported to have spun for a living. He died there in 1095 and is buried
there.

Two things this leaves for the site.

The ending on this record is typed `conquest` with the Almoravids as agent, and
the mechanism is not one the vocabulary catches: the polity was destroyed by
the army it had itself imported, on a legal argument its own scholars endorsed,
after a battle fought on its behalf and won. It is the sharpest instance in
this corpus of a state ending by getting exactly what it asked for.

And the judgement is still contested. Al-Mu'tamid is either the man who saved
al-Andalus from Castile for a century and a half, or the man who ended
Andalusi self-rule to avoid paying a tribute. Both readings were current within
a generation and both are current now, and this page does not decide between
them.
