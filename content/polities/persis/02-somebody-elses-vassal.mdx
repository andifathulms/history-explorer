---
title: Five centuries as somebody's vassal
drafted_from: wiesehofer-1996
phase: peak
---

After the Frataraka the rulers of Fars call themselves *mlk* — king — on their
coins, and they went on doing so under Parthian suzerainty for roughly three
hundred and fifty years.

The Parthian empire was not a unitary state and never tried to be. It was a king
of kings over great noble houses with hereditary offices and their own armed
followings, and under him a ring of vassal kingdoms — Persis, Elymais,
Characene, Adiabene, Media Atropatene, Armenia — each with its own dynasty and
its own coinage. Persis was one of the smaller ones.

The record of those centuries is thin and almost entirely numismatic. Kings are
known because coins exist; the order of them is inferred from style and
overstrike; there are decades with nothing in them. No source names a greatest
king of Persis, or says how it raised soldiers, or how a throne passed from one
holder to the next. Five hundred years, and the evidence is a drawer of silver.

What is recorded, and matters, is the religious establishment. Fars was the
Zoroastrian heartland, and Istakhr — a few kilometres from Persepolis, and built
partly out of it — held a major temple of Anahita whose custodianship was a
hereditary office of standing. The temple had estates, and the estates are the
reason a priestly family in a small vassal kingdom had any resources at all.

That is the whole of the position at the end of the second century AD: a small
hereditary kingship in an agricultural province, under a Parthian king of kings
who was fighting Rome and his own nobles, next to a temple with land and a
family that held it.
