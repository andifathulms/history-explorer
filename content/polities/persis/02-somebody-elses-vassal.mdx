title: Five centuries as somebody's vassal
drafted_from: wiesehofer-1996

After the Frataraka the rulers of Fars call themselves *mlk* — king — on their
coins, and they went on doing so under Parthian suzerainty for roughly three
hundred and fifty years.

This site's Parthian record describes the structure they were part of: a king of
kings over great noble houses with hereditary offices and their own armed
followings, and vassal kingdoms in Persis, Elymais, Characene, Adiabene, Media
Atropatene and Armenia, each with its own dynasty and its own coinage. Persis is
the one this corpus now carries, and the reason is what came out of it.

The record of those centuries is thin and largely numismatic. Kings are known
because coins exist; the sequence is inferred; there are long gaps. This page's
`peak` field is null because no consulted source identifies a high point, and
its `military_basis` and `succession_rule` fields are null because nothing
describes them.

What is recorded, and matters, is the religious establishment. Fars was the
Zoroastrian heartland, and Istakhr — a few kilometres from Persepolis, and
built partly out of it — held a major temple of Anahita whose custodianship was
a hereditary office of standing. The temple had estates. This record's
`revenue_basis` field says `land-tax` and the comment names the temple lands
beside it, because they are the reason a priestly family in a vassal kingdom had
resources at all.

That is the whole of the position at the end of the second century AD: a small
hereditary kingship in an agricultural province, under a Parthian king of kings
who was fighting Rome and his own nobles, next to a temple with land and a
family that held it.
