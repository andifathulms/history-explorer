---
title: What was left in Fars
drafted_from: wiesehofer-1996

Alexander burned Persepolis in 330 BC and this site's Achaemenid record ends
there. What happened afterwards to the province the empire had come from is a
question the imperial narratives do not answer, because nothing imperial
happened in it for five hundred years.

Fars — Persis to the Greeks, and the origin of the words Persia and Persian —
was a Seleucid province and then a Parthian one, and throughout it was governed
by local rulers of its own. The earliest of them use a title read as *frataraka*,
which is an Achaemenid administrative word meaning something like foremost or
governor, and they struck their own silver.

The coins are the whole of the early record and they are unusual in a way that
has been argued about for a century. The legends are in Aramaic, not Greek —
which in the third century BC, in a Seleucid province, is a deliberate choice
and not an accident of literacy. The reverse shows the ruler standing before a
structure usually identified as a fire temple, or before a standard, sometimes
with a figure in a winged disc above. The dress is Persian.

Two readings compete. One is that the Frataraka were consciously reviving
Achaemenid forms — the language of the old chancery, the imagery of the old
kingship, the rejection of the Greek coinage of the conquerors — as an assertion
that Fars was not a Hellenistic province but the homeland of an empire that had
been interrupted. The other is more cautious: that this is local religious and
administrative continuity, of the kind that persists in any province, and that
the Achaemenid revival is something modern scholars have read into it.

This site does not settle it. What the page can say is narrower and is not in
dispute: two hundred kilometres from the ruins of Persepolis, in a province of
somebody else's empire, local rulers spent five centuries striking coins with
Persian imagery and Persian titles in an Iranian administrative language, and
one of their descendants eventually founded an empire that governed Iran for
four hundred years and claimed to be restoring something.
