Around 205 a man called Papak, whose family held the temple at Istakhr, deposed
the sitting king of Persis and took the throne. He wrote to Artabanus IV, the
Parthian king of kings, asking that his son be confirmed as his successor.
Artabanus refused.

The son was Ardashir. He took Persis anyway, then the neighbouring kingdoms of
Kerman and Elymais and the Gulf coast, refused tribute, and in 224 met
Artabanus in battle at Hormozdgan and killed him.

## Nothing arrived

It is worth being exact about what happened to the kingdom of Persis in that
year, because the ordinary words all mislead.

No foreign army took it: the man who ended it had been born in it and had ruled
it. It did not break into pieces. It was not usurped in 224, because the
usurpation had happened twenty years earlier and by then the usurper's son was
the legitimate king. It was not taken over by a rival house, because the house
went on ruling, in the same province, from the same family shrine, for another
four hundred years.

What happened is that the kingdom stopped being a distinct thing by becoming
the middle of something much larger. Fars gave the new empire its dynasty, its
name in Persian, its ancestral fire and the language its chancery ran on. A
sub-kingdom of the Parthians became the core province of the Sasanian empire,
and there is no moment at which anybody conquered anybody for that to happen —
only a battle in which the vassal killed the overlord, after which the vassal's
kingdom was not a vassal kingdom and not a kingdom either.

## The five hundred years before it

That transition is famous and takes a paragraph. The interesting part is behind
it.

For half a millennium a small hereditary kingship in an agricultural province
had gone on striking silver in Aramaic with a fire and a Persian king on it,
under Seleucid and then Parthian suzerainty, through wars it took no decisive
part in, while the temple of Anahita at Istakhr accumulated land and the family
that kept it accumulated standing. Nobody wrote its history. It fought no
battle anyone recorded. Its kings are known because the coins exist.

And at the end of it, without ever having been a power, it produced a dynasty
that governed Iran for four hundred years and told everyone it was restoring
something.
