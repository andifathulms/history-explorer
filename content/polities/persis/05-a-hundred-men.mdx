Istakhr did not stop when Persis stopped. It became one of the largest and most
important cities of Sasanian Fars, and when the Arab armies arrived in the
seventh century it was taken by capitulation rather than by storm.

Le Strange, assembling the Arabic and Persian geographers province by province,
can follow it from there.

In the tenth century Ibn Hawkal describes a town about a mile across. It had
been walled, and the wall, he notes, had recently been pulled down. At the city
gate a fine bridge crossed the Pulvar, called the Khurasan bridge for reasons
nobody records. The houses ran well out past the bridge into the country, among
gardens growing rice and pomegranates. On the hills to the north-west stood
three fortresses called collectively the Three Domes, and in the largest of them
the Buyid ruler 'Adud al-Dawla had built roofed cisterns on twenty columns,
holding enough water for a thousand men to withstand a year's siege, with a
parade ground laid out on the hilltop.

That is a working provincial city with money spent on it.

## What happened next was politics

Mustawfi says the ruin of Istakhr was caused by the turbulence of its own
inhabitants, and the specific occasion is recorded. In the later tenth century
Samsam al-Dawla, son of the man who had built the cisterns, was obliged to send
an army against the town under the amir Kutlumish. It was laid in ruins.

From that point Istakhr was a village. The *Fars Namah*, written at the
beginning of the twelfth century, puts it at perhaps a hundred men.

Five hundred years of kings striking silver, a temple of Anahita with estates,
a family that took an empire out of it — and nearly nine hundred years after
Ardashir left, the place he left is a hundred men in the ruins of its own
walls. Le
Strange notes in passing that hardly any trace of the Muslim town remains
either.

## The ruins next door

Two facts sit beside each other in his account and are worth putting together.

Istakhr lay a few miles up the Pulvar from the Achaemenid platform. The
geographers who describe the town in careful detail — its bridge, its gardens,
its rice — add nothing of interest about the palaces and rock tombs a short
walk away, because they did not know what they were. The buildings were
generally ascribed to Jamshid, a king out of legend, and to Solomon. The tomb
of Cyrus at Pasargadae, up the same river, was called the Shrine of the Mother
of King Solomon — Mashhad-i-Madar-i-Sulayman — and Le Strange, writing in 1905,
gives that as its current name.

So the ruins were never lost. They were visible, visited, named and explained.
What had gone was the information that they were Persian and that the people
living beside them were the descendants of the men who built them.

And the last piece of it is the piece that did survive, which is the word. Pars
is the province; Persis is what the Greeks made of it; Fars is what Arabic made
of Pars, having no *p*. From that province come the name of a country and the
name of its language. Nothing at Istakhr was remembered accurately, and the
name of the place was carried out and put on everything.
