---
title: What a coin can and cannot show
drafted_from: wiesehofer-1996
phase: aside
---

This corpus now has three records whose evidence is principally coinage —
Greco-Bactria, the Indo-Greek kingdoms, and this one — and they show three
different problems.

For Greco-Bactria the coins supply a dynasty that no text supplies: twenty-odd
kings, their order, their mints, their approximate dates, reconstructed from
overstrikes and hoards. The difficulty there is chronology.

For the Indo-Greeks the coins supply an argument about audience: bilingual
legends in Greek and Prakrit, on Indian-standard flans, which say who the money
was for.

For Persis the coins supply something harder, which is an intention. The Aramaic
legend instead of Greek; the fire temple instead of a Greek god; the Persian
dress; the winged figure. Every one of those is a choice, and the question is
what the choice meant to the people making it.

Wiesehöfer's method is the relevant one and is why this page is drafted from
him: build the account from Persian material rather than from the Greek and
Roman narratives, and be careful about what the material will bear. The
Achaemenid-revival reading is attractive precisely because we know what happened
next — a dynasty from this province founded an empire that presented itself as a
restoration — and reasoning backwards from an outcome is how the decline
narrative got onto this site's Achaemenid page. The corpus has already had to
say once that an empire falling is not evidence that it was failing. The same
caution applies here in reverse: a province producing a dynasty is not evidence
that the province had been waiting to.

What the record does with the uncertainty is code `divine-sanction` for the
legitimation, on the fire-temple imagery and the priestly office, and put the
Achaemenid question in prose where it can be left open. The alternative would be
to code `titulature` — a predecessor's title taken as one's own ground — which
would assert the revivalist reading in a field, quietly, where no reader could
see it being asserted.
