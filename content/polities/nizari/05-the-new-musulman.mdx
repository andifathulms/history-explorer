Jalal al-Din Hasan succeeded in 1210 and spent the next decade demonstrating, as
publicly as it could be demonstrated, that Alamut was an ordinary Sunni power.

He made formal profession of fealty to the Abbasid caliph al-Nasir. He opened
relations with the Muslim princes around him. He sent his mother to Mecca to
perform the pilgrimage. And because the jurists of Qazvin were the neighbours
and therefore the hardest people in the world to convince that the conversion
was genuine, he invited them to send a deputation up to the castle, go through
his libraries, and burn whatever they judged heretical. They went, and they were
convinced, and so was everyone else; Browne records that all were at last
persuaded of the sincerity of his professions.

The caliph then honoured him so conspicuously that Muhammad Khwarazmshah became
jealous, and Browne dates the estrangement between Khwarazm and Baghdad — which
had consequences out of all proportion to its cause — to that jealousy.

The rest of the reign is a small state discovering how much room orthodoxy
buys. He allied with the atabeg Uzbek against a rival; then with Jalal al-Din
the Khwarazmian; and he is the only grand master of Alamut who ever lived
outside his own fortresses, spending a year and a half in Iraq, Arran and
Azerbaijan among people who a decade earlier would have killed him on sight. For
a polity whose defining physical fact was that it could not be reached, that is
an extraordinary thing to have done.

Then Chinggis Khan crossed the Oxus, and Jalal al-Din Hasan sent envoys to
submit to him — the first ambassadors of any Muslim power to do homage to the
Mongols. He died suddenly a few weeks later, on 2 or 3 November 1220, supposedly
poisoned by women of his household. Browne's reading is that the homage finished
whatever patience his own community still had with him, and the reading is hard
to argue with: his heir was nine, and the regent inaugurated the reign by putting
a number of the dead man's female relatives to death, several of them by burning,
on suspicion of the poisoning.

Twelve years earlier the community had been living under a declaration that the
law was abolished and the Resurrection had come. It was now governed by a
regency that had proved its orthodoxy to the satisfaction of the jurists of
Qazvin, was in correspondence with Baghdad, and had sent its submission to a
Mongol camp on the Oxus. Every one of those moves was rational. Together they
gave up the only thing that had ever protected the place, which was that it had
nothing to negotiate with anybody about.
