The state that no army could finish was talked into opening its gates, and the
man who did the talking had been living in it for twenty-odd years as a
prisoner.

## The hostage

Nasir al-Din Tusi is one of the great figures of Islamic science — astronomer,
mathematician, author of the *Akhlaq-i Nasiri*, the best-known work on ethics
written in Persian.

He was at Alamut because he had been kidnapped. Nasir al-Din, the Isma'ili
governor of Quhistan, seized him and sent him to the fortress, where he remained
— in Browne's phrase, an honoured if unwilling guest — until the Mongols took
it. The book on ethics was written there. It is named after the governor who
abducted him and was originally dedicated to that man, and in a later recension
Tusi apologises both for the dedication and for certain concessions he had made
to Isma'ili sentiment.

Bear that in mind, because it is the fairest thing that can be said for what he
did next.

## 1256

Hulegu came west in 1252 with a thousand Chinese engineers to work the
catapults, and the castles that had defeated the Seljuks for a hundred and sixty
years were now facing an enemy that could reduce them one at a time and had no
reason to stop.

Rukn al-Din Khurshah had been grand master for about a year. His father Ala
al-Din, who had come to the throne at nine under a regent and had developed, on
Rashid al-Din's account, a moody melancholia that made it dangerous to bring
him unwelcome news, had been killed.

It was Tusi, Browne says, who first induced Rukn al-Din to surrender himself
into the hands of the Mongols.

He did. The castles were handed over and systematically dismantled. Alamut was
taken that December; Juvayni, Hulegu's secretary, went through the library,
chose a few things to save, and had the rest burned. Rukn al-Din was sent east
towards the great khan and killed on the road, and Möngke ordered the Persian
Nizaris put to death.

## And what became of the negotiator

Tusi went straight into Hulegu's service. Two years later, when Hulegu was
deliberating whether it was safe to execute the last Abbasid caliph, it was
Tusi who assured him that no heavenly vengeance was likely to follow. He built
an observatory at Maragha with Mongol money and compiled his astronomical tables
there, assisted by Chinese astronomers.

Browne's verdict is unqualified: what irony that this double-dyed traitor should
be the author of one of the best-known works on ethics written in Persian.

It is worth entering the other side of it. Tusi had been taken by force and held
for decades by a state that was about to be destroyed whatever he did, in a
world where a scholar's only protection was a patron. Advising the man with the
catapults is not obviously worse than continuing to write ethics for the people
who had abducted you. Browne is a Victorian Persianist with strong views about
loyalty; the situation had no honourable exit in it.

What is not in dispute is the outcome. A polity whose entire method for a
hundred and sixty-six years had been that it could not be reached was ended by a
conversation, conducted by a man it had kidnapped.
