The Syrian half of this polity is the half Europe met, and it is where the
legend in the last chapter of this page was manufactured.

Nizari missionaries had been working in Syria since early in the twelfth
century, with the usual difficulty: Syria's towns were held by Turkish
garrisons who did not want them, and the first attempts to hold urban ground —
at Aleppo, at Damascus — ended in massacres. What worked was the method that
had worked in Iran. From the 1130s the mission took fortresses in the Jabal
Bahra, the hill country between the Orontes and the coast, and made itself
unremovable in terrain nobody wanted enough to pay for.

Rashid al-Din Sinan governed that network for about thirty years from the
1160s, from Masyaf, with a degree of independence from Alamut that the sources
notice and do not fully explain. He is the figure the Crusader chroniclers
called the Old Man of the Mountain, and the negotiations they record — with
Amalric of Jerusalem, with the Templars over tribute, with Saladin — are why
this small polity is in the European record at all.

Saladin is the useful case. He besieged Masyaf in 1176 and withdrew without
taking it, having survived two attempts on his life. The circumstances are
reported several different ways, including a story in which he wakes to find a
dagger and a note beside his bed; what is not disputed is that he lifted the
siege, came to terms, and afterwards left them alone. A power with a few
thousand people and no field army had made itself expensive enough for the
strongest ruler in the Levant to prefer an arrangement.

That is the same calculation the first chapter of this page describes the
Seljuks failing to escape, running in Syria against different opponents. And it
explains the shape of what Europe took home: Crusader lords dealing at close
quarters for a century with a sect they could not defeat, could not read, and
whose method was the targeted killing of individuals, produced the vocabulary —
and then, at a safe distance, the story.
