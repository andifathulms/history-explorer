---
title: The garden that was not there
drafted_from: daftary-2007
phase: aside
---

Almost everything widely known about this polity comes from people who hated
it or had never been near it, and the Alamut library burned in 1256, which
means the correction has had to be assembled from what survived elsewhere.

The famous story is Marco Polo's, written a generation after the state was
destroyed and set in a place he did not visit: a secret garden of wine and
women behind the fortress, into which young men were drugged, allowed to
believe they had seen paradise, drugged again, and told they would return there
if they died on command. It is a good story. It has no support in any Isma'ili
source, no support in the Persian histories written closest to the events, and
its structure — the hidden garden, the counterfeit paradise — belongs to a
family of tales told in Europe about several groups.

The drug is a philological accident on top of that. Contemporary hostile Syrian
and Egyptian writers called the Nizaris *hashishiyya*, from which the European
word derives, and the term appears to have been an insult of the ordinary kind
— low, disreputable, of no account — rather than a description of what anybody
consumed. No source close to the community reports its use, and a hierarchy
that trained agents to spend years in place under cover is not obviously
running on intoxicants.

Why this matters to the site rather than only to the Nizaris: it is the
clearest case in the corpus of a polity whose record was written by its
enemies and then improved by strangers. The Umayyad page carries Hawting's
warning that the dynasty's history was written under the dynasty that
overthrew it; the Rashidun page has a chapter on sources compiled a century
and more after the events. This is the extreme version — the archive
deliberately destroyed, the survivors dispersed, and the vacancy filled by
a traveller's tale that is still the first thing most readers have heard.

The honest position is smaller and duller than the legend. A religious
minority held a network of fortresses for a hundred and sixty-six years against
every power in the region, governed the valleys it held, kept a library good
enough that a Mongol official salvaged part of it before the burning, and used
targeted killing as one instrument among several. That is remarkable enough
without the garden.
