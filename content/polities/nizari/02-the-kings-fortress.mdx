Within seventeen years of Alamut the same method had been worked on Malikshah's
own castle outside his own capital.

Shahdiz — Dizh-i Kuh, the hill fortress, renamed the King's Fortress because the
sultan had built it — stood close to Isfahan and held arms, treasure, some of
the royal pages and girls of the court, and a garrison of Daylami soldiers.
Ahmad ibn Attash went up to it regularly on the understanding that he was giving
the young people lessons, and over time, with fair words and presents, brought
the garrison over. His father Abd al-Malik had been a literary man of Isfahan
driven out for Shi'i sympathies, who went to Rayy, met Hasan-i Sabbah and wrote
to a friend: I have fallen in with the Grey Falcon, and it has made up to me for
what I left behind. The son, who traded in linen, was left alone in Isfahan
because he professed to detest everything his father believed. He then opened a
mission house at the gates of the city and, according to the Seljuk chronicler
Browne is following, brought thirty thousand people over to him.

What happened next is the part to handle carefully, because it is the single
most lurid story in this history and its source is a chronicle written at the
court of the sultan who was besieging the fortress. People in Isfahan began
disappearing. A beggar-woman asking alms at a house heard groaning inside,
called out a blessing on the sick, was invited in for food, took fright and ran;
a crowd broke the door down and found four or five hundred people in the
cellars, dead, crucified, or not quite dead. The house belonged to a blind man
called Alawi Madani, who at dusk used to stand at the end of the long dark lane
leading to it asking God to reward whoever would take a poor blind man's hand
and lead him home. He and his wife and several accomplices were burned in the
market-place. The story has been retold for nine hundred years and no
independent account of it exists; what can be said is that it is what the
besieging side believed and repeated, and that a city which had lost thirty
thousand of its people to an enemy inside the walls will believe a great deal.

The siege itself was decided by a plot that failed for domestic reasons. Ibn
Attash, nearly out of supplies, sent word to the sultan's minister Sa'd al-Mulk
that he could not hold on and wanted terms. Be patient a week, the minister
answered, until I have destroyed this dog — meaning Sultan Muhammad, who was bled
once a month and whose surgeon-barber had been bribed to poison the lancet. The
minister's chamberlain knew, and told the chamberlain's beautiful wife, who told
her lover, who told an officer, who told the sultan. The barber was summoned and
scratched with his own knife, turned black, and died in front of everybody. The
minister was crucified with four others.

Two days later Ibn Attash surrendered the King's Fortress. He was paraded
through Isfahan on a camel, pelted, mocked in verses the chronicler thought worth
recording, then crucified and left on the cross seven days while people shot
arrows at him, and his body burned. He had a reputation as an astrologer, and
someone standing under the cross asked him whether his science had shown him
this. He said that his horoscope had told him he would cross the streets of
Isfahan with more than royal pomp and parade, and that it had not told him in
what fashion.

The state of castles had reached into the imperial capital and taken the
sultan's own stronghold with tutors and presents rather than with an army, and
lost it, and the losing cost one fortress. Sultan Muhammad then resolved on the
systematic extirpation of the sect, sent his atabeg against Alamut, and died
before anything came of it. That is the shape of the whole first century: the
network extends, a piece of it is destroyed at enormous cost and effort, and the
man who organised the destruction does not outlive the campaign.
