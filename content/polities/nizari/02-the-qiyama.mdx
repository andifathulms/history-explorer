---
title: The day the law was abolished
drafted_from: daftary-2007
phase: peak
---

On a day in August 1164, in the courtyard at Alamut, Hasan II had a pulpit set
up facing the wrong way — away from Mecca — and announced to the assembled
community that the imam had lifted the obligations of the outward law. The
Resurrection, the *qiyama*, had arrived. What the law had commanded was
fulfilled and no longer binding on those who recognised the imam; the fast was
broken there and then, and the day was kept as a festival afterwards.

It is the most radical thing any polity in this corpus did to itself, and it
needs holding at arm's length from the sensationalism it usually attracts. The
doctrine is intelligible: an Isma'ili tradition had always distinguished the
outward form of religion from its inward meaning, and the claim was that for a
community living in the imam's presence the form had done its work. It is also
a political act by a state that had spent seventy years being besieged,
declaring that the world outside its walls was not merely hostile but
superseded.

The cost is legible in what happened next. Hasan II was murdered eighteen
months later by a brother-in-law. His son maintained the doctrine; his
grandson, Jalal al-Din Hasan, reversed it publicly and completely around 1210,
proclaiming himself a Sunni, importing jurists, building mosques and inviting
inspection — and was recognised by the caliph al-Nasir, whose reign this site's
Abbasid page describes as the forty-five years when it nearly worked. A state
that had abolished the law in 1164 was seeking Sunni respectability inside two
generations.

Both moves are usually read as cynicism and neither has to be. What they show
is a polity whose doctrine and whose security were the same instrument, adjusted
as the pressure changed — first separating itself absolutely when separation
was its only defence, then rejoining the world when the Khwarazmian and Mongol
storm made isolation lethal. It did not save them. Rukn al-Din Khurshah
surrendered to Hulegu in 1256; Alamut was taken that December and its library
burned, which is why so much of what follows on this page rests on what its
enemies wrote.
