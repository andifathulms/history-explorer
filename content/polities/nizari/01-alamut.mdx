In 1090 Hasan-i Sabbah took the castle of Alamut, on a rock in the Alborz north
of Qazvin, without fighting for it. He had been in the valley for some time,
the population had been converting, and when enough of the garrison had come
over the Seljuk-appointed holder found the place was no longer his. He was paid
off and allowed to leave.

<Figure id="alamut" />

That is the founding and it is also the method. Over the following decades the
same thing happened in Quhistan in the east, in the Alborz valleys, and from
the 1130s in the Jabal Bahra in Syria: a mission arrives, converts, and a
fortress changes hands. What resulted was a polity with no middle — dozens of
strongholds and the cultivated valleys beneath them, scattered across a
thousand miles of country belonging to somebody else, with no contiguous
territory at any point in its existence.

The shape was a choice and it worked. A conventional state can be defeated by
beating its army; this one had no army to beat, and taking a castle in Quhistan
did nothing to a castle in Daylam. The Seljuks campaigned against them
repeatedly, took individual fortresses, and could not finish the thing, because
there was no thing to finish — only a network with local governors who had wide
initiative and a hierarchy that survived the loss of any part of it.

Assassination belongs in this paragraph rather than in a lurid one of its own.
A polity that cannot field an army and cannot be conquered is still vulnerable
to whoever decides to spend a decade trying, and the answer was to make that
decision personally expensive: the killing, in public and usually by a man who
made no attempt to escape, of the commander or minister who had ordered the
campaign. It is a weapon of the structurally weak, chosen because the
alternatives were unavailable, and it worked well enough that the word for it
in several European languages is still a corruption of a name for these people.

Nobody has ever published a figure for how much territory this polity held, and
nobody could. A state of castles and the valleys beneath them has no area of the
kind a map is drawn from. Shading it in would be a lie about its shape, and the
shape is the whole of what it was.
