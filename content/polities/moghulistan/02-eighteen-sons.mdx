There were five Dughlat brothers, and the office of ulusbegi — the amir over the
amirs, which their family held as of right — passed from one to the next. When
Amir Bulaji died it should have gone on to his son, Khudaidad, who was seven years
old.

## The grievance

Qamar al-Din went down on his knees before Tughluq Timur and put the obvious case:
the office should come to him, because his brother's son was seven and not fit for
it. The khan would not listen, and appointed the seven-year-old.

Qamar al-Din was a violent man and could do nothing about it while the khan lived.
When the khan died he revolted, and — the Moghul traditions say — put to death in a
single day eighteen of Tughluq Timur's sons, and took the style of khan himself,
which as a Dughlat he had no right to.

One son at the breast was hidden by Khudaidad and his mother and kept out of the
reach of Qamar al-Din's searchers. That child was Khizr Khwaja, and every later
khan of Moghulistan descends from him.

## What it cost

Moghulistan fell into disorder and stayed there for a generation, and the disorder
attracted Timur.

The *Zafar-Nama*, which Mirza Haydar quotes at length because he has nothing
better for those years, records the expeditions by number: a third invasion of
Jatah, a fourth, a fifth. Armies were sent to the Ili, to Almaty, to the Karait on
the Aisha Khatun river. Timur was never content, it says, until he had carried a
business through to the end, and he was not content with the peace his own amirs
made. The country was raided, its amirs went over to him one by one, and Qamar
al-Din was hunted through it until he disappeared.

Timur never annexed Moghulistan. He did not need to. What he did was keep it from
becoming a state again for thirty years, which had the same effect while he lived
and none at all afterwards.

## The horseshoe nail

The khanate reassembled around the hidden child's line. Khizr Khwaja reigned, then
his son Muhammad Khan, and it was under Muhammad that the conversion Tughluq Timur
began was finished — by means that Mirza Haydar records with approval and without
euphemism.

He was a wealthy prince and a good Muslim, and persisted in the road of justice
and equity, and during his reign most of the tribes of the Moghuls became Muslims.
It is well known, the historian goes on, what severe measures he used to bring
them to it. If a Moghul did not wear a turban, a horseshoe nail was driven into
his head. Treatment of this kind was common. *May God recompense him with good.*

Amir Khudaidad, the child whose appointment caused the whole catastrophe, lived
long enough to raise six khans to the throne, and then went on pilgrimage to
Mecca.

## The building at Chadir Kul

Muhammad Khan built a caravanserai on the northern side of the Chadir Kul defile,
out of stones so large that Mirza Haydar thought them wonderful until he saw the
temples of Kashmir. An entrance hall twenty gaz high; a passage thirty gaz long on
the right; a dome of about twenty gaz, beautifully proportioned, with a gallery
round it and cells in the sides of it; on the west a mosque fifteen gaz high with
more than twenty doors; solid blocks of stone over every door.

It stood on the pass road at about eleven thousand feet. A khanate remembered
mostly for raids put up a stone building in the high Tian Shan for travellers to
sleep in, and the man who described it had stayed there.
