Mirza Haydar Dughlat began his history in the 1540s because, he said, there was
nobody left who remembered. At this present date, he wrote, there remains not one
among them who knows these traditions, and did he not make the venture the story
of the Moghul khaqans would be obliterated from the pages of the world's history.

He was writing about his own family's khans, and he starts with a boy and a dog.

## The boy at Aksu

Tughluq Timur was a great-great-grandson of Chagatai, born in 730 — 1329 or 1330 —
and brought out of the Kalmak country at sixteen by Amir Bulaji of the Dughlat.
He was made khaqan at eighteen. What he ruled at first was Aksu and the amirs who
had put him there.

At eighteen he was still a pagan, and the country he was raised over was the
eastern half of a khanate that had split apart: while the western Chagatai lands
turned to towns and Islam, the Moghuls — the word is simply *Mongol*, in a Persian
mouth — kept the pastures, the herds, the tribal amirs and the old religion. Their
western neighbours called them Jatah, which means brigands, and the name is used
straight through the chronicles.

## The Shaikh

There came to the neighbourhood of Aksu a man who had walked out of a town that
was buried in sand. Shaikh Jamal al-Din had preached to the people of Katak, near
Turfan, that a calamity was coming and that he had been given leave to escape it.
The muezzin followed him, turned back at three farsakhs to call the evening prayer
one last time, and found while he was calling that something dry like snow was
falling out of the sky. When he came down the minaret door was blocked. He climbed
back up, saw that it was raining sand and that the town was going under, and threw
himself off the tower onto the drift.

Sometimes, Mirza Haydar adds, a wind comes and lays bare the minaret or the top of
the dome; sometimes it uncovers a house, and a man who goes in finds everything in
perfect order and the master of it turned to white bones.

## The question

The khan had ordered a hunt and forbidden anybody to absent himself, and the
shaikh and his companions were found sitting apart and dragged in as offenders.
They were strangers from a ruined town, the shaikh said, who knew nothing about
the hunt or the ordinances of the hunt.

The khan let the Tajik go. He was at that moment feeding his dogs with pork, and
he asked, angrily:

> Are you better than this dog, or is the dog better than you?

> If I have faith I am better than this dog; but if I have no faith, this dog is
> better than I am.

The khan went away. Then he sent a man to bring the shaikh back, on the man's own
horse and with all due respect. The shaikh saw that the saddle was stained with
pig's blood and said he would walk; the Moghul insisted on the order; so he spread
a clean handkerchief over the saddle and mounted.

He found the khan standing alone in a retired place with sorrow on his face, and
the khan asked him what this thing was that made a man better than a dog. The
shaikh said: faith. And explained what it was.

The khan wept, and promised that if he ever became khan with absolute authority
the shaikh must come to him, and he would become a Muslim.

The shaikh died soon after. He left the charge to his son.

## The morning call

When Tughluq Timur did become khan in Moghulistan, ruling in pomp and splendour,
Maulana Arshad al-Din could not get an audience. So every morning he went and
called the prayer near the khan's tent, until the khan sent a man to find out who
kept waking him.

*I am the son of the man to whom, on a certain occasion, you made the promise to
become a Musulman.*

The khan's answer is the sentence the whole story exists for: ever since he
ascended the throne he had had it on his mind that he had made that promise, and
the person he gave the pledge to never came.

He washed, declared his faith, and then the two of them decided to put the amirs
to the question one at a time.

## The wrestler

The first in was Amir Tulik, Mirza Haydar's own great-great-uncle, who burst into
tears and said he had been converted three years earlier at Kashgar and had not
dared say so. The khan rose and embraced him. So it went, one prince at a time,
until Jaras refused — and offered terms. He had a man called Sataghni Buka whom he
had seen lift a two-year-old camel. If the Tajik could throw him, Jaras would
believe.

Everyone protested. The maulana said let it be so: if he did not throw him he
would not require them to become Muslims.

A crowd assembled. The infidel came on with a conceited air and the maulana looked
very small beside him. The maulana struck him full in the chest and he went down
senseless, came to, raised himself, and fell at the maulana's feet uttering words
of belief.

On that day, Mirza Haydar writes, a hundred and sixty thousand persons cut the
hair off their heads and became Muslims, and the khan was circumcised.

## Why it is the beginning

Because it is the point at which the eastern Chagatai lands become a distinct
thing with a case for itself. Barak Khan and Kebek had been Muslims before, but —
in the historian's judgement — neither they nor the people had known the true road,
and it was Tughluq Timur's conversion that put Islam into the country and kept it
there. Two years after taking the faith he crossed the Khojand river with an army
and took the western half of the inheritance as well, the first khan in a
generation to hold both.

He died at thirty-four, in 764. What he had built lasted about eighteen months
longer.
