Almost everything known about the Moghul khans comes from one man, writing far
from home about relatives who had turned on him, and he says so on the first page.

## Who he was

Muhammad Haydar Mirza was a Dughlat — the family that held the office of ulusbegi,
that raised Tughluq Timur to the khanate and six khans after him, and that ruled
Kashgar in its own right for generations. His mother was a daughter of Yunus Khan.
That made him first cousin to Babur on one side and cousin to the khans of Kashgar
on the other.

Orphaned at sixteen, he went to Sultan Said Khan's court and was treated with
fatherly kindness and so much favour that he became an object of envy. He served
the khan for the rest of his life, fought his campaigns, escorted his princesses,
went to Tibet for him and was at Lhasa when he died.

Then Abd al-Rashid came to the throne and destroyed the Dughlats. Mirza Haydar's
own summary, in the preface, is four words longer than it needs to be and all the
more pointed for it:

> I thank God that He has, in His graciousness, made me absolutely independent of
> the Moghuls. For though the Khakans of that tribe are of my own race, nay, are
> my own cousins, I received very bad treatment at their hands.

## Where he ended

In Kashmir, ruling it. The country he had helped negotiate a marriage alliance with
in the khan's last campaign became, a few years later, his own; he governed it in
the name of a Mughal emperor and wrote the *Tarikh-i-Rashidi* there in 951 to 953 —
1544 to 1546.

He dedicated it to Abd al-Rashid. That is where the title comes from: partly from
Maulana Arshad al-Din who converted Tughluq Timur, partly from the *rushd* or true
road that the Moghuls came to know through him, and partly, he says, because Abd
al-Rashid, the last of the Moghul khaqans, is reigning, and this book has been
dedicated to and written for him.

A man dispossessed by a prince, writing the history of that prince's house, in the
prince's name, from a kingdom he took for himself two thousand kilometres away.

## What the book is

It is two books bound together. Part I runs from Tughluq Timur to the present and
is compiled, for the early reigns, from oral tradition and such contemporary
accounts as did not contradict each other — conflicting traditions omitted, he
says, on account of their probable inaccuracy. For the century he could not reach
he transcribes Yazdi's *Zafar-Nama* and says so. Part II is his own life and what
he saw.

He is therefore both the only narrative source for this khanate and a participant
in its last thirty years, which is a combination to read carefully. He is a Dughlat
writing about khans, and the Dughlats come out of it well. He is a pious man
writing about a conversion, and the conversion comes with a wrestling match and a
buried city.

He is also, on the evidence of the sheep army and the servant given away as a
present, a man who did not tidy up. The best argument for trusting him is how often
he records things that make his own side look small.

## What became of the name

The khanate at Yarkand outlasted him by a century and a half, ruling the southern
Tarim until the Dzungars came down and took it. The steppe kept the name longest:
Moghulistan stayed on maps of the Tian Shan after the Moghuls had all gone south,
which is the usual fate of a country named for people who left.
