Vais Khan was the last of six khans Amir Khudaidad raised to the throne
with his own hand, and the only one whose whole reign was fought against a
single enemy: the Kalmaks, the Oirat Mongols pressing in on Moghulistan
from the east under their own chief, Isan Taishi.

## A robber before he was a khan

Vais was the son of Shir Ali, a prince who had never himself been khan,
and after his father's death he was raised in the household of his uncle,
Shir Muhammad Khan. The arrangement chafed. Vais fled the court and took
to living as a *kazak* - the word Mirza Haydar uses for a prince turned
highway robber, waiting out his exclusion from power on the strength of
whoever chose to ride with him. Distinguished young Moghuls volunteered
in numbers, among them the chronicler's own grandfather, Amir Sayyid Ali,
who would go on to serve him for the rest of his reign.

## Sixty-one engagements, one victory

Against Isan Taishi's Kalmaks, tradition counted sixty-one battles and a
single win. Twice Vais Khan was taken prisoner. The first time, Isan
Taishi approached him with visible deference to test whether he really
was a descendant of Chinggis Khan - and Vais, certain the gesture meant
his death had come, refused to bow to an infidel captor even so, a
Muslim's refusal that Isan Taishi read, backwards, as the confidence of a
man who could afford it, and let him go unharmed. The second capture cost
him more directly: taken again near Turfan, he bought his freedom by
handing over his own sister, Makhtum Khanim, as the ransom.

The worst defeat produced the story the Moghuls kept longest. Retreating
across the Ili with the Kalmaks in pursuit, Vais Khan's horse went down
in the riverbank mud. Amir Sayyid Ali gave the khan his own horse,
dragged a Kalmak officer along by his coat as a human shield for a full
farsakh of running fight, then swam the flooded river holding the bridle
of the khan's horse above the current while two of the khan's own cousins
drowned within his sight, out of reach. Vais counted five separate acts
of service in that one retreat and rewarded the Amir with five tribal
grants on the spot - the debt that made the house of Dughlat the khanship's
most trusted amirs for two generations after.

## A pious, poor king

Between campaigns Vais Khan farmed a scrap of desert at Turfan by hand,
digging his own well and irrigating a plot too small to be worth an
ass-load of grain, wore clothes his mother spun from camels he had
skinned himself, and followed a Naqshbandi teacher with a king's leisure
he otherwise refused to take. Amir Khudaidad, who had put him on the
throne, left on pilgrimage to Mecca during his reign and died at Medina
before word of it ever reached Moghulistan - the amir's own son
inheriting his authority in his place, in time to watch what came next.

## An arrow from his own side

Vais Khan died fighting not the Kalmaks but a Timurid-backed rival,
Satuk Khan, sent against him by Ulugh Beg. Charging to force his horse
across a stream that separated the two sides, he was thrown when the
animal sank into the mud, and one of his own household's finest archers,
mistaking his fallen khan for an enemy in the confusion, shot him through
the waist before recognising him and running to hold his dying body. It
was, by his own account only days earlier, the death he had already told
a friend his freshly-shaved head was worthy of - "not a jewelled crown,
but martyrdom."
