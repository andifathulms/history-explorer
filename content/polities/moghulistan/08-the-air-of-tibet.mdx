Sultan Said Khan died on the way home from the highest campaign anybody in his
family had attempted, of something the Moghuls called *dam-giri* — the seizing of
the breath.

## The expedition

Having lost the pastures, he went the other way, into the mountains south of his
new country. Mirza Haydar was sent ahead into Tibet and Kashmir and conducted the
business: peace was made with the kings of Kashmir, the daughter of Muhammad Shah
was married to Iskandar Sultan, and the Friday sermon and the coinage in Kashmir
were put in the khan's name. All the wealth of Kashmir that could be collected was
brought up to him in Tibet the following spring.

He sent Mirza Haydar on further east, towards Lhasa, and turned back for Kashgar
himself.

## The illness

He had arrived already weak and much reduced from dam-giri and never quite
recovered during the whole time he was up there. He had to make the journey home
regardless. When he reached a place where dam-giri was prevalent, his pious soul
took flight to the regions of the blessed.

That was at the close of the year 939 — the end of 1533. He was nearly forty-eight.

Mirza Haydar's portrait of him is affectionate and specific in a way the rest of
the book is not. His conversation was graceful and eloquent in both Turki and
Persian; he was always gay, open-hearted, generous; when he was about to show
somebody a favour he used to blush before he spoke. A man called Maqsud Ali had
once put an arrow into his left shoulder in a battle and the wound troubled him for
two years and nearly killed him, and when Maqsud Ali was captured during that time
the khan let him go.

## What ended

Not the dynasty. Abd al-Rashid, his son, took the throne, and Mirza Haydar was
still calling him *the most excellent son of Sultan Said Khan* in 953.

What ended was Moghulistan. The khanate kept the name and stopped having the place:
its herds, its mountain pastures, its summer camps at Yulduz and Kochkar and
At-Bashi, the ground from which every Moghul amir's power had come since Tughluq
Timur was raised at Aksu. From the 1530s the state was Kashgar, Yarkand and Khotan
— walled towns, irrigated fields, a land tax and a garrison.

There was no battle. There was a migration of two hundred thousand Kazakhs into
pastures nobody could defend, a bad winter, a campaign that captured a hundred
thousand sheep and no enemy, and a decision taken in Kashgar not to go back up
that year.

Both halves of the Chagatai inheritance ended in the same way in the end. The
western half became a country of towns and lost its khans to the amirs who ran
them. The eastern half held out for two centuries longer, and then became a country
of towns.
