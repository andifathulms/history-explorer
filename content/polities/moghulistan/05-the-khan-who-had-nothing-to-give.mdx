Yunus Khan was the greatest of all the Chagatai khans, his great-grandson-in-law's
cousin says, and before him there was in many respects no one like him in his
family. He is also the one whose life shows most clearly what was wrong with the
khanship he held.

## A Timurid education

None of the Chagatai khans before him had passed the age of forty; most never
reached it. Yunus lived to seventy-four.

He spent much of his youth away — twelve years studying under Sharaf al-Din Ali
Yazdi, the historian of Timur whose *Zafar-Nama* Mirza Haydar quotes for the
century he cannot otherwise cover — and travelled widely. He read the Qur'an. He
excelled in penmanship and painting, was trained in singing and instrumental
music, had an even temper and charming conversation and a quick perception, and
was unequalled in archery.

He was, in short, a cultivated Persianate prince who happened to be the lawful
khan of a nation of horsemen in the mountains, and the two halves did not fit
easily together.

## The gift

His first attempt on Kashgar failed. Esen Buqa came from the Yulduz with sixty
thousand men and reached Kashgar in eleven days — so fast that only six thousand
had caught up with him — joined the old Amir Sayyid Ali with thirty thousand more,
and beat Yunus three farsakhs outside the town. The khan's wives and children were
captured, including his eldest daughter, who was still at the breast; they were
given what they needed and sent on after him.

He got back to Moghulistan and his followers left him. Unable to remain, he went
to Khurasan to present himself to the Timurid Sultan Abu Sa'id — and was so
destitute that he could find nothing to offer as a present.

So he said to Buyan Quli, one of his most devoted servants, that he was going to
give him to the Mirza as a slave, by way of a gift. The man made no objection. The
khan handed him over, and then asked Abu Sa'id for a district to hold, so that he
might inspire the Moghuls with confidence, and was given Yatikand on the edge of
Andijan.

When the time came to leave, Buyan Quli began to weep. The Mirza asked why; he
told the story; the Mirza took pity on him, gave him a horse and provisions, and
sent him back with his master.

## What the story is about

A Chinggisid khan of the senior eastern line arriving at a Timurid court with
nothing to present but a man, and going home with a Timurid district to rule so
that his own people would take him seriously.

This is what the khanship had become in the eastern half. The amirs had fortified
themselves wherever they happened to be and had been acting independently so long
that when they came in to Yunus they did not fulfil their duties or offer him
fitting allegiance, and he humoured them in every way possible. The khan was
necessary and powerless: a Chinggisid was required, and no Chinggisid could
command.

## What he left

He acquired Tashkent in the end, and made peace among the Timurid sultans, and
died old. His descendants are the point.

Of his seven children, one daughter married the Timurid Umar Shaikh Mirza and bore
a son whom Mirza Haydar describes as having illumined the world with the radiance
of his power. That was Babur. Another daughter was Mirza Haydar's own mother.
Yunus's two sons, Mahmud and Ahmad, divided the inheritance between them, and from
Ahmad came Sultan Said Khan, who took Kashgar, and Mansur, who took Turfan.

Every thread in the last century of this khanate — and a good part of the first
century of the Mughal empire in India — runs back through the old man who had to
give away his servant.
