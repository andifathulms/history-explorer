Vais Khan left two sons, and Moghulistan split behind them before either
one had ruled a day. What happened to the faction that lost is the reason
the Moghuls remembered the year as a disaster rather than an ordinary
succession.

## Two princes, two parties

Yunus Khan, the elder, was thirteen when his father died. Isan Bugha
Khan was the younger. Most of the amirs backed Isan Bugha and stayed in
Moghulistan with him; two commanders named Irazan Barin and Mirak
Turkoman backed Yunus instead, broke with the rest of the amirs by force,
and set out for Samarkand with the boy khan and, by one count, thirty
thousand households behind them - a bid to build his following from
outside the country rather than inside it, where Isan Bugha's party
already held every advantage.

## What Ulugh Beg did with them

Mirza Ulugh Beg, the Timurid ruler at Samarkand, met the migration with
an offer: come into the fort household by household, give your names,
and each family will receive its ration and pass through. The Moghuls
went in hopeful. Nobody who entered came out again to tell what happened
inside. Irazan and Mirak's chiefs were killed and the rest of their
people taken prisoner in a single engineered massacre - remembered
afterward among the Moghuls simply as the Ruin of Irazan, an era-marking
disaster on the scale of a defeat in open war, though no battle had been
fought.

Yunus Khan himself was spared, sent on with a fifth of the plunder to
Ulugh Beg's own father, and raised at the Timurid court rather than
killed with his followers - the "Timurid education" that shaped the rest
of his life, twelve years under the historian Sharaf al-Din Ali Yazdi
before he ever saw Moghulistan again as an adult claimant rather than a
captured child.

## What Isan Bugha kept

Isan Bugha Khan, left in sole control of the country his brother's party
had abandoned, held the whole of Moghulistan without a rival for the next
several decades - the khan whose sixty-thousand-man relief force would
later catch Yunus outside Kashgar and hand him the destitution that opens
the next chapter of this khanate's history. The two brothers spent their
whole adult lives as rivals for one khanship, and it was Isan Bugha's own
branch that held the country when Yunus finally came home a grown man
with nothing to offer his own followers but a Timurid education and a
claim by birth order that the amirs who had stayed no longer felt bound
to honour.
