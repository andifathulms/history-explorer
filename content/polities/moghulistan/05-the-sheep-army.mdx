Mirza Haydar rode on the campaign that lost Moghulistan, and his account of it is
one long anticlimax, which is exactly why it is worth reading. Nothing was decided
in a battle. The country simply filled up with other people.

## The arrival

In 928 — 1522 — the khan took Rashid Sultan into Moghulistan and subdued the whole
of it and the Kirghiz, and many of its people attached themselves to the young
prince. Then the Kazakhs arrived.

Beaten out of the Kipchak steppe by the Manghit and no longer able to remain
there, they came over into Moghulistan two hundred thousand strong. Mirza Haydar
gives the consequence in six words: *it was impossible to resist them*. Rashid
Sultan withdrew to Kashgar.

## The winter

Rashid came back and wintered at Kochkar. Then Tahir Khan of the Kazakhs, pushed
out of Uzbekistan, came and settled near Kochkar, and half the Kirghiz went over to
him and were taken under his protection. Rashid took fright and fled in the depth
of winter to At-Bashi; his father came at the end of the same winter to join him
there, and the followers were reassured.

In the spring the Uzbeks pushed into the eastern quarter of the country. The
remaining Kirghiz wanted to join the ones who had gone. Mirza Haydar was sent with
Rashid to drive them out of Bumghal and Kochkar back to At-Bashi, and then left
behind in Moghulistan to ensure law and order among the inhabitants; the khan went
down to Kashgar to gather the people and see whether any agreement could be made
with the Uzbeks; Mirza Haydar was sent to Yarkand to fetch the khan's elder
kinswoman Sultan Nigar Khanim up into the mountains to mediate.

A khanate reduced to shuttling princesses across a range to negotiate about its own
pastures.

## The expedition that found nothing

Word came that the Kirghiz had separated from the Uzbeks, so the khan set off to
subdue them and covered forty days' march in twelve. Mirza Haydar went ahead with
five thousand men.

They reached the camp at Arish Lar and found the tents standing and everybody gone.
Arms and baggage were tumbled about. Further on there were dead bodies, horses
killed or wounded by arrows, broken arrows everywhere. They found a man who was
half dead, and learned from him that somebody else had got there first: Babajak
Sultan had come from Kusan, fought the Kirghiz three days earlier and been beaten,
and the Kirghiz had sent their families off to the Uzbeks and gone after him.

Advancing further the army came on about a hundred thousand Kirghiz sheep and drove
them along. Since the Kirghiz had united with the Uzbeks there was no more to be
done, so they turned round and went back.

The campaign was given a name. They called it *Kul Jariki* — the sheep army.

## The removal

Then the Moghuls put the case to the khan themselves. The Kirghiz had joined the
Uzbeks; the Uzbeks meant to settle in Moghulistan; he had not the strength to
match their numbers; it would be dangerous to stay in the country that winter.

So Sultan Said Khan brought Rashid Sultan and all the Moghuls of Moghulistan down
to Kashgar. News came there that the Uzbeks had been routed after all, but by then
it was the end of spring and difficult to go back, so they stayed. In the
meanwhile Tahir Khan carried off the Kirghiz left at At-Bashi and the horse-herds
the Moghuls had left behind.

Mirza Haydar's uncle had warned the khan years earlier against sending a prince to
live up there, because the Moghuls had an unbounded love for Moghulistan and would
all follow him. The problem in the end was the reverse of the one he foresaw. The
khan went up, found he could not hold it, and brought them all down — and the love
was not enough to take them back.
