At Lalibela in the Ethiopian highlands there are eleven churches that were not
built. They were excavated: a trench cut down into solid volcanic rock to isolate
a block, and the block then carved from the top down into a building, complete
with roof, windows, columns, arches, doorways and interior chambers, with drains
and a tunnel system connecting them.

Bete Giyorgis, the best known, is a cross in plan, about twelve metres deep,
standing in a pit of its own making. It is a single stone.

They are conventionally attributed to the Zagwe king Lalibela, in the late
twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, and are still in use. Priests serve in
them daily and have for eight hundred years.

## What it took to make one

Eleven monolithic churches, cut down into the rock from the surface, with the
interiors hollowed out afterwards — columns, aisles, vaults and windows carved in
place out of the living stone, so that every cubic metre removed had to be
carried up and out.

There is no scaffolding on a building like that, no correcting a mistake, and no
possibility of building up what you cut away wrongly. The work runs in one
direction only.

A dynasty that could commission eleven of them was directing organised labour at
a scale comparable to the Aksumite stelae four centuries earlier, and the
comparison is worth making because it points somewhere. Aksum put that capacity
into royal tombs. The Zagwe put it into churches — publicly usable buildings,
laid out as a representation of Jerusalem, with a stream beside them named the
Jordan.

The difference between what a state builds for its dead kings and what it builds
for its congregations is a statement about what had changed in four hundred
years.

## The usurpers who built it

The Zagwe ruled the highlands from about 1137 and were overthrown in 1270. They
were not Aksumite in origin — they came from the Agaw of Lasta, further south —
and the tradition that replaced them spent the following six centuries calling
them usurpers.

That is worth holding against the buildings. The dynasty the official tradition
treats as an interruption is the one that produced the most remarkable thing
anybody in the region ever made; the dynasty that called them usurpers produced
nothing of the kind. The Zagwe held the state, the church, the script
and the liturgy for a hundred and thirty years, and cut eleven churches out of a
mountain.

Between the end of Aksum around 940 and the Zagwe in 1137 there is very little
anybody can say. What is not in doubt is that the church, the script, the
liturgical language and the highland Christian kingdom carried through the gap
without a break, and that what came out the other side was recognisably the same
civilisation with a different family on the throne.
