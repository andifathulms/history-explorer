At Lalibela in the Ethiopian highlands there are eleven churches that were not
built. They were excavated: a trench cut down into solid volcanic rock to isolate
a block, and the block then carved from the top down into a building, complete
with roof, windows, columns, arches, doorways and interior chambers, with drains
and a tunnel system connecting them.

Bete Giyorgis, the best known, is a cross in plan, about twelve metres deep,
standing in a pit of its own making. It is a single stone.

They are conventionally attributed to the Zagwe king Lalibela, in the late
twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, and are still in use. Priests serve in
them daily and have for eight hundred years.

## What the corpus does with a building

Nothing, in the coded fields, which is a problem this site has met before.

This corpus's Egypt Old Kingdom record has the same difficulty with the pyramids
and says so: the `TURNING_POINT_TYPES` vocabulary is battle, siege, treaty,
revolt, succession-crisis, conversion, capital-move, catastrophe and reform, and
six of those nine are military. There is no value for *built something that
changed what the state was for*.

So Lalibela is not a turning point on this record and this chapter is where it
goes. What can be said about it in the site's terms is indirect and still worth
saying: a dynasty that could commission eleven monolithic churches was directing
organised labour at a scale comparable to the Aksumite stelae four centuries
earlier, which this corpus's Aksum record reads as the institutional evidence for
a state that left no administration.

The same argument works here and points the other way. Aksum put that capacity
into royal tombs. The Zagwe put it into churches, which is a statement about what
had changed between them.

## The Zagwe, and where this record starts

The Zagwe ruled the highlands from about 1137 and were overthrown in 1270. They
were not Aksumite in origin — they came from the Agaw of Lasta, further south —
and the tradition that replaced them spent centuries calling them usurpers.

This record starts with them rather than with the Solomonic restoration, and the
choice matters. The alternative would be to begin in 1270 and treat the Zagwe as
an interruption, which is what the Solomonic sources want and is not what
happened: the Zagwe held the state, the church, the script and the liturgy, and
built the most remarkable thing anybody in the region built.

## And where it stands to Aksum

This corpus's Aksum record ends in 940 and its last chapter refuses to supply a
moment of fall — it argues, with Phillipson, that the transformation into
medieval Christian Ethiopia is stronger than the older accounts allowed and that
looking for a collapse is the wrong shape of question.

Between 940 and 1137 there is very little. This site therefore carries two
records with a gap of nearly two centuries, and does *not* join them with the
`resumes` field, which says two records are one object seen twice.

They are joined by an edge instead — `aksum -> ethiopia`, type `claimed
legitimacy of` — because what the Solomonic dynasty did in 1270 was assert
descent from the kings of Aksum, and a claim is not an identity. The next chapter
is that claim, and it closed a field on the Aksum record that had been null with
a comment saying nobody had looked.
