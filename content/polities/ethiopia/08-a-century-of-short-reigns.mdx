Yasous I's assassination set a pattern later Gondarine successions
would repeat with grim regularity. Of the kings who followed him across
the next seventy years, most died violently, several within a handful
of years of their own coronation - a run of short reigns that hollowed
out royal authority even while the castles at Gondar kept being built
and the court kept functioning.

## A dynasty that stopped dying quietly

Tecla Haimanout I, the son whose faction had hastened his father's
death, was himself assassinated while hunting within two years of his
own accession. His successor Tifilis survived barely three years
before rebellion and, by most accounts, poison ended his reign; Oustas,
who seized the throne after him, died in confinement under
circumstances Bruce's own sources could not agree on. David IV
convened church councils, executed Catholic priests found still
working in the kingdom, and was himself killed by poison after barely
five years on the throne. Bacuffa, who followed, survived a series of
conspiracies against him partly by counterfeiting his own death to draw
out conspirators, and died of natural causes after a decade - one of
the few Gondarine kings of this stretch who did not die by violence.

## A longer reign, and a war it could not win

Yasous II, who took the throne in 1729, broke the pattern of short
reigns and ruled for over two decades, remembered for hunting,
building and the arts of peace as much as for war. His one major
military campaign, against the Funj sultanate of Sennar on the Blue
Nile, ended in the destruction of his own army - a defeat serious
enough that it marked the practical limit of Gondarine Ethiopia's reach
into the Sudanese lowlands for the rest of the kingdom's history, even
as Yasous himself kept the throne until his death in 1753.

## Joas, and the general who made himself indispensable

Yasous II's son Joas inherited a court already destabilized by his own
mother's extended family, whose members had married into most of the
kingdom's leading houses and crowded out the older aristocratic
lineages that had traditionally staffed the great offices of state.
Joas's own reliance on Oromo relatives through his mother's side
deepened resentment among the Amhara and Tigrayan nobility who had
supplied the crown's officers for generations. Into this instability
stepped Ras Mikael Sehul, the governor of Tigray, who arrived at Gondar
with his own disciplined provincial army at the start of the reign and
spent the following years accumulating military power the crown
increasingly depended on to hold its own capital secure. By the reign's
final years, Mikael Sehul's forces were no longer merely one power
among several at court but the decisive military force in the kingdom
- the position from which, in 1769, he would depose the king he had
spent Joas's whole reign serving, and open the era Ethiopian
historiography would remember as the Zemene Mesafint.
