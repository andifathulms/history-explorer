For five centuries Ethiopia had no capital. The emperor moved, and the court
moved with him, and the court was enormous.

Portuguese visitors in the 1520s describe it: a camp of tents laid out to a fixed
plan with the emperor's enclosure at the centre, the churches in their appointed
places, the great officers ranged around, streets and markets, and a population
they estimated in the tens of thousands. It stayed somewhere for a few months, ate
the district, exhausted the firewood, and moved.

For most of its history this empire had no capital city at all, and that is the
single most distinctive fact about how it was governed.

## Why a mobile court

Because governing the highlands meant being present in them.

The provinces were held by hereditary lords and by the church, and both were
loyal in proportion to how recently the emperor had been there. A court that
travelled was a court that collected its own tribute, judged its own cases, and
reminded a governor annually that the crown existed. Taddesse Tamrat treats the itinerant
court as the administration itself rather than as an eccentricity of it.

The costs were exactly what they look like. The camp destroyed the country it
passed through. It could not accumulate anything: no archive, no treasury
building, no permanent institution of state beyond the church.

## The gult

The fiscal arrangement that went with it was the *gult*: a grant of the right to
collect the tribute of a district, made to a church, a monastery, a soldier or an
official, in place of a salary.

It is the same instrument, arrived at independently, that the Byzantines called
*pronoia* and the Ottomans called *timar* — pay a man with the revenue of a
district rather than with money, because you have districts and you do not have
money. Revocable in principle. Hereditary in practice, especially where
the grantee was a monastery, because taking land back from a church is a
different proposition from taking it back from a man.

The chewa regiments — settled soldiers on granted land, garrisoning the
provinces and answering to the emperor rather than to the local lord — are the
military half of the same idea, and they sat alongside the levies the provincial
lords raised. The tension between those two is most of Ethiopian political
history: an emperor who could hold the chewa governed, and one who could not was
governed by the men who raised the levies.

## The mountain

The emperor named his heir, and the instrument that made the designation stick is
one nobody else thought of.

Male relatives of the emperor were confined on a mountain — Amba Geshen in the
medieval period, Wehni later — a flat-topped massif reachable by a single guarded
path. They lived there in reasonable comfort, with servants and priests, and they
could not leave and could not conspire. When an emperor died, the designated heir
was brought down and the others stayed.

It is worth comparing with the alternatives, because the problem was universal
and the solutions mostly were not solutions. The Ottoman answer to the same
question was fratricide: the successful son had his brothers strangled, by law,
for a century and a half. The usual answer everywhere else was a civil war every
generation. Ethiopia's answer was a comfortable prison on top of a mountain, and
it worked for several hundred years.

What it could not survive was the collapse of imperial authority after Gondar.
Once the regional lords were stronger than the crown, they took to fetching
princes off the mountain themselves and installing them, which turned the
instrument inside out: the mountain had been where the crown kept its rivals, and
became a shop where the nobility went to pick an emperor.
