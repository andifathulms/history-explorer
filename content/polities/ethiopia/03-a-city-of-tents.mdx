For five centuries Ethiopia had no capital. The emperor moved, and the court
moved with him, and the court was enormous.

Portuguese visitors in the 1520s describe it: a camp of tents laid out to a fixed
plan with the emperor's enclosure at the centre, the churches in their appointed
places, the great officers ranged around, streets and markets, and a population
they estimated in the tens of thousands. It stayed somewhere for a few months, ate
the district, exhausted the firewood, and moved.

That is what this record's `capitals` field carries a comment about, and it is
the single most distinctive fact about how this state was governed.

## Why a mobile court

Because governing the highlands meant being present in them.

The provinces were held by hereditary lords and by the church, and both were
loyal in proportion to how recently the emperor had been there. A court that
travelled was a court that collected its own tribute, judged its own cases, and
reminded a governor annually that the crown existed. Taddesse Tamrat's account —
this record's source for the medieval period — treats the itinerant court as the
administration rather than as an eccentricity of it.

The costs were exactly what they look like. The camp destroyed the country it
passed through. It could not accumulate anything: no archive, no treasury
building, no permanent institution of state beyond the church.

## The gult

The fiscal arrangement that went with it is this record's `revenue_basis` field,
and the word is *gult*: a grant of the right to collect the tribute of a district,
made to a church, a monastery, a soldier or an official, in place of a salary.

It is recognisably the same instrument this corpus codes `land-grant` for from
Ur III to the Ottomans, and the same one the Byzantine and Serbian records carry
as *pronoia*. Revocable in principle. Hereditary in practice, especially where
the grantee was a monastery, because taking land back from a church is a
different proposition from taking it back from a man.

The chewa regiments — settled soldiers on granted land, garrisoning the provinces
and answering to the emperor rather than to the local lord — are the military
half of the same idea, and they are why this record's `military_basis` reads
`land-grant, conscript`. The tension between the two values is most of Ethiopian
political history: an emperor who could hold the chewa governed, and one who
could not was governed by the men who raised the levies.

## The mountain

The succession field on this record reads `nomination`, and the instrument that
made designation stick has no parallel in this corpus.

Male relatives of the emperor were confined on a mountain — Amba Geshen in the
medieval period, Wehni later — a flat-topped massif reachable by a single guarded
path. They lived there in reasonable comfort, with servants and priests, and they
could not leave and could not conspire. When an emperor died, the designated heir
was brought down and the others stayed.

It solved the problem this corpus watches states fail at repeatedly — the Ottoman
record's answer to the same question was fratricide, and this site's Second
Bulgarian record has twenty transfers in two hundred and eleven years — and it
solved it for several centuries.

What it could not survive was the collapse of imperial authority after Gondar.
Once the regional lords were stronger than the crown, they took to fetching
princes off the mountain themselves, and the fifth chapter is what that produced.
