Two of the four hundred Portuguese who landed at Massawa in 1541 wrote the
campaign up, and between them they are the only eyewitness account of the war
that decided whether the Ethiopian highlands stayed Christian.

Miguel de Castanhoso was a soldier in the force. João Bermudes said he was
patriarch of Ethiopia, probably was not, and wrote a version in which he features
more. Whiteway printed both and set the Ethiopian and Arabic accounts against
them at the end of each chapter, which is the right way to read them.

## What arrived in the winter

The Portuguese beat Ahmad ibn Ibrahim twice in the first season — in the first
battle he was wounded by a matchlock ball — and Castanhoso's account of what he
did about it is the clearest statement anywhere of what the war was actually
about.

Finding himself, in Castanhoso's phrase, with his people dead or cowed, Ahmad
sent secretly to the Ottoman commander at Zabid across the Red Sea, told him to
regard him as a vassal of the Grand Turk, and sent much money. Nine hundred Turks
came, all arquebusiers, *very fine and good men*, with ten field bombards —
because, Castanhoso notes, Ahmad had worked out that the damage done to him had
been done by artillery and matchlocks, and he had never had field pieces before.
Arabs came too, and among them twenty Turkish horsemen with gilt stirrups and
their horses shod with iron, *for in the Preste's country the horses go unshod*.

All of that arrived over the course of one winter without anybody on the other
side knowing. In August 1542 Ahmad caught Cristóvão da Gama with about half his
force and destroyed it. Da Gama got away wounded into a thicket with a few men.

## The letter

What happened next is in Bermudes' narrative, and it is the best thing in the
book.

A woman fleeing the Moors ran into the same thicket, and the pursuers following
her found da Gama. They asked who he was and he told them. They fetched a eunuch
who had been in Portuguese service to identify him, and took him to the king, who
was delighted, and offered him great honour if he would turn Muslim. Da Gama
answered *sneeringly that he was the servant of Jesus Christ, whom he would not
change for a lying dog*, and was buffeted and had his beard plucked out.

Four days later Ahmad had him brought back, spoke to him pleasantly, told him not
to be afraid, and swore to give him his liberty and a ship home if he would write
to the surviving Portuguese telling them to abandon the Ethiopians and go back to
their own country. Da Gama agreed and wrote the letter. The eunuch was set to
check it, and it said exactly what he had been told to say and nothing else.

> But by his signature he put two calthrops, by which he meant "Look out what you
> do."

The letter was carried to the Portuguese camp. The queen mother read it and *was
as one dead*, thinking the message genuine — until the caltrops beside the
signature were pointed out to her, at which she was pleased and took it in good
part. The commander wrote back thanking the king of Zeila very much for his good
will, declining it, and saying that they expected instead to complete the
enterprise their own king had sent them on, which was to capture or kill him.

Whiteway adds, in a footnote, the detail that makes the drawing remarkable: da
Gama's right arm had been broken above the elbow less than a week earlier.

He was executed shortly afterwards.

## Wainadega

The hundred and twenty surviving Portuguese joined the emperor, and in February
1543 the combined force met Ahmad's army near Lake Tana. Castanhoso gives the
order of battle with a quartermaster's precision: on their side two hundred and
fifty Ethiopian horse and three thousand five hundred foot in the van with the
Portuguese, the emperor behind with another two hundred and fifty horse and the
rest; against them the king of Zeila in person leading two hundred Turkish
matchlockmen, six hundred horse and seven thousand foot, and a second division of
the same size behind.

The Turks were beating them until the sixty Portuguese horse charged, and the
Ethiopians, ashamed to see them fight so, went in hard. Then:

> When the King saw that his men were losing ground, he in person led them on,
> encouraging them, and with him was his son, a young man, helping him; they came
> so near that he was recognised by the Portuguese, who, seeing him close, fired
> at him with their matchlocks … one ball should strike him in the breast, and he
> fell over his saddlebow and left the press; when his followers knew that he was
> wounded to the death, they lost heart and took to flight.

Castanhoso then spends as much space on the Turkish captain who refused to run —
bare-armed with a long broadsword, sweeping a space in front of him, wrenching a
javelin out of one horseman's hand and houghing another's horse, and killing the
Portuguese who finally speared him before he died of the wound.

Of the two hundred Turks, forty got away.

## What it settled

The empire survived, and the shape of the modern Horn was set by fourteen years
of one man's campaigns and the two seasons it took to reverse them.

The Portuguese wanted something for it and did not get it: the union with Rome
that Bermudes claimed to have been promised was refused, and the Jesuit attempt
to impose it a century later produced a civil war and their expulsion. What
Ethiopia took from the episode was the firearms, the knowledge of what they did,
and a story — a man with a broken arm, writing a letter to order under the eye of
his captors, signing it with two small iron spikes.
