On 1 March 1896, near Adwa in Tigray, an Ethiopian army destroyed an Italian
one. About seventeen thousand Italian and Eritrean troops advanced in three
columns over ground they had mapped badly; Menelik II met them with something in
the region of a hundred thousand men, most with modern rifles, and about seven
thousand Italians were killed and three thousand taken prisoner.

Italy recognised Ethiopian independence in the treaty that followed.

## The one that did not go the other way

Adwa is the only occasion in the partition of Africa on which an African state
met a European invasion in the field, destroyed it, and kept its sovereignty.

It is worth saying what that meant at the time rather than in retrospect. In 1896
the partition was essentially complete and the assumption behind it — that
European armies beat African ones as a matter of course — had not been seriously
tested by an African state with a modern army, because there were almost none.
Seven thousand Italian dead in a single day, in a war Italy had started, ended
the Italian government within a fortnight and made Ethiopia a subject of
international law rather than of colonial administration for the next forty
years.

## The rifles

Adwa was not won by spears against machine guns, and the version in which it was
does the Ethiopians no favours.

Menelik had spent a decade buying modern breech-loading rifles — from France,
from Russia, and from Italy, which had supplied them under the treaty it was now
attempting to enforce. He had artillery. He had spent that decade conquering
southwards, which paid for the rifles and produced the army. And he had the
diplomatic sense to keep the European powers divided about him.

What the Italians had was a colonial expedition operating on bad maps at the end
of a long supply line against a numerically superior force with comparable
weapons, sent forward by a government in Rome that wanted a victory before the
budget debate.

## What paid for it

The part that should not be passed over.

Menelik's expansion in the 1880s and 1890s roughly doubled the empire, into the
Ogaden, the south and the south-west — Oromo, Sidama, Kaffa and other territories
that had not been part of the Christian highland state. The conquests supplied
the tribute, the ivory and the manpower, and they were conducted with a
brutality Ethiopian historiography was slow to describe and that Marcus is direct
about.

The empire that survived the scramble for Africa did so partly by conducting one
of its own, at the same time, in the same decade, with the same rifles. Both
things are true and neither cancels the other: Adwa was a genuine defeat of a
European invasion, and the army that won it had been assembled and paid for by
conquering peoples who had no more chosen Ethiopian rule than the Ethiopians had
chosen Italian.

## The Zemene Mesafint, and how it ended

There nearly was not an empire to defend, and the reason is the century before
Menelik.

From 1769 the emperors at Gondar reigned without ruling. Regional lords fought
over which prince would hold the throne and be controlled; the Ethiopian sources
call it the Zemene Mesafint, after the era in Judges when there was no king in
Israel and every man did what was right in his own eyes.

It was not an interruption in the state so much as a hollowing of it. The throne
went on being occupied throughout, and the men fighting over it were fighting to
control the emperor rather than to abolish him — which is a different condition
from an interregnum, and a more durable one, because nobody involved had any
interest in the office ceasing to exist.

It ended in 1855 when Tewodros II, a provincial soldier with no dynastic claim
worth the name, had himself crowned emperor and set about forcing the country
back together. He failed, besieged by a British expedition at Magdala in 1868,
and shot himself with a pistol Queen Victoria had sent him as a gift. What he
began, Yohannes IV continued and Menelik finished.
