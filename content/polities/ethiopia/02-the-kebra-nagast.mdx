In 1270 Yekuno Amlak overthrew the last Zagwe king. The dynasty he founded ruled
for seven hundred and four years, and the claim it ruled on is the most
elaborate genealogical assertion in this corpus.

The queen of Sheba visited Solomon. She bore his son. The son, Menelik, went to
Jerusalem as a young man and came back with the Ark of the Covenant, which the
priests' sons had taken and which has been in Ethiopia ever since. Therefore the
kings of Ethiopia are Solomon's line, the country is the new Israel, and the
church at Aksum where the Ark rests is the reason the emperors were crowned
there.

The text that assembles all of this is the *Kebra Nagast* — the Glory of Kings —
compiled in the fourteenth century, and it is a national epic that is also a
constitutional document.

## What the site counts

Three fields on three different records changed when this one was written.

This record's `legitimation` carries `descent`, and the entry needs no
argument: rule 1 asks for descent invoked publicly as the ground of a right to
rule, and this was invoked continuously for six centuries and then written into
the constitution of 1955, which reserved the throne to the line of Haile
Selassie, descendant of Menelik I, son of Solomon and the queen of Sheba. A
twentieth-century constitution citing the Book of Kings as a title deed.

This corpus's Aksum record now counts a successor claim. Its field had been null
with a comment saying that the Zagwe and Solomonic dynasties were not in the
corpus and that nobody had looked at whether either asserted Aksum's legitimacy.
Somebody looked. The answer is yes, by way of the coronation at Aksum Zion and
the Kebra Nagast's routing of the Solomonic line through the kingdom.

And this corpus's **Saba** record now counts two rather than one. The queen of
Sheba is Saba', and that record's own chapter on the South Arabian alphabet
already described the claim — a dynasty that ruled until 1974 claiming descent
from a queen this kingdom may or may not have had — before there was a record to
attach it to.

## A claim on something that may not have existed

That last point is worth taking seriously rather than treating as a curiosity,
because it tests the rule.

There is no evidence outside the Hebrew Bible for the queen of Sheba. Whether she
was Sabaean, Ethiopian, both or neither is argued about, and the Sabaean record
in this corpus is careful not to claim her.

Coding rule 1 counts a claim when a later polity asserts an earlier polity's
legitimacy in its own public self-description, and it counts unrecognised claims
exactly as it counts recognised ones — on the ground that the count measures
whose legitimacy was worth claiming rather than who succeeded in claiming it.

Nothing in the rule requires the object of the claim to have been real. That
seems right and it is the first time this corpus has had to say so. What the
Sabaean record's count now measures is that a South Arabian kingdom's name was
worth building a dynasty on, three thousand kilometres away and two thousand
years later, and the edge is marked contested because the connection it asserts
runs through a woman nobody can find.

## What it was for

Not antiquarianism. The Solomonic claim did specific work.

It delegitimised the Zagwe retrospectively, which is why the dynasty that made it
needed it. It made the church and the crown a single project, which is Taddesse
Tamrat's subject and the reason this record's institutions are coded from him.
And it gave a Christian kingdom surrounded by Muslim powers a story about itself
that was not about being surrounded — the new Israel, holding the Ark, rather
than a highland remnant cut off since Egypt fell in the 640s.

The fourth chapter is what happened when the surrounding nearly finished it.
