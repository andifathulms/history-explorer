In 1270 Yekuno Amlak overthrew the last Zagwe king. The dynasty he founded ruled
for seven hundred and four years, on the most elaborate genealogical claim any
dynasty anywhere has advanced.

The queen of Sheba visited Solomon. She bore his son. The son, Menelik, went to
Jerusalem as a young man and came back with the Ark of the Covenant, which the
priests' sons had taken and which has been in Ethiopia ever since. Therefore the
kings of Ethiopia are Solomon's line, the country is the new Israel, and the
church at Aksum where the Ark rests is the reason the emperors were crowned
there.

The text that assembles all of this is the *Kebra Nagast* — the Glory of Kings —
compiled in the fourteenth century, and it is a national epic that is also a
constitutional document.

## What the site counts

The claim was invoked continuously for six centuries and then written into the
constitution of 1955, which reserved the throne to the line of Haile Selassie,
*descendant of Menelik I, son of Solomon and the queen of Sheba*.

A twentieth-century constitution citing the Book of Kings as a title deed.

## A claim on something that may not have existed

That last point is worth taking seriously rather than treating as a curiosity,
because it tests the rule.

There is no evidence outside the Hebrew Bible for the queen of Sheba. Whether she
was Sabaean, Ethiopian, both or neither is argued about, and the South Arabian
inscriptions — which are numerous, and which name a great many Sabaean rulers —
do not contain her.

Which does not matter in the least to the force of the claim, and that is the
interesting part. A dynasty ruled a country for seven hundred years, and was
still ruling it in the age of aircraft, on a descent from a woman nobody can
demonstrate existed, by a king whose historicity is itself thinly attested, via a
son who is known only from the story. The claim did the work regardless. What a
legitimising genealogy has to be is *believed*, and it has to be unfalsifiable,
and this one was both by construction.

## What it was for

Not antiquarianism. The Solomonic claim did specific work.

It delegitimised the Zagwe retrospectively, which is why the dynasty that made it
needed it. It made the church and the crown a single project, which is Taddesse Tamrat's
subject.
And it gave a Christian kingdom surrounded by Muslim powers a story about itself
that was not about being surrounded — the new Israel, holding the Ark, rather
than a highland remnant cut off since Egypt fell in the 640s.

It was tested, four hundred years later, when the surrounding nearly finished
it.
