Between the Solomonic dynasty's founding and its confrontation with
Ahmad ibn Ibrahim, one reign gets credit for turning a restored
kingship into a functioning empire: Zara Yaqob's, and it began with a
succession crisis serious enough that a monk was mutilated for
predicting it would end in his favor.

## A throne fought over for four reigns running

King Dawit died around 1412 after a reign of over three decades, and
the kingdom did not settle again for more than twenty years. His son
Tewodros reigned only months; his successor Yishaq held the throne
until his own death in battle around 1430; Yishaq's son Indiryas
lasted only a matter of months before Hizba Nagn seized power. When
Hizba Nagn himself died after a short reign, his own officials crowned
his son Mihrka Nagn, who died soon after, and Mihrka Nagn's followers
then crowned his own younger brother, Badl Nagn, still a small child,
rather than surrender power. It was during this chaos that a monk
named Sinoda was accused before Hizba Nagn's court of declaring that
another king, Zara Yaqob, would yet rise to the throne - a prophecy
that earned him and four companions the amputation of their hands and
feet, from which all of them died. The faction that had backed Zara
Yaqob's own claim outlasted the accusation. His supporters overthrew
the child-king's regents, and Zara Yaqob was crowned in 1434.

## A council that ended a century of schism

Zara Yaqob inherited a church divided since before his grandfather's
time between the Egyptian-appointed bishops and the followers of the
monastic reformer Ewostatewos, who had broken with the bishops over
the observance of the Sabbath. Where his father Dawit had left the
dispute to the Egyptian bishop to manage, Zara Yaqob took personal
charge of it. In 1450 he convened a council at his newly built church
of Debre Mitmaq, presiding over it himself with both bishops, the
leading Ewostathian monks and the abbots of the kingdom's major
monasteries in attendance. The settlement he brokered had both sides
give real ground: the bishops formally authorized the observance of
two Sabbaths, Saturday alongside Sunday, and the Ewostathians agreed in
return to accept ordination from the Egyptian prelates they had spent a
century refusing to recognize. It was the reconciliation his father's
generation had failed to reach, achieved by a king willing to preside
over the theological argument in person rather than delegate it.

## A kingdom he saw as permanently besieged

What drove Zara Yaqob's religious energy, by his own words, was a
conviction that Ethiopia's isolation was itself a danger: "our country
Ethiopia is surrounded by pagans and Muslims in the east as well as in
the west." He read every shortcoming in his subjects' Christianity -
the thin education of provincial clergy, the survival of older
Kushitic religious practice among newly absorbed peoples - as evidence
of a kingdom that could not fully defend itself against neighbors on
every side, and he pushed reform hard enough into the reign's final
years that the pattern of an activist, doctrinally engaged monarchy he
established would outlast him. It was that same kingdom, unified under
a functioning church and a centralized throne, that his own
descendants would need everything Zara Yaqob had built to defend once
Ahmad ibn Ibrahim came out of the eastern lowlands two generations
later.
