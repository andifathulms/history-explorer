In 1529 Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi, imam of the sultanate of Adal in the lowlands
to the east, beat the Ethiopian army at Shimbra Kure and then spent fourteen
years taking the highlands apart.

He had Ottoman matchlockmen and artillery. The Ethiopians had cavalry, spears and
a country they could not defend from a force that could shoot into it. By the
middle of the 1530s most of the plateau was his, the emperor was a fugitive
moving between mountains, and the churches and monasteries were being burned —
including the treasuries where the kingdom's manuscripts were kept, which is why
so little written before 1530 survives and why this record's medieval evidence is
thinner than it should be.

Ethiopian tradition calls him Gragn, the left-handed.

## Two interventions

In 1541 four hundred Portuguese musketeers landed at Massawa under Cristóvão da
Gama, Vasco da Gama's son. They had come because an Ethiopian embassy had spent
years asking, and because Portugal wanted a Christian ally behind the Ottoman
position in the Red Sea.

They were badly mauled and da Gama was captured and executed. The survivors
joined the emperor Gelawdewos, and in February 1543 at Wayna Daga near Lake Tana
Ahmad was shot and killed and his army dispersed within days.

That is this record's second turning point, and what makes it worth the entry is
the shape of it. An Ottoman-equipped Muslim army from the Horn nearly destroyed a
Christian African kingdom, and was stopped by a Portuguese expedition sent round
the Cape. Two of the largest powers of the sixteenth century fought a proxy war
in the Ethiopian highlands, and this corpus carries one of them — the Ottoman
record — and the other not at all.

## What it cost

Nearly everything, and then the state came back.

The kingdom was reduced to a fraction of itself and never recovered the
south-eastern provinces. Adal collapsed too, exhausted, within a generation. And
into the emptied country came the Oromo, moving north in a large migration
through the second half of the century, settling in territory both states had
just finished ruining and permanently changing the demography and eventually the
politics of the highlands.

That is the outcome of the fourteen years that mattered most and it belongs to
neither side of the war.

## The record this cannot show

This record's `reach_km2` is null and this chapter is a good place to say what
that costs.

A state that loses most of its territory in a decade, holds a remnant, and is
back at something like its old extent within a century has a shape the site's
single peak figure could not show even if there were one. The `measures` block
allows a dated series — an `extent` list, one figure per year, each point sourced
— and this record has none, because nobody has published one for Ethiopia and
this project will not construct one.

Hard rule 9 would forbid drawing a line between two such points anyway. What the
site would show is columns standing at the years the sources name, with nothing
between them, and for this polity there are no years and no sources. The
narrative is in the chapters and the column is empty, which is the honest
arrangement and not a satisfying one.
