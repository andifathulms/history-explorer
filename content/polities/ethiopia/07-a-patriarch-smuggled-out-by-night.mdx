Fasilides inherited a kingdom his own father Susenyos had nearly torn
apart by converting to Catholicism and trying to impose it by force.
His reign undid that decision and built, in its place, the fixed
capital the Solomonic dynasty had never had.

## Expelling the mission his father had welcomed

Within months of his accession in 1632, Fasilides moved to end the
Jesuit mission his father had sponsored. He ordered the Catholic
patriarch and priests confined to the mission station at Fremona,
demanded they surrender their firearms and ammunition, and directed
that they be escorted out of the kingdom through the Red Sea port of
Massawa. The patriarch tried to evade the order rather than comply
with it outright, secretly arranging for a rebel frontier lord to
smuggle the mission out of Fremona by night rather than accept
deportation on the king's own terms - a plan that succeeded, though it
bought the mission little beyond a longer and more dangerous route out
of the country. Fasilides's own uncle Sela Krestos, suspected of
conspiring with the Jesuits and of encouraging his nephew Claudius to
rebel with Portuguese military backing, was banished and ultimately
executed on the king's order after continuing to correspond with the
exiled mission from confinement. The Catholic interlude that had cost
the kingdom a civil war under Fasilides's own father was over within
the first years of his reign, and no later Ethiopian monarch would
invite a Catholic mission back on comparable terms.

## A capital that stayed in one place

Fasilides's other lasting act was to abandon five centuries of
practice and build a permanent capital. Gondar, founded in the 1630s
north of Lake Tana, gave the Solomonic monarchy something it had never
had since before the Zagwe interruption: a fixed seat rather than a
moving camp that consumed a district's food and firewood and moved on
once it had. Over Fasilides's own reign and those of his immediate
successors, Gondar grew into a walled complex of stone castles, each
emperor typically adding his own, along with churches whose interior
murals would define Ethiopian religious painting for the whole
Gondarine period.

## Yasous the Great, and a son who could not wait

Fasilides's grandson Yasous I, who came to the throne in 1680, extended
the kingdom's reach with a sustained run of campaigns against the Agaw
and Oromo peoples on its frontiers, earning the posthumous title Tallac
- the Great - for a reign his own contemporaries remembered as one of
victory after victory. It ended in a killing arranged by his own
household. Devastated by the death of a favored consort, Yasous
retreated into a prolonged mourning that left the succession
question open long enough for rival factions to organize around it; his
son Tecla Haimanout, urged on by his mother Malakotawit and told that
his father meant to pass him over for a half-brother, let his own
faction at Gondar swear to fight for his succession while his father
was still alive and in the field. When illness forced the ailing
Yasous to retreat to an island on Lake Tana rather than march on the
capital, the queen sent her own brothers there with soldiers who shot
and then ran the old king through with a sword. His son, now unopposed, showed every
public mark of grief and reverence toward the father he had allowed to
be killed - burying him among his ancestors with full royal honors even
as the succession his own faction had forced stood unchallenged.
