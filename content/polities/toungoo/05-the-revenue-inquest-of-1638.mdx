Anaukpetlun's own reign ended in 1628 when his son had him
assassinated; the murderer was seized within days by followers
disgusted at his cowardice and tyranny, and the throne passed instead
to Thalun, one of Anaukpetlun's uncles, who executed the killer on
arrival and rejected his plea to be spared as a monk. Even Thalun's
own coronation at Pegu was not free of violence - conspirators tried
to rush the royal armoury while he was still in the coronation
palace, and though the Guard drove them off, the men who escaped held
Moulmein by force for a time before being hunted down in large
numbers, touching off a wave of Talaing refugees into Siam that the
Siamese king flatly refused to hand back.

## A capital moved for good

In 1635 Thalun crowned himself a second time at Ava and moved the
royal palace there permanently, abandoning Pegu as a capital once and
for all. The Delta had lost its old strategic logic - the dream of
using it as a springboard against Ayutthaya was dead, its river to
the sea had silted shut around 1600, and its population was still
recovering from decades of war. The move back to Upper Burma marked,
in plain terms, the final abandonment of Tabinshwehti's own founding
ambition of a single Burmese-Talaing kingdom governed from the coast:
the dynasty had tried coalescence with the Delta and failed, and now
retreated for good into the dry-zone heartland its founders had come
from.

## A palace coup his own sons survived

Thalun's reign was not free of family violence either. He named as
his own heir the brother who had stood by him during the campaign
that first brought him to power, but that brother died in 1647; the
following year Thalun's son Shintalok, backed by disaffected
household men, seduced part of the palace Guard and drove his own
father out. Thalun took refuge in a nearby monastery, defended for a
month by several hundred monks armed with nothing but sticks, until
his other sons raised enough armed men to retake the palace -
improvising cannon out of local timber since the rebels held the
royal artillery. Shintalok was shot dead trying to break out on his
own war elephant, and the defenders who survived him were finished
off, by the ordinary custom of the day, under the feet of trained war
elephants kept for exactly that purpose.

## Counting what a century of war had left

Despite these disruptions at court, Thalun's own reign is remembered
chiefly for a peace his predecessors had never had the stability to
attempt. He rebuilt the canal system feeding the Kyaukse granary, and
went further still, overhauling the revenue administration of the
entire kingdom and compiling, in 1638, the first revenue inquest in
Burmese history - the same survey a later Konbaung king, Bodawpaya,
would consciously imitate a century and a half afterward. How much of
the system Thalun invented outright and how much he simply restored
from the wreckage of Bayinnaung's own wars is impossible to say for
certain, Harvey notes, but even restoring what war had shattered was
achievement enough to rank him among Burma's better kings. The scale
of that wreckage is visible in what the inquest turned up: even
Shwesettaw, one of the kingdom's holiest shrines, had been so
completely cut off by decades of war and depopulation that its own
sacred footprint relics lay forgotten under scrub jungle until monks
sent by Thalun rediscovered them in the same year.
