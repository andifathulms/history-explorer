By the early 1750s the Toungoo dynasty, ruling by then from Ava, was
a shell of Bayinnaung's own empire. A Talaing rebellion out of Pegu -
the same Lower Burma the dynasty had first conquered two centuries
earlier - steadily stripped away the countryside around the capital,
burning crops, driving farmers from their fields, and cutting Ava off
from Kyaukse, the one district that could have kept its granaries
full. The reigning king left the war to his uncle Toungooyaza,
appointed joint king in his place, while an appeal for help sent all
the way to the Chinese viceroy of Yunnan produced only an observer
and no actual troops. Harvey's own verdict on the period is blunt:
"an object lesson in incompetence," in which capable leaders existed
but the government had no means of finding or using them.

## A city too weak to lift its weapons

The end came fast once it came. A besieging Talaing army, on the
verge of giving up the siege for lack of supplies, learned from
deserters that Ava's own garrison was starving badly enough that its
defenders could barely lift their weapons. They forced their way into
the city in April 1752, sacked it, and burned it to the ground; the
royal family and crowds of other captives were deported to Pegu.
Most of the surviving princes fled to the Shan hills and made no
further attempt at resistance. The one exception, Toungooyaza
himself, escaped his own captors in disguise as a fisherman and tried
to rally support from Mogaung, but died within the year - "not an
able man," Harvey judges, "but at least a faithful servant."

## What the Talaings did not finish

The occupying army made one decision that Harvey singles out as
decisive in the opposite direction: rather than press north while the
country was still disorganized, the Talaing crown prince withdrew
most of his own forces back to Pegu within weeks, leaving only a
fraction of his strength to hold Ava, out of a misplaced fear that
Siam might exploit the moment instead. A detachment sent to secure
the allegiance of one small village, Moksobomyo, was cut to pieces
by its own inhabitants - a warning the retreating prince brushed off
with an order to make an example of the place, "whatever they call
it." The Toungoo dynasty itself was finished. Harvey's own closing
judgment on the moment makes the distinction explicit: "the Ava
dynasty was played out, but there was nothing to show that the
Burmese people were played out" - and the very village whose
resistance the Talaings had just dismissed would, within the year,
give Burma the leader who proved it.
