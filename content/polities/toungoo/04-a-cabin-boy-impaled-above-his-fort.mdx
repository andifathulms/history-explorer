Filipe de Brito y Nicote had started life as a ship's cabin boy, and
was left behind at the port of Syriam in 1600 by an Arakanese force
with fifty fellow Portuguese and nothing but his own nerve. He spent
the interval repairing the port's defenses, ambushed and destroyed an
Arakanese flotilla sent to bring him to heel, and captured the crown
prince of Arakan in the process - treating him with chivalry in
person while still exacting a heavy ransom before letting him go.
Rather than wait for someone else to recognize what he had built, de
Brito went to Goa himself; the Portuguese viceroy gave him his own
niece in marriage and quietly sanctioned proceedings he had never
formally ordered, and when de Brito disregarded instructions anyway,
nothing came of it. He returned to Syriam a commandant in fact if not
yet in universal recognition, married his son into the ruling family
of Martaban, and built a church - governing well enough, by Harvey's
own account, that the people of the country seem to have genuinely
liked him, a rare thing for a foreign adventurer's garrison.

## A fortune built on tolls, temples and cannon metal

What made de Brito rich also made him hated further inland. He forced
all foreign trade in Burma through Syriam alone, patrolling the coast
to intercept any ship that tried to land elsewhere, which drove up
prices on every imported good the interior depended on. Worse, he
turned to systematically looting Buddhist shrines - stripping gems
from images, melting temple gold into ingots, and breaking up sacred
bronze bells for cannon metal rather than pay to import it. In 1612 he
and the ruler of Martaban went further still, sacking the city of
Toungoo itself and allying with its lord, Natshinnaung - King
Anaukpetlun's own cousin and vassal - against the crown.

## A siege, a betrayal, and a debt of loyalty repaid in blood

Anaukpetlun marched on Syriam with, by contemporary report, well over
a hundred thousand men and hundreds of ships. De Brito, caught short
of powder with most of his own men away in India, held out for
thirty-four days behind stockade walls before the besiegers mined and
pulled down the defenses with grappling hooks. During the siege
Anaukpetlun tried to split his two enemies apart, offering de Brito
generous terms if he would surrender Natshinnaung alone; de Brito,
unable to read the Burmese letter himself, handed it to Natshinnaung
directly and sent back word that the Portuguese kept their word once
given. Natshinnaung, moved by a loyalty he had not expected from a
foreign mercenary, took Christian baptism with de Brito standing as
his own sponsor, and when Syriam finally fell, asked to die alongside
him rather than be spared. Both wishes were granted. De Brito was
impaled on a stake raised above his own former fort, dying slowly
over two days in view of the port he had built his fortune on; his
wife was sent to Ava among the common slaves after refusing the
king's own advances with what witnesses called scorn and courage in
equal measure. Anaukpetlun then forced the king of Martaban to
execute his own son-in-law - de Brito's own son, married into
Martaban's ruling family - so that no trace of the man's line would
remain in Burma at all.
