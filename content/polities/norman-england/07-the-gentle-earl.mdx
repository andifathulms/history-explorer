---
title: The gentle earl

Edward II's reign opened with a favourite the baronage could not
stomach. Peter of Gaveston, a Gascon knight Edward's own father had
once exiled for his corrupting influence over the prince, was recalled
within days of the old king's death and made Earl of Cornwall - a
title the barons regarded as a crown jewel handed to a foreign
upstart. Vain, ostentatious, and given to mocking his enemies by
nicknames, Gaveston made powerful men his enemies faster than Edward
could protect him, and three exiles and returns later the magnates
forced the king to accept a standing council, the Lords Ordainers,
empowered to reform the royal household over his head.

## A gospel oath broken within weeks

Gaveston's fourth return proved fatal. Besieged at Scarborough, he
surrendered on the earls' sworn promise, taken on the Gospels, that he
would come to no harm before Parliament decided his fate. The Earl of
Pembroke, entrusted with his custody, left him resting at Deddington
in Oxfordshire while attending to business elsewhere - and at sunrise
the Earl of Warwick, "the black dog of Arden" by his own family's old
nickname, burst into the bedchamber with an armed party and declared
him a traitor taken. Marched before Lancaster at Blacklow Hill,
Gaveston knelt and begged the "gentle earl" for mercy; the three earls
withdrew a short distance, and two Welshmen in Lancaster's service ran
him through and struck off his head. It was, even by the standards of
the age, a plain breach of a sworn oath, and it left Edward with a
grievance against Lancaster that neither man ever set aside.

## An unhappier attachment, and a queen who would not forgive it

The dead favourite was replaced, over the following decade, by two
more - Hugh Despenser, father and son - whose greed for confiscated
estates provoked a fresh baronial revolt that ended with Lancaster's
own capture and execution in 1322, an unmistakable act of revenge for
Blacklow Hill. But Edward's queen, Isabella of France, had by then
turned irrevocably against her husband and his favourites. Sent to
Paris on a diplomatic mission in 1325, she formed an alliance - and,
the chroniclers agreed, an affair - with the exiled baron Roger
Mortimer, and the following September the two landed in England with
a small mercenary force. Resistance collapsed almost at once; the
elder Despenser was executed within weeks, and the king and the
younger Despenser were captured in November trying to escape by sea
from Wales.

## A resignation extracted from a fainting king

Rather than risk the odium of formally deposing an anointed king,
Isabella's party sent a delegation to Kenilworth to receive Edward's
own resignation in favour of his fourteen-year-old son. Dressed in
black, the king collapsed in a swoon at the delegates' feet when told
his people wished him gone; revived, he agreed to yield the crown "lest
the estates should choose someone not of the royal line." Edward III
was proclaimed within days. His father, moved under guard from castle
to castle and finally immured at Berkeley, proved too robust a
constitution to die quietly of neglect. On the night of 21 September
1327, the peasantry near the castle heard piercing shrieks from
within its walls; Edward of Carnarvon was announced the next day to
have died naturally, his body displayed to still the rumors that
followed him into the same abbey church at Gloucester where his tomb
would soon draw pilgrims of its own.
