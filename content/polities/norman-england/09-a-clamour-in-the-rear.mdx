Henry V's invasion of France in 1415 revived a war that had gone
largely quiet for a generation, and Agincourt, fought on 25 October,
gave England a victory on a scale not seen since Edward III's day. A
much larger French force, its knights exhausted by a long advance
through deep mud before they ever reached English lines, was cut down
in waves; contemporaries described heaps of French dead and dying
piled five feet high at points along the front. The Duke of Alençon
fought hard enough to fell the king's own brother and clip a piece
from Henry's crown before he too was killed.

## An order given in fear

Near the battle's end, with much of the English army scattered taking
prisoners and a mounted French reserve still forming for a final
charge, a clamour broke out behind the English line: raiders had
struck the baggage train in the rear. Fearing he might yet lose a
battle already half won, Henry ordered every soldier to kill his own
captives and return to the ranks. Some refused, and the king sent his
own bodyguard to see the order carried out before it became clear the
alarm was a false one - a local raid by a handful of camp followers,
not a fresh army. Hundreds of prisoners had already been killed by
then.

## A victory that reopened a settled question

The immediate cost to England was light; the French dead included
much of their own nobility, among them the Duke of York on the English
side, who died of exhaustion rather than wounds. What Agincourt
achieved was less a lasting conquest than a revival - it put English
claims in France back on the same footing they had held under Edward
III two generations earlier, and set the stage for Henry's own
recognition as heir to the French crown within five years. Whether
that revival could actually be sustained was a question Henry did not
live to see answered; he died in 1422, and the war he had reopened
would end, under his son, in a very different way.
