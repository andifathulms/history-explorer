Richard III came to Bosworth Field on 22 August 1485 having slept
badly, his face by every account haggard and pale with what
chroniclers called dismal forebodings - yet he promised his doubting
captains victory in tones of haughty confidence, and his position on
Ambion Hill looked strong. His army, drawn up in three divisions
across the higher ground, outnumbered Henry Tudor's invading force
substantially. What Richard could not control was how many of his own
men actually wanted him to win.

<Figure id="en-richard3" />

## Betrayal in the middle of the fighting

The Earl of Northumberland, commanding Richard's rear guard, held his
own division back from the fighting entirely. When Richard, seeing
Henry's own position in the opposing line, led a mounted charge
directly at him and very nearly broke through - killing Henry's
standard-bearer and reaching the pretender himself in close combat -
it was Sir William Stanley, who had been edging his own force nearer
throughout the battle without committing to either side, who decided
the day. Stanley's men fell on Richard's flank and rear, and the royal
line broke with cries of treason.

<Figure id="en-bosworth" />

## A king who would not run

Richard's own attendants brought him a horse and begged him to flee
while they held the enemy back. He refused, saying he would at least
die King of England, and rode back into the fighting - cut down at
last, his helmet battered through, still shouting accusations of
treason as he fell. Even an admiring enemy, a retainer of the very
Stanleys who had turned the battle against him, gave Richard's memory
some of the finest verse written in England that century. Henry Tudor
took the crown from the field. The line that had ruled England, with
one nineteen-year interruption, since William's coronation at
Westminster four hundred and nineteen years before, ended that
afternoon in a Leicestershire field with a usurper's own usurper
killed in the mud.
