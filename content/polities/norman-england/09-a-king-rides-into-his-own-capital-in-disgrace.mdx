Richard II had confiscated the vast Lancastrian inheritance the moment
its owner, his exiled cousin Henry, refused to renounce his claim to
it. It was a mistake serious enough to unite much of the kingdom
against him: Henry landed to reclaim his lands rather than the crown,
by his own declared purpose, but Richard's own support collapsed
around him with startling speed. Royal officials were seized and
executed without trial at Bristol; the Earl of Salisbury's levies in
Wales melted away once Richard's own flight became known; by the time
the king reached Conway Castle with a bodyguard reduced to some five
score men, there was no army left to fight with.

## Surrender at Flint

Richard's own terms for surrender asked only for his life, an
honourable income, and safe conduct for the handful of followers still
with him. Henry received him at Flint with what the chronicles call
hypocritical courtesy, informing him that he had come to help govern
England better than it had been ruled for twenty-two years. Richard
was then separated from his remaining friends, forced to ride into
London on a plain hackney in a common gown while crowds jeered him,
and shut up in the Tower while Henry took possession of Westminster.

## A deed read aloud

Parliament had already been summoned in Richard's own name to meet on
30 September. The day before it sat, Richard executed a formal deed of
abdication, declaring himself "insufficient and useless" and
unworthy to reign, and read it aloud in his Tower chamber before
witnesses. His manner, by every account, was calm, even cheerful - an
incongruity that puzzled contemporaries as much as it puzzles readers
now. It was the first time since the Conquest that a crowned English
king had been removed from the throne by a rival claimant rather than
dying in office, and it opened a question about the crown's own
legitimacy that would not be fully closed for nearly a century.
