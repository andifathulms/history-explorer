title: Three centuries in Gascony end in an afternoon

By 1453 English rule in Gascony, held in one form or another since the
twelfth century, was down to its last defensible positions. When a
French army laid siege to Castillon on the Dordogne that July, the
aging commander John Talbot marched out with every man, Gascon and
English, he could gather to relieve it - and, finding the besiegers
too well entrenched to outflank, formed his whole force into a single
column and drove straight at their lines.

## Talbot's last charge

The assault crossed the ditch under heavy artillery fire and struggled
repeatedly against the French earthworks before outlying French
detachments began closing on the English flanks and rear. A cannonball
broke Talbot's leg; his sons and household knights fought and died
around him one by one until the whole column broke and the French
overran the field. Talbot and his companion commander Lisle were found
dead side by side. Bordeaux itself, though it knew no relief was
coming, held out through an eighty-day siege before finally
surrendering that October.

## What ended with it

The defeat at Castillon closed the Hundred Years' War in total French
victory and, within a few years, cost England every remaining
possession in France but Calais - three centuries of rule in Gascony
gone in a single autumn, with towns as far as Bordeaux remembering
their old English allegiance for generations afterward without any
realistic hope of restoring it. At home the disaster discredited the
government that had presided over it beyond any recovery; a parliament
that had shown itself compliant right up to Castillon's own eve found,
once the news reached England, that a war fought abroad for a century
was about to be replaced by one fought at home. Oman's own chapter
title for the years that followed says as much as any summary could:
the outbreak of the Wars of the Roses.
