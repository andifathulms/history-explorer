By the spring of 1215 King John had exhausted whatever goodwill his
barons still had for him. Years of heavy taxation to fund failed wars
in France, arbitrary justice, and a running dispute with the pope had
left him with almost no support outside the garrisons his foreign
mercenaries held for him. In May the rebel barons entered London
itself, and the machinery of royal government effectively stopped -
the exchequer's ordinary business ceased, sheriffs found no one
willing to pay the king anything or obey him at all.

## Runnymede

With no real alternative, John granted the barons a safe conduct to
negotiate and met their representatives at Staines in June; the barons
themselves camped in the meadow of Runnymede nearby. Terms already
drafted as a set of "Articles" were presented, accepted, and worked up
into the Great Charter, sealed on 15 June 1215.

## What the charter actually claimed

Read against later ideas of liberty, Magna Carta's specific clauses
disappoint: no consent to taxation in any modern sense, no jury trial
as a check on royal power, nothing resembling Parliament. It was, in
its own century, a feudal document through and through, drawing its
substance from the existing law governing king and barons rather than
inventing new rights - a selective codification of points the king had
persistently violated, restated so his pledge to observe them, and a
means of enforcing that pledge, could be secured in writing. What gave
it lasting weight was not any individual provision but the single
principle running under all of them: that the king was bound by the
same law as his subjects, and could be compelled to keep it. The
charter settled nothing in the short term - war between John and the
barons resumed within weeks, and John died the following year with the
kingdom still in arms - but the principle itself proved harder to
kill than the crisis that produced it.
