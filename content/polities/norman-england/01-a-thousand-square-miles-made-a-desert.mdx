William's coronation at Westminster on Christmas Day 1066 did not
settle England. For three years the north kept rising against him -
Norman garrisons massacred at Durham, castles attacked at York,
exiles returning from Scotland to stir fresh revolt - and in 1069 the
danger nearly became fatal. A Danish fleet under Sweyn's brothers
landed at the Humber, joined the English rebels, and took York itself,
burning much of the city, including its cathedral, in the process. It
was the most serious threat William had faced since Hastings.

## An army returns to the north

William drove the Danes back to their ships and put down a scatter of
risings in the west, but it was what he did next in the north that
would be remembered longest. Returning from a campaign in the west
with little delay, he marched his army through the country between
York and Durham and laid it waste with deliberate, methodical cruelty.
Fugitives were hunted down and killed; houses burned; livestock
slaughtered; stores of food and farm tools destroyed so thoroughly
that survival across roughly a thousand square miles became
physically impossible. Even contemporary Norman writers, generally
favourable to William, were troubled by what they described - one
noted that conquerors and conquered alike suffered under the resulting
war, famine and plague.

## What obedience actually cost

The country did not recover for a generation. What the Harrying
achieved, in narrowly military terms, was final: no army rose again in
Northumbria to seriously contest Norman rule. But it set the pattern
for how the Conquest would be secured more broadly - not through
negotiated submission alone, as at Berkhamstead in 1066, but through a
readiness, when resistance persisted, to make an example severe enough
that no one dared repeat it.
