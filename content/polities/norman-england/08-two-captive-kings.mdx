Edward III's claim to the French throne, pressed through his mother's
descent from the Capetian kings, produced a war whose opening decade
gave England a run of victories no earlier English king had come
close to matching. At Crécy in August 1346, English and Welsh archers
broke a French army built around its own chivalry, the Prince of
Wales commanding one wing in his own first major battle; Philip of
France's dead included the blind King John of Bohemia, who had ridden
into the fighting anyway, his horse's bridle tied to those of his
attendants.

## Two fronts, one autumn

Barely two months later, while England's fighting men were still
occupied in Normandy, David II of Scotland invaded the north in
support of his French ally, expecting to find nothing but monks and
shepherds behind. He met the levies of the northern border lords
instead, drawn up at Neville's Cross outside Durham on 17 October, and
was defeated and taken prisoner - joining the Tower's other royal
guest a decade later, once the war in France produced a second
captive king to match him.

## Poitiers

That second capture came at Poitiers in September 1356, where the
Black Prince's smaller army destroyed the main French force in a
battle contemporaries described as unlike any other in living memory
- neither side's archers or men-at-arms giving way for hours together.
King John II of France was taken prisoner fighting to the last, along
with his youngest son. Led through the streets of London the following
spring, John found in the Tower an old acquaintance: David II of
Scotland, still held there from Neville's Cross a decade before.
England, for a brief span in the 1350s, held the captured kings of
both its neighbours in the same prison at once - a position no English
king before or after would ever occupy again.
