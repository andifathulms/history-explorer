Henry II's quarrel with Thomas Becket, his own former chancellor and
the archbishop he had promoted expecting a loyal ally, was fundamentally
a dispute over jurisdiction: whether clergy accused of crimes should
answer to the church's own courts or the king's. Years of confrontation
and an uneasy reconciliation had settled nothing by the time Becket
returned from exile in December 1170, and he returned bringing
conflict rather than peace - excommunicating bishops who had taken
part in crowning Henry's son without his own participation as
archbishop, and refusing any concession short of unconditional royal
submission to the pope's judgment.

## Words spoken in anger

News of Becket's Christmas excommunications, or possibly of reports
brought by the bishops he had struck at, reached Henry in Normandy and
provoked a furious outburst against a man he had once trusted and
promoted, blaming his own household for failing to rid him of the
priest. Four knights - Reginald Fitz Urse, William of Tracy, Hugh of
Morville and Richard le Breton - took the words as authorization
enough. They crossed to England, confronted Becket in his own
cathedral, and when he refused to yield ground, cut him down within
the church's doors on 29 December.

## A murder that outgrew its cause

The killing of an archbishop inside his own cathedral shocked Latin
Christendom in a way the underlying jurisdictional dispute never could
have on its own. Henry, whose words had provoked the act even if he
had never ordered it, was forced into public penance at Becket's tomb,
and the murdered archbishop was canonized within three years, his
shrine at Canterbury becoming one of the most visited pilgrimage sites
in Europe. The argument over criminous clergy that had started it all
was, in the end, barely settled either way - what outlasted it was the
memory of the murder itself, and the church's own enhanced standing
that came from having produced a martyr a king's own men had killed.
