Seventeen years after Hastings, William's government still did not
have a full written record of what it actually ruled. At the Christmas
court of 1085, after debate that probably centred on the land tax
inherited from the old English geld, the king ordered a complete
survey of the kingdom's occupied land - who held it, what it was
worth, and what it had been worth under Edward the Confessor.

## Juries under oath, not a royal guess

The method was as notable as the result. Royal commissioners rode a
circuit of counties, summoning each shire court to meet them; within
each hundred, a sworn local jury answered a fixed set of questions
about every manor - its holders past and present, its ploughs, its
population, its value - with testimony reinforced where necessary by
the men of the vill or the whole shire. It was an early and
strikingly thorough use of the sworn inquest, an institution the
Conquest itself had introduced to England, and a rehearsal, though no
one at the time could have known it, for the itinerant justice and
jury system a great-grandson of William's would later build into
common law.

## Domesday

The finished record was kept in the royal treasury and treated almost
immediately as conclusive in disputed cases - within a century a
writer on the exchequer explained its popular name by comparing its
verdicts to those of the Last Judgment, final and without appeal. No
earlier English king had commanded anything like it. Domesday Book
gave the Norman crown a working knowledge of its own kingdom's
resources that made the elaborate feudal and fiscal machinery of the
next two centuries possible - not a static portrait of 1086 so much
as the foundation stone of how England would be governed for
generations afterward.
