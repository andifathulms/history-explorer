Henry I left no surviving legitimate son, only a daughter, Matilda,
whom he had made his barons swear to accept as his heir. When he died
in 1135 many of the same barons instead accepted his nephew Stephen,
who moved fast enough to be crowned before opposition could organize.
The result was nineteen years of civil war between Stephen's party and
Matilda's, fought less through pitched battles than through castles
built without royal licence and a countryside fought over piecemeal by
whichever local lord could hold it.

## An ending nobody had planned

The war's resolution came almost by accident. Stephen had spent 1153
trying to secure the succession for his son Eustace, only to be
refused by the church leadership on the grounds of an earlier papal
prohibition. Then, in the middle of a siege at Wallingford that had
drawn Matilda's son Henry of Anjou back into England, Eustace died
suddenly in August - struck, chroniclers said, with madness after
plundering the lands of a saint known for avenging such offences.
Stephen, his own wife already dead and his surviving son uninterested
in the throne, had nothing left to fight for.

## The Treaty of Winchester

At Winchester that November the two men and their supporters met
before what may have been the first full council of the realm in over
a decade. Stephen formally recognized Henry's hereditary right to the
kingdom; Henry agreed Stephen should keep the throne for the rest of
his life; and both sides swore to destroy the "adulterine" castles
raised during the war, numbered by contemporaries anywhere from 375 to
over a thousand. Stephen died the following October, and Henry
succeeded exactly as the treaty had promised - the crown's authority
badly weakened by two decades of disorder, but a single line of
succession restored where two had spent a generation contesting it.
