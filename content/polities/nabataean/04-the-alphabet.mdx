The Nabataeans wrote Aramaic. Everyone in the Near East wrote Aramaic; it had
been the administrative language from the Achaemenids onward and this corpus's
records from Assyria to the Sasanians are full of it. What the Nabataeans did
was develop their own cursive hand for it — letters that lean, join and simplify
in ways the square Aramaic of the chancery does not.

That hand is the ancestor of the Arabic alphabet.

The chain can be followed. Nabataean inscriptions at Petra and Hegra; then a set
of transitional texts running down through the Hejaz and up into Syria, in which
the letterforms progressively become Arabic while the language shifts from
Aramaic to Arabic underneath them; then the earliest inscriptions everyone
agrees are Arabic. The Namara epitaph of 328, which names its subject king of
all the Arabs, is in Nabataean script and Arabic language and sits in the middle
of the sequence.

## The count

`descendant_scripts` on this record is 1 and the item is the Arabic script. It
is among the largest single claims on this axis anywhere in the corpus — the
writing system of a quarter of the world's population, and of every Islamic
polity on this site from the Rashidun onward, deriving from the cursive
bookkeeping hand of a caravan kingdom.

Coding rule 3 distinguishes derivation, where letterforms are genealogically
descended, from adaptation, where an existing script is extended to write a new
language. Both count and both are named. This is the first kind, and the
strongest form of it: the letters themselves change shape into the letters of
the successor script, and the intermediate stages survive.

## The rival claim

The Nasrid record entering this corpus alongside this one carries 0 on the same
axis, and its comment explains why, because there is a competing tradition and
it is not a fringe one.

Later Arabic writers derive the script from al-Hira and al-Anbar on the
Euphrates, name the men who devised it, and name the man who carried it to
Mecca. That account is old, circumstantial and internally consistent, and it was
the standard view for a long time.

The epigraphic evidence does not support it. The transitional inscriptions are
in the west, not in Iraq, and the letterforms come out of Nabataean. Coding rule
3 excludes an adaptation that a source attributes to a different polity, and
this site follows the epigraphy — which means the count goes on the record of a
kingdom that had been extinct for two hundred years before the Namara stone was
cut, and not on the record of the Arab court that later tradition credits.

Two records entering in the same pass, one carrying a claim the other has a
better-known case for, is an awkward pair to publish and the right one. The
alternative is to split the count or to code both at nothing, and both of those
would be this site deciding by omission rather than by rule.

## What the script did not carry with it

Nabataean Aramaic the language died. The kingdom's own inscriptions are in it,
its coins are in it, and by the fourth century the people writing in the
descendant script are writing Arabic. This record's name field is empty for the
script — the Nabataean alphabet is in Unicode and this site does not set a name
in letters it cannot proofread — which is a small irony worth naming on the page
that carries the alphabet as its only influence count.
