Two and a half centuries after the kingdom ended, a Roman staff officer wrote a
short description of the province that replaced it, and he says something about
the annexation that is not what the modern account says.

Ammianus Marcellinus is describing the eastern provinces one after another.
Arabia, he writes, is a land full of the most plenteous variety of merchandise,
and studded with strong forts and castles which the watchful care of its ancient
inhabitants erected in suitable defiles, in order to repress the inroads of
neighbouring nations. Besides its many towns it has some mighty cities: Bostra,
Gerasa, Philadelphia, fortified with very strong walls.

Then the sentence about how it was acquired. It was Trajan who first gave this
country the name of a Roman province, appointed a governor over it, and
compelled it to obey Roman law — *after having by repeated victories crushed the
arrogance of the inhabitants*, when he was carrying his arms into Media and
Parthia.

## What is odd about that

The annexation of 106 is usually described as quiet. No source narrates a war;
no triumph was celebrated for it; there is nothing that reads like a campaign.
The Nabataean kingdom appears to have been absorbed at the death of its last
king with something closer to administration than to conquest.

Ammianus, who was a serving officer with access to army tradition and who is
generally careful about the eastern frontier, records repeated victories and
crushed arrogance.

Both things cannot be flatly true, and the disagreement is worth leaving
standing rather than resolving. A Roman officer of the fourth century believed
that Arabia had been taken by fighting. Either something happened in 106 that
left no trace in the surviving narrative sources, or the army's own memory had
converted an administrative absorption into a conquest, because a province
without a conquest is an anomaly and armies do not keep anomalies.

## The forts

The rest of the passage is the more interesting survival, and it is not
contested at all.

The strong forts and castles in the defiles were built, Ammianus says, by the
*ancient inhabitants* — the Nabataeans — to keep raiders out. He is writing in
the 380s and describing them as a present feature of the province: still
standing, still positioned where they were needed, still doing the job they were
built for.

That is what the kingdom actually left. Not a dynasty, not a claim, not a
successor: a functioning security system for a caravan country, inherited intact
by an empire that kept using it, along with the cities and the merchandise. Rome
had annexed a going concern and went on running it in more or less the shape it
was handed over in.

## What is not in the passage

One absence is worth noting, because it is the kind of absence that tells you
what a source is for.

Ammianus names the cities of the province — Bostra, Gerasa, Philadelphia — and
does not name Petra. The carved capital in the gorge, the thing the kingdom is
now known for, is simply not among the places a fourth-century officer thought
worth listing when describing Arabia to a Roman readership.

It was still inhabited. It had a bishop. But the centre of the province had
moved north to Bostra, where the governor and the legion were, and the caravan
capital had become one town among several in a country whose importance was now
its position on the frontier rather than its position on a road.
