The Nabataeans wrote Aramaic. Everyone in the Near East wrote Aramaic; it had
been the administrative language of the Near East from the Achaemenids onward.
What the Nabataeans did
was develop their own cursive hand for it — letters that lean, join and simplify
in ways the square Aramaic of the chancery does not.

<Figure id="shudu-inscription" />

That hand is the ancestor of the Arabic alphabet.

The chain can be followed. Nabataean inscriptions at Petra and Hegra; then a set
of transitional texts running down through the Hejaz and up into Syria, in which
the letterforms progressively become Arabic while the language shifts from
Aramaic to Arabic underneath them; then the earliest inscriptions everyone
agrees are Arabic. The Namara epitaph of 328, which names its subject king of
all the Arabs, is in Nabataean script and Arabic language and sits in the middle
of the sequence.

<Figure id="hegra-tomb" />

## What kind of descent this is

There are two ways one script can come out of another. It can be adapted — taken
over more or less as it stands and used to write a different language — or the
letters themselves can change shape, generation by generation, until they are
different letters. This is the second kind, and the strongest form of it: the
intermediate stages survive on stone, and the shapes can be watched turning into
each other.

<Figure id="cis-plate" />

The writing system of a quarter of the world's population comes out of the
cursive bookkeeping hand of a caravan kingdom.

## The rival claim

There is a competing tradition and it is not a fringe one.

Later Arabic writers derive the script from al-Hira and al-Anbar on the
Euphrates, name the men who devised it, and name the man who carried it to
Mecca. That account is old, circumstantial and internally consistent, and it was
the standard view for a long time.

The epigraphic evidence does not support it. The transitional inscriptions are
in the west, not in Iraq, and the letterforms come out of Nabataean.

So the credit belongs to a kingdom that had been extinct for two hundred years
by the time the Namara stone was cut, rather than to the Arab court at al-Hira
that the later Arabic writers name. It is an uncomfortable result — the tradition
is old, circumstantial and specific, and it has to be set aside on the strength
of letter shapes — but the letter shapes are what there is.

## What the script did not carry with it

Nabataean Aramaic the language died. The kingdom's own inscriptions are in it,
its coins are in it, and by the fourth century the people writing in the
descendant script are writing Arabic.

That is the ordinary shape of these things and it is worth stating. A script
outlives the language it was invented for, and goes on carrying whatever is
spoken next. The Nabataeans left no state, no successor and no literature — and
the hand their clerks used for receipts is now used for the Qur'an.
