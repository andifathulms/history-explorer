Frankincense and myrrh grow in the Hadramawt and in the highlands behind the
Yemeni coast — this corpus's Saba and Himyar records are about that country from
the producing end. Everything else in the ancient Mediterranean world burned
them: temples, funerals, medicine, private houses. The distance between the two
facts is about two thousand kilometres of desert, and the Nabataeans owned the
top of it.

The route ran up the western side of Arabia through Dedan and Hegra to Petra,
and from Petra to Gaza and the sea. Strabo describes the traffic and describes
the Nabataeans taxing goods in transit. What the archaeology adds is the
infrastructure: cisterns, dams, wells, watchtowers, staging posts and caravan
halts, and at Hegra a second Nabataean city of rock-cut tombs seven hundred
kilometres south of the first.

## The fourth of a kind

This record's `revenue_basis` reads `trade-toll` and nothing else, which makes
it one of eight polities in this corpus coded that way — with Palmyra in this
same region, Characene at the head of the Gulf, Derbend at the Caspian gate, the
Zurayids of Aden, the Rustamids of Tahert, Srivijaya on the Malacca strait and
Trebizond on the Black Sea.

They span six regions and sixteen centuries and have almost nothing else in
common: different languages, empires, religions and continents. What they share
is a structural position — a chokepoint on a route somebody else's goods had to
travel, held by a polity too small to conquer the trade and well enough placed
to charge for it. The Characene record has the chapter that sets the type out,
and it was written when there were three of them.

Two things distinguish this one. It is the earliest: 168 BC, before Characene by
a generation and before all the rest by centuries. And it is the only one of the
eight that turned the position into a territorial kingdom with provinces, an
agriculture and a script, rather than remaining a city with a customs house.

## What is not coded

The Negev.

From the first century the Nabataeans built an agriculture there that is
genuinely remarkable — runoff farming, in country receiving under a hundred
millimetres of rain a year, with hillside channels feeding terraced fields and
towns at Avdat, Shivta and Mampsis living off it. The Hawran in the north was
grain country and they developed that too.

None of it is in the `revenue_basis` field, and the omission is deliberate rather
than an oversight. Coding rule 6 asks what a source treats as the polity's
income, and what the sources treat as this kingdom's income is the route. The
farming is what a caravan kingdom does with the surplus and with the water
expertise it already has. It belongs in this chapter, where it can be described,
rather than in a field, where it would read as a claim about the treasury that
nobody has made.
