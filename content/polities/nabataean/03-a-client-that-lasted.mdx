Aretas III took Damascus in 84 BC and was intervening in the dynastic quarrel
between the two Hasmonean brothers in Judaea when Pompey arrived in Syria in 63.
That ended in one season. Scaurus, Pompey's officer, was sent against Petra,
came away with a payment rather than a city, and later struck a coin in Rome
showing a Nabataean king kneeling beside a camel with an olive branch.

The kingdom kept its king, its capital, its coinage, its script and its revenue,
and stopped being independent. Every Nabataean king after 63 BC is a Roman
ally, and this record's third turning point in 106 is the completion of what
happens here rather than a separate event.

## A hundred and sixty-nine years of it

The remarkable part is the duration. Rome absorbed client kingdoms routinely and
usually quickly — Pontus, Commagene, Judaea, Emesa, Cappadocia all went inside a
century or two of contact, mostly for cause. The Nabataeans lasted from Pompey
to Trajan without once giving Rome a reason.

Aretas IV, whose forty-nine years from 9 BC are this record's peak, styled
himself *rahem ammeh* — lover of his people — and his reign is when most of what
is visible at Petra was built. He supplied troops for Roman operations. He
married a daughter to Herod Antipas of Galilee and went to war with him when
Antipas set her aside for Herodias, which is the war the Gospel of Mark's
account of John the Baptist sits inside; Tiberius ordered the governor of Syria
to intervene and then died before it happened. It is the only occasion in a
century and a half when a Nabataean king did something Rome had to think about.

## What being a client meant here

This record's `legitimation` reads `descent, divine-sanction`, and what it
notably does not read is `investiture` — the value this site added for rulers
whose public ground was a grant from an external sovereign, and which the
Jafnid and Nasrid records in this region both carry.

The Nabataean kings never claimed Rome. The coins are in Nabataean Aramaic with
Nabataean legends naming the king and the queen. The inscriptions invoke Dushara
and Allat, not the emperor. Dead kings were divinised — Obodas I received a cult
at Avdat — which is a claim about where kingship comes from that leaves no room
for a Roman grant. Rome was the overlord in fact for a hundred and sixty-nine
years and does not appear in this kingdom's account of itself.

That is a real difference from the two Arab clients five hundred years later at
the other end of this region, and it is the sort of distinction the coded fields
exist to hold. Al-Harith the Jafnid *was* a Roman patrician and said so. The
Nasrid king wore a Persian diadem. Aretas IV was the beloved of his people.
