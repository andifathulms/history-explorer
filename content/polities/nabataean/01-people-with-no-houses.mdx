In 312 BC Antigonus One-Eye, holding Syria in the wars over Alexander's
inheritance, sent an officer named Athenaeus against a people in the desert
south of the Dead Sea. Diodorus describes what he found, and the description is
the earliest anyone wrote about the Nabataeans.

They had no houses and built none. They planted no grain and drank no wine, and
a law among them punished anyone who did. They kept their water in cisterns cut
into rock, plastered, sealed and filled in at the mouth so that nobody who did
not know the ground could find them. And they were rich, because they carried
frankincense, myrrh and spices up from Arabia and sold them at the sea.

Athenaeus surprised their gathering-place while the men were away at a fair,
took hostages and a quantity of silver and incense, and was destroyed on the
march home by the men returning. A second expedition under Demetrius found
nothing to attack, was met by envoys who pointed out that a people with no
fields and no cities cannot be compelled, and came away with gifts.

## Why that is a chapter and not a turning point

This record's span opens in about 168 BC with Aretas I, the first Nabataean
anyone can name as a king, and coding rule 4.2 keeps turning points inside a
polity's own life. So 312 sits here instead.

It earns the space because it establishes the thing the next four centuries
consist of. A power on this ground could not be taken by an army. It could be
paid, joined, married into or eventually inherited, and every empire that dealt
with the Nabataeans arrived at one of those. Rome, four hundred and eighteen
years later, took the kingdom by outliving its last king and issuing an
administrative order.

It also explains this record's `military_basis`, which reads `tribal-levy` and
nothing else for a polity that at its height held the Hawran, the Negev, the
northern Hejaz and Damascus. There is no Nabataean army in the sources in the
way there is a Seleucid or a Roman one. There are men who know where the water
is.

## Becoming a kingdom anyway

By the second century they had a king, a capital, a coinage and a script. The
transition is not documented and can only be inferred from its results, and the
results are considerable: Petra, a city in a sandstone gorge reached through a
cleft two hundred metres deep, with monumental tombs cut into the cliffs in a
façade architecture that borrows Hellenistic vocabulary and assembles it in
ways no Greek would have.

Diodorus's law against building houses is worth keeping in mind while looking at
it. Either the law was a projection by an outsider, or the Nabataeans changed
their minds about what they were, and the second is the more interesting
possibility. A people whose defence had been the ability to disappear built the
least portable city in the ancient world.
