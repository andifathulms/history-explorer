Strabo had a friend who had lived at Petra, and a source who had commanded the
Roman army that failed to reach Arabia Felix. Between them they produce the
fullest description of the Nabataean kingdom that survives, and the two halves
of it disagree about what sort of people the Nabataeans were.

The friend was Athenodorus, a philosopher. What struck him about Petra was the
foreigners. He found many Romans living there and many other strangers, and he
used to relate with surprise that the strangers were frequently in litigation —
with each other and with the natives — while the natives never had any dispute
among themselves at all, and lived together in perfect harmony.

<Figure id="qasr-al-bint" />

Strabo's own account of the constitution is brief and unusual. The city is
always governed by a king of the royal line. The king has a minister drawn from
the Companions, and the title of that minister is Brother. And the place, he
says, has excellent laws for the administration of public affairs.

<Figure id="temenos-gate" />

Then the other half, which is the same kingdom seen from a Roman command tent.

Augustus sent Aelius Gallus to find out what Arabia was and, if possible, to take
it, on the strength of a report that had been current from all time: that these
people were very rich, that they exchanged aromatics and precious stones for
silver and gold, and that they never spent any part of what they received on
foreigners. He expected either wealthy friends or wealthy enemies. The
Nabataeans promised to co-operate in everything, and the minister called Brother
— Syllaeus — came along as guide.

Strabo, who had the story from Gallus, says Syllaeus wrecked the expedition on
purpose. He guided the fleet along a harbourless coast full of submerged rocks
and shallows, and the army by routes that were impassable or enormous detours
through country with nothing in it. Gallus had begun by building eighty warships
for a naval campaign that was never going to happen — the Arabians, Strabo
observes, being traders and not a very warlike people even on land, much less at
sea — and then had to build a hundred and thirty transports instead. He reached
Leuke Kome in fifteen days having lost many ships and their crews, without ever
meeting an enemy, and then had to winter there because the army had come down
with two local diseases, one affecting the mouth and one paralysing the legs,
which Strabo attributes to the water and the plants they had been eating.

And then the sentence that gives the whole affair away. Syllaeus had insisted
there was no land road to Leuke Kome. In fact, Strabo says, the camel-traders
went to and from Petra by that road easily and in safety, with so large a body of
men and camels as to differ in no respect from an army.

<Figure id="great-temple" />

Strabo's explanation is that Syllaeus meant to spy out the country, use the
Romans to destroy rival cities and tribes, and then make himself master of
everything once famine and disease had finished them. He adds that king Obodas
paid little attention to public business — as, he says, is the custom with all
Arabian kings — and had put everything into the minister's hands.

That may be true. It is also exactly what a Roman general who had lost most of an
army without fighting anybody would want said about him, and Strabo had it from
the general.

What is not in doubt is the last item in the account, and it is the kingdom's
future. Goods came from Leuke Kome to Petra and from Petra to Rhinocolura on the
Egyptian frontier and thence to everybody else — but at present, Strabo writes,
the greater part goes up the Nile to Alexandria instead, landed at Myos Hormos
and carried by camel to Coptos. The route had moved. Petra was still rich, still
orderly, still the place where foreigners went to law and the locals did not; and
the traffic it existed to tax was already going somewhere else.
