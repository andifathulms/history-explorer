A Greek merchant's sailing handbook of the mid-first century describes the Red
Sea coast port by port, and its notice of the Nabataean kingdom is four
sentences long. It is one of the most informative things anybody wrote about
what had happened to Petra.

Two or three days east of Berenice, he says, there is a harbour and fortified
place called White Village, from which there is a road to Petra, which is
subject to Malichus king of the Nabataeans. It serves as a market-town for the
small vessels sent there from Arabia.

<Figure id="red-sea" />

Then: *and so a centurion is stationed there as a collector of one-fourth of the
merchandise imported, with an armed force, as a garrison.*

## What that sentence contains

A Roman officer, with troops, inside the territory of an independent allied
kingdom, taking a quarter of the value of everything landed.

The Nabataean state was a customs operation. Its whole existence — the water
cisterns cut across the desert, the caravan stages, the carved city in the
gorge — was financed by charging for the safe passage of other people's goods.
Whoever collected the duty at the ports was the state.

And by the time this handbook was written, at one of those ports, the collector
was a centurion.

There is no record of a war that produced this, no treaty anybody quotes, no
moment when the kingdom lost anything. The king is still named as the king; the
road to Petra is still his; Malichus is a real monarch with a real capital. He
simply no longer collected the harbour dues at White Village, and the man who
did had soldiers.

<Figure id="periplus-map" />

## The other half of the problem

The same book explains why the loss mattered so much and why it was not
recoverable.

The whole of the rest of the handbook is about ships. It describes the direct
run from Egypt to India on the monsoon, the ports of the Malabar coast, what
sold at each, and the sailing seasons — a trade route that had been in serious
operation for perhaps two generations and which did not touch Nabataean
territory at any point.

<Figure id="merchant-ship" />

Incense and pepper had once gone north overland from Arabia through the caravan
stages, and every stage was somebody's revenue. Now the bulk of it went by sea
to Egyptian ports, and what reached the Nabataeans was the residue: small
vessels from Arabia, landing at a harbour where a Roman took a quarter.

<Figure id="pepper" />

A state that lived on a road was outlasted by a sea route, and then taxed on
what was left of the road by the power that owned the sea route. The annexation
in 106 removed a kingdom that had already stopped being able to pay for itself.
