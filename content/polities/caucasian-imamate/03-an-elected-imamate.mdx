---
title: The fourth elected imamate
drafted_from: gammer-1994
phase: aside
---

This record carries `election` in the succession field, and it is the fourth
polity in this corpus to do so. The others are the Rustamids at Tahert in the
Maghreb from 776, the Ibadi imamate of Oman from 750, and the Ya'ariba imamate
of Oman from 1624.

Setting the four beside each other is worth doing, because they are separated by
a thousand years, four thousand kilometres and two entirely different theologies,
and they have the same shape.

All four are mountain or desert polities in country an imperial army can reach
and cannot hold. All four constitute an office rather than a dynasty: the imam
is chosen by those competent to judge, on qualifications that have to be
demonstrated, and is not the son of the last one. All four are governed by law
in a strong sense — the imam is a judge before he is a commander, and the
scholars who elect him are the same men who interpret what he is enforcing. And
in all four the office proved more durable than any state built under it: Oman
reconstituted its imamate at least six times across eleven hundred years, and
the corpus's Zaydi record in Yemen describes the same thing in a different
idiom.

The theologies have nothing in common. Ibadism is the surviving moderate wing of
the Kharijite movement and holds that leadership belongs to the most qualified
Muslim regardless of descent. Zaydism requires descent from Ali and a rising.
The Caucasian imamate came out of a Naqshbandi Sufi network and a programme of
replacing customary law with the sharia. What they share is not doctrine but a
problem: how a society with no hereditary sovereign and no capacity to sustain
one produces an authority it will obey.

The answer all four arrived at is an elected office held by a scholar, and the
recurring cost is also the same, and this corpus has recorded it twice. An
elective office invites disputes about procedure that cannot be settled by
force, and both Omani imamates ended in exactly that — a quarrel over whether a
deposition was lawful in 886, and over whether an election was valid in 1719,
with the losing side inviting a foreign army in each time.

This one ended differently, because the foreign army did not need inviting.
