The imamate's largest decade began with its complete destruction, and the
connection between the two is not a paradox.

Akhulgo is a double promontory above the Andi Koysu, cut in two by a chasm and
surrounded on three sides by cliffs that are in places perpendicular or
overhanging. Baddeley, who reconstructed the siege from the Russian staff
histories, describes the approach to the upper half as a razor-edged path, and
the only other way in as a plank bridge across the chasm seventy feet above the
stream. Shamil fortified it and the Russians came for it in the summer of 1839
with nine battalions.

<Figure id="akhulgo-site" />

The siege took eighty days. The tower dominating the rock was held by about a
hundred men picked for desperate courage out of a garrison not lacking in it, and
some of them went down to the river every night under the fire of Russian
sharpshooters to fetch water for the rest. Three assaults on the upper position
failed. By August the rock was covered with dead and wounded and the survivors
were encumbered with a crowd of half-starved women and children, and Shamil put
out a white flag and sent his twelve-year-old son Jamal al-Din into the Russian
camp as a hostage. Baddeley's measure of what that cost him is the persistence
with which he negotiated, many years later, for the return of a young man who had
by then become a stranger to him and his country and a uniformed servant of the
Tsar.

The negotiations failed on terms — Shamil wanted to go on living in his own
mountains — and the assault resumed on the 21st and failed again. At dawn on the
22nd the Russians went forward and found the outwork empty.

What happened in the next hours is described in the Russian accounts Baddeley is
translating and he does not soften it. The people who had not got across the
chasm fought where they stood; the women defended themselves with fury and threw
themselves unarmed against whole rows of bayonets. Guns were brought up and
trained on the far half while crowds were still climbing the opposite cliff, and
a Russian column got to the plank bridge before the garrison knew it was there.
Women flung their children off the cliffs and jumped after them, and Baddeley
puts a footnote at that sentence pointing out that Xenophon describes the same
scene in the same mountains two thousand three hundred years earlier.

More than a thousand bodies were counted and large numbers went down the river;
the stench in the chasm was such that the men clearing it had to be relieved
every few hours. Nine hundred prisoners were taken, mostly women, children and
old men, and some of those, Baddeley writes, gathered up their last strength,
snatched the bayonets from their guards and attacked them, preferring death to a
degrading captivity. The Russians lost about three thousand killed, wounded and
contused. By the 29th of August there was not a single mountaineer left on
Akhulgo.

<Figure id="akhulgo-roubaud" />

And they could not find Shamil. Every hole and cavern and corner was ransacked
again and again and every body examined, and there was no information to be got
from the survivors. On the night of the 21st, seeing that it was over, he had
taken one wife and one child and a few followers into a cavern in the cliff above
the river, and gone out the following night.

The Russian command treated the campaign as the end of the war and reduced its
commitment accordingly. Within two years Shamil had rebuilt the imamate across
Avaria and Chechnya on a scale it had never reached before 1839, and it was the
state built after Akhulgo — not the one destroyed there — that took the Russians
another twenty years to finish.
