Half a century after the surrender, John Baddeley wrote the standard English
account of the war from the Russian archives, and the two things he says at the
end of it are worth having together, because one of them is a defence and the
other is a demolition.

## The defence

Russian writers had explained the whole movement as fanaticism and licence.
Baddeley does not accept it, and he refuses the corresponding move of denying
that the mountaineers were patriots, though he notes that their idea of a
country was mostly limited to the community they came from.

His account of what happened to the doctrine is the sharpest thing in the book.
Muridism began as religious reform, on the mystical teaching of the *tariqat*.
But mysticism, he says, is for individuals, and can never shape the faith or
control the destinies of nations — so as the movement spread its leaders had to
restrict their teaching to the practical rules of the sharia. And from the
moment they aimed at political independence, it was inevitable that the worldly
element would come to dominate the spiritual.

That is not an accusation of hypocrisy, and Baddeley says so explicitly. It is a
description of what happens to any religious movement that acquires a frontier
to defend.

His verdict on Shamil is that he failed because success was impossible: he was
fighting Russia and internal division at once and could not defeat either, and
that had the whole Caucasus been one people it would probably, under such a
leader, have held out against anything sent at it. The one charge Baddeley does
press is cruelty — the apostle of liberty becoming the most despotic of tyrants,
and the power delegated to his son and his favourite naibs abused past what even
that population would bear.

## The demolition

And then the observation that turns the whole record over, which Baddeley
credits to Shamil himself.

In captivity at Kaluga, the imam claimed a particular merit. By his
administrative measures and their merciless enforcement, he said, he had made it
easy for his conquerors to hold in peace what it had cost them so much to win.
He had accustomed the tribes to habits of discipline. He had taught them to
fight together in a common cause. He had rigorously repressed their feuding with
one another.

All of which was true, and all of which Russia inherited.

Baddeley draws the conclusion in one sentence: in the long run, paradoxical as
it sounds, Muridism was the instrument and not the enemy of Russian domination.

## What that means

A state was built in mountains that had never had one, for the specific purpose
of resisting an empire. It replaced customary law with a written one, put
appointed officials over hereditary elders, taxed, kept a chancery and stopped
the blood feud. It failed, and the empire took the country.

And the country the empire took was governable in a way it had not been in 1828,
because somebody had spent twenty-five years making it so.

That is the imamate's afterlife, and Shamil worked it out himself, in a house in
Kaluga, before anybody else did.
