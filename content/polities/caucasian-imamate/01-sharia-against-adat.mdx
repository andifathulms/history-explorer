The mountains of Daghestan and Chechnya were not governed by anybody in the
1820s, and had not been for as long as there were records. What they had instead
was *adat*: customary law, varying by valley, administered by village assemblies
and elders, regulating land, marriage, injury and — centrally — the blood feud,
which was the mechanism by which a society with no state kept order and also the
mechanism by which it stayed at war with itself.

<Figure id="kubachi" />

The movement that eventually fought Russia for twenty-five years began as an
argument about that, and the argument came first.

The Naqshbandi-Khalidi Sufi order had spread into Daghestan in the 1810s and
1820s through a network of scholars — Muhammad al-Yaraghi, Jamal al-Din
al-Ghazi-Ghumuqi — teaching that a Muslim community governed by adat was not
governed by Islam, and that the sharia had to replace it. Russian writers called
the result Muridism, after the murids, the disciples of a Sufi shaykh, and the
name stuck in a literature that treated it as fanaticism.

It was a social programme. Replacing adat with sharia meant abolishing the blood
feud, ending the authority of the village elders in favour of qadis, changing
inheritance and property law, banning tobacco, wine, music and dancing, and
transferring the settlement of disputes from the community to a court. It
attacked the khans and the beks of the Daghestani principalities as directly as
it attacked Russia, and a good deal of the imamate's fighting for its first
decade was against other Muslims.

In 1828 Ghazi Muhammad was proclaimed imam and declared jihad. He was killed in
1832 defending Gimry. His successor Hamzat Bek massacred the Avar khans' family
at Khunzakh and was assassinated in a mosque two years later.

<Figure id="gimry" />

The third imam was an Avar from Gimry named Shamil, who had been at Ghazi
Muhammad's side and had escaped the same battle with several wounds. He held the
office for twenty-five years.
