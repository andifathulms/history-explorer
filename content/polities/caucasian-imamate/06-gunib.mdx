The Crimean War gave Shamil his last opportunity and it did not amount to
anything: Ottoman and allied assistance was discussed, an allied landing was
hoped for, and what arrived was correspondence. When the war ended in 1856 Russia
was free to concentrate, and Prince Baryatinsky was appointed viceroy with
resources the Caucasus command had never had.

The last three years are a contraction. Chechnya was taken district by district
as the roads reached it, naibs defected, and the communities that had paid for
the imamate stopped. Vedeno fell in April 1859. Shamil withdrew to the mountain
of Gunib with a few hundred men, was surrounded, and surrendered on 25 August.

What happened to him afterwards is unusual enough to record. He was taken to St
Petersburg and presented to Alexander II, who embraced him; he was settled at
Kaluga and later Kiev with a pension and his family, received visitors, and was
by all accounts treated as a defeated sovereign rather than as a rebel. In 1869
he was permitted to make the pilgrimage. He died at Medina in 1871 and is buried
in the Baqi cemetery — the burial ground the Wahhabis had levelled sixty-five
years earlier, in the name of a purification not unlike the one Shamil had
imposed on Daghestan.

One thing more belongs here, because a reader who stops at 1859 will come away
with the wrong impression of how the Caucasus was conquered.

The imamate's fall is not the end of the Caucasian wars. The
western Caucasus — Circassia, which the imamate never governed — fought on until
1864, and its defeat was followed by the expulsion of the great majority of the
Circassian population to the Ottoman empire, with mortality in the crossings and
the camps that is not seriously disputed and is the subject of a live argument
about how it should be named. That is a different war on the other side of the
range from the one Shamil fought on, and it happened five years after he
surrendered.
