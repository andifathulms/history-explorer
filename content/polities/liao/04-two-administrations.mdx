The Khitan were a nomadic people of the Manchurian steppe whose ruler Abaoji
declared himself emperor in 916 and whose successors took sixteen prefectures of
northern China from a collapsing Tang successor state in 938. That left them
governing pastoral nomads and Chinese farmers in the same polity, which is the
recurring problem of every conquest dynasty on the Chinese frontier.

Their answer was to not integrate them. The Liao ran a Northern Administration
for the tribal population on steppe principles and a Southern Administration for
the Chinese population on Tang bureaucratic lines, with separate personnel,
separate law and a mobile court that moved between five capitals on a seasonal
circuit. It is a deliberate refusal of the assimilation that had dissolved
previous steppe conquerors into Chinese officialdom.

They also commissioned two scripts of their own, a large one adapted from Chinese
characters and a small one influenced by the Uyghur alphabet, neither of which is
fully deciphered. Two writing systems designed to order, by a state, for a language
that had never been written — which is a thing almost no dynasty has ever bothered
to do, and which tells you what the Liao thought they were.

The Song paid them an annual tribute in silver and silk from 1005 to keep the
peace, and described it in their own records as a gift.

## How the two halves actually worked

The Northern Administration governed the tribes: the Khitan proper, the Xi, the
Jurchen and the rest, by tribal units under their own chiefs, judged by their own
customary law, liable for military service and for tribute in kind, and answerable
to officials with Khitan titles.

<Figure id="lo-halahaichang" />

The Southern Administration governed the Chinese and Bohai population of the
sixteen prefectures: prefectures and counties, salt and iron, land tax and corvée,
civil examinations, Tang statute law, and officials with Tang titles, many of them
Chinese.

<Figure id="lo-dule" />

A man's law depended on what he was, not where he lived. The two systems had
separate treasuries, separate archives and separate career ladders, and the emperor
stood on top of both as *khagan* to one and *huangdi* to the other.

## Why they refused to merge them

Because every previous conquest dynasty on that frontier had merged them and had
ceased, within a few generations, to have an army.

The Tuoba Wei is the case everybody in North China knew. A Xianbei conquest state
that took the Yellow River plain, moved its capital to Luoyang, adopted Chinese
dress, Chinese surnames and Chinese court ritual by decree, forbade the Xianbei
language at court — and within thirty years faced a mutiny of its own frontier
garrisons, who had been left on the steppe and left behind, and which destroyed it.

The Liao answer was that a conquest dynasty must remain two things at once,
permanently and by design, because the moment it becomes one thing it becomes the
weaker one.

It held for two hundred and nine years, which is longer than any of the dynasties
that took the other road, and the Jurchen who destroyed it and the Manchus who
eventually took all of China both built systems that were recognisably the same
idea.
