Before the Khitan were an empire they were one of a pair of tribes on the Sira
Muren, and the Chinese record of them begins with a funeral.

## The prayer

The Khitan and the Kumo Xi both laid their dead in the mountains, among the trees
or hung upon them. Of the Khitan it is added that after three years the bones were
collected and burned, a libation of liquor was poured, and this prayer was said:

> In winter at noon may I eat towards the south, in summer towards the north, and
> always find plenty of swine and deer in my hunts.

Parker seizes on the pigs, because they place the people. The Tungusic forest
peoples kept swine and the Turkic and Mongol steppe peoples did not; a tribe whose
idea of a good afterlife includes hunting pig is a forest people, not a steppe one.

Everything else about the Liao follows from that. They were not nomads of the open
grass like the Xiongnu or the Türk. They came out of the forest and the river
valleys of what is now Manchuria, they had agriculture and pigs as well as horses,
and they were therefore able to do a thing that pure steppe powers never managed:
hold farmland and work it.

## The record before the record

They appear in the Chinese histories as a nuisance and then as a client.

The Xianbei had pushed them and the Xi north of the Sira Muren; the Tuoba emperor
punished the Xi for raiding in 388; from 440 the Khitan and the Xi sent regular
tribute of horses; in 479 they moved south under a chief Parker renders Bagatur
Murkan to the Loha river. In 493 the Xi raided and were beaten, forgiven, allowed to
trade inside the Wall, and thereafter sent horses and sables regularly. In the sixth
century the Khitan had to be punished again, separated from the Xi for twenty years,
and went back north of the river; and the Northern Qi built three hundred miles of
new Great Wall from the Nankou pass to Datong to keep them out.

Five hundred years of that, before anything that could be called a state.

## Why it kept happening

Parker's explanation of the mechanism is the most useful thing in his book, and it
applies to every polity in this part of the record.

The dynasties of North China that dealt with the Khitan — Tuoba, Yuwen — were
themselves the more civilised tribes of the same race. Among the eastern Tungus the
pattern repeats exactly: the earlier-civilised group founds a kingdom, the
less-civilised founds an empire after it, and the most obscure tribe of all
eventually rears its head over both. That sequence runs Bohai, then the Jurchen,
then the Manchus — three states in eight centuries out of the same forest, each
raised by a people the previous one had regarded as backward.

> Horse-back forms the state.

It is always a petty tribe which, under some hero or some great provocation, takes
the lead and gives a new name to a kingdom. Otherwise there is no idea of
nationality, and things remain much the same.

The Manchus, when they had conquered China and Mongolia, decided that the Solon of
the Amur were the descendants of the ancient Khitan — which is to say that the
imperial dynasty of the seventeenth century looked at the imperial dynasty of the
tenth and identified its survivors among the hunting bands of the far north.

## What they were when Abaoji found them

Eight tribes, federated, electing a leader for a fixed term.

That is the condition out of which every steppe and forest empire in this history
begins, and the thing that has to be broken before anything larger can be built.
The Khitan had been in it, on and off, for five centuries — long enough to have a
tributary relationship with four Chinese dynasties, a settled quarrel with their
Xi cousins, and no state at all.

What changed was one man deciding not to stand down at the end of his term.
