---
title: Calling tribute a gift
drafted_from: franke-twitchett-1994
phase: aside
---

The Treaty of Chanyuan in 1005 ended a war between the Liao and the Song with an
agreement that the Song would pay the Liao an annual sum in silver and silk. The
Song called it a gift. The Liao called it tribute. Both recorded the arrangement
in their own terms and neither corrected the other, and it held for a hundred and
twenty years.

The Chinese diplomatic tradition had a single template — the tribute system, in
which foreign rulers acknowledged the emperor's superiority and received gifts in
return — and Chanyuan does not fit it. The two courts addressed each other as
formal equals, with a fictive kinship between the emperors, negotiated borders,
and regular embassies. It is the first treaty of its kind in Chinese history and
it was made because the Song could not win the war and the payments cost
substantially less than continuing it.

That calculation is worth stating plainly, because the Song are conventionally
remembered as weak for making it. The annual payment was a small fraction of the
Song state budget and a large fraction of Liao revenue, the frontier stayed
quiet, and both economies profited from the border markets it opened.

The arrangement's real cost was strategic and arrived a century later, when the
Song allied with the Jurchen to destroy the Liao — as this site's Jin page
describes — and discovered they had removed a neighbour that took payment in
exchange for one that took Kaifeng.
