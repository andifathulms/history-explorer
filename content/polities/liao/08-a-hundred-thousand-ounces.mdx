In 1042 the Liao emperor massed troops in the Peking plain, demanded territory
from the Song, and was talked out of it by an envoy who explained to him that his
own ministers wanted a war and he did not.

## The demand

Xingzong — Parker gives his Khitan household name as Mukpuku — saw an opportunity
while the Song were occupied with the Tanguts of Xia, and wrote to his *elder
brother* the emperor of China.

The letter reopened the question of the ten districts the Later Zhou had recovered,
complained about the Song attempt on the Peking plain in 986, complained that two
of the districts were still in Chinese hands, and complained about a frontier
governor making strategic use of a river near Bazhou.

The Chinese saw through it at once. Parker's judgement is blunt: the Khitan envoys,
while ostensibly claiming territory, were not skilful enough to disguise the fact
that larger annual subsidies were really what they wanted.

## The argument

The Song envoy Fu Bi found the emperor at his *nabo* — his seasonal hunting camp —
living in a travelling tent, and made an argument in three moves.

First, gratitude: after the previous war the Chinese emperor, against his generals'
advice, had let the Khitan army withdraw unmolested.

Second, arithmetic. When Cathay had taken a Chinese capital in the tenth century,
what had it got? The monarch who did it lost more than half his best troops and
horses and died himself; the only gain was that the survivors filled their pockets
and stuffed their houses with plunder. China has since recovered its territory and
has millions of seasoned troops, so victory is uncertain — *whereas further loss of
Cathayan life is a matter of complete certainty*.

Third, and this is the one that landed: whose war is it?

> In the event of peace being preserved, not only will the monarch not lose his
> soldiers' lives, but all the subsidy goes to himself alone, whilst his ministers
> get nothing but what they can make out of being occasional envoys. Thus it is
> plainly the interest of the counsellors to have war and plunder, the ruler losing
> his men and his subsidy.

A Chinese diplomat, in a tent on the steppe, explaining to a Khitan emperor that
the men advising him to fight are the men who do not receive the tribute.

## The offer

Then the settlement, and it is the most candid sentence in the whole exchange.

The emperor's position, Fu Bi said, was that the desire for the land was at bottom a
desire only for the profits on the land, and that he would not spend lives on a
question of profits. He therefore increased the subsidy *by the amount of taxation
leviable on the disputed territory*.

Buy the revenue and keep the ground. If Cathay insisted on the land itself, that was
evidently a pretext for overturning the treaty of 1005, and in that case his
majesty could not refuse war.

## The counter-offer

Xingzong was visibly impressed and proposed a marriage alliance instead.

And here Fu Bi, seeing his advantage, said the thing that shows exactly what kind of
relationship this had become:

> Marriage connections easily give rise to disputes, and are not so durable as
> subsidies. A hundred thousand ounces of silver is the dowry given with a
> first-class princess, which is immeasurably below the value of an annual subsidy.

Do not take a wife. Take the money; there is more of it.

The emperor said: go back, and when you return I will tell you which I will have —
*but bring your full powers with you*.

## What the negotiation shows

That by the eleventh century the Song and the Liao had a relationship neither of
them could describe honestly and both of them understood perfectly.

To the Song court the payments were gifts to a younger brother, entered in the
records in language that preserved the fiction. To the Liao they were tribute from
a tributary. In practice they were a standing purchase of peace, priced against the
tax yield of the disputed districts, negotiated by men who compared them openly with
the cost of a princess's dowry.

It worked. The frontier held for a hundred and twenty years, the longest peace
between a Chinese dynasty and a northern power in the whole of this history, and
both states grew rich in it. What it could not survive was the arrival of somebody
who had not been paid — which is what happened when the Jurchen of the forest, whom
the Liao had been taxing in falcons and pearls, decided they would rather have the
empire.
