The Liao raised its armies from tribal levies, which is true of every steppe
state and explains nothing. What was unusual is what happened to them
afterwards.

The Liao emperors each raised an *ordo*: a standing household guard, drawn from
tribal levies and from captured populations, with its own herds, its own
territory and its own revenue. It went to war with him, and when he died it did
not pass to his successor. It became the permanent establishment of his tomb.

So each emperor built an army from scratch, and the dead accumulated armies.

## What that is for

It looks like waste and it is a solution to a specific problem. A steppe ruler
whose guard passes intact to his heir has handed the heir's rivals a target and
handed the guard commanders a vote in the succession — which is exactly what
happened to the Yuan, whose guard commanders murdered an emperor at the summer
camp in 1323 and did the same sort of thing repeatedly for thirty years.

An ordo that dissolves into a tomb establishment cannot be inherited, cannot be
bargained with, and cannot make an emperor. The cost is that every reign begins
with the ruler recruiting.

The succession itself was never fixed. There is a Chinese-style heir apparent in
the arrangements and a steppe assembly behind it, and the empress dowagers of the
Xiao consort clan — which supplied every Liao empress and much of the officer
corps — settled several transitions by force. The ordo did not solve that. It
made sure the army was not the thing that decided it.

## And two revenue systems

The Song payments are the famous half of the Liao's income and they were never
the larger half.

The Southern Administration taxed a settled Chinese and Bohai population on
agricultural land, in the ordinary way, and that population was the majority of
the empire's people. An empire whose whole subject is governing two things at
once had two treasuries as well as two governments, and the tribute was the one
that got written about because it was the one the Song had to explain to
itself.
