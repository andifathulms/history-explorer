In 938 the Liao acquired sixteen prefectures of settled Chinese territory
south of the Great Wall, including the site of what is now Beijing, and they
were not taken by conquest. They were paid over as a fee.

## The transaction

North China in the 930s was in the middle of the Five Dynasties — a succession
of short-lived military regimes in the old imperial heartland, each one
overthrown by a general of the last.

Shi Jingtang was one such general, in revolt against the dynasty he served, and
losing. He asked the Liao emperor for an army. The price was the sixteen
prefectures, an annual payment of three hundred thousand bolts of silk, and Shi
Jingtang's formal acknowledgement of the Liao emperor as his father — he was
about ten years older than the man he was calling father, which contemporaries
noticed.

He got the army, won, and founded the Later Jin dynasty as a Liao client.

## Why it mattered for four hundred years

The sixteen prefectures are the mountain line. The passes through the ranges
north of the plain are the reason a Chinese state can hold the north China
plain against cavalry at all, and after 938 they were on the wrong side.

Every subsequent Chinese dynasty tried to get them back. The Song attempted it
twice, in 979 and 986, and was beaten badly both times; the second failure led
directly to a defensive posture that shaped Song military policy for the rest of
the dynasty and to the treaty of 1005 that made the Song a tribute-payer. The
territory did not return to a Chinese-ruled state until the Ming took it in
1368, four hundred and thirty years later.

## What the Liao did with them

This is the part that makes the dynasty unusual. They did not try to make the
prefectures Khitan, and they did not try to make the Khitan Chinese.

They ran two governments. The Northern Administration governed the tribal
population by Khitan custom, with Khitan officials and the ordo system of
imperial camps. The Southern Administration governed the settled Chinese
population by Tang institutions — prefectures, counties, a civil bureaucracy,
examinations — with largely Chinese officials, in Chinese, on Chinese law.

The emperor was head of both and the two systems did not merge. That
arrangement is the subject of a chapter of its own, and it is the single most
important administrative invention on the northern frontier: the Jin copied it,
and the Yuan and the Qing both governed their composite empires on some version
of the same principle.

## What being the senior power looked like

By the eleventh century the Liao held the steppe frontier from Manchuria to the
Altai, took tribute from Korea and from the Tanguts, received Song silver and
silk annually under a treaty its own armies had extracted, and had a court that
foreign envoys attended.

They were also, by then, the state that everyone west of them meant by China.
The word travelled: *Khitan* becomes *Kitai* in Russian, and *Cathay* in Latin
Europe, and it stayed the western name for the country for six hundred years
after the dynasty that carried it had ceased to exist.
