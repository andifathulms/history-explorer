The Khitan were a confederation of eight tribes on the Liao river, and their
paramount chief was elected for a three-year term.

Abaoji was elected in 907. In 916 he was still in office, had killed most of
the people who objected, had taken the title of emperor with a Chinese-style
reign name, and had made the office hereditary in his family.

That transition — from a rotating tribal chieftaincy to a dynastic throne — is
the founding act, and it is the one every steppe state has to perform and most
of them fail at.

## How he did it

By not letting the term expire, which produced the obvious result: his brothers
revolted, three times, between 911 and 913. He put down all three and, according
to the histories, executed several hundred of the participants.

Then he dealt with the other seven tribal chiefs. The account that survives says
he invited them to a feast at a salt lake — pointing out that they had all been
using his salt and had never thanked him for it — and killed them there.

Whether that happened as told is the usual question with a founding massacre.
What is not in doubt is that after about 915 there were no other chiefs with a
claim, and that the Khitan state thereafter had a single hereditary ruling house.

## What he built it out of

Not a steppe empire on the old model. Abaoji's Khitan sat on the boundary
between the Mongolian grasslands and the settled farming country of what is now
Manchuria and northern Hebei, and he took from both.

He captured Chinese populations in raids and settled them in walled towns of
their own — the histories describe towns built to replicate the ones the
captives had come from, so that they would go on farming and paying taxes.
Chinese advisers were used from the beginning; one of them, Han Yanhui, is
credited with organising the agricultural settlements and much of the
administrative structure.

He also commissioned a script. The Khitan large script was created in 920 on
the model of Chinese characters, and a smaller script derived partly from
Uyghur followed a few years later. Both were used, both are only partly
deciphered, and the decision to have one at all is the point: a ruler who
orders a writing system made for his own language is not planning to be a
confederation.

## 926

The last thing he did was take Bohai.

Bohai was a settled kingdom in what is now eastern Manchuria and northern
Korea, three centuries old, literate, organised on Tang lines with five
capitals and a full bureaucracy. Abaoji destroyed it in about three months in
926, renamed it Dongdan — the Eastern Khitan state — and installed his eldest
son as its king.

He died on the way home from that campaign.

What his successors inherited was therefore not a tribal following but a
composite: a steppe confederation, a settled agricultural population in walled
towns, and an entire conquered kingdom with a functioning Chinese-style
administration already in it.

The problem of how to govern all three at once is what the next century of Liao
history is about, and the answer they arrived at has no real parallel anywhere
else.
