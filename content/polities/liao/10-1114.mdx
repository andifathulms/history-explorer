The Jurchen were a subject people of the north-eastern forests, organised in
tribes, paying the Liao in furs, horses, pearls and gyrfalcons. In 1114 they
revolted. Eleven years later the Liao empire did not exist.

What makes the sequence worth a chapter is that it then happened again, twice,
on schedule.

- 1114: the Jurchen, a forest people the Liao extracted tribute from, destroy the
  Liao in eleven years.
- 1211: the Mongols, a steppe people the Jin extracted from and manipulated,
  destroy the Jin in twenty-three.
- 1616: a Jurchen leader in the same forests declares a state, names it after
  this second dynasty, and his son's dynasty takes China in 1644.

Three times in five centuries, a conquest dynasty holding north China was ended
by a population on its own frontier that it had been managing as a resource.

## Why the same shape

The Liao and the Jin were both governing two things at once: a settled Chinese population under a Chinese
administration, and a steppe or forest constituency under its own. The
arrangement worked, and it required that the outer constituency stay outer —
tributary, divided, and supplying men and goods.

Managing a frontier people takes attention, and the machinery that does it is
also the machinery that teaches them how the empire works. The Jurchen who
revolted in 1114 had been trading with, campaigning for and being administered by
the Liao for two centuries.

Calling it a conquest is true and it misses the mechanism. The conqueror was a
component of the thing conquered — a people the empire had built into its own
administrative structure as a supplier, who had spent two hundred years learning
how the machine worked from inside it.

## How fast it went

The speed is the part that needs stating, because it is barely credible.

In 1114 Aguda, chief of a Jurchen tribe that had been supplying the Liao court with
falcons and river pearls, refused to perform the dance at the emperor's fish feast
and went to war. He had, on the Jurchen account, a few thousand men.

In 1115 he declared himself emperor of a dynasty he called Jin, gold — chosen,
the story goes, because iron rusts and gold does not, and *Liao* was said to mean
iron.

By 1120 the Song had agreed to attack the Liao from the south in concert with him.
By 1122 the Liao capitals were falling. In 1125 the last emperor was captured in the
west, and the dynasty that had taken tribute from China for a hundred and twenty
years had ceased to exist.

Eleven years, from a refusal to dance to the end of an empire.

## Why the Song joined in

Because they wanted the sixteen prefectures back, and this looked like the moment.

The alliance of 1120 is one of the worst decisions in Chinese diplomatic history and
it was taken for an entirely intelligible reason: the territory had been lost in 938,
every Song emperor had been expected to want it back, and here was a chance to get
it without facing the Liao army alone.

What the Song got was the destruction of the buffer state that had been keeping a
forest people out of north China, and a demonstration — conducted in front of the
Jurchen — that its own armies could not take walled cities the Jurchen took in
weeks. Within two years of the Liao collapse the Jin turned south, and in 1127 they
took the Song capital and carried the emperor, the retired emperor and three
thousand of the court into captivity in Manchuria.

The Liao had cost the Song two hundred thousand ounces of silver and two hundred
thousand bolts of silk a year. The peace that replaced it cost them the north of
China.
