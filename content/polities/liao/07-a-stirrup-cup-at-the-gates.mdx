Chanyuan settled the war. What Shengzong did with the quarter-century of
peace it bought him is a fuller measure of the reign than the treaty alone.

## What normal relations actually looked like

Markets opened at three border points within months of the treaty, customs
houses attached to them, and the elaborate traffic of embassies that followed
give a closer view of the Liao court than any battle account could. A
Chinese envoy sent to mark the ruling queen mother's birthday found himself
received at the summer residence, feted, loaded with presents, and seen out
through the gates by the khan himself, who shared a stirrup-cup with the
party before they rode home - a level of personal warmth no earlier
Song-Khitan exchange had managed. Another envoy left a sharper account of
what he saw underneath the hospitality: the khan dressed formally in hat and
robes to receive the Chinese mission and then changed at once into riding
clothes to go hunting with his own officers, "mixing promiscuously with his
cavaliers." Asked about Khitan law, the same envoy was told plainly that the
khan's own people "were like wild animals, and not to be ruled by civil laws
in Chinese fashion" - and the punishments he describes bear it out, offenders
condemned to death "always hacked to death in a fearful manner."

## The regency that made it work

Real authority through most of this period rested with the queen mother and
her own chief minister, whose rule over Liao's government was, in the
envoys' account, nearly absolute - shrewd enough that the Khitan nobility
had come to genuinely esteem a Chinese-born administrator running their own
court. It was a partnership that lasted until 1010, when the queen mother
died and her minister followed her within a short time. Shengzong, ruling
without the two people who had done most of the actual governing for him,
is recorded declining sharply afterward - the same court that had impressed
Song envoys with its dignity a few years earlier now judged, by the same
kind of report, incompetent and unpopular.

## A war that went badly

The clearest sign of that decline came on Liao's eastern frontier rather
than its southern one. Korea had built its own defensive citadels against
Khitan pressure, and around 1010 Liao went to war to take them - calling on
tributary peoples for troops and provisions in a recruitment system so
extractive, by the source's own account, that a conscripted soldier's eventual
share of any plunder rarely covered what the campaign had cost him to equip
himself for. The Jurchen, who had already begun to chafe under Khitan
exactions, sided with Korea rather than supply the men Liao asked for, and
the campaign that followed was a rout: Liao lost nearly its entire army and
half its own nobility in a single Korean counterattack, and had to rebuild
its forces afterward from conscripts raised on the Peking plain itself.

## What the reign actually adds up to

Set beside each other, the two halves of Shengzong's post-Chanyuan years make
an uncomfortable pair. The peace with Song, backed by his mother's own steady
government, produced the most genuinely normal diplomatic relationship any
Chinese court had yet had with a steppe power - envoys who came home with
real ethnographic detail rather than propaganda, and a frontier that stayed
quiet for decades. The same reign, once that steadying hand was gone, could
not manage a war against a smaller neighbour on its own eastern border. Both
facts belong to the same forty-nine years, and neither one by itself is the
whole of what made Shengzong the reign later observers would call Liao's own
peak.
