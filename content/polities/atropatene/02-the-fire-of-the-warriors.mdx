Zoroastrianism recognised three great fires, each associated with an estate of
society and each burning at a named place: Adur Farnbag for the priests, in
Fars; Adur Burzen-Mihr for the farmers, in Parthia; and Adur Gushnasp for the
warriors and the kings, in Atropatene.

The site is Takht-e Soleyman — Solomon's throne — a volcanic crater in the
mountains of what is now Iranian Azerbaijan, with a deep lake of mineral water
in the middle of it that never freezes and never empties. The fire temple stood
beside the lake, inside a walled oval, and the Sasanian kings of kings made a
pilgrimage to it after their coronation. The tradition is that they walked the
last stage on foot.

<Figure id="takht-e-soleyman" />

That is the largest thing in this small kingdom, and it is worth noticing what
it does to the record's shape. Atropatene is politically marginal for five
centuries — a client kingdom appearing in other people's narratives when
somebody invades it — and it held the royal fire of the Iranian world for the
whole of that period and afterwards, through the Sasanian empire, until a
Byzantine army sacked the sanctuary in 624 in retaliation for the Persian
capture of Jerusalem.

Note what kind of importance that is. Atropatene sent nothing out. It did not
propagate Zoroastrianism, did not found a school, did not conquer a neighbour
for the faith. It held a place, and the place was where other men's kingship was
made legitimate. A king of kings ruling from Ctesiphon, five hundred miles away,
had to come to this crater in the mountains and walk up to the fire in order to
be a king of kings properly.

That is the kingdom's largest export and it never left the country. The other
one is a word.
