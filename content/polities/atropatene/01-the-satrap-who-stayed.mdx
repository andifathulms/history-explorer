Atropates was the Achaemenid satrap of Media. He commanded the Median
contingent against Alexander at Gaugamela, and afterwards did what a good many
Persian governors did: he submitted, and was confirmed in his own province.

When Alexander died in 323 the settlement at Babylon divided the satrapies among
the officers, and Media was split. The southern part went to a Macedonian. The
northern part — Media Minor, the mountain country from the Araxes down to the
Zagros — stayed with Atropates, and he never handed it over.

His descendants ruled it for five hundred and fifty years.

<Figure id="at-drachm" />

That is a rarer thing than it sounds. Dynasties all over Iran would spend the
next thousand years claiming descent from the Achaemenids — the Parthians did,
the Sasanians did, the Shirvanshahs claimed the Sasanians, and each of those is
an assertion of connection to something that had ended. Atropates' house was not
claiming succession from the Achaemenid empire. It was a piece of the
administration of that empire which had simply never been handed over, running
the same mountains under the same family while the claimants argued.

The kingdom was a client of whoever was strongest — Seleucid, then Parthian,
with periods of effective independence and one Roman alliance — and the strategy
is the one every durable mountain principality has used: submit early, pay, hold
country that is expensive to take, and outlast the people who take it.
Atropatene did it for longer than almost anyone.
