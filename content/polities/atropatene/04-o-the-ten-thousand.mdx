The retreat from Phraata is the part of Antony's Median campaign that everyone
who writes about it reaches for, and Plutarch earned that by noticing what
happened to individual men rather than to the army.

## What they were eating

The baggage animals were dead or carrying the wounded, so almost everything had
been left behind. Plutarch gives the prices: an Attic quart of wheat sold for
fifty drachmas, and barley loaves went for their weight in silver.

So they ate roots and vegetables, and the familiar ones ran out, and they began
trying whatever they could find.

One of the plants they found was fatal, and Plutarch describes what it did before
it killed:

> He that had eaten of it remembered nothing in the world, and employed himself
> only in moving great stones from one place to another, which he did with as much
> earnestness and industry as if it had been a business of the greatest
> consequence.

Through the whole camp, he says, there was nothing to be seen but men grubbing at
stones and carrying them from place to place. Then they threw up bile and died.
The one antidote was wine, and the wine had run out.

An army in retreat, under daily attack, with its surviving strength spent
watching its comrades solemnly rearrange rocks until they died of it.

## What Antony said

Plutarch reports that as the men died and the Parthians kept up the pursuit,
Antony was heard to exclaim, several times over: *O, the Ten Thousand!*

He meant Xenophon's Greeks, who had marched out of a deeper Asia against a
stronger enemy and got home. It is a general comparing himself unfavourably with
a four-hundred-year-old book in front of his own staff, and it is the most
revealing thing he says in the whole campaign — he is not thinking about how to
save the army, he is thinking about how this will read.

## What Atropatene got out of it

The kingdom itself barely appears in the Roman account, which is characteristic
of how it survives in the record at all.

What it had done was straightforward and decisive. Antony had left his siege
train behind with a small guard to move fast on the capital; an Atropatenian and
Parthian force destroyed it; and without engines he could not take a walled city,
so he sat outside Phraata into the autumn and then had to leave. A mountain
kingdom beat the largest army Rome ever sent east by killing its wagons.

And then Atropatene did the other thing it was good at. Its king quarrelled with
the Parthians over the division of the spoil, and went over to Antony — and later
to Rome — as an ally. The state's method was never mass or wealth. It was
position: high, defensible, between two empires that both needed it and neither
could easily hold, and willing to change sides when the price was right.

Rome sent four invasions east in this period and moved the frontier not at all.
This was the one that came closest, and it failed in a valley in Media because a
baggage train was left insufficiently guarded.
