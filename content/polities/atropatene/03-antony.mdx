In 36 BC Mark Antony invaded Iran with something like a hundred thousand men,
the largest force Rome ever sent east. The objective was to avenge Carrhae,
where a Roman army and its commander had been destroyed seventeen years before;
avenging it had become a standing political obligation at Rome, and it would
eventually produce four invasions and no change in the frontier.

He came through Armenia into Atropatene and made a decision that cost him the
campaign: he left his siege train behind with a small guard in order to move
fast on the capital. A Parthian and Atropatenian force destroyed it. Without
siege engines he could not take a walled city, and he sat outside Phraaspa into
the autumn achieving nothing.

The retreat through the mountains in early winter is described by Plutarch and
is one of the set pieces of Roman military disaster writing: continuous
harassment, hunger, dysentery from bad water, and something between a fifth and
a third of the army lost. Antony got out. He did not come back.

<Figure id="at-antony" />

Two things follow, and the second is why a small kingdom has a turning point for
it.

Rome did not attempt the conquest of Iran again from the west for a hundred and
fifty years, until Trajan, who took Ctesiphon, declared a province, found
revolts everywhere and abandoned the whole thing inside two years. The Euphrates
frontier that Carrhae had drawn was confirmed by Antony's failure and held, in
one form or another, for six centuries.

And Atropatene, having helped defeat the invasion, promptly quarrelled with
Parthia over the spoils and allied with Antony instead — which is the whole
strategy of a small kingdom between large ones, executed within a year of the
event, and is why the dynasty was still there two hundred and fifty years later.
