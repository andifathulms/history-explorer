The kingdom ended in 226 and the fire did not.

Adur Gushnasp burned on beside its lake for another four centuries under the
Sasanians, and the obligation attached to it hardened into law. Ibn Khurdadbih,
writing in Arabic in the ninth century, records the rule as the Persians had
kept it: each Chosroes, immediately after his accession to the crown, was bound
to come here as a pilgrim, walking on foot the whole way from Ctesiphon, with a
halt at Shahrazur at the half-way stage. Persian tradition is more modest and
has the king walking only the last stage; the Arabic geographers have him
walking the lot. Either way the point of the ritual is the same, and so is the
direction of travel — the king of kings came to the fire.

The Arabs called the place Shiz. The Persians, Yaqut says, called it Jis or
Gazn, of which Shiz was the Arabic corruption.

## A prospector's report, AD 943

The best description of it was written by a man who had gone there for the
wrong reason.

Ibn Muhalhal visited in 331 of the Hijra — 943 — looking for gold mines said to
exist in the mountains around it, and wrote up what he saw. The town walls
enclosed a lake about a third of an acre across which was unfathomable, and
whose level never changed although seven streams flowed continuously out of it;
the water of those streams, he reports, turned objects left in them to stone.

And the fire temple was still working. The sacred fire kept there, he says, was
the one from which fire was taken to all the other temples throughout Persia,
and for seven hundred years it had never been allowed to go out.

That claim needs holding at arm's length. A Byzantine army sacked this
sanctuary in 624, and a fire that is extinguished can be rekindled but the
seven hundred years cannot then be counted through the gap. What Ibn Muhalhal
is reporting is what the custodians told a visitor in the tenth century, three
hundred years into Islamic rule: that this fire was the source of all the
others, and that it had never gone out. It is the same claim the kingdom had
been living beside since before Christ, still being made, to an Arab looking
for gold.

## The name goes, the buildings stay

After that it drifts.

Mustawfi, writing in the fourteenth century, gives Shiz as the chief town of
its district and says the Mongols called it Saturik. He describes a great
palace there, which report said had been built by King Kay-Khusraw — a hero of
Persian legend, not a Sasanian — with the bottomless pool in its courtyard,
which kept its level however much ran out of it and did not overflow when the
outflow was dammed. He adds that Abaqa Khan the Mongol had built himself a
palace on the spot, because the pasture in the neighbourhood was excellent.

So: a crater where kings of Iran walked up to be crowned, converted into a
summer residence for a grandson of Genghis Khan on the grounds that the grazing
was good.

The last stage is the name. Nobody now called it the fire of the warriors, and
nobody remembered whose palace the ruins had been, so they were assigned to the
one builder that unexplained ancient stonework was always assigned to in this
part of the world. They are called Takht-i-Sulayman — Solomon's Throne — and
that is still their name.

Two things outlasted the kingdom of Atropates, and both of them lost their
attribution on the way. A region kept his name and forgot he existed. A
sanctuary kept its lake and its walls and was given to Solomon.
