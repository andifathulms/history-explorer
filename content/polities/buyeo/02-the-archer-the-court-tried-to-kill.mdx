The egg-born boy took the name Chu-mong - "Skillful Archer" - for a
talent with the bow that became apparent almost as soon as he could
hold one. Geumwa protected him and made him keeper of the royal
stables, but the boy was never popular with the rest of the court, and
his own seven half-brothers watched his growing skill with open
resentment rather than pride in a sibling.

<Figure id="by-mural" />

## A stable-keeper who outwitted his own rivals

Chu-mong turned his humble post to quiet advantage: he starved the
kingdom's best horses just enough to make them lean and unremarkable
while fattening the poorer ones for show, keeping the finest mounts
effectively invisible until a hunt began, when he alone could outrun
every other rider in the field. His brothers, already jealous of his
archery, now had a fresh grievance, and between them they resolved
that the foundling's foundling had to die.

## A mother's warning and a river that answered a prayer

Warned in the night by his own mother, Chu-mong fled south before dawn
with three loyal companions, only to find his path blocked by a river
with no boat, bridge or ford in sight and his brothers' pursuing
cavalry already audible behind him. Striking the water with his bow
and calling on the river's own spirit for aid, he watched - so the
tradition insists - as a shoal of fish and tortoises rose from the
depths and pressed their backs together into a living bridge.

## A kingdom his own flight would come back to haunt

Crossing safely while his brothers were left stranded on the far bank,
Chu-mong continued south, gathered new followers along the way, and
founded a kingdom of his own at Chŭl-bon - naming it Goguryeo, after
his own family name and a mountain from his native Buyeo. The court
he had fled had lost, in that single night, the one member of its own
royal household who would go on to found the kingdom that would
eventually return and conquer it outright.
