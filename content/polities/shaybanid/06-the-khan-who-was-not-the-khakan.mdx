For four decades after Ghijduvan the Shaybanid state was never quite
what its own succession implied. The man who actually ran it was
usually not the one holding the senior title, and the appanage system
that had let the family conquer Transoxiana together made sure no one
of them could ever rule it alone for long.

## An empire with two kinds of khan

The Uzbek princes settled Ghijduvan's spoils the way they settled
everything: by appanage. Kuchkunji, the senior surviving line, was
elected khakan - the over-khan every appanaged prince nominally
answered to - and held Samarkand. Ubaydullah, Sheibani's own nephew and
the general whose victory had actually won the battle, received Bukhara
and Karshi. For the next twenty years it was Ubaydullah, not Kuchkunji,
who did the fighting that mattered: crossing the Oxus in 1513 and again
in 1525 to raid Khurasan, taking Merv, massacring the garrison at Tus
after a broken capitulation, and briefly holding Meshed itself against
a Safavid empire that kept recovering faster than the Uzbeks could
consolidate what they'd taken.

## Bukhara's own khan

When Kuchkunji's son and successor Abusaid died after a short reign in
1533 - possibly poisoned, Vambery suspected, by the man who benefited
most from it - the khakanship passed for the first time to Ubaydullah
himself. He ran it from Bukhara, which for the length of his reign
became, by Mirza Haidar's account, what Herat had been under Sultan
Husayn: a court of poets, musicians and calligraphers around a khan who
copied out the Quran with his own hand and still crossed the Oxus
almost every year he could manage it.

The wars did not soften. In 1532 Ubaydullah besieged Herat for a year
and a half, so thoroughly that the starving garrison was reduced to
eating dogs and cats before Shah Tahmasp forced him off by force of
arms. In 1536 he took the city outright after a five-month siege,
stripped the surrendering garrison naked and marched them to Bukhara,
most of them dying on the road. He conquered Khwarazm the same decade,
executed its captured khan in revenge for an old killing, and installed
his own son at Urgench - only to lose it within a year to a rival who
ambushed his relief column at night and routed it. Ubaydullah died in
1540, worn down, Persian chroniclers said, by chagrin rather than any
single defeat.

## The khanship nobody could hold

What followed him was the appanage system running exactly as it was
built to: fast, orderly, and incapable of producing a strong ruler two
reigns running. Kuchkunji's other son Abdulla held the khakanship for
six months and is remembered for nothing else. His brother Abdul Latif
held it for a decade, kept the peace with Persia mostly by being too
occupied with his own cousins to cross the river, and died in 1552.
Nauruz Ahmed of Tashkent succeeded him - and it was during his own
khakanship that a minor appanage-holder at Kermineh, a grandson of the
old regent Janibeg named Iskandar, first appears fighting for scraps of
territory against his own kinsmen. Iskandar never held the khakanship
himself. His son would, and would be the one Shaybanid who finally
worked out how to stop the appanages from eating every reign that
followed a strong one.
