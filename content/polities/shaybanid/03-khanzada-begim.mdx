The Shaybanid conquest of Transoxiana cost the Timurids their cities, and Babur
records what it cost him personally in four sentences, in the middle of a list of
his sisters.

## The transaction

He had taken Samarkand in 1500 despite being beaten at Sar-i Pul, and held it
through a five-month siege. No beg or ruler anywhere near sent any help or
reinforcement at all. In the end he left it in despair and got away.

> In that throneless time Khan-zada Begim fell to Muhammad Shaibani Khan.

That is all he says about the mechanism, and the phrase — *that throneless time*,
*fatrat* — is the one he uses throughout for the years when he had no country. She
was his full sister, five years older than he was, the eldest daughter of Umar
Shaikh Mirza. She was handed to the conqueror as part of the terms on which he was
allowed to leave his own capital.

Babur was eighteen.

## The ten years

She had a child by Shaybani — *a pleasant boy*, Babur calls him, named Khurram
Shah, who was given the Balkh country and died a few years after his father.

In 1510 Shah Isma'il of Persia destroyed the Uzbek army near Merv and killed
Shaybani Khan. Khanzada Begim was in Merv when it happened.

> For my sake he treated her well, giving her a sufficient escort to Qunduz where
> she rejoined me.

The shah who had made a drinking cup of her husband's skull sent her back to her
brother, with an escort, as a courtesy.

## The recognition

Then comes the passage, and it should be read slowly.

> We had been apart for some ten years; when Muhammadi kükildash and I went to see
> her, neither she nor those about her knew us, although I spoke. They recognized us
> after a time.

Two people who had been children together, meeting in a Central Asian fortress
after ten years — one of them a queen of the house that had destroyed her family,
the other a landless prince who had lost three kingdoms — and neither the woman nor
her household could tell who he was until he had been standing there for a while.

*Although I spoke.* He expected his own voice to be enough, and it was not.

## Why it is worth having

Because it is the only thing in any of these sources that gives the conquest a
price in the currency people actually pay in, and because of who wrote it.

Babur is not making a case. The sentence sits in a genealogical list between his
half-sisters' names and their mothers' names, and he goes on immediately to Mihr-banu
Begim, two years younger than himself. He is a man who lost everything to the
Shaybanids, wrote a long book about it, and put the single worst thing that happened
to him in a family register without raising his voice.

She outlived all of it, and Babur — who records in the same list that another sister
was eight years younger than he was, and that two more were born after their
father's death — never says another word about what those ten years had been like.
The memoir is famous for its frankness. This is the one place where it simply
stops.
