When Abdullah Khan II was raised to the khanship in 1583, the ceremony was
performed on a piece of white felt that had been dipped at Mecca in the water of
the well of Zamzam.

That object is the whole Shaybanid settlement in one sentence.

## The ceremony

His father Iskandar died while he was away at Khujand, and the head of the clergy
and the other notables buried the khan and then rode out to find him, meeting him
on the road. He summoned them to a council — Howorth calls it by its Mongol name,
the *quriltai* — to consider the succession, and the chief cleric did not take long
to proclaim him the fittest man for the post of khaqan.

Then they raised him on the felt.

Raising a new khan on a felt carpet is a steppe rite older than Chinggis; it is how
the Türk khagans were enthroned and it is what the word for enthronement meant on
that grass. Zamzam water is the water of the Hajj, brought back by pilgrims from
the well beside the Ka'ba.

A Muslim clergy, in a Mongol assembly, raising a descendant of Jochi on a Turkic
felt sanctified in Arabia. Nobody in the room thought any of it incongruous, and
that is the point about this dynasty: it was not a Mongol regime that became Muslim,
nor a Muslim regime with Mongol trappings, but a working synthesis in which both
sets of forms were operative at once.

## What he had already done

Howorth's assessment is unequivocal — Abdullah was the greatest of the Abu'l-Khayrid
line — and his reason for saying so is structural rather than military.

Under cover of his father's shield he had crushed or displaced every rival from the
Kazakh deserts to the frontiers of Kabul, so that *the Uzbegs were for the first
time subject to a single strong grasp, instead of being broken in pieces among a
number of appanages*.

That sentence describes the correction of the defect that had wrecked every
Chinggisid state in this history. Muhammad Shaybani had conquered Transoxiana and
then divided it among his kinsmen as appanages, exactly as Chinggis, Dayan Khan and
every other steppe founder had done; and the khanate had spent eighty years being
a federation of cousins who fought each other. Abdullah spent his father's lifetime
removing them one at a time before taking the title himself.

*Thus the apple which had been so long in ripening fell on his knees.*

## Mashhad

With a unified khanate he could do what no Uzbek ruler had managed since 1512:
attack Khurasan seriously. Persia was between strong shahs; the Uzbeks raided across
the frontier and withdrew when the Persian army appeared. In 1585, with Shah Abbas
occupied against the Ottomans, Herat fell after a nine-month siege, its governor and
several chiefs were executed, the city was plundered, a large number of prisoners
were carried off to Bukhara, and north-eastern Khurasan was laid waste.

And then something happened that is more revealing than the campaign.

The guardians of the shrine of the Imam Reza at Mashhad — who administered the
enormous endowment of fields, gardens and benefices attached to it — wrote to
Abdullah Khan, and asked him a question. How did he reconcile with his religious
feelings the destruction of the Imam's property, and the waste of the income by
which so many thousand pious pilgrims were supported, *including many Sunnis*?

## The reply

The mullahs in Abdullah's camp answered with a treatise.

By their principles and their faith, they wrote, the followers of the Shia were
worse than unbelievers, whose destruction God himself had ordained. If it was every
Muslim's duty to make war on unbelievers, how much more on those who had wandered
from the right path and had fallen into grievous sin in spite of living around the
bones of the saint.

As for the shrine's fields and gardens, they knew perfectly well that these were
devoted to pious uses. But it was an open question, they said, who had the better
right to the enjoyment of them: the warriors fighting for the cause of God and right
and deprived of all means of subsistence, or those who had sinned against God and
treated the guardians of the faith with contempt.

The Shia scholars, Howorth notes, had a rejoinder ready, and proposed a council of
equal numbers from both sides to determine whether the Shia were in fact traitors to
the faith.

## What the correspondence settles

It is easy to read the Uzbek-Safavid wars as dynastic quarrels dressed in religion.
This exchange shows the doctrine doing real work, in writing, at the moment of
application.

A formal legal opinion that the property of Shia Muslims is lawful plunder for
Sunni soldiers is the same opinion that makes Shia Muslims lawfully enslavable, and
it is the reason the slave markets of Bukhara and Khiva were stocked with Iranians
for the next three hundred years. The argument was not invented for the occasion by
cynics. It was argued out with the other side, by name, in letters, and both parties
were prepared to submit it to a joint commission.

That is what an ideological frontier looks like from the inside while it is being
built.
