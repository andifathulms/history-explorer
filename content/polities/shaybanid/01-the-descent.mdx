title: The qualification Timur lacked

This site's Timurid page opens on a technicality: Timur was not a descendant of
Chinggis Khan, could therefore never legitimately be khan, and spent
thirty-five years ruling as *amir* beside a puppet Chinggisid he kept at court.
The page argues that the constraint explains the career — a man who cannot
command by right has to be followed for some other reason, and the reasons
available get used up.

The Shaybanids are the people who had the right.

Shiban was the fifth son of Jochi, Chinggis Khan's eldest, and his descendants
held territory in the eastern half of the Jochid ulus — the steppe north and
east of the Aral, in the part of the Golden Horde's world that fragmented
earliest. In the fifteenth century one of them, Abu'l-Khayr Khan, assembled a
tribal confederation out of the Turkic and Turco-Mongol groups of that steppe.
They were called Uzbeks, after a fourteenth-century khan of the Horde, and the
name has since attached to a country, a language and a nation none of which
existed then.

Abu'l-Khayr's confederation broke up after a defeat by the Oirats, and part of
it split away and became the Kazakhs, which is where that distinction begins.
His grandson Muhammad Shaybani spent his youth as a mercenary commander for
whoever was hiring in Transoxiana — including, at times, for Timurid princes —
and reassembled the following.

Then he used it. In 1500 he took Bukhara and Samarkand. By 1507 he had Herat.
The Timurid century in Central Asia ended, and the man who ended it required no
puppet, no marriage into a khan's family, and no title that meant something
other than khan. He simply was one.
