title: Abdullah Khan

Abdullah Khan II ruled in his father's name from the 1550s and in his own from
1583, and he is the one Shaybanid who tried to make the khanate into something
other than a family holding land in common. He spent thirty years reducing the
appanages of his own relatives by force, centralised what he could, reformed the
coinage, and built — caravanserais, bridges, reservoirs, the covered markets of
Bukhara that are still standing.

Then he used the instrument. In 1588 he took Herat and much of Khurasan from a
Safavid state that was in the middle of its own succession crisis, with a
sixteen-year-old shah who had just come to the throne over a country the
Ottomans were also invading. It is the widest extent Uzbek power ever reached.

It did not stick. The sixteen-year-old was Abbas I, and this site's Safavid page
records what he did next: made peace on bad terms to buy time, broke the
Qizilbash, built a standing corps of ghulams, and then came back. Khurasan and
Herat were Safavid again by 1603.

The reach is therefore a measure of something narrower than it looks. The
khanate could take a great deal when the neighbour was distracted and could not
hold it when the neighbour was not, because what it had was tribal cavalry and
what it did not have — as the corpus has now said on a dozen pages between
Otlukbeli and Nadir — was a paid standing force and a revenue system to support
one.

Abdullah Khan died in 1598. His son was murdered within months by his own
kinsmen, and the Shaybanid male line ran out.
