---
title: What it did to Babur
drafted_from: di-cosmo-2009

Zahir al-Din Muhammad Babur was a Timurid prince of Farghana, a
great-great-great-grandson of Timur, and he spent his youth trying to hold
Samarkand. He took it three times and lost it three times, twice to Muhammad
Shaybani. He wrote about all of it afterwards in Chagatai, in a memoir that is
one of the great autobiographies in any language and is unusually frank about
being beaten.

The Shaybanid conquest is therefore visible from inside, from the losing side,
in a way that very little in this corpus is. Babur's account of Shaybani is not
the account of a barbarian invasion — he records the man as a competent
commander with a legitimate claim, and records his own difficulty in raising
troops against someone the Timurid amirs regarded as a plausible sovereign.

In 1504 Babur gave up on Central Asia and took Kabul. He came back once: in
1511, after Shah Isma'il had killed Shaybani Khan at Merv, he retook Samarkand
with Safavid help — and lost it within a year, at Ghijduvan in 1512, partly
because accepting Shi'i backing and the Safavid red cap had cost him the support
of a Sunni population that had been prepared to have him. It is the last time a
Timurid ruled Timur's capital.

He went back to Kabul and, in 1526, took northern India, where the dynasty he
founded ruled for three centuries and called itself Gurkani after Timur's own
title. This site's Timurid record now counts that as its single successor claim.

The chain is worth stating plainly because it runs across four pages. The
Shaybanids took Transoxiana because they had Chinggisid descent and the Timurids
did not; a dispossessed Timurid went south and founded the Mughal empire; and
the reason the Mughals were in India at all is that a Jochid house had a better
claim to a steppe title than they did.
