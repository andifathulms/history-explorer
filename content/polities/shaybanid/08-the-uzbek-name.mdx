The Shaybanids gave their confederation's name to a country, and the country is
named after a khan who was not one of them and who ruled two thousand kilometres
away.

## Where "Uzbek" comes from

The name attaches to Uzbeg Khan of the Golden Horde, who ruled at Sarai from
1313 to 1341 and made that khanate definitively Muslim. The nomads of the
eastern Jochid lands — the ulus of Shiban — came to be called Uzbeks after him,
and it was under that name that Muhammad Shaybani led them south across the
Syr Darya at the end of the fifteenth century and took Transoxiana from the
Timurids.

The people who moved were nomads. The country they took was one of the oldest
settled regions in the world. Within a few generations the name had transferred
from the confederation to the population of the country it had conquered, which
is what usually happens, and the modern Republic of Uzbekistan carries it.

The Kazakhs are the other half of the same split: the groups who declined to
follow the Shaybanids south in the 1450s and stayed on the steppe took a
different name and kept the nomadic life for another four hundred years. Two
modern nations, one confederation, divided by a decision about whether to go and
take the cities.

## Three khanates and a protectorate

What the Shaybanids left institutionally lasted until the twentieth century.

The state passed to the Toqay-Timurids without a break, and then, in the
eighteenth century, fragmented into three: the emirate of Bukhara, the khanate
of Khiva in Khwarazm, and the khanate of Kokand in the Ferghana valley. All
three were governed on the pattern the Shaybanids had established — a Sunni
Muslim court, an ulama with real authority, a revenue drawn from irrigated
agriculture and what was left of the transit trade.

Russia conquered them in the 1860s and 1870s and left two of them standing as
protectorates. The emir of Bukhara and the khan of Khiva were still on their
thrones in 1917, which means the political order the Uzbeks established in 1500
outlived the Romanovs by a few months.

## The buildings

The other inheritance is visible from any street in Bukhara.

The Shaybanid century is when the city acquired the form it still has: the great
madrasas facing each other across a square, the covered crossroads bazaars, the
Kalyan complex, the domed markets for the moneychangers, the hat-sellers and the
jewellers. Samarkand's Registan was completed under their successors on the
plan they worked in.

Those buildings are the last great expenditure of the overland caravan trade
before it stopped mattering, and they were put up by a dynasty of nomads who had
been in the country for two generations, in the styles of the Timurids they had
driven out.

## What the descent was worth to the end

The last thing worth saying about this dynasty is the thing the whole Mongol
world turns on.

The Shaybanids ruled because they were descended from Jochi. When their male
line failed in 1599 the throne went to another line descended from Jochi,
without a war, because the qualification was the descent rather than the family.
The men who actually governed — the Uzbek tribal amirs — could no more take the
title in Bukhara than Timur could take it in Samarkand two centuries before.

Chinggis Khan died in 1227. In 1785, in Bukhara, the answer to who was entitled
to be khan was still: one of his descendants. It is difficult to name any other
rule about legitimate authority anywhere that ran for five and a half centuries
and was obeyed by people who had every practical reason not to.
