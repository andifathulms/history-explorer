The Shaybanid khanate was built on a crossroads that was closing, and the
closing had nothing to do with anything anybody in Bukhara did.

## Three walls going up at once

To the south, Iran became Safavid and Shi'i after 1501, and the Uzbeks were
Sunni. What had been a frontier between two Muslim states became a confessional
one, fought over continuously — Merv, Herat and Khurasan changed hands
repeatedly — and the road south stopped being a road and became a front.

To the west, Russia took Kazan in 1552 and Astrakhan in 1556, which put a
Christian power across the lower Volga and ended the old northern route to the
Black Sea as a Muslim thoroughfare.

To the east, the Ming had shut the maritime expeditions down in the 1430s and
was managing its western frontier through a tightly restricted tribute trade
that let a fixed number of missions in and treated everything else as a security
problem.

A state whose whole position was that it sat between Iran, Russia, China and
India found, within about half a century, that three of the four approaches had
become either a war or a permit.

## And the sea

The fourth thing was happening a long way away and was not visible from
Transoxiana at all.

Portuguese ships reached India in 1498 and the Indian Ocean trade began moving
by sea. That did not empty the overland routes overnight — the older view that
the caravan trade collapsed the moment the Cape route opened is too neat, and
Central Asian caravans went on running well into the seventeenth century. But
the direction was set. Bulk goods between India, Iran and Europe increasingly
went round Africa, and the share of the world's long-distance trade that had to
cross Central Asia declined from something enormous to something regional.

The khanate's revenue was transit and its cities were built on it. There was
nothing to be done about any of this from Bukhara, and nobody in Bukhara appears
to have understood that it was happening until it had.

## What was done instead

The one thing available: taking Khurasan.

Abdullah Khan II spent his reign fighting the Safavids for it and took Herat in
1588, and it did not solve the problem, because the problem was not that the
Uzbeks lacked territory. Khurasan was a rich province at the end of a road that
was carrying less every decade, and holding it required a permanent war with a
larger neighbour.

The other thing available was building, and the Shaybanids did a great deal of
it. The madrasas, mosques and covered bazaars that give Bukhara and Samarkand
their sixteenth-century character were put up by this dynasty out of the last
good decades of the caravan revenue.

That is what the contraction of this state looks like: not ruin, but an
increasingly magnificent city on an increasingly quiet road, paid for by a
frontier war that could not be won and could not be given up.
