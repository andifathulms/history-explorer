---
title: Bukhara, and the decline that has to be argued for
drafted_from: di-cosmo-2009
phase: aside
---

The standard story about Central Asia after 1500 is decline: the Portuguese
rounded the Cape, the overland routes died, the caravan cities withered, and the
region became a backwater until Russia arrived. It is a tidy story, it was told
for a century, and the scholarship this page is drafted from does not accept it.

The trade did not die. It reorganised. The volume of goods moving between India,
Iran, Russia and China through Transoxiana appears to have held up or grown in
the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; Bukhara and Balkh were substantial
commercial cities; Indian merchant communities were resident in Central Asian
towns in numbers; and the Russian trade through Astrakhan expanded. What
changed is that the region stopped being on the main road between Europe and
Asia and became the hub of a set of regional systems instead, which is a
different thing from collapse.

What the Shaybanids built with the money is still there. Ubaydullah Khan's
Mir-i Arab madrasa at Bukhara, finished in the 1530s, faces the Kalyan mosque
across a square and is the visual centre of the city; Abdullah Khan's markets and
caravanserais are the fabric of its bazaar quarter. The waqf endowments that
funded the madrasas made the ulama an independent economic power in a way that
shaped Central Asian politics for three centuries — the same mechanism this
site's Mamluk page describes in Cairo, for the same reason, with a different
ruling class.

Two things follow for this corpus. The first is that a polity's `reach_km2` and
its influence counts, which are all null here, would tell a reader nothing about
what the sixteenth century was like in Bukhara. The second is a caution the site
should apply to itself: "decline" is a judgement, it is usually a judgement made
from somewhere else about where the important traffic went, and this corpus has
now had to argue against it three times — for the Ottoman seventeenth century,
for the Safavid one, and here.
