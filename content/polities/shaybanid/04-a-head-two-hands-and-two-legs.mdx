The Uzbek conquest of Transoxiana is recorded in detail by the man it happened
to. Babur held Samarkand against Shaybani Khan for five months in 1501, lost it,
and wrote the siege up thirty years later from the inside of the walls.

## The five months

The fighting he reports as a professional. Four of his men ran up to a stretch of
rampart the Uzbeks were climbing and drove them all off by *energetic drubbing*;
one beg at the Needle-makers' Gate held out well to the end; another led a sortie
out of that gate and chased the Uzbeks as far as a village, unhorsing some and
coming back with a few heads. He names them all, and notes which of them did best.

Then the supply.

> It was now the time of ripening rain but no-one brought new corn into the town.
> The long siege caused great privation to the towns-people; it went so far that
> the poor and destitute began to eat the flesh of dogs and asses and, as there
> was little grain for the horses, people fed them on leaves.

He records which leaves worked — mulberry and elm — and that some people scraped
dry wood and gave the damp shavings to their horses. That is a besieged
commander's memory, and nobody who had not been inside would keep it.

Shaybani Khan did not assault. For three or four months he kept the fort invested
at a distance and moved round it from post to post. Then, at midnight, when the
defenders were off their guard, his men came up to the Turquoise Gate, beat their
drums and flung out their war-cry. Babur was in the college, undressed. *There
was great trepidation and anxiety.* After that they came night after night,
drumming and shouting.

## Why nobody came

The passage that follows is the most honest thing in the book, and it is about
Babur rather than about the Uzbeks.

> Although envoys and messengers had been sent repeatedly to all sides and
> quarters, no help and reinforcement arrived from any-one. No-one had helped or
> reinforced me when I was in strength and power and had suffered no sort of
> defeat or loss; on what score would any-one help me now?

Then the maxim he had been taught and was now testing:

> The old saying was that to hold a fort there must be a head, two hands and two
> legs, that is to say, the Commandant is the head; help and reinforcement coming
> from two quarters are the two arms and the food and water in the fort are the
> two legs.

He had the head. He had neither arm and both legs had gone. He left in despair
and got away.

## The sister

The price of the withdrawal is recorded elsewhere in the memoir, in the section
where Babur lists his father's daughters, and he does not dwell on it.

His elder sister Khanzada Begim — who, he says, had been with him from his birth
— fell to Shaybani Khan in the throneless time that followed. She bore him a son,
who was given Balkh and died a few years after his father. Nine years later Shah
Ismail destroyed the Uzbeks near Merv, where she was, and sent her back to Babur
with an escort, *for my sake*.

> We had been apart for some ten years; when Muhammadi kukildash and I went to
> see her, neither she nor those about her knew us, although I spoke. They
> recognized us after…

## What the Uzbeks had done

Babur's account is the best evidence for the character of the conquest, and what
it shows is not a raid.

Shaybani did not storm Samarkand. He sat down outside it for five months,
starved it, ensured that no grain came in from the countryside at harvest, and
broke the defenders' sleep for the last stretch. That is the method of a man who
intends to keep the city rather than sack it, and he kept it: the Uzbek
dynasties held Transoxiana for the next four centuries.

And Babur's complaint — that nobody came because he had never helped anybody
either — is a description of what the Timurid system had become. A country full
of princes of the same house, each holding a town, none of whom would combine,
was taken one town at a time by a confederation that would.
