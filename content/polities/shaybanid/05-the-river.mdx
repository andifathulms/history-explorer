In 1510 Shah Isma'il met Muhammad Shaybani near Merv, destroyed the Uzbek army,
and killed him. He then had the skull mounted in gold as a drinking cup, and the
skin stuffed with straw and sent to the Ottoman sultan. Isma'il was twenty-two
and had been shah for nine years.

<Figure id="sh-merv" />

The Uzbeks recovered — Ubaydullah Khan held Transoxiana and beat Babur and the
Safavids at Ghijduvan in 1512 — but Khurasan and Herat were gone, and something
larger had changed. Two years earlier this had been a dynastic quarrel over
which house held which cities. After Merv it was a confessional war: a Sunni
khanate whose legitimacy rested on Chinggisid descent against a Shi'i empire
whose legitimacy rested on the imams, fighting along the Amu Darya for two
centuries.

The consequences are still legible on a map and they run in both directions.

Transoxiana was cut off from Iran, with which it had been a single cultural
region since the Samanids, whose world had held Bukhara and Nishapur in the same
conversation. The
Persian literary culture continued on both sides of the river and stopped
circulating across it. Central Asian scholars went to India instead, which is
part of why the Mughal court became what it was.

And the frontier acquired a slave trade. Uzbek raiding into Khurasan carried off
Iranians who, being Shi'i, could be argued by Central Asian jurists to be
lawfully enslaved in a way Sunni Muslims could not; the markets at Bukhara and
Khiva were supplied that way into the nineteenth century. It is the ugliest
single consequence of the settlement of 1501, and it belongs here rather than in
an account of the Safavids because these were the people who profited from
it.
