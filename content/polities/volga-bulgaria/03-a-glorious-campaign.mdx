By the second half of the twelfth century Bulghar's most active
neighbor to the west was no longer Kiev but Suzdal, a principality on
the upper Volga whose princes had turned their ambitions inward
rather than toward the old Dnieper capital. Andrei Bogoliubsky, prince
of Suzdal from 1157 to 1174, was of a different temper from the
"joyous," easygoing princes the sources describe at Kiev - an
ambitious, restless and imperious ruler who built up Vladimir and his
own new settlement of Bogoliubovo rather than compete for a Kievan
throne he had come, by the end of his reign, to regard as worth
plundering rather than holding.

## A reputation built on Bulghar's account

It was against this newly assertive Suzdal that Bulghar found itself
tested. Andrei is credited with a campaign against the Volga Bulghars
substantial enough to raise his own standing across Russia - called
only "glorious" by the chronicler who preserved word of it, with no
further account of its course, its targets, or its outcome for
Bulghar itself, and no precise year attached to it beyond its place
within his seventeen-year reign, before the sack of Kiev that closed
it. It stands as the clearest sign that Bulghar's
long-standing relationship with the Rus principalities to its west
had shifted, by this point, into open military contest with a rising
Suzdalian power that the kingdom's own earlier trading partners at
Kiev had never quite posed.
