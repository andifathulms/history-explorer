When Ibn Fadlan visited in 922, Bulghar was a seasonal market three
miles from the Volga's eastern bank, with no permanent buildings at
all - only the felt yurts of a still semi-nomadic people, and a king
who was himself a vassal of the Khazars, his own son held hostage
at the Khazar court in Itil. The Rus destruction of the Khazar
khaganate in 969, and the sack of Khazaran and of Bulghar itself
that came with it, might have been expected to end the kingdom
outright. Instead the Bulghars used the vacuum the Khazars' fall
left behind to extend their own reach south, eventually as far as
Saqsin, the city that grew up on the old Khazar capital's own site.

## The leading fur market in the world

By the time the traveller Abu Hamid al-Andalusi lived at Bulghar in
the 1130s, the transformation was complete. The yurt-camp Ibn Fadlan
had described was gone; in its place stood a walled city of wooden
houses ringed by an oak stockade, with villages in its own
countryside and a king who levied tribute on what Abu Hamid called
"infinite peoples" to the north. Bulghar had become the leading fur
market in the world, sending beaver, sable and mink in enormous
quantities to buyers across the Islamic world, India and China.

## Winter raids and the slave trade

Some things had not changed. Slaving remained a central part of the
kingdom's economy exactly as it had been in Ibn Fadlan's day: when
the rivers froze hard enough to cross, the king led raids north
against pagan peoples beyond his own tributaries, seizing their
women, children and horses and selling them on at Bulghar for
distribution through the cities of the Islamic heartland. Two
centuries after Almish's embassy to Baghdad, the memory of Ibn
Fadlan's own visit had faded into legend - Abu Hamid records a
different, garbled story of the Bulghars' conversion, in which a
travelling healer cured the king and queen on condition of their
becoming Muslim - but the kingdom the embassy had once recognized
was, by any material measure, at the height of its own power.
