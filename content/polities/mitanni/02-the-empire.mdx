title: From the sea to the mountains

At its widest, under Barattarna and Saushtatar in the fifteenth century, Mitanni
ran from the Mediterranean coast of northern Syria across the Euphrates and the
Khabur to the Zagros foothills. Alalakh in the west was a vassal; Assur in the
east was a vassal; Nuzi east of the Tigris was inside it. Kizzuwatna in Cilicia
went back and forth. It was one of the four or five powers that mattered, and it
was the one in the middle.

Saushtatar sacked Assur and took a door of gold and silver back to his capital.
The detail is worth keeping because of who recorded it: the Assyrians, centuries
later, in inscriptions that remember the century of Mitanni overlordship as the
thing their own empire existed to reverse. This site's Neo-Assyrian page begins
in 911 BC, five hundred years after Saushtatar. The grudge is older than the
empire.

The war that consumed the fifteenth century was with Egypt, over the same
northern Syrian cities. Thutmose III campaigned into Syria seventeen times,
crossed the Euphrates, and set up a stele next to his grandfather's; Mitanni
lost battles and did not lose the country, because Egypt could reach it and
could not hold it. The two powers fought over Syria for roughly a century
without either being able to finish the other, which is the ordinary condition
of a frontier and is why the eventual settlement mattered so much.

Below the kings there was a chariot aristocracy called the maryannu, holding
land in return for service — the arrangement that this site codes `land-grant`
here and at Ptolemaic Alexandria and in half the Islamic corpus. The coding for
Mitanni rests on documents from Nuzi and Alalakh, which is to say from the
provinces, because the centre has produced nothing.
