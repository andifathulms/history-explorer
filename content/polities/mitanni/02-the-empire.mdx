---
title: From the sea to the mountains

At its widest, under Barattarna and Saushtatar in the fifteenth century,
Mitanni ran from the Mediterranean coast of northern Syria across the Euphrates
and the Khabur to the Zagros foothills. Alalakh in the west was a vassal; Assur
in the east was a vassal; Nuzi east of the Tigris was inside it. Kizzuwatna in
Cilicia went back and forth. It was one of the four or five powers that
mattered, and it was the one in the middle.

Saushtatar sacked Assur and took a door of gold and silver back to his capital.
The detail is worth keeping because of who recorded it: the Assyrians,
centuries later, in inscriptions that remember the century of Mitanni
overlordship as the thing their own empire existed to reverse. The Assyrian
empire that eventually did reverse it began five hundred years after
Saushtatar. The grudge is older than the empire.

The war that consumed the fifteenth century was with Egypt, over the same
northern Syrian cities. Thutmose III campaigned into Syria seventeen times,
crossed the Euphrates, and set up a stele next to his grandfather's; Mitanni
lost battles and did not lose the country, because Egypt could reach it and
could not hold it. The two powers fought over Syria for roughly a century
without either being able to finish the other, which is the ordinary condition
of a frontier and is why the eventual settlement mattered so much.

Below the kings there was a chariot aristocracy called the maryannu, holding
land in return for service, which is the standard arrangement of the Late
Bronze Age and of a good deal that came after it. What is known about it here
comes from documents at Nuzi and Alalakh — from the provinces, because the
centre has produced nothing.
