---
title: The gods in the treaty
drafted_from: wilhelm-1989

The treaty by which Suppiluliuma I installed Shattiwaza over what was left of
Mitanni ends, as such documents do, with a list of divine witnesses. Among the
Hurrian and Mesopotamian gods there are four more: Mitra, Varuna, Indra and the
Nasatya twins.

Those are Indo-Aryan gods. They are the gods of the Rigveda, invoked in a treaty
in northern Syria, in a Hittite archive, in the fourteenth century BC. They are
not the only trace. The Mitanni kings' throne names — Artatama, Tushratta,
Shattiwaza — have Indo-Aryan etymologies. The horse-training manual by Kikkuli
of Mitanni, written in Hittite, uses Indo-Aryan numerals for the number of laps:
*aika*, *tera*, *panza*, *satta*, *na*. The word maryannu, for the chariot
warrior, has been connected to the Indo-Aryan *marya*, young man.

The population was Hurrian and the language of government was Hurrian and
Akkadian. What the Indo-Aryan element was — a ruling group that arrived and was
absorbed, a prestige vocabulary borrowed for horses and chariots and kingship, a
substrate from an earlier movement — has been argued over for a century and is
not settled, and this site is not going to settle it.

It is here because of what it is evidence of. These are the same gods, in
recognisable form, that appear in the Rigveda, which this corpus's Classical
India region stands downstream of. The Mitanni attestation is the earliest
securely dated appearance of any of them anywhere, and it is two and a half
thousand miles west of where the Vedic tradition was written down. A treaty
drafted in Anatolia to dismember a Syrian kingdom is a fixed point in the
chronology of Indian religion.

The corpus has no field for that either. `descendant_scripts`,
`religions_carried` and `successor_claims` are all null on this page and all
correctly null: Mitanni propagated nothing, and the gods in the treaty are not
a religion it carried but a fact about where its rulers came from.
