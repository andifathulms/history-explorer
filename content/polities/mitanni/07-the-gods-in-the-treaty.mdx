The treaty by which Suppiluliuma I installed Shattiwaza over what was left of
Mitanni ends, as such documents do, with a list of divine witnesses. Among the
Hurrian and Mesopotamian gods there are four more: Mitra, Varuna, Indra and the
Nasatya twins.

<Figure id="suppiluliuma-treaty" />

Those are Indo-Aryan gods. They are the gods of the Rigveda, invoked in a
treaty in northern Syria, in a Hittite archive, in the fourteenth century BC.
They are not the only trace. The Mitanni kings' throne names — Artatama,
Tushratta, Shattiwaza — have Indo-Aryan etymologies. The horse-training manual
by Kikkuli of Mitanni, written in Hittite, uses Indo-Aryan numerals for the
number of laps: *aika*, *tera*, *panza*, *satta*, *na*. The word maryannu, for
the chariot warrior, has been connected to the Indo-Aryan *marya*, young man.

The population was Hurrian and the language of government was Hurrian and
Akkadian. What the Indo-Aryan element was — a ruling group that arrived and was
absorbed, a prestige vocabulary borrowed for horses and chariots and kingship,
a substrate from an earlier movement — has been argued over for a century and
is not settled and will not be settled here.

It is here because of what it is evidence of. These are the same gods, in
recognisable form, that appear in the Rigveda. The Mitanni attestation is the
earliest securely dated appearance of any of them anywhere, and it is two and a
half thousand miles west of where the Vedic tradition was eventually written
down. A treaty drafted in Anatolia to dismember a Syrian kingdom is a fixed
point in the chronology of Indian religion.

It is not an influence Mitanni exerted on anybody. The kingdom propagated no
religion and no script, and nobody claimed to succeed it. The gods in the
treaty are not something it spread; they are a fact about where its rulers had
come from, preserved by accident in a document written to take its territory
away.
