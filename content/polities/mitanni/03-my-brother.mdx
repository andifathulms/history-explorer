In the early fourteenth century Mitanni and Egypt stopped fighting and became
allies, sealed the way such things were sealed: Mitanni sent princesses to the
Egyptian royal household — Gilu-Hepa to Amenhotep III, Tadu-Hepa later — and
Egypt sent gold.

The correspondence survives, because the Egyptian court's diplomatic archive
was dumped at Amarna and dug up in the 1880s. It is the reason a modern reader
can hear a Mitanni king at all, and what he mostly does is complain.

<Figure id="tushratta-letter-bm" /> Tushratta
writes to the pharaoh at length about gold: that he has asked for gold, that
gold is plentiful in Egypt as dust, that the gold last sent was underweight
when melted down, that his father-in-law's promises are not being kept. He
addresses the pharaoh as brother. He expects to be treated as an equal, and in
the protocol of the period he was one.

That protocol is the thing worth taking away, because nothing comparable is
attested again for two thousand years. Late Bronze Age Egypt, Hatti, Babylonia,
Assyria and Mitanni operated a recognised system of great kings who called each
other brother, married each other's daughters, exchanged physicians and
craftsmen, and used a common diplomatic language — Akkadian, which was nobody's
native tongue at three of the five courts. Smaller kings were not brothers and
knew it.

Mitanni was in that club for about a century and a half. Its membership ends
inside a single reign, and the letters stop.
