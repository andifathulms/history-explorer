Hurrian is a language related to nothing much except Urartian, spoken across
northern Mesopotamia and eastern Anatolia from the third millennium onwards.
Hurrian names appear in the records of Akkad; Hurrian populations are attested
across the Khabur and the upper Tigris for centuries before anything that could
be called a Hurrian state. Wilhelm's survey is a study of that world rather
than of one kingdom, and the kingdom is what happened to part of it.

Around the middle of the sixteenth century BC — after the Hittite raid that
finished Babylon and in the political vacuum it left — the Hurrian
principalities of northern Syria and the Jazira were assembled into a single
power. How is not known. There is no foundation account, no conquest narrative,
and no royal inscription of the kind Assyria and Egypt produced in quantity.

What the country was called depends on who is speaking. Egypt called it
Naharin, "the river-land". Assyria called it Hanigalbat. The Hittites called it
the land of Hurri. The form Mittani, which is what modern scholarship uses, is
the one that turns up in documents from the kingdom's own orbit. There is no
securely established self-designation at all: cuneiform was the writing system
and Hurrian the language, and what the kingdom called itself in its own words
is simply not known.

The technology usually attached to Mitanni's rise is the light spoked-wheel
chariot and the trained horse. It is a real association — the kingdom's chariot
elite is documented, and the horse-training manual written by a Mitannian named
Kikkuli survives in the Hittite archives — but it should be stated carefully:
everyone in the Late Bronze Age Near East had chariots by 1500, and what
distinguished Mitanni was not the invention of anything but a hundred and fifty
years of being better organised around it than its neighbours.
