The best evidence for what was happening to Mitanni is a run of letters its own
king sent to Egypt, because of what he asks for and how he asks.

Tushratta — Dushratta in the readings of Petrie's generation — wrote to Amenhotep
III and then to his son. The tablets were found at Amarna, filed in the Egyptian
records office, and they cover perhaps twenty years at the end of Mitanni's
independence.

## How he came to the throne

The first letter is not a diplomatic formality. It is a man explaining that he
has survived.

His brother Artashumara had been murdered. A man called Pirhi, who had backed the
murderers, attacked Tushratta's land and people; Tushratta beat him and killed
the murderers. Then the Hittites came into his country, and — he says — his god
Teshub gave them into his hand.

He sends the proof to Egypt: a chariot, two horses, a boy and a girl, from the
Hittite booty. Then five more chariots with teams, and for his sister Gilukhipa,
already in the Egyptian harem, gold breast ornaments, gold earrings, a gold
vessel and a jar of oil.

A king announces his accession by demonstrating that he has put down a
succession war and beaten off a Hittite invasion, and encloses samples.

## The bargaining

What follows is marriage diplomacy conducted as commerce, and Petrie's comment is
that the kings of Mitanni were entirely familiar with the oriental art of
politely screwing a present or a promise out of a friend.

Amenhotep sends his agent Mani to ask for Tushratta's daughter as *mistress of
Egypt*. Tushratta agrees — and asks for a great deal of gold, citing what
Amenhotep had sent his father Shutarna: a dish, a cup and a brick of solid gold.
He sends, on his own side, a gold goblet set with lapis, necklaces of lapis and
gold and *khulalu* stone, an amulet, ten pairs of horses, ten chariots and thirty
women.

Later he sends the statue of Ishtar of Nineveh to Egypt to be honoured, and asks
for her back.

## The count that gives it away

The longest letter in the series — about a hundred and ninety lines — contains
the sentence that tells you what has changed, and Tushratta puts it in himself,
as history.

When Amenhotep III's father asked Tushratta's grandfather Artatama for a
daughter, she was granted only at **the seventh application**. When Amenhotep
asked Tushratta's father Shutarna for a daughter, she was granted at the sixth.
When he asked Tushratta, Tushratta sent her.

Three generations, and the price of a Mitannian princess falls from seven
requests to none.

Petrie's reading is that Mitanni had gone from an independent frontier neighbour
to a vassal who had to agree to Egyptian proposals with what dignity it could
manage. That is his inference and it goes further than the letters do — the
correspondence keeps up the language of brotherhood throughout, and Tushratta
addresses the pharaoh as *my brother* and himself as father-in-law. But a state
that used to refuse six times and now does not refuse at all has moved, whatever
the salutation says.

## The last letter

Amenhotep III dies. Tushratta is uncertain enough about the son that he writes
first to the queen mother, Tiye, appealing to her memory of the old friendship
and telling the young king to consult her, because she knows what was agreed.

Then he writes to Akhenaten about money. Amenhotep, he says, had promised golden
images, lapis, instruments and much gold as the price for a wife — and they have
not been sent.

That is where the Mitannian correspondence stops. Not with a defeat or a treaty
but with an unpaid bill, and then silence, because the route the messengers used
was cut within a year or two by fighting in the north.

The state that had been beating Hittites off its territory a generation earlier
now depended on an Egyptian court that had stopped answering, and the letters
that record it were filed in Egypt and left there when the city was abandoned.
