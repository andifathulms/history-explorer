title: Taken apart twice

Suppiluliuma I of Hatti spent his reign dismantling Mitanni, and he did it the
efficient way: he took the Syrian vassals first, one at a time, until the
kingdom's western half had changed sides, and only then went for the centre.
The Egyptian alliance did not help, because Egypt was in the middle of the
Amarna period and was not answering.

Tushratta was murdered by one of his own sons. A rival claimant, Artatama II,
was backed by Assyria; Suppiluliuma backed another son, Shattiwaza, married him
to a Hittite princess, and installed him over a rump kingdom west of the
Euphrates as a vassal. The treaty recording the arrangement is the fullest
surviving document the kingdom produced, and it was written by the people who
had just finished it.

The second ending took another eighty years. Assyria, which Mitanni had ruled
for a century, took the eastern territories under Adad-nirari I and completed
the work under Shalmaneser I around 1260, deporting populations and
administering the country as a province called Hanigalbat. Whatever was left of
the Mitanni state ceased to be one.

There is no single conqueror to name. The kingdom was broken by Suppiluliuma
and finished off by the Middle Assyrian kings, and the Assyria that eventually
made the whole Near East its own is a different state three and a half
centuries later — continuous as a place and a tradition, and not the same
polity.

What Mitanni left is the country it had held, which stayed Hurrian in
population long after it stopped being Hurrian in government, and a hole in the
record where its own account of itself should be.
