Mitanni did not end at a date. It was broken by Suppiluliuma, reduced to a rump
under a Hittite-installed king, fought over by Hatti and Assyria for a
generation, and then absorbed into Assyria as a province with a new name:
Hanigalbat, which is what the Assyrians had always called it anyway.

Its people did not go anywhere. Hurrian-speaking populations remained across
northern Mesopotamia for centuries; the name Hanigalbat is still in use in
Assyrian records long after there is anything to attach it to. What ended was
the kingdom, and what disappeared was any account of it written by anyone who
lived in it.

## Known entirely from outside

Everything about Mitanni comes from its neighbours' archives — kings,
extent, institutions, gods, all of it read off documents somebody else wrote
for their own purposes, never its own. That dependence is not a recent
discovery.

Sayce, writing in 1888 before any of the Anatolian archives had been dug,
already had the shape of the problem: he could locate the district called
Mitanni from Egyptian inscriptions, quote Thutmose III on slaying and spoiling
in Naharin in a single day and hunting there with arrows and spears, and go no
further, because there was nothing further to go on.

A century and a half of excavation since has added a great deal about what
Mitanni did and almost nothing about how it thought.

## Washukanni

The reason is a hole in the map. The capital has never been found.

It is named repeatedly — Washukanni, the city the Hittite army marched on and
the Assyrians later sacked — and no excavation has produced it. Candidate
mounds have been proposed in the Khabur headwaters and none has been confirmed.
Whatever archive a state of that size kept, and it certainly kept one, is
either under a mound nobody has dug or was destroyed when the city was.

That single absence is why a great power of the fourteenth century is a
silhouette. Hattusa was excavated from 1906 and Hatti became legible in a
decade. Had the archaeology gone the other way — Washukanni found, Boğazköy
missed — every judgement anybody has since made about which of the two was the
more sophisticated state would have been made in reverse.

## What is actually left

A word for a chariot horse's training in a Hittite manual written by a
Mitannian. Four Vedic gods in a treaty. A hundred-odd letters in somebody
else's palace at Amarna, addressed to a brother-in-law who was letting the
correspondence lapse. A sacked door of gold and silver, remembered by the
people it was taken from.

For two hundred years Mitanni was one of the five powers that mattered between
the Nile and the Zagros. It is the only one of them whose own voice has not
been recovered at all.
