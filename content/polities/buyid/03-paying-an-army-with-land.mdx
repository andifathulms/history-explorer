---
title: Paying an army with land
drafted_from: chi-4
phase: peak
---

The Buyids arrived in Iraq with an army they could not afford and a treasury
that had been failing for fifty years. What they did about it changed how
states in this part of the world paid soldiers for the next six centuries.

Instead of cash from a central treasury, a commander was assigned the revenue
of a district and told to collect it himself. The assignment is an *iqta*. It
solved the immediate problem completely: the soldier was paid, the treasury did
not have to move money it did not have, and the chain of clerks between the
land and the wage disappeared.

Then it did what such arrangements do. A man collecting his own pay from a
district has every reason to take the maximum this year and none to maintain
what produces it next year, and the thing being taken in southern Iraq was the
yield of an irrigation system two thousand years old that required constant
work. The Sawad — the alluvium that had funded every empire in Mesopotamia since
Babylon — declined under the Buyids, and the sources
describe canals silting, villages abandoned and revenue falling. The state was
consuming the asset it was assigned against.

Two things follow, and they run a long way past this dynasty.

The first is that the *iqta* did not die with the Buyids. Nizam al-Mulk is
usually credited with it, and what he systematised and made durable under the
Seljuks — the standard way of paying an army across the Islamic world for the
next several centuries — had been improvised in Buyid Iraq a hundred years
earlier by men who needed the soldiers paid by the end of the month.

The second is that the Buyids were paying two armies with it, and they hated
each other. The Daylamite infantry who had brought the family to power were
Shi'i highlanders; the cavalry the state increasingly needed were Turks, Sunni,
bought on the market the Samanids ran. They competed for
assignments, and their commanders competed through them. By the eleventh
century the Turks predominated in Iraq and the Daylamites in Iran, and the
instrument that had raised the family had been superseded inside its own army.
The Abbasids, the Samanids and the Ayyubids all reached the same position by
different roads.
