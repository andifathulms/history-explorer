Miskawayh, who spent his working life inside the administration the Buyids ran,
stops his year-by-year narrative to ask directly how Ali ibn Buya got from being
one captain among several to being a sovereign, and gives an answer in four
parts: liberality, courage, a run of lucky coincidences, and a favourable
horoscope. Then he tells the story about the mule, which is really an argument
about the first two.

Ali and his brother Hasan had been captains under Makan ibn Kaki. When Makan's
fortunes collapsed they asked his permission to desert to Mardavij, putting it to
him that they would be doing him a kindness by taking their upkeep off his hands,
and promising to come back when his luck turned. He gave them leave, and half
his other commanders followed their example. Mardavij received the brothers with
robes of honour and parcelled the districts of the Jibal out among the newcomers:
Karaj to Ali, Dinawand to Lashkari, Hamadan to Sulayman, and so on down the list.

All of them went to Rayy to collect their deeds of appointment. Rayy was run by
Mardavij's brother Vushmgir, with an administrator called al-Amid doing the
actual work. Al-Amid was shown a good mule belonging to Ali ibn Buya, who was
selling it because he needed the three thousand dirhams; al-Amid bought it and
sent the money. When Ali learned who the buyer was he led the mule to al-Amid
himself, swore he would take nothing for it, and followed that up with a stream
of other attentions. Not long after, Mardavij changed his mind about the
appointments and wrote to Rayy ordering that none of the new governors be allowed
to leave, and that any who had already gone be stopped. The official post reached
al-Amid first, as it always did; he read the order, sent word privately to Ali to
get to his province at once, and only the next morning laid the correspondence
before Vushmgir. By then Ali had been riding without resting at the stages and
was far away. Every other commander was detained. Miskawayh's verdict: this was
the post that led to his conquests and his sovereignty, and the only cause of it
anyone could name, after the divine decree, was his open-handedness.

From Karaj he went south. The brothers took Arrajan and then worked through Fars,
extracting enormous sums and digging up treasures that had belonged to the
Sasanian kings and had descended in local families. In 934 the governor of Fars,
Yaqut, cut him off at a bridge on the road to Kirman and forced him to fight.
The battle ran three days. On the second night Ali told his men he would dismount
and fight on foot as one of them, made lavish promises, and took oaths from them
that they would hold.

Then Yaqut made two mistakes that Miskawayh treats as a lesson in command. Two
Daylamite chiefs deserted to him, and he had them beheaded on sight — which
informed every Daylamite on the other side that surrender was not available, so
they fought like men seeking martyrdom. And he opened the third morning by
putting naphtha-throwers in front of his line; the wind shifted and blew the fire
back into his own ranks, and the Daylamites came through and killed the
flame-troops where they stood. When his cavalry broke, Yaqut took four thousand
men up a hill and waited for the enemy to scatter into his baggage so that he
could counterattack, which is a manoeuvre that has retrieved many lost battles.
Ali rode out in front of his own line and told his men exactly what was being
planned, that they were to keep their ranks and not touch the plunder, and that
the plunder was not going anywhere. They held; Yaqut fled.

In his baggage were boxes of burnouses and fetters, made ready for the prisoners
he had expected to take. Ali's officers proposed using them on Yaqut's men — the
hoods on their heads and the irons on their legs, paraded first through the camp
and then through the town. He refused, and said that they should rather pardon
the enemies God had put in their power and give thanks, as being the way to
receive more and to avoid arrogance. He then rode to Shiraz picking up deserters
the whole distance, pardoning everyone who was reported to him as having spoken
against him, and camped outside the city.

That is how a mercenary captain acquires a province. It is also, almost item for
item, how he keeps one.
