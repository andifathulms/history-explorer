The Buyids held Baghdad for a hundred and ten years as occupiers of a city that
was not theirs, keeping a caliph they did not believe in and taxing an
irrigation system they were letting decay. Two things they built there are the
answer to what they left, and one of them lasted three hundred years.

## Mu'izz al-Dawla's dyke

The first Buyid to take Baghdad also did the least glamorous thing any of them
did. Le Strange, assembling the topography of the city out of the Arabic
authorities, singles it out: Mu'izz al-Dawla especially entitled himself to the
lasting gratitude of the people of Baghdad by erecting the huge dyke which, when
kept in repair, prevented the inundation of the city by the flooding of the
streams that ran out into the Tigris at the Shammasiyah lowlands.

A great deal of what a government does that matters looks like that. It is a
bank of earth, and its whole merit is that a flood did not happen, and it needed
maintaining forever.

## Adud al-Dawla's hospital

The second is the one with the name on it.

Adud al-Dawla built a hospital in West Baghdad on the ruins of the Khuld Palace
— the pleasure palace of the early caliphs, whose review ground and royal
stables had disappeared long before, and whose site became the new quarter of
the Bimaristan. Muqaddasi, writing within a few years, describes it as newly
built, opposite the bridge of boats leading to East Baghdad.

For three centuries the Bimaristan Adudi was a school of medical science famous
throughout the east.

That is the Buyid afterlife, and it is worth pausing on what kind of thing it
is. A Daylamite amir occupying somebody else's capital, holding a caliph he
regarded as theologically wrong, whose army was eating the province it was paid
out of, put up a teaching hospital that outlived his dynasty by two hundred
years and taught medicine to people who had never heard of him.

## Where he was buried, and what happened to all of it

Adud al-Dawla died at Baghdad in 982, and was buried — as Le Strange puts it,
as all good Shi'ahs should be — at Mashhad Ali on the Euphrates, the shrine at
Najaf. Not in the capital he had ruled from, and not in the Caspian mountains
his family came from.

After his death the Buyid power declined and the internecine wars began, and
Muqaddasi, writing at exactly that moment, laments the ruin of the great city
and fears it will soon rival Samarra in chronic insurrection and misrule. He was
not far wrong. In 1055 Tughril Beg the Seljuk suppressed the last Buyid prince
and took Baghdad.

The hospital kept working. By the fourteenth century it was a ruin standing on
the right bank of the Tigris where the old main bridge of boats had crossed to
Rusafah. By Le Strange's own day every vestige of it had gone — along with the
Mosque of Mansur and the Rusafah Mosque, the last three great buildings of the
older capital that any traveller records seeing.

So a dynasty that claimed the title king of kings and had it read in the Friday
prayer is remembered, in the topography of the city it ruled, by a flood defence
and a hospital, and neither of them is there.
