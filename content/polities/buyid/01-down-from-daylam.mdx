Daylam is the mountain country south-west of the Caspian. It converted late,
stayed poor, and exported soldiers. In the early tenth century Daylamite
infantry were the most sought-after troops in western Iran, and three brothers
— Ali, Hasan and Ahmad, the sons of a man named Buya — were among the many
Daylamites hiring themselves out to whoever was paying.

They were mercenary captains. Then, in the ordinary progression of that trade
in a decade when nobody could pay reliably, they became the employers.

Ali took Fars in 934. Hasan took the Jibal. In 945 Ahmad walked into Baghdad.

What happened in Baghdad is the thing the Buyids are for. Ahmad did not depose
the caliph. He did not claim the caliphate, could not have held it as a Shi'i,
and evidently did not want it. He took the title *amir al-umara*, commander of
commanders, received a caliphal grant confirming him in everything he had taken,
and left al-Mustakfi on the throne — briefly, before blinding him and
installing a more cooperative one.

For the next hundred and ten years the Abbasid caliph was a Buyid appointment.
The institution survived, its authority intact enough to be worth issuing
grants in, and entirely under the control of men who did not share its
confession.
