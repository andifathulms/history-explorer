title: Down from Daylam

Daylam is the mountain country south-west of the Caspian: high, poor, and late
to Islam — it held out for two centuries after the plains below it had been
conquered. What it exported was soldiers. Daylamite infantry were sought across
the Iranian world at a time when almost every other army was cavalry, and in
the early tenth century, when nobody in western Iran could pay reliably for
long, there were a great many of them for hire.

Three brothers — Ali, Hasan and Ahmad, sons of a man named Buya — were among
them. They took service under a Daylami commander in Samanid employ, and then
did what mercenary captains occasionally do in a decade when their employers
are failing: they stopped being the hired and became the hirers. Ali took Fars
in 934. Hasan took the Jibal. In 945 Ahmad walked into Baghdad.

What Ahmad did there is the thing the Buyids are for. He did not depose the
caliph. He did not claim the caliphate — he could not have held it as a Shi'i
and evidently did not want it. He took the title *amir al-umara*, commander of
commanders, accepted a caliphal grant confirming him in everything he had
already taken, and left al-Mustakfi on the throne, briefly, before blinding him
and installing someone more cooperative. For the following hundred and ten
years the Abbasid caliph was a Buyid appointment: the institution intact, its
grants still worth having, and entirely in the hands of men who did not share
its confession.

That last point is usually run together with the first and the two need
separating.

The Buyids were Shi'i — Zaydi in origin, moving towards Twelver Shi'ism — and
they kept a Sunni caliph for a century without removing him, when they had both
the power to do it and an alternative allegiance available in the Fatimid
caliph at Cairo, who shared their confession. They never took it. What they did
instead was patronise Shi'i institutions inside a Sunni polity: the shrines at
Najaf and Karbala, the public observance of Ashura and Ghadir Khumm at Baghdad
from 962, and the scholars who gave Twelver Shi'ism its legal and theological
shape.

The reason is the arithmetic of what they held. The caliph's legitimacy was the
only thing that made a Daylami mercenary family the lawful rulers of Iraq.
Destroying it would have destroyed the title deed to their own conquest. So
they kept him, paid him, governed in his name — and reached back four centuries
for a second claim of their own, which is the next chapter but one.
