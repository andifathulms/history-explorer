title: Down from Daylam

The Buyids came from Daylam, the mountainous country south-west of the Caspian —
a region that had held out against Islam for two centuries after the conquest of
the plains below it, and whose men were valued across the Iranian world as
infantry at a time when almost every other army was cavalry.

Three brothers, sons of a fisherman named Buya, took service as mercenaries under
a Daylami commander in the service of the Samanids, and within twenty years held
Fars, Rayy and Iraq between them. In 945 Ahmad entered Baghdad and the caliph
made him *amir al-umara*, commander of commanders, which is the moment this site's
Abbasid page describes as the end of caliphal government in fact.

Two things about the arrangement are worth separating, because they are usually
run together.

The Buyids were Shi'i — of the Zaydi tradition in origin, moving toward Twelver
Shi'ism — and they held the Sunni caliph in their keeping for over a century
without ever removing him. They could have. The Fatimids in Cairo were an
available alternative allegiance and a rival caliph of their own confession, and
the Buyids never took it. What they did instead was patronise Shi'i institutions
inside a Sunni polity: the shrines at Najaf and Karbala, the public observance of
Ashura and Ghadir Khumm in Baghdad from 962, and the scholars who gave Twelver
Shi'ism its legal and theological form in this period.

The reason is the second thing. The caliph's legitimacy was the only currency
that made a Daylami mercenary family the lawful rulers of Iraq, and destroying it
would have destroyed the thing they had taken. So they kept him, paid him, and
governed in his name — while reviving the Sasanian title *shahanshah* for
themselves, which is the claim this site records on its Sasanian page.
