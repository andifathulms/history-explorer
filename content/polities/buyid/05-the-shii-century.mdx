The hundred years when the Buyids held Baghdad are called the Shi'i century,
and the label is accurate about patronage and misleading about almost
everything else.

What the Buyids paid for is remarkable and it is not confessionally tidy. Their
viziers were men of letters famous in their own right: Ibn al-Amid at Rayy,
whose prose style was studied for centuries, and al-Sahib ibn Abbad, who ran
the Jibal administration and kept a library said to require camels to move.
Miskawayh — philosopher, historian, and the Sahib's librarian — wrote a
history of the period that is one of the very few contemporary narratives
surviving for any tenth-century state. Shaykh al-Mufid gave Twelver Shi'ism its
systematic theology in Baghdad, and al-Sharif al-Radi compiled the *Nahj
al-Balagha*, the collection of sayings attributed to Ali that became the
central Shi'i literary text after the Qur'an.

And they did nearly all of it in Arabic. A Persian-speaking Daylamite dynasty,
in the exact century when a court at Bukhara was turning New Persian into a
literature, patronised Arabic almost exclusively. That is the
opposite choice from the one their contemporaries and neighbours were making,
and it is worth registering rather than smoothing over: the Buyids were
governing the Arabic-speaking imperial centre and behaved accordingly, while
the dynasties out on the plateau were building something else.

The confessional picture is equally unclean. Twelver institutions were built,
funded and made public under Buyid protection — but so was the Sunni caliphate
they were built next to, and the Ashura processions the Buyids licensed at
Baghdad from 962 produced Sunni counter-processions and periodic riots between
the two, in a city that had both. This is the period in which Sunni and Shi'i
Islam acquired the institutional and legal definition that made them separable
traditions rather than positions in an argument, and it happened under a
government that was patronising both ends of it at once, for reasons that were
mostly about who held Baghdad.

What actually counts as spreading a faith, rather than simply holding one, is
the shrines, the observances and the scholarly establishment the Buyids paid
to build in territory that had not had them — not the amirs' own confession,
which is a fact about the rulers and not about what they propagated.
