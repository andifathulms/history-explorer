title: Three titles in one year

Adud al-Dawla died in 983 and the succession was managed, for about a fortnight,
brilliantly. He had left instructions and the men to carry them out, and Abu
Shuja — who continued Miskawayh's chronicle and had the papers of people who
were there — records the machinery admiringly. The death was concealed. The
chosen son was summoned to the palace as though his father had sent for him, and
informed on arrival that he was the heir. Letters went out to the provinces in
the dead man's name, carrying that news along with an arrest, a ministerial
appointment and a copy of the oath of allegiance every governor and officer was
to swear. A message went to the caliph al-Ta'i asking for a deed in favour of the
heir, with robes, titles and a banner; the caliph obliged and supplied the title
Samsam al-Dawla, Scimitar of the Empire. Only when all of it was in place and the
new amir had held his public reception was the father allowed to be dead.

Within a year there were three of them.

Samsam al-Dawla arrested his brother Abu'l-Husayn in the palace at Baghdad. Their
mother, who was a daughter of the king of the Daylamites and therefore had the
ear of the most dangerous body of men in the city, laid a plan to get into the
palace in disguise on a day the Daylamites were assembled and beg them to storm
it and free her son. Samsam al-Dawla heard about it and made her a civil offer
instead: he would release Abu'l-Husayn and make him governor of Fars, if he went
at once and got there before their other brother Sharaf al-Dawla did. Abu'l-Husayn
went, stopped at Ahwaz to extract money and clothes from the governor — who
refused him publicly and supplied him privately, being afraid of Baghdad — and
reached Arrajan to learn that Sharaf al-Dawla was already in Shiraz. So he turned
back to Ahwaz, made the obliging governor his vizier, declared himself sovereign
under the title Taj al-Dawla, Crown of the Empire, and had the Friday prayer said
in his own name.

The state Adud al-Dawla had spent his life consolidating now had three rulers,
each with a caliphal-sounding title, and no rule for deciding between them except
which of them could pay soldiers.

Which brings the arrangement to its own arithmetic. In January 987 Sharaf
al-Dawla came to Baghdad with an army of nineteen thousand Daylamites and three
thousand Turkish retainers, and the Daylamites, proud of the numbers, treated the
Turks with contempt. Two men quarrelled over a house and a stable. The Daylamites
assembled in the racecourse, the Turks mounted, and the two halves of the Buyid
army fought a battle in the capital.

Samsam al-Dawla, by then a prisoner, was lying in a hut separated from his
brother's tent by nothing but felt and canvas, and heard the eunuch Nizr urging
Sharaf al-Dawla to kill him at once, on the reasoning that if the Daylamites won
and freed him they would all change places. His brother argued against it; as the
fighting worsened, a retainer with a drawn sword was posted at the door of the
hut. Samsam al-Dawla said afterwards that he read the Qur'an he had in his hand
and prayed.

The Daylamites won the opening and lost the battle by winning it. The Turks fell
back, meaning to get out; the Daylamites, contemptuous of so small a force, broke
their line to chase them, and once Daylamite infantry loses its alignment it has
nothing left. The Turks found ground their horses could work in, charged front
and rear, and killed about three thousand of them in under an hour, then rode into
the city and plundered the Daylamite houses. The survivors scattered; most of them
took refuge in Sharaf al-Dawla's tent, and the next morning he entered Baghdad
with the Daylamites crowding round his stirrups to beg. The caliph came down the
river in a barge to congratulate him on his safety, and Sharaf al-Dawla went out
and kissed the ground in front of him.

Both of the things the family had been paying for since Fars had now happened at
once. The army was at war with itself, and the caliph was still the person whose
congratulations mattered.
