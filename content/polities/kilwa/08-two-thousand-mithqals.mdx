The sultanate was already in trouble before the Portuguese fleet appeared, and
the trouble was of the ordinary kind: somebody had taken the throne who had no
business on it.

## A usurper on the throne

When Vasco da Gama first came down the coast in 1498 he was told at Mozambique,
by a pilot who had worked out what he wanted to hear, that there was a
Christian population at Kilwa. He could not land and discovered the deception
at Mombasa.

Pedro Álvares Cabral called in 1500, and da Gama came back on his second voyage
in 1502. Both were received with barely concealed hostility by the man then in
power — Mir Ibrahim in the Portuguese spelling, whom Dames, editing Barbosa,
calls flatly a usurper.

Da Gama extracted a tribute of two thousand mithqals of gold, and from that
moment Kilwa was held in Lisbon to be tributary to the king of Portugal.

That is three years before Almeida, and it is the whole shape of the collapse.
A city with no army, whose ruler had no claim his own people accepted, agreed
to pay an annual sum to a power that had visited twice and could not be
reached.

## What was still there to take

Barbosa, writing about 1518 from what he had been told and partly seen,
describes what the town had been in the years just before, and his description
is the best one there is.

> a Moorish town with many fair houses of stone and mortar, with many windows
> after our fashion, very well arranged in streets, with many flat roofs. The
> doors are of wood, well carved, with excellent joinery. Around it are streams
> and orchards and fruit-gardens with many channels of sweet water.

The people, he says, some fair and some black, finely clad in rich garments of
gold and silk and cotton, the women likewise, with gold and silver chains and
bracelets on their legs and arms and jewelled earrings.

And then the sentence that confirms, from the other side and by a hostile
witness, exactly what the sultanate lived on:

> Before the King our Lord sent out his expedition to discover India the Moors
> of Sofala, Cuama, Angoya and Mozambique were all subject to the King of
> Quiloa, who was the most mighty king among them. And in this town was great
> plenty of gold, as no ships passed towards Sofala without first coming to
> this island.

A Portuguese factor with every reason to belittle the place records that
before his own countrymen arrived, every ship going for the gold had to stop
here first.

## Ibn Battuta's town, and Barbosa's

There is a discrepancy between the two outside descriptions and it is worth
stating rather than smoothing.

Ibn Battuta, who came in 1331, calls Kilwa a large city that consists of wooden
houses, its people Zunuj of the Shafi'i school, of religious and peaceful
habits. Barbosa, two centuries later, describes a stone town with glazed
windows and carved joinery. Dames concluded the town must have developed
rapidly in between.

The likelier reading, and the one the excavation supports, is that both are
describing the same place from different heights: the stone houses were always
a minority among a majority of mud and thatch, and a traveller notices what
most of the town is made of while a factor notices what the merchants live in.

Which also means that when the Portuguese sacked Kilwa they were sacking the
minority. The families in the stone houses were the ones with the gold, the
warehouses and the boats, and they were the ones with something to lose.
