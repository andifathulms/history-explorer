Sofala is a river mouth on the coast of what is now Mozambique, about sixteen
hundred kilometres south of Kilwa, and for four centuries it was where the gold
of the interior reached salt water.

The gold came off the Zimbabwe plateau — this corpus added a record for the state
on that plateau in the same pass as this one, and its own first chapter observes
that the two pages describe two ends of one trade and have no edge between them,
because nothing passed between them but metal and pots.

## How a monopoly on a coastline works

Kilwa did not conquer Sofala in any sense that would show on a map. What it did
was own the leg.

The mechanism is in this record's first chapter and it is meteorological. A ship
out of Arabia, Persia or India rides the north-east monsoon down the coast from
November and must be back on the south-west monsoon by September. Kilwa is
inside that year. Sofala is not. So a merchant from Aden who wanted Zimbabwean
gold could either winter twice on a foreign coast or buy it from somebody at
Kilwa who had already fetched it.

Kilwa made sure there was always somebody, and that the somebody was Kilwan. The
southern run was made in smaller vessels by Kilwan crews on Kilwan account, and
the town's position was not a garrison but a schedule.

This record's `revenue_basis` reads `trade-toll, monopoly`, and the second value
is that arrangement. It is the reason this page sits just outside the
eight-record type the Characene record tracks, whose members had a position and
nothing else. Kilwa had a position and the boats.

## What went the other way

Cloth, mostly. Indian cotton, in enormous quantity, was what the interior wanted
and what gold bought; beads by the tens of thousands, which turn up in the
Zimbabwe excavations; and glazed ceramics.

The exchange rates were reportedly favourable enough at the inland end that a
sixteenth-century Portuguese account calls the trade an exchange of cloth for
gold at rates the Portuguese could not initially believe. That is the kind of
claim a new arrival makes about a market he does not understand, and it should be
read as evidence about Portuguese expectations rather than about African prices.

## Ivory, and the other cargo

Two more commodities, and one of them belongs on this page rather than only in a
data comment.

Ivory went north in quantity — African elephant tusks were softer and easier to
carve than Indian ones and were preferred in India for that reason, which is a
neat inversion of what a reader expects the trade in worked goods to look like.

And people. The East African coast supplied the Indian Ocean slave trade for
something over a thousand years, north to Arabia and the Gulf and east to India,
and this corpus's Abbasid record carries the Zanj rebellion in southern Iraq —
one of the largest slave revolts in recorded history, by East Africans shifted
from this coast to drain salt marshes.

None of it is in a coded field here, for the reason this record's third chapter
gives about the same problem: `revenue_basis` records where a polity's money came
from, and the traffic in people was a trade Kilwa taxed and supplied rather than
an instrument it ran.

## What Sofala was worth

Enough that when the Portuguese arrived they went for it directly.

Almeida sacked Kilwa in 1505 and abandoned the fort within eight years. In 1506
Portugal occupied Sofala and began buying at the source, and that — not the sack
— is what ended this record's position. A carrack coming round the Cape did not
need the monsoon calendar and did not need to stop.
