One traveller wrote down what Kilwa looked like at its height, and almost
everything he says is at odds with what has been dug up there.

Ibn Battuta came down the coast in 1331, in the reign of the sultan who built the
palace on the cliff. He gives the town four sentences.

## The four sentences

He reached it from Mombasa, an island abounding in bananas, lemons and citrons,
with no grain at all — everything brought in from elsewhere — and people he found
religious, chaste and honest.

Then:

> I set out, by sea, for the city of Kulwa, which is large, and consists of
> wooden houses. The greater part of the inhabitants are Zunuj of the sect of
> Shafi'i, of religious and peaceful habits. The king of this place, at the time
> I entered it, was Abu'l-Muzaffar Hasan, a person who had obtained great
> victories over the countries of the infidel Zunuj. He gave much away in alms.
> The greatest gift bestowed by the people of these countries is ivory, which is
> the elephant's tooth: they seldom give gold.

That is the whole of it in this translation. Then he went on to Zafar in Yemen.

## What is wrong with it

Two things, and both are worth taking seriously rather than explaining away.

*Wooden houses.* Kilwa in 1331 had a congregational mosque of coral stone with
domed and vaulted bays, and on the cliff above the harbour the hundred-room
complex of Husuni Kubwa — a palace and warehouse in one, with an octagonal
bathing pool cut into the coral — under construction in the reign of the very
sultan Ibn Battuta names. He stayed in the town. He does not mention any of it.

*They seldom give gold.* Kilwa's whole position rested on controlling the outlet
for the gold of the Zimbabwe plateau through Sofala. The sultan Ibn Battuta was
looking at was rich on it.

## How to read that

The likeliest answer to the first is that both are true. A stone town in the
Swahili world meant a stone core — mosque, palace, the houses of the ruling
families — surrounded by a much larger settlement of timber, mud and thatch. A
visitor describing the place as consisting of wooden houses is describing most of
the buildings, correctly, and passing over the few that were not.

The answer to the second is about what a guest sees. Ibn Battuta is reporting
what he was *given*, and what visiting scholars were given on that coast was
ivory. Gold moving from Sofala to the Red Sea in a sultan's account books is not
a thing a guest is handed at the door.

And Lee's translation is an abridgement made in 1829 from incomplete manuscripts.
Fuller versions of the *Rihla* give Kilwa more space and praise the town's
construction. The passage as it stands here is short partly because a translator
two centuries ago was working with less than the whole.

## Why it is worth having anyway

Because it is the only eyewitness description of the town at its height by
someone who was not Portuguese, and because of what it does record.

A sultan whose reputation with a visiting Moroccan jurist rested on holy war
against the non-Muslim mainland and on the scale of his almsgiving. A population
of African Muslims following the same legal school as the visitor, which is why
he could be lodged and fed and consider the place unremarkable. An island to the
north living entirely on imported grain. And a gift economy in which the
prestigious thing to hand a distinguished stranger was a tusk.

None of that is in the archaeology, and none of the archaeology is in Ibn
Battuta. The record of this town is two bodies of evidence that barely overlap,
and the honest position is to hold both.
