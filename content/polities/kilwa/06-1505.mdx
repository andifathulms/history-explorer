Francisco de Almeida arrived in July 1505 with a fleet and about five hundred
men. The sultan did not pay the tribute demanded; the Portuguese landed, took
the town in a morning against no organised resistance, sacked it, built a fort,
and installed a man of their own choosing.

They abandoned the fort within eight years. Kilwa never mattered again.

## A city with no army

There is no Kilwan army in the sources, and that is a finding rather than a
gap. No standing force, no military titles in the chronicle, no campaign
against another coastal town, and — until the Portuguese built one — no
fortification facing the sea. The excavated town has a palace, a mosque,
warehouses and stone houses, and nothing designed to be defended.

For five hundred years it did not need one. Kilwa's position rested on the
monsoon and on owning the boats that made the southern run, and neither of
those could be taken by an enemy who arrived by sea, because an enemy who
arrived by sea still needed somewhere to stop.

Then somebody arrived who did not.

## What the Portuguese actually took

Not the town, in the end — they left it. What they took was the thing the
town's whole position depended on.

In 1506 they occupied Sofala directly and began buying the gold at source. The
transshipment monopoly Kilwa had lived on simply stopped existing, because a
Portuguese carrack could round the Cape, run up to Sofala, load, and go home
without stopping anywhere, and the monsoon calendar that had made Kilwa
necessary did not apply to a ship coming the other way.

That is the mechanism, and it is worth stating precisely because the usual
version — Portuguese guns destroy Swahili civilisation — is both too dramatic
and not the point. The coast survived. Mombasa, Malindi, Pate and Lamu went on;
the Omanis took Mombasa back from the Portuguese in 1698, and Zanzibar became
the seat of an Omani sultanate in the nineteenth century.

What ended in 1505 and 1506 was a particular chokepoint on a particular route.
