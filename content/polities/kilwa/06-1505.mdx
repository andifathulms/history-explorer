Francisco de Almeida arrived in July 1505 with a fleet and about five hundred
men. The sultan did not pay the tribute demanded; the Portuguese landed, took the
town in a morning against no organised resistance, sacked it, built a fort, and
installed a man of their own choosing.

They abandoned the fort within eight years. Kilwa never mattered again.

## A city with no army

This record's `military_basis` field is null, and the data comment says it is a
finding rather than a gap.

There is no Kilwan army in the sources. No standing force, no military titles in
the chronicle, no campaign against another coastal town, and — until the
Portuguese built one — no fortification facing the sea. The excavated town has a
palace, a mosque, warehouses and stone houses, and nothing designed to be
defended.

For five hundred years it did not need one. Kilwa's position rested on the
monsoon and on owning the boats that made the southern run, and neither of those
could be taken by an enemy who arrived by sea, because an enemy who arrived by
sea still needed somewhere to stop.

Then somebody arrived who did not.

## What the Portuguese actually took

Not the town, in the end — they left it. What they took was the thing the town's
whole position depended on.

In 1506 they occupied Sofala directly and began buying the gold at source. The
transshipment monopoly this record's `revenue_basis` is coded on stopped
existing, because a Portuguese carrack could round the Cape, run up to Sofala,
load, and go home without stopping anywhere, and the monsoon calendar that had
made Kilwa necessary did not apply to a ship coming the other way.

That is the mechanism, and it is worth stating precisely because the usual
version — Portuguese guns destroy Swahili civilisation — is both too dramatic
and not the point. The coast survived. Mombasa, Malindi, Pate and Lamu went on;
this corpus's Ya'ariba record has the Omanis taking Mombasa from the Portuguese
in 1698 and its Al Bu Sa'id record has Zanzibar becoming the seat of an Omani
sultanate in the nineteenth century.

What ended in 1505 and 1506 was a particular chokepoint on a particular route.

## `by` is empty again

This record's `ended.by` field is empty because Portugal is not in this corpus.

It is the fourth African record here to end that way or close to it. The Aksumite
record ends in a `gradual absorption` with no conqueror at all. The Mali record
ends in a `fragmentation` with an empty `by`. This one and the Songhai record
both end against powers from outside the continent, and only Songhai's — the
Saadi sultanate in Morocco — has a page.

Four records out of seven in this region whose endings cannot be fully written in
the site's own fields, and the reason in three of the four cases is the same: the
power that ended them is not here.

That is a statement about a corpus rather than about Africa, and this region's
blurb says so. What Kilwa adds to it is a Portuguese-shaped absence, which is
going to keep appearing on this site — on the Ya'ariba record, on the Swahili
coast, at Hormuz, at Malacca, at Goa — until somebody writes the page.
