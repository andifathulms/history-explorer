The Indian Ocean has a schedule. The north-east monsoon blows from November to
March and carries ships from Arabia, Persia and India down the East African
coast; the south-west monsoon blows from April to September and carries them
home. A merchant who leaves Muscat in December must be back on the wind by
April, and the distance he can reach and return from inside one year is the
whole geography of this record.

That distance ends at about Kilwa.

South of Kilwa is Sofala, sixteen hundred kilometres further down, where the
gold from the Zimbabwe plateau reaches the sea. A ship from the north could not
get to Sofala and back on one year's winds. So the last leg was done by coastal
vessels, and whoever controlled the point where the two systems met controlled
the gold.

## What that produced

Not an empire. A customs house with a fleet of small boats and a sultan.

This record's `revenue_basis` reads `trade-toll, monopoly` and the second value
is the transshipment: Kilwa did not mine gold, did not produce ivory, and grew
almost nothing on a small coral island. It sold the right to pass, and it
enforced that right by owning the vessels that made the southern run.

This corpus tracks eight polities whose revenue field reads `trade-toll` and
nothing else — Palmyra, Characene, Derbend, the Nabataeans, the Zurayids, the
Rustamids, Srivijaya and Trebizond — and the Characene record's chapter on them
argues that eight instances arrived one page at a time, by a rule that never
asked the question, is a type rather than a coincidence.

This record is not one of the eight, and neither is the Ghana record added in
the same pass. Both carry a second value: Ghana a royalty on gold production,
Kilwa a monopoly on the vessels. But the mechanism here is the clearest version
of what the whole type rests on, because it is meteorological rather than
political. Derbend held a pass three kilometres wide. Kilwa held a date in the
calendar.

## What the town was

Coral rag and lime mortar, on an island a few hundred metres off the mainland: a
mosque, a hundred-room palace on the cliff above the harbour, a mint, and houses
of stone in a coast where most people lived in mud and thatch. The fourth
chapter is about the buildings and the money, and about what it meant that only
some families built in stone.

## Chinese porcelain in the walls

The excavated material is the argument, and the fifth chapter is about the ocean
it came across.

Chinese celadon and blue-and-white, Islamic glazed wares from the Gulf, glass
beads from India, and enough Song and Yuan porcelain that some of it was set
decoratively into the plaster of tombs and mosque niches, where it still is.
Kilwan coins go the other way, turning up in the Comoros, in Oman and at Great
Zimbabwe.

Zheng He's fleets reached this coast in the 1410s and 1420s — this corpus's Ming
record's first chapter has them bringing back tribute and a giraffe. A record on
this site about an island off Tanzania and a record about a Chinese dynasty
describe the same voyages from opposite ends, and neither has an edge to the
other because nothing passed between them but goods.
