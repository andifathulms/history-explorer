On the cliff above the harbour at Kilwa there is a building with something over a
hundred rooms. It is called Husuni Kubwa — the great fort, though it was not one
— and it was built in the early fourteenth century, in the reign of the sultan
Ibn Battuta came to see.

<Figure id="k-husuni" />

It is a palace and a warehouse in the same structure. On the seaward side, a
domed audience court, a set of residential rooms, and an octagonal bathing pool
cut into the coral. On the landward side, a large courtyard surrounded by
storerooms, entered through a gate wide enough for cargo.

It was never finished, and it seems to have been substantially abandoned within a
generation of being built.

## Coral

The material is the thing worth noticing, because it explains what a Swahili
stone town was and why so few people lived in one.

The walls are coral rag — reef limestone quarried in blocks — set in lime mortar
made by burning more coral. The decorative work is porites, a fine-grained living
coral cut from the reef while soft and carved before it hardens, which is how the
inscription bands and the panelled niches were made.

Building in stone on this coast meant destroying reef, burning it for lime, and
employing people to do both. It was expensive, and the archaeology across the
whole coast shows the same pattern: a minority of stone houses among a majority
of mud and thatch, and the stone-house families are the trading families.

Horton and Middleton's argument is that the stone town is not a colony or a
foreign quarter but the top of a local society — the same conclusion the Shirazi
pedigree points to from the other direction.

## The Great Mosque

The other building, and the older one.

The prayer hall was first built in the eleventh century and enlarged twice. The
southern extension of the fourteenth century roofed a hall of sixteen bays with
domes and barrel vaults on octagonal coral columns, and a good part of it is
standing.

<Figure id="k-mosque" />

It is the earliest surviving multi-domed mosque in sub-Saharan Africa and one of
the largest mosques anywhere on the East African coast, on an island a few
hundred metres wide.

## The money

Kilwa minted its own copper, in quantity, from about the eleventh century, and
the coins are the furthest-travelled thing the town ever made.

<Figure id="k-coin" />

They carry the sultan's name in Arabic on one face and a rhyming epithet on the
other — *al-Hasan ibn Sulaiman*, and then something like *trusts in the master of
bounties*. There is no denomination and no date. They are small change, struck
for local circulation rather than for the ocean trade, which ran on gold by
weight and on credit.

They turn up along the coast, in the Comoros, in Oman, and at Great Zimbabwe
four hundred miles inland, where a coin of a thirteenth-century Kilwan sultan
came out of the excavation and is one of the few hard links between the two
ends of the gold trade. Five more were found in 1945 by an airman on a beach in
the Wessel Islands off northern Australia. What that means is argued about, and
the honest answer is that nobody knows.

## And what none of it is

Kilwa founded no dynasty anywhere else, carried its religion nowhere, and passed
its script to nobody — it had borrowed the script itself. A town that built the
largest mosque on its coast, minted coin for five hundred years and put up a
hundred-room palace exported none of the things by which influence is usually
reckoned.

What it exported was gold it did not mine and cloth it did not weave. Venice is
the same shape at the other end of the world: enormously consequential,
impossible to score, because what it left behind was a way of doing business
rather than a religion or an alphabet.
