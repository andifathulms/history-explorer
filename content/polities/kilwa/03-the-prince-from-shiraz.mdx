The Kilwa Chronicle, written down in the sixteenth century, says the dynasty was
founded by Ali ibn al-Hasan, a son of the sultan of Shiraz, who left Persia with
his six brothers in seven ships and bought the island from its African ruler for
enough cloth to cover it.

Dynasties from Mogadishu to the Comoros told versions of the same story. It is
the Shirazi tradition, and it is almost certainly not what happened.

## What the ground says instead

Horton and Middleton — an archaeologist writing with an anthropologist — put
the case from the excavation. The Swahili coast's towns grow
out of local Iron Age settlement, in continuous occupation, with material culture
that develops in place. The language is Bantu with Arabic loanwords rather than
the reverse. The stone architecture, when it appears, is a coral-rag tradition
with no clear Persian antecedents. There is no colonising population in the
ground.

What there is, is a coastal African society that became Muslim, became wealthy on
the ocean trade, and acquired the vocabulary and the pedigree of the world it was
trading with.

## And the sultans went on claiming it

A false pedigree is still a pedigree, and it does the same work. The question
worth asking about the Shirazi story is not whether it happened but what it
was for.

What it bought was commercial standing. In an Indian Ocean where credit ran on
reputation and reputation ran on descent, being the son of a Persian prince was
worth having whether or not you were. A merchant in Aden deciding whether to
advance goods against a cargo that would not return for a year wanted to know
who he was dealing with, and "of the house of Shiraz" was an answer his own
world understood.

Ethiopia's kings did the same thing with Solomon and the queen of Sheba, and
for the same reason: a dynasty on the edge of a literate world acquires the
genealogy that world recognises. The Portuguese, when they turned up, took the
Shirazi story at face value, and de Barros printed it in 1552 from a chronicle
of the kings of the city that he does not say how he came by.

## Ibn Battuta

He came in 1331 and liked it, which for him was not guaranteed.

He calls Kilwa one of the most beautiful and well-constructed towns in the world,
entirely built of wood — meaning, probably, the ordinary houses rather than the
stone ones he would also have seen. He describes the sultan, al-Hasan ibn
Sulaiman, as devoted to raiding the mainland for slaves and to giving the
proceeds away in charity, and tells a story of him handing his own clothes to a
petitioner.

Twenty years later the same traveller was at Mali, complaining about unveiled
women and matrilineal inheritance. He is the best witness anybody has for
fourteenth-century Africa, and one of very few who saw both the Indian Ocean
coast and the Niger and can be read against himself.

## The raiding

Ibn Battuta's own sentence has it, and it should not be read past. The sultan he
admired for his charity was funding that charity with people taken off the
mainland.

The East African coast supplied the Indian Ocean slave trade north to Arabia
and the Gulf and east to India for something over a thousand years, before the
Atlantic trade began and long after it ended. Kilwa's part in it was as a market
and a tax point rather than as a raiding power, which is a distinction worth
making and not a defence. The gold and the porcelain and the hundred-room palace
are the visible half of what the ocean carried; this is the other half, and it
moved in the same boats.
