The Kilwa Chronicle, written down in the sixteenth century, says the dynasty was
founded by Ali ibn al-Hasan, a son of the sultan of Shiraz, who left Persia with
his six brothers in seven ships and bought the island from its African ruler for
enough cloth to cover it.

Dynasties from Mogadishu to the Comoros told versions of the same story. It is
the Shirazi tradition, and it is almost certainly not what happened.

## What the ground says instead

Horton and Middleton — this record's source, and an archaeologist writing with an
anthropologist — put the case from the excavation. The Swahili coast's towns grow
out of local Iron Age settlement, in continuous occupation, with material culture
that develops in place. The language is Bantu with Arabic loanwords rather than
the reverse. The stone architecture, when it appears, is a coral-rag tradition
with no clear Persian antecedents. There is no colonising population in the
ground.

What there is, is a coastal African society that became Muslim, became wealthy on
the ocean trade, and acquired the vocabulary and the pedigree of the world it was
trading with.

## And this record codes `descent` anyway

Its `legitimation` field reads `descent`, and the descent is from Shiraz.

Coding rule 1 counts a claim when it is publicly asserted as the ground of a
right to rule, and counts unrecognised claims exactly as it counts recognised
ones, on the stated reasoning that the count measures whose legitimacy was worth
claiming rather than who succeeded in claiming it. Nothing in the rule requires
the claim to be true.

This corpus made that argument explicitly one pass ago, on the Ethiopian record,
where the Solomonic dynasty's descent from the queen of Sheba is counted on the
Sabaean record's page even though there is no evidence outside the Hebrew Bible
that the queen existed. The reasoning is the same here and the shape is the same:
an African dynasty acquiring a foreign genealogy that made it legible to the
people it did business with.

What the Shirazi claim bought was commercial standing. In an Indian Ocean where
credit ran on reputation and reputation ran on descent, being the son of a
Persian prince was worth having whether or not you were.

## Ibn Battuta

He came in 1331 and liked it, which for him was not guaranteed.

He calls Kilwa one of the most beautiful and well-constructed towns in the world,
entirely built of wood — meaning, probably, the ordinary houses rather than the
stone ones he would also have seen. He describes the sultan, al-Hasan ibn
Sulaiman, as devoted to raiding the mainland for slaves and to giving the
proceeds away in charity, and tells a story of him handing his own clothes to a
petitioner.

This corpus's Mali record has the same traveller at another African court twenty
years later, complaining about unveiled women and matrilineal inheritance. He is
the single most useful witness this site has for fourteenth-century Africa, he
was there in both places, and the two records are the only two pages in the
corpus that can quote him from the inside.

## What is not in the fields

The slaving is. It is in the sentence above and in nothing this site codes,
because `revenue_basis` records where a polity's money came from and the coast's
export of people was a trade Kilwa taxed rather than a revenue instrument it
operated.

This corpus's Mali and Songhai records both carry `slave-soldier` and both say
plainly that the Niger empires ran on slavery. The East African coast ran on it
too, in the other direction — north and east, across the ocean, for a thousand
years before the Atlantic trade and continuing after it. The fields on this page
do not show it and this paragraph is where the site says so.
