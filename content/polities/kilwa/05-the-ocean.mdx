The Indian Ocean is the only ocean with a predictable wind that reverses, and
almost everything about the world this record belongs to follows from that.

The south-west monsoon blows from April to September and the north-east from
November to March, and both are reliable enough that a sailing schedule could be
built on them and was, for at least two thousand years. The Periplus of the
Erythraean Sea — the first-century Greek merchant's handbook this corpus's Aksum
record uses for the Red Sea end — already describes the pattern and the ports.

What that produced is a trading system that worked without anybody governing it.

## Nobody was in charge

This is the part that is hard to hold beside the rest of this corpus.

The Mediterranean of the Roman record is a single tax area with one currency and
no piracy because one state held the whole rim. The Indian Ocean of this record
was never held by anybody. Between the eighth century and the fifteenth it
carried a larger volume of long-distance trade than the Mediterranean, across
four thousand kilometres, among Arabs, Persians, Gujaratis, Tamils, Malays,
Chinese and Swahili, with no hegemon, no treaty system, no shared coinage and
almost no naval warfare.

What held it together was Islam, credit and the calendar. A merchant from Aden
and one from Kilwa shared a legal vocabulary, a set of commercial instruments and
a set of dates. A dispute could be taken to a qadi in either place under
recognisably the same law.

This corpus's Srivijaya record describes the eastern end of the same system and
carries `trade-toll` for the same reason this one nearly does.

## The porcelain

Chinese ceramics are the index, because they are datable and indestructible.

The Kilwa excavations produce Yue ware, then Longquan celadon, then Jingdezhen
blue-and-white, in a sequence that tracks Chinese production closely enough to
date deposits by. Some of it was set into the plaster of tombs and mosque niches
as decoration, where a good deal of it still is.

It reached the coast almost entirely through intermediaries. Chinese ships in the
western Indian Ocean are rare before the fifteenth century and the porcelain
came by way of the Gulf and India.

## And then, once, the fleet

Zheng He's expeditions reached this coast in the 1410s and 1420s — this corpus's
Ming record has them in its first chapter, bringing back tribute and a giraffe.

The giraffe came from Malindi, four hundred kilometres north of here. It was
presented at the Ming court as a *qilin*, an auspicious beast whose appearance
signals a virtuous reign, and there is a painting.

The fleets were enormous — hundreds of ships, tens of thousands of men — and they
came four or five times and stopped. The Ming record's own chapter is about the
decision to stop and what it cost. From this end the visits look like an
interruption rather than a turning point: no Chinese quarter was established, no
Chinese trading houses stayed, and the porcelain went on arriving by the same
route it always had.

Two records on this site describe the same voyages from opposite ends. One treats
them as the road not taken and the other barely registers them, and both are
accurate.

## What the coast was, in the end

Not an empire and not a colony. A chain of independent towns — Mogadishu,
Barawa, Lamu, Pate, Malindi, Mombasa, Kilwa, the Comoros, Sofala — sharing a
language, a religion, an architecture and a trade, quarrelling constantly, and
never once unified by any of them.

This record carries the largest and richest of them, because a site organised
around polities needs a polity. What it cannot carry is the coast, which is the
actual object, and the fifth chapter of the Nabataean record makes the same
complaint about the Phoenician cities from two and a half thousand years earlier.
