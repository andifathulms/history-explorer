In 916 a Baghdadi scholar sailed north from an island off the East African coast
to Oman in a ship owned by two brothers from Siraf on the Persian Gulf. He wrote
it up decades later in a book about everything, and the passage is the earliest
first-hand account of the sea route the Swahili towns were built on.

Al-Mas'udi is not describing a voyage of discovery. He is describing a commute.

## The run

The island he sailed from he calls Kanbalu, a well-cultivated place whose people
were Muslims but spoke the Zanj language — meaning a coast where the religion had
arrived and the language had not changed, which is what the Swahili towns would
be for the next thousand years.

He names his shipowners: Ahmad and Abd al-Samad, brothers of Abd al-Rahim ibn
Ja'far of Siraf. And then the detail that tells you what kind of route this was:

> I passed then the spot where, subsequently, the vessel of Ahmad and Abd
> es-Samad was wrecked, and where these two men perished with their whole crew.

He mentions another Sirafi shipowner, Muhammad ibn Zindibud, who also went down
in that sea with his whole crew. Two named owners drowned on the same run, and
the man recording it had sailed with one of them.

That is the Indian Ocean as it was actually worked: a predictable wind, a regular
schedule, and a death rate high enough that a traveller could list the owners he
had sailed with who did not come back.

## The song

The sailors were Arabs of the tribe of Azd, and Mas'udi records what they sang
while working the ship in heavy water — lifted up by the waves and then sinking
between them:

> O Berbera and Jofuni and thy enchanted waves. Jofuni, and Berbera, and their
> waves, as thou seest them.

It is a work chant naming two stretches of the African coast, and it is one of
the oldest fragments of anything anybody sang on that ocean.

## Where it reached

Mas'udi is precise about the far end. The sailors of Siraf and Oman went down
this sea as far as Kanbalu and the *sufala* — the low country — of the Zanj, at
the extremity of their country.

*Sufala* is a common noun in Arabic for a low-lying coast, and Mas'udi uses it
that way, of a whole stretch of shoreline. Some centuries later it had become a
proper name attached to one port, the outlet for the gold of the plateau inland,
and the town whose trade the Kilwa sultans took over and lived on.

The word arrives in the record as a description of a coastline that Omani and
Persian shipowners had been reaching for at least two hundred years before
anybody in Kilwa built anything in stone.

## What the passage is good for

Not for Kilwa, which did not exist yet, and not for the gold, which Mas'udi does
not mention.

It is good for establishing that the system was already old. The monsoon
schedule, the Sirafi and Omani shipowners, the Muslim island communities speaking
an African language, the run to the far southern coast and back — all of it is in
place in the tenth century, in the first-person account of a man who did the
voyage and could name the drowned.

Everything the Swahili towns later were is a local elaboration on a trade that
was already running past them.
