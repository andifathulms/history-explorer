The Portuguese held the fort at Kilwa a few years and then pulled it down —
de Barros dates the demolition to 1507 and other accounts put it later, and the
disagreement matters less than the reason, which Barbosa gives in the flattest
possible terms.

> Afterwards he ordered that it should be pulled down, as its maintenance was
> of no value nor profit to him, and it was destroyed by Antonio de Saldanha.

Dames adds the judgement that the Portuguese occupation was not a success, and
that the formerly great trade was ruined by their efforts to regulate it.

Both halves of that are the afterlife. Kilwa's position was destroyed, and the
people who destroyed it got nothing they wanted out of the ruins.

## What Mombasa got

The same treatment, a few weeks apart, and Barbosa's account of it is worth
setting down because it is the fullest inventory of what a Swahili town
contained.

Mombasa's king also refused to obey, "and through this arrogance he lost it".
The Portuguese took it by force, killed many of his people, took many men and
women captive, and left the town ruined and plundered and burnt. The booty was
gold and silver — bangles, bracelets, earrings, gold beads — and a great store
of copper, with other rich wares in quantity.

Barbosa records all of it in the register of a man noting a satisfactory
outcome. He is not describing a catastrophe; he is describing a return.

## The remedy at Sofala

And then, a few pages earlier in the same book, there is a paragraph that shows
what the coast did about it, and it is the most interesting thing in Barbosa.

At Sofala, he says, they have lately begun to grow cotton in quantity and to
weave it, and they make much white cloth from it. They cannot dye it — they
have not got the dyes — so they buy Gujarati cloth, blue or otherwise coloured,
*unravel it*, and weave the coloured thread up again with their own white.

> so that it becomes a new thing. With this thread and their own white they
> make much coloured cloth, and from it they gain much gold.

Then Barbosa says why:

> This they did as a remedy after they had perceived that our people were
> taking from them the trade of the zambucos, and that they can only obtain
> goods through the hands of the factors whom the King our Lord has there in
> his factories and forts.

An import monopoly was imposed. Within a few years the people it was imposed on
had started manufacturing the import, by taking the monopolist's own product to
pieces and rebuilding it into something the interior would pay gold for.

That is not the response of a destroyed civilisation. It is the response of a
commercial society doing what commercial societies do when a supplier turns
predatory, and it happened inside a decade.

## What actually ended

Not the coast. Mombasa, Malindi, Pate and Lamu went on; the Omanis took Mombasa
back in 1698 and ran the coast from Zanzibar in the nineteenth century; the
language, the law and the architecture continued, and the towns are still
there.

Not the trade. Gold went on leaving Sofala and cloth went on arriving; only the
hands changed, and then changed again.

What ended was one arrangement: the monsoon chokepoint, and a town that had
lived on it for five hundred years without ever needing a wall facing the sea.

## The last object

The most widely travelled thing Kilwa ever made was its small change.

The copper coins — the sultan's name on one face, a rhyming epithet on the
other, no denomination, no date — turn up along the coast, in the Comoros, in
Oman, at Great Zimbabwe three thousand kilometres inland, and in 1945 on a
beach in the Wessel Islands off northern Australia, where an airman found five
of them. What that last find means is argued about, and the honest answer is
that nobody knows.

A town that exported no religion, no script and no dynasty exported its coppers
further than any of them would have gone, and mostly by accident, in other
people's pockets.
