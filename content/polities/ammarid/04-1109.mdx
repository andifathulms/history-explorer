Raymond of Saint-Gilles arrived in 1102 and did not try to storm Tripoli. He
built a castle on the hill above it — Mount Pilgrim, which Arabic writers call
Qal'at Sanjil after him and which is still standing over the modern city — and
occupied the plain. The city kept its walls, its harbour and its fleet, and
lost everything that made those worth having. It was a blockade conducted at
the pace of a lease.

Raymond died in 1105 with the siege unfinished. It went on.

By 1108 the last Ammarid had a city that could not feed itself and a bought army
he could no longer pay, and the appeals for relief had produced ceremonial robes
and no soldiers. Tripoli passed to a Fatimid governor who arrived by sea with
grain while its own lord was away looking for help.

The Fatimids held it for a year. In July 1109 a Genoese fleet and Baldwin I of
Jerusalem finished it.

What replaced the emirate was the County of Tripoli, and that is the sentence
worth slowing down on. The Crusader states are usually written about as
arrivals. They were also replacements, and the things they replaced were
working states with judges, libraries, harbour dues and a foreign policy.

Fakhr al-Mulk never got back. He was given Jabala on the coast for a time, lost
it, and ended his life in the Seljuk east as a pensioner of the sultan he had
gone to ask for help.
