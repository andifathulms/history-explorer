Raymond of Saint-Gilles arrived in 1102 and did not try to storm Tripoli. He
built a castle on the hill above it — Mount Pilgrim, which Arabic writers call
Qal'at Sanjil after him and which is still standing over the modern city — and
occupied the plain. The city kept its walls, its harbour and its fleet, and lost
everything that made those worth having. It was a blockade conducted at the pace
of a lease.

Raymond died in 1105 with the siege unfinished. It went on.

By 1108 the last Ammarid, Fakhr al-Mulk, had a city that could not feed itself
and a bought army he could no longer pay, and he did the thing that ends this
record: he left. He travelled to Baghdad to petition the Seljuk sultan and the
Abbasid caliph in person for an army — a journey of a thousand kilometres from a
city under siege — and was received with every honour and given nothing. While
he was away a Fatimid governor arrived by sea, took over the defence, and held
Tripoli until July 1109, when a Genoese fleet and Baldwin I of Jerusalem
finished it.

The `ended` field on this page names the County of Tripoli, and the edge runs to
that record. It is the second predecessor edge this corpus carries into a
Frankish state — the Fatimids into Jerusalem in 1099, the Banu Ammar into
Tripoli in 1109 — and both are worth having for the same reason. The Crusader
states are usually written about as arrivals. They were also replacements, and
the things they replaced were functioning polities with judges, libraries,
harbour dues and foreign policies, one of which is on this page.

Fakhr al-Mulk never got back. He was given Jabala on the coast for a time, lost
it, and ended his life in the Seljuk east as a pensioner of the sultan he had
gone to ask for help.
