Ibn al-Qalanisi was writing in Damascus while this was happening, and his
account of the last two years of Tripoli is a record of help that was real, was
promised, was dispatched, and arrived at the wrong place or the wrong time.

By 1108 the situation in the city was, in his words, critical: the siege had
gone on too long, the watch for reinforcements had gone on too long, and the
sending of assistance had been continually put off. Fakhr al-Mulk's own
correspondence had already failed to move Damascus, so he went to Baghdad
himself and put the case to the sultan directly.

## The relief that went to Mosul instead

He was received with honour, and the sultan agreed to the request in full: a
number of the great amirs were ordered to go back with him and relieve the
city.

The instruction that decided the whole thing came inside the same order. The
army detailed to go with him was to make a brief halt on the way, at Mosul,
and take the city out of the hands of the amir holding it.

Nobody came to Tripoli. Fakhr al-Mulk was kept at the sultan's court until he
lost patience, and at the end of August he went to Damascus instead. He had
been away four months and the relief he had been granted had been folded into
somebody else's quarrel over Mosul.

## The fleet

While he was away a Fatimid governor arrived by sea and took over the defence.
Egypt then did what Egypt was capable of doing when it chose to.

The fleet it sent was, Qalanisi says, the largest the Egyptians had ever
dispatched, in numbers of men, of vessels, of equipment and of provisions:
enough food, men and money to hold Tripoli for a year, and to reinforce the
other coastal towns as well.

It reached Tyre on the eighth day after Tripoli fell.

The ships stayed on the coast a while and distributed the supplies among the
other ports. The people of Tyre, Sidon and Beirut begged the fleet to remain,
explaining their condition and their inability to fight the Franks on their
own, and it could not stay; when the wind was right it sailed for Egypt.

## What was left to be

Tripoli fell in July 1109 after seven years of blockade. Within the year the
Franks had moved on to the next place and were negotiating with Damascus over
who got what share of the Biqa' harvest.

Fakhr al-Mulk never came back. He was given Jabala on the coast for a time,
lost that too, and ended as a pensioner in the Seljuk east, of the court he had
travelled a thousand kilometres to ask for an army.

The judges of Tripoli had governed for thirty-nine years on money, diplomacy
and the prestige of an office, without a tribe or an army of their own. All
three worked until the moment when what was needed was soldiers standing in a
particular place on a particular day, and then the whole structure came down in
the space of eight days' sailing.
