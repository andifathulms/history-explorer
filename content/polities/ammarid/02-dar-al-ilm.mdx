The thing the Banu Ammar are remembered for is a library.

The Dar al-Ilm at Tripoli was founded by the family in the late eleventh century
on the Fatimid model — an endowed institution with copyists on the payroll, open
to scholars, collecting across the disciplines rather than only in law and
tradition. The figures the later sources give for the number of volumes are
enormous and vary wildly between accounts, and this record prints none of them.
Hard rule 2 governs the case exactly: the numbers in circulation are not
measurements that disagree, they are a topos about a lost library, and averaging
or picking one would manufacture a fact.

What is not in doubt is that it existed, that it was among the notable
collections of the Islamic Mediterranean, and that it did not survive 1109.
Whether the Franks burned it deliberately, whether it was destroyed in the sack,
or whether it had already been dispersed during the seven-year siege is
contested in both the medieval and the modern literature, and this page does not
adjudicate it.

The library is on this page for a reason beyond its own interest. This corpus
codes an `influence` block for every polity — descendant scripts, religions
carried, successor claims — and all three counts on this record are null. By
those measures the Banu Ammar registered nothing. They founded no religion,
adapted no script, and no later state claimed to continue them.

That is a true description of what the coding rules can see and a poor
description of the polity. A family of judges endowed a research library in a
port city and it was the most consequential thing they did. The rulebook does
not have a count for it, this site will not invent one to be generous, and the
gap renders. The chapter is where the site says what the number cannot.
