title: The judges take the city

In the middle of the eleventh century the Fatimid state came apart. A famine
and a currency collapse in Egypt under al-Mustansir, a Turkish and Berber
soldiery fighting each other in Cairo, and a general withdrawal of Fatimid
authority from Syria left the coastal cities to whoever was standing in them.

In Tripoli that was the qadi. The Banu Ammar were the city's judges, appointed
under Fatimid authority to a Shi'i city they belonged to, and around 1070 they
stopped remitting the revenue and began governing on their own account. There
was no rising, no declaration and no battle. The office simply expanded until
it was the state.

It is worth setting beside the other secessions of the period, because it does
not look like any of them. The Tulunids and the Ikhshidids were military
governors who kept their armies and stopped sending money. The Samanids were
appointed governors who inherited an empire. The Banu Ammar were civilians —
lawyers holding a religious office — and they went on naming the Fatimid caliph
in the Friday prayer for decades after they had stopped obeying him, because
the authority they had was the authority he had given them.

What they had instead of a tribe was money. Tripoli was the wealthiest port on
that coast: sugar and silk from the plain behind it, and the customs of a
first-rank Mediterranean harbour. So they hired their soldiers, which is what a
state with a revenue and no manpower does. It worked for forty years and then
it was the thing that killed them, because a bought army cannot be paid by a
city that has lost its countryside.
