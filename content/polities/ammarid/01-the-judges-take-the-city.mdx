title: The judges take the city

In the middle of the eleventh century the Fatimid state came apart. A famine and
a currency collapse in Egypt under al-Mustansir, a Turkish and Berber soldiery
fighting each other in Cairo, and a general withdrawal of Fatimid authority from
Syria left the coastal cities to whoever was standing in them.

In Tripoli that was the qadi. The Banu Ammar were the city's judges, appointed
under Fatimid authority to a Shi'i city they belonged to, and around 1070 they
stopped remitting the revenue and began governing on their own account. There
was no rising, no declaration and no battle. The office simply expanded until it
was the state.

This is worth setting beside the other secessions this corpus records, because
it does not look like any of them. The Tulunids and the Ikhshidids were military
governors who kept their armies and stopped sending money. The Samanids were
appointed governors who inherited an empire. The Banu Ammar were **civilians** —
lawyers holding a religious office — and this site's `legitimation` coding for
the page reads `caliphal-investiture` on that basis, since they went on naming
the Fatimid caliph in the khutba for decades after they had ceased to obey him.

What they had instead of a tribe was money. Tripoli was the wealthiest port on
that coast: sugar and silk from the plain behind it, and the customs of a
first-rank Mediterranean harbour. The `military_basis` coding is `mercenary`,
which in this corpus usually signals a state with a revenue and no manpower, and
that is exactly what this was. It worked for forty years and then it was the
thing that killed them, because a bought army cannot be paid by a city that has
lost its countryside.
