Tripoli was besieged for seven years. Its lord spent most of that time writing
to other Muslim rulers asking for an army, and Ibn al-Qalanisi — who was in
Damascus, receiving some of those letters at second hand — records what came of
it.

## The sally that worked

It did not begin badly. In September 1104 Fakhr al-Mulk ibn Ammar went out of
the city with his own *askar* and the town levies and attacked the castle
Saint-Gilles had built facing Tripoli to blockade it.

The garrison was taken by surprise and killed. The place was plundered, set on
fire and reduced to ruins, and Fakhr al-Mulk came back the same day with a great
quantity of weapons, money, rich stuffs and silver.

A besieged city had destroyed its besiegers' fortress and looted it. On the
strength of that alone Tripoli could reasonably expect to hold — if anyone came.

## The letters

Nobody came, and the chronicle's account of why is a study in how a frontier
gets abandoned by people who all sincerely mean to help.

Letters and envoys arrived constantly at Damascus from Fakhr al-Mulk, asking for
aid against the Franks besieging his city, urging that every available soldier be
sent to relieve his distress. The atabeg Zahir al-Din was seized by an illness at
exactly that moment, grew worse, and became consumed with worry about what would
happen to his own family, his men and his subjects if he died.

There were two amirs, Sukman ibn Ortuq and Jikirmish of Mosul, who had made a
formal compact with each other to prosecute the holy war and aid the Muslims.
Fakhr al-Mulk's letters reached them too, urging them to hurry. Zahir al-Din,
meanwhile, was inviting Sukman to Damascus for a different reason — to nominate
him as successor and hand him the defence of the city.

Everyone was corresponding. Everyone had prior business. The compact to fight the
Franks existed, in writing, between men who were at that moment arranging the
succession of a third city three hundred kilometres inland.

## Going in person

By the spring of 1108 the situation had become critical — the siege, its length,
the long watch for reinforcements, and what the chronicle calls the continued
procrastination in sending assistance.

So Fakhr al-Mulk left. He went to Baghdad himself, to put the case to the caliph
and the sultan in person.

He was received. Magnificent robes were bestowed on him by the caliph and by the
sultan both. Then he came back to Damascus in early August 1108, stayed a few
days, and set out with a troop of Damascene cavalry attached to his own — not to
Tripoli, but to the port of Jabala, which gave him its allegiance.

He had gone to the centre of the Islamic world to ask for an army and had been
given clothes.

## What happened at home

While he was away, the people of Tripoli sent to al-Afdal in Egypt and asked the
Fatimids for a governor — with ships, grain and provisions.

The governor came. Sharaf al-Dawla arrived by sea with the grain, established
himself in the town, and then arrested every partisan and officer Fakhr al-Mulk
had left behind. Their stores, his equipage and his furniture were put on ships
and sent to Egypt.

The city did not fall to the Franks that year. It changed sides, to a different
Muslim power, because that power sent food and the others sent letters.

Fakhr al-Mulk ibn Ammar was still alive, still lord of somewhere — Jabala, for a
while — and no longer lord of Tripoli, which he had left in order to save it.
