Al-Mundhir III held al-Hira for fifty years and spent most of them raiding Roman
Syria. Procopius, who loathed him, calls him the most difficult and dangerous
enemy the Romans had, says he was king for fifty years, and complains that no
Roman commander ever managed to meet him in the field — he arrived, took
captives and livestock, and was gone before a response could be organised.

<Figure id="sari-plate" />

The Roman answer was structural rather than military. In 529 Justinian put every
Arab ally of Rome under one man, al-Harith ibn Jabala, and gave him patrician
rank, specifically so that there would be a single command capable of doing to
al-Mundhir what al-Mundhir was doing to Syria. Al-Mundhir is the reason that
office exists.

## 531, and what a client was for

At Callinicum on the Euphrates in 531, al-Mundhir's cavalry broke the Arab
allies on the Roman right and the Persian army beat Belisarius.

<Figure id="euphrates" />

That is the arrangement working, on both sides at once. Two empires that could
not fight in the steppe fielded forces that could, forty kilometres apart, and
the outcome of a set-piece battle between two regular armies turned on which
empire's Arab client performed better. Neither of these dynasties is
intelligible on its own, and the interesting fact about each of them is usually
what the other was doing.

## 528 and 554

Two dates bracket al-Mundhir's reign.

In 525 he lost al-Hira to al-Harith ibn Amr, a chief out of central Arabia
backed by a South Arabian kingdom — the only occasion when a power from the
Arabian interior held the
Euphrates frontier. He took the city back in 528, defeated al-Harith and killed
him with much of his family, and the Persians supported the recovery throughout.
From Ctesiphon it reads as a client being restored rather than a war being won,
which is the correct reading: it is a change in the Nasrid position, not in the
Persian one.

<Figure id="khosrow-drachm" />

In 554 al-Harith ibn Jabala, the Jafnid, killed him.

## The same day, two conclusions

The Jafnid record carries 554 as its second turning point and draws a conclusion
from it about patrons: a client that has finished its task becomes a different
proposition to the empire paying for it, and the following thirty years of
Jafnid history are about what Rome did with one.

From this side the conclusion is about the client. Al-Mundhir's fifty years are
the dynasty's peak and they are also the only period in which the Nasrids were
independently formidable. His successors are competent and unremarkable. Within
fifty years of his death both dynasties had been dismantled by the empires that
made them, and there is no evidence either empire thought the two decisions had
anything to do with each other.
