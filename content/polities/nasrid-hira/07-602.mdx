Khusrau II had al-Nu'man III seized, imprisoned and killed — the accounts differ
on whether by execution or by plague, in the way accounts do when the answer is
inconvenient — and replaced the client kingship with a Persian governor. The
Nasrid dynasty ended after three centuries.

<Figure id="khosrow-relief" />

The immediate reasons given are personal and probably real: a refusal of a
marriage demand, a quarrel over tribute, a king who had grown too independent
and had converted to a church with connections across the frontier. The
structural reason is the one the Jafnid record gives for 582 and it is the same
reason. A client that has been useful for a long time looks, to the empire
paying for it, like a standing concession rather than a service.

## Dhu Qar

Two years later an Arab coalition met a Persian army at a place called Dhu Qar
and beat it.

The later Arabic tradition made a great deal of this — the first time the Arabs
defeated the Persians, and a portent — and modern accounts are more restrained
about its scale. What is not in dispute is the sequence. Iran removed the
dynasty that had held its desert frontier for three hundred years, put a
governor in its place, and inside twenty-four months discovered that a governor
could not do what the dynasty had done.

Thirty years after that, Arab armies took Iraq.

<Figure id="khosrow-bishapur" />

It does not follow that abolishing the Nasrids caused the conquest, and that
claim is not being made here. What can be said is narrower and still substantial:
both empires dismantled their own Arab frontier arrangements within twenty years
of each other, each for reasons that were sound in its own terms, and neither
had anything in place when the frontier became the decisive one. A third empire,
Himyar, had done the same thing to its own Arabian clients in the 530s. Three
empires, three sound decisions, one outcome.

## Two endings for one decision

The two dynasties ended differently on paper and almost identically in fact.

Rome arrested its supreme phylarch in 582 and divided his following among
fifteen chiefs, which is fragmentation carried out deliberately by the polity's
own patron. Iran killed its king in 602 and put an official in his place, which
is a dynasty replaced by direct administration — not by another dynasty, which
is what the vocabulary word implies, and there is no better value on the list.

Two closed-vocabulary endings, chosen honestly, that make an identical decision
by two empires look like two different kinds of event when they were one
decision taken twice.

## Not a succession

Neither of these dynasties succeeded the other. They were exact contemporaries,
exact opposites, and killed each other's kings. What connects them is not
inheritance but symmetry: two Arab houses, on two sides of one frontier, doing
the same job for two empires that each concluded, twenty years apart, that the
job was no longer worth paying for.
