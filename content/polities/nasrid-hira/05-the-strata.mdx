In 539 Khusrau I wanted a war with Rome and did not want to be the one who
broke the treaty. What he did about it is the clearest illustration anywhere of
what the Arab client kingdoms were actually for, and of the position it left
them in.

Procopius reports the instruction without embarrassment. Khusrau conferred with
al-Mundhir on the matter *and commanded him to provide causes for war*.

So al-Mundhir brought a complaint against Arethas, the Roman client on the other
side, alleging that Arethas was doing him violence over boundary lines. He
attacked him in time of peace, and began raiding Roman territory on that
pretext.

## The legal argument

And then he made an argument which Procopius, who has no reason to help him,
concedes is correct.

Al-Mundhir declared that he was not breaking the treaty between the Persians
and the Romans, because neither party had included him in it.

*And this was true.* No mention of Saracens was ever made in the treaties
between the two empires, on the ground that they were covered by the names of
Persians and Romans.

That is the whole position of these dynasties stated in two sentences. They were
sovereign enough to command armies and be called kings, and invisible enough not
to appear in the documents. Which meant that anything they did was deniable by
the empire that had told them to do it, and that nothing done to them was a
breach of anything.

## The land

The territory in dispute is called Strata and lies south of Palmyra. Procopius
describes it: nowhere watered, entirely without trees or crops, burnt dry by the
sun, and used for pasturing a few flocks.

Justinian referred the arbitration to two men. Summus, who had commanded the
troops in Palestine, insisted that Rome should not give the country up.
Strategius, the patrician in charge of the imperial treasures, begged the
emperor not to do the Persians the favour of handing them a pretext for the war
they already wanted — for the sake of a small piece of ground of absolutely no
account, unproductive and unfit for crops.

The emperor took the matter under consideration, and a long time was spent on
the settlement of the question.

## What the quarrel was worth

Khusrau then produced a counter-charge: that Justinian had broken the treaty by
trying to detach al-Mundhir from Persia — that Summus had offered him great sums
of money to come over, and that there were letters. Procopius records the
accusation and adds, characteristically, that whether the man was telling the
truth he cannot say.

Both empires, in other words, spent the run-up to a major war arguing about
which of them had tampered with an Arab king, over a strip of waterless pasture
neither of them wanted.

The Nasrids were at the height of their usefulness in that moment and it is also
the moment their limit is visible. A power whose value is that it can be used
without consequence is a power that will one day be discarded without
consequence too, and sixty years later Khusrau II did exactly that.
