The best measure of what al-Mundhir of al-Hira achieved is the office the Roman
empire had to invent in order to deal with him, and the fact that inventing it
did not work.

Procopius, secretary to Belisarius and present on the eastern frontier, sets out
the problem plainly. This man, he writes, proved himself the most difficult and
dangerous enemy of all to the Romans. And he explains exactly why.

Al-Mundhir held the position of king and *ruled alone over all the Saracens in
Persia*. He could therefore make his inroad with the whole army, wherever he
wished, in Roman territory. And nothing on the Roman side could match that: not
a *dux*, the commander of the troops of a district, and not any of the phylarchs
— the Arab leaders allied to Rome — because the Roman forces were distributed
among the districts and the garrisons were not, individually, a match for him.

## The Roman answer

So the emperor Justinian did something new. He put Arethas son of Gabalas, who
ruled the Arabs of Roman Arabia, in command of as many clans as possible, and
bestowed on him the dignity of king — *a thing which among the Romans had never
before been done*.

An empire that had spent six centuries refusing to let allied Arab leaders hold
a royal title conceded one, in order to concentrate enough Arab force under a
single command to face the man on the other side.

## And it made no difference

Procopius's verdict on the experiment is one sentence long and quite brutal.

Al-Mundhir went on injuring the Romans just as much as before, if not more,
since Arethas was either extremely unfortunate in every raid and every
engagement, or else turned traitor as fast as he could — *for as yet we know
nothing certain about him*.

The result, he says, was that al-Mundhir had nobody to stand against him and
plundered the whole East for an exceedingly long time, because he lived to a
very advanced age.

## What that describes

Read it as a description of the polity rather than of the man and it is exact.

The Nasrid position had one structural advantage over everything Rome could put
opposite it: unity of command. The Roman east was administered in districts,
each with its own commander and its own troops, and its Arab allies were several
groupings with several leaders. Al-Hira had a king who commanded all the Arabs
of the Persian side and could move the whole of that force at once, at a time of
his own choosing, against a frontier that had to be strong everywhere.

That is not a raiding advantage. It is an institutional one, and it is why a
city on the edge of the Euphrates marshes could be a strategic problem for the
Roman empire for fifty years.
