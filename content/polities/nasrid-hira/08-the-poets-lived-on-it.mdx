Al-Hira was destroyed as a political entity in about 602, and what it had paid
for outlived it by a very long way.

The court of al-Nu'man, the last king of al-Hira, was — in the summary of the
scholar who put the pre-Islamic poetry into English — the resort of the most
famous poets of the day, who lived luxuriously on the bounty their poems brought
them. The same was true at Ghassan on the Roman side, with a smaller circle.
And inside the peninsula the great chiefs competed with one another over the
magnificence of the presents they gave to whoever praised them in verse.

That is a description of an economy, and the Nasrids were at the top of it.

## What patronage did to the poetry

The verdict on it is not flattering, and it comes from the same page.

The earliest poets, on this account, practised the art *neither for fear nor
favour*. By the end of the pre-Islamic period it had become customary to reward
panegyric lavishly, and the poets had become professionals living on it — which
led, the argument runs, straight to the extravagances of the following
centuries.

So al-Hira's contribution to Arabic literature was money, and money changed what
the literature was for. A tradition that had produced boasting, lament and the
description of a journey acquired a paying market for praise, and the men who
supplied it went where the payment was.

## Why any of it survives

The transmission is the point, and it explains why a small Christian Arab
kingdom on the Euphrates matters more to Arabic than to the history of Iraq.

Pre-Islamic verse was not written down at the time. It was carried by
professional reciters attached to individual poets and passed on by memory, and
it reached paper in eighth-century Iraq when scholars went looking for it. What
they found was disproportionately the poetry of the courts — because court poems
had been paid for, performed publicly, and preserved by people whose living
depended on knowing them.

Al-Hira was one of the two or three places where that was true. Its kings bought
poems for a century and a half, and the language of the poems is the language
that became the standard of Arabic eloquence before the Qur'an, against which
the Qur'an was first heard.

## And the city

Al-Hira itself did not last. Within a generation of the Muslim conquest the
population had drifted the few miles to the new garrison town of Kufa, which
became one of the two great centres of Arabic philology — the place, along with
Basra, where the eighth-century scholars did the work of collecting, editing and
arguing about the old poetry.

So the men who assembled the pre-Islamic canon were working a short walk from
the ruin of the court that had paid for a good deal of it. That is not a
coincidence either. Scholarship goes where the material is, and the material was
in the memories of people whose grandfathers had recited for kings.
