Arabic tradition remembered almost nothing about the two centuries
between the dynasty's founder, Amr ibn Adi, and the reign of Nu'man I,
called al-A'war - the One-Eyed - in the first quarter of the fifth
century. What survived instead was legend, attached to a single
building and a single moral.

<Figure id="bahram-azada" />

## A castle built to turn with the sun

The Sasanian king Yazdegerd I asked Nu'man to build a residence for
his own son, Prince Bahram Gor, away from the court intrigues of
Ctesiphon - a request that produced Khawarnaq, a castle near al-Hira
famous enough to outlive the dynasty that built it. When the building
was finished, Nu'man had its architect, a Byzantine subject named
Sinimmar, thrown headlong from its own battlements - either because
Sinimmar boasted he could have built something still more wonderful,
one that would turn to follow the sun, or because he alone knew which
single stone held the whole structure together and could bring it
down if removed. "The reward of Sinimmar" became an Arabic proverb for
gratitude repaid with betrayal, still current centuries after anyone
remembered which king had ordered it.

<Figure id="bahram-hunt" />

## A vizier's answer that ended a reign

The best-known story about Nu'man is not the murder but what followed
it. Sitting with his vizier one spring day, admiring Khawarnaq's view
over the Euphrates and the irrigated fen-country to the west, Nu'man
asked whether his companion had ever seen anything to equal it. "Not
if it would last," the vizier answered. Nu'man asked what could last;
the vizier answered, "That which is with God in Heaven." Nu'man, the
story goes, resolved on the spot to give up his kingdom - he put on
sackcloth that same night, slipped away from his own court unnoticed,
and lived out his life as a wandering ascetic. The tale almost
certainly grew out of a poem by Adi ibn Zayd rather than recording an
actual abdication, but it fixed Nu'man permanently in later Arabic
memory as the king who built the most famous palace in pre-Islamic
Arabia and then walked away from it.

## A son who made and unmade a Sasanian king

Nu'man's own son Mundhir I proved a far more conventional ruler. When
Yazdegerd I died and the Persian priesthood tried to block Bahram
Gor's succession, Mundhir intervened with enough force to secure the
throne for his father's old ward regardless of clerical objections -
a Nasrid king making a Sasanian one. The loyalty was tested almost
immediately: war broke out between Persia and Rome soon afterward, and
Mundhir, fighting as Persia's vassal, was defeated by Roman forces
with heavy losses in 421. Arabic tradition records little else of
substance about the reigns between Mundhir I and Mundhir III a
century later - a run of obscure kings ruling a dynasty whose court
had already, in two generations, produced both a palace legendary
enough to give the language a proverb and a king remembered as
readily for renouncing his throne as for building one.
