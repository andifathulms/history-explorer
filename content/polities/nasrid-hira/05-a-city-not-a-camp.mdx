The Jafnids had no capital. Their court moved; Jabiya in the Golan is the place
the sources keep returning to, and calling it a capital would import a fixed
seat they are not recorded as having had.

Al-Hira was a city. It had churches, monasteries, a bishop from the fifth
century, a palace, markets, a resident Persian garrison, a school of poets, and
a permanent population of Arabs, Persians and Aramaeans who were mostly
Christian. Toral-Niehoff's book is about the city rather than the dynasty, and
that is the right way round: the Nasrids are the least interesting thing that
happened at al-Hira.

## The Church of the East

The tradition al-Hira carried outward was the Church of the East — the
Christianity the Sasanian empire tolerated precisely because Rome persecuted it.

It is worth being exact about what is and is not being claimed. The Nasrid
kings were not Christians for most
of the period; they were pagans presiding over a substantially Christian
population, and Procopius has al-Mundhir sacrificing a captured Roman prince's
sons to al-Uzza. What al-Hira did was serve as the northern base from which the
Church of the East reached the tribes of eastern and central Arabia. Its bishops
appear in the synodal lists. Its monasteries ringed the desert edge. When
Christianity turns up among the Arabs of the Gulf coast and the interior in the
sixth century, the channel is usually this city.

That is a count about effect rather than about faith, and the ruler being a
pagan while it happened is a feature of the rule rather than a problem for it.
The Jafnid record's count is made under the same test and is more conventional,
the Jafnids having actively obtained bishops for a confession that had none.

## The poets

The other thing al-Hira produced cannot be counted at all.

The Nasrid court was a centre of pre-Islamic Arabic poetry, and the poetry is
not a decoration on the history — it is a substantial part of the surviving
evidence for the whole period, and it is the foundation of the later Arabic
literary canon. The Mu'allaqat tradition runs through this court. Al-Nabigha
al-Dhubyani, Adi ibn Zayd and Tarafa are associated with it, the last of them in
a story about a king having him killed for a satire.

A court that a Persian shah maintained as a military convenience on his desert
edge turned out to be one of the two or three places where the classical Arabic
literary tradition was assembled. There is no way of registering that alongside
territory and revenue, and no reason to try: it is simply the most consequential
thing the place did.

## Kufa

The last thing worth saying about the city is what happened to it after the
dynasty was gone.

Around 638 the Arab armies founded a garrison town called Kufa a few kilometres
away, and al-Hira emptied into it over the following century. Toral-Niehoff's
argument is that this was continuity rather than replacement: the Arab urban
culture assembled at al-Hira — the Christianity, the poetry, the script, the
mixed population, the habits of a court — moved down the road and became the
culture of the city that produced Kufic script and a great deal of early Islamic
scholarship.

It is tempting to call Kufa a continuation of al-Hira and the temptation should
be resisted. One place emptied into another over several decades while both
existed; there was no interruption and no succession. What moved was a
population and the habits it had — the Christianity, the poetry, the script, the
Arabic-writing administrative class — into a new town founded for a different
purpose by a different power.
