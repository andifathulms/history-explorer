This corpus's Jafnid record opens by saying that neither Rome nor Iran could
police the steppe between them with regular troops, and that both paid Arab
dynasties to do it. It then names the Nasrids at al-Hira in its second sentence
and cannot link them, because they had no page.

This is that page.

The arrangement was symmetrical and the two empires arrived at it independently.
Iran got there first: the Nasrids are on the lower Euphrates from around 300,
two centuries before Rome consolidated its own Arab allies under a single
phylarch in 529. What each empire bought was cavalry that could operate where
regular armies could not, intelligence about the interior, and — the part that
mattered most — a counterpart capable of answering a raid with a raid, because
nothing else answers one.

## Where they were

Al-Hira sits on the western edge of the Euphrates floodplain near modern Kufa,
at the point where the desert routes out of Arabia meet the irrigated country.
That location is the whole of the polity's logic. It is close enough to
Ctesiphon to be supervised and far enough into the desert to be useful, and it
is a market before it is anything else.

It is worth naming a geographic awkwardness here rather than leaving it for a
reader to notice. This record sits in a region called The Eastern Mediterranean
after Alexander, and al-Hira is eight hundred kilometres from the Mediterranean.
The region is defined by a political space rather than a coastline — the country
between Rome and Iran, and the powers that grew in it — and putting the two
mirror-image Arab client dynasties in one region is worth more to a reader than
geographic tidiness. The alternative would have been Iran before Islam, beside
Characene, Adiabene and Elymais, which are the same kind of polity on the same
frontier three hundred years earlier.

## Lakhm, Nasrid, and what to call them

Later Arabic tradition calls the confederacy Lakhm and the dynasty the Lakhmids,
and that is the name most readers will know. This site follows Fisher in using
Nasrid for the ruling family, for the same reason its Ghassanid page is called
Jafnid: the confederacy and the house are different objects, the sources for the
confederacy are centuries later than the events, and a corpus that codes
institutions needs to know which of the two it is describing.

The id in this site's data is `nasrid-hira` rather than `nasrid`, because the
Nasrids of Granada are already here, eight hundred years and four thousand
kilometres away and entirely unrelated.

## The Namara stone

The earliest object anyone connects to this house is an epitaph cut in 328 for
Imru' al-Qays, which calls him king of all the Arabs, lists the tribes he
subdued, and is written in the Nabataean script with the Arabic language
underneath it.

Whether he was a Nasrid, and whether he served Iran or Rome or both in turn, is
argued about, and this record's span opens at a round 300 rather than resting on
him. The stone belongs to this page anyway, because it is the hinge of the other
argument in this pass: the Nabataean record entering alongside this one carries
the Arabic script as its single influence count, and the Namara epitaph is the
document in the middle of that chain. The Arabic tradition credits al-Hira with
the alphabet. The stone is in Nabataean letters.
