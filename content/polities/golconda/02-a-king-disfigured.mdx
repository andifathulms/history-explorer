Jamshid Qutb Shah, who had struck down the slave sent to murder his
own father rather than let the man live to name him as its instigator,
came to the Golconda throne in 1543 and soon joined Ahmadnagar's
Burhan Nizam Shah in a war against Bijapur, building a fort at Kakny
and laying siege to the fortress of Etgeer. His ally proved as
unreliable as Jamshid himself had once been: Burhan Nizam Shah made a
separate peace and proposed dividing disputed territory without
consulting him, and when Bijapur's forces stormed Kakny and put its
garrison to the sword, Jamshid was forced to abandon the siege and
retreat, pursued the whole way by the Bijapur general Asad Khan.

## A wound that could not heal

In the last of the running engagements of that retreat, Asad Khan cut
Jamshid down with a sabre stroke that took off the tip of his nose,
part of his cheek and his upper lip - a disfigurement that left him
unable to eat or drink easily for the rest of his life. He made peace
with Bijapur and turned his attention to reducing Hindu territory
closer to home, but fell into a lingering illness that lasted two
years and, as it worsened, curdled his temper: he had men put to death
for trivial offenses until his own brothers, Haidar and Ibrahim, fled
the court for Bidar out of fear for their lives. Jamshid died in early
1550, after a reign of not quite seven years, leaving an infant son on
a throne two courtiers were already fighting to control.
