Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah came to the throne in 1580 and ruled for
nearly thirty-four years, presiding over Golconda at the height of its
wealth and cultural patronage. His reign renewed alliances with the
neighboring sultanates through marriage — a Golconda princess, his own
sister, was sent to wed Ibrahim Adil Shah II of Bijapur under a treaty
of perpetual friendship sealed at the fortress of Naldurg — even as the
Mughal empire's presence in the Deccan grew more difficult for every
sultanate in the region to ignore.

## A capital moved for want of water

By 1590 Muhammad Quli judged Golconda's own fortress capital
increasingly unhealthy, cramped and short of water, and resolved to
build an entirely new city five miles away on the banks of the Musi
river. He named the new foundation Bhagnagar, after a favored
companion, and only after her death ordered it renamed Hyderabad — a
change of name the population was slow to adopt, so that for years the
older name persisted in common use alongside the new.

## A patron who built a skyline

Muhammad Quli filled his new capital and its surroundings with public
works on a scale the source records in careful detail: palace gardens,
a grand mosque, a college, a public hospital, an almshouse dedicated to
the twelve Imams, and numerous smaller mosques and baths besides. His
own treasurer's accounts, examined after his death, showed nearly
eighty lakh hoons spent on these public works across his reign, with a
further sixty thousand hoons distributed annually to the poor — a fifth
of that sum given away during the month of Muharram alone. The city he
founded on the banks of the Musi would carry his name into it for
centuries, long after the dynasty that built it had itself passed from
power.

## A death two days in the making

Muhammad Quli fell suddenly ill and died within two days, on 17
December 1611, at forty-nine years old. Rioting broke out briefly at
the palace as some of his own Deccani subjects turned on the foreign
courtiers who had long surrounded the king, before the city's own
constable dispersed the crowd by directing their attention to the
young successor's coronation instead. What followed for Golconda under
that new king is not part of this account: the source's own narrative,
like the reign it had just finished recording, was itself approaching
its end.
