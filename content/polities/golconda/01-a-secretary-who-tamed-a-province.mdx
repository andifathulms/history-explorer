Sultan Quli, a Turkoman said to hail from Hamadan in Persia, arrived in
the Deccan while still young and joined the Bahmani sultan's own
bodyguard of Turkish soldiers. His education won him a post as a
government secretary, and when reports reached court that landholders
in Telangana had stopped paying revenue and risen in open defiance,
Sultan Quli made an unusual offer: he would go alone, without troops,
and settle the matter himself. He succeeded well enough to recover
several districts the local population had seized outright, and was
ennobled with the title Qutb-ul-Mulk for his trouble.

## A rebellion joined late

When Yusuf Adil Shah of Bijapur and Ahmad Nizam Shah of Ahmadnagar
broke from Bahmani authority and began reading the Shia prayer formula
in their own courts, Sultan Quli, still nominally the sultanate's own
general and commander-in-chief, adopted the same religious forms
himself without yet declaring outright independence. He continued
sending the Bahmani king lavish annual gifts even after taking on every
other trapping of sovereignty — introducing Persian court customs, the
five-times-daily royal drum ceremony, and staffing his own government
with countrymen from Persia. Only in 1512, once the Bahmani sultan's
own incompetence and his minister's total dominance had become
undeniable to everyone in the Deccan, did Sultan Quli finally cast off
the last pretense of subordination.

## A murder committed by a son too eager to inherit

Sultan Quli reigned for nearly thirty-three years, mostly at peace with
his fellow Deccan sultans, before his own eldest son Jamshid, weary of
waiting for the throne, arranged his murder. A slave sent to attend the
aging king while he examined some jewels drew a hidden dagger and
stabbed him through the heart; Jamshid, fearing the assassin might
reveal his own involvement, killed the man on the spot to silence him.
The founder who had spent decades building a kingdom out of loyal
service to a dying sultanate died at the hand of the family he had
built it for.
