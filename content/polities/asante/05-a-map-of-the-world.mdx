The most interesting thing the British mission found at Kumasi in 1817 was not
the court. It was a Muslim scholar's house where men kept arriving from the other
side of the Sahara.

Thomas Bowdich called on Baba three or four times a week. Baba wrote with a reed
and vegetable ink, preferred them to the pens and pencils the Englishman offered,
and had a manuscript full of intricate figures like horoscopes. He put his finger
on one and explained what it was for:

> if you have any hard palaver, this can make me settle it for you when no other
> person can; or if you have any dear friend in England you wish to see, tell me
> the name, and this shall bring him to you.

Bowdich answered that when Englishmen knew their palaver was right they left it to
God, and that England was too good a place for him to wish anyone he regarded to
leave it.

## The business

Baba's pupils wrote on wooden boards. When somebody applied for a charm, one of
the older ones wrote the body of it and passed it to Baba, who added a cabalistic
mark and a mysterious fold; the customer took it eagerly, paid gold, and hurried
off to have it put in the richest case he could afford.

That is one end of a very long supply chain, and Bowdich saw the other end of it
at the same house. Every time he called there were strange Moors just arrived from
different parts of the interior, staying with Baba. They *always affected to
deplore the ignorance of the Ashantees*, and assumed the Englishman must find it
as irksome as they did. One tested him by addressing him in several languages,
none of which he recognised except one the man called Hindi, and named great
cities Bowdich had never heard of — until he said *Room*, and told him that if he
did not know Rome he was not a Christian.

Bowdich once asked Baba to draw him a map of the world. He drew one large
continent encircled by a sea, and the sea bounded by a girdle of rocks.

## The man from Timbuktu

On the second visit Baba sent hurriedly for a Moor he said was very learned and
had just come from Timbuktu. The man showed no surprise at the sight of a
European, and Baba explained why: he had seen three white men before, at Bussa.

Bowdich asked for the particulars and they were repeated through Baba:

> that some years ago, a vessel with masts, suddenly appeared on the Quolla or
> Niger near Boussa, with three white men, and some black. The natives encouraged
> by these strange men, took off provisions for sale, were well paid and received
> presents besides … The next day, perceiving the vessel going on, the natives
> hurried after her … but the white men mistaking, and thinking they pursued for
> a bad purpose, deterred them. The vessel soon after struck, the men jumped into
> the water and tried to swim, but could not, for the current, and were drowned.

He thought some of their clothes were at Wawa, and did not believe there were any
books or papers.

That is the death of Mungo Park, who had gone down the Niger in 1805 and never
been heard of again. It reached a European for the first time in a scholar's
house in Kumasi, from a man who had walked there from Timbuktu, told
spontaneously and without being asked, in the course of explaining why he was not
startled by the colour of Bowdich's face. Bowdich says the artlessness of it made
a powerful impression on him. He heard the same account later from another Moor
who had not been there.

## What the house was

The point is not the anecdote, remarkable as it is. It is where the anecdote was
standing.

Kumasi in 1817 is usually described as the capital of a forest kingdom facing the
Atlantic: the gold went south to the coast, the guns came north from the
Europeans, and the whole structure of Asante power is normally explained by that
axis. Baba's house is the other axis, and it was working at the same time. The
Asantehene kept Muslim scribes who wrote Arabic; charms made in Kumasi were sold
for Akan gold; scholars arrived from Jenne and Timbuktu and stayed; and news
travelled that road faster than it travelled to London.

Bowdich made his round of calls in the afternoons and found strange Moors in the
house every time. He got a chart from one of them and sent certificates back to a
British officer by his hand. And when he asked the resident scholar to draw the
world, he was given one continent in a ring of sea inside a wall of rock — a
cosmology as confident and as wrong as anything in the mission's own baggage.
