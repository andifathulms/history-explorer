In March 1900 the British governor of the Gold Coast, Frederick Hodgson, came to
Kumasi, assembled the chiefs, and asked where the Golden Stool was. He wanted to
sit on it.

The Asantehene Prempeh I had been deported four years earlier — arrested in his
own capital, taken to the coast, and eventually exiled to the Seychelles with
members of his family. The Asante state had been dismantled. What Hodgson had
apparently not been told, or had not believed, was what the object he was asking
about actually was.

The Golden Stool was not a throne. No Asantehene had ever sat on it. It was
understood to contain the *sunsum* — the soul — of the Asante nation, it had its
own attendants and its own regalia, it was rested on its own side-cushion so that
it never touched the ground, and it was brought out at the Odwira and set beside
the king rather than under him.

A governor demanding to sit on it was demanding to sit on the nation.

## The war of the Golden Stool

The chiefs said nothing at the meeting and went home, and the country rose.

Yaa Asantewaa, the queen mother of Ejisu — an *Asantehemaa* of her own district,
holding exactly the office the previous chapter describes — is remembered as the
one who put the question to the assembled chiefs and shamed them into fighting.
The tradition has her saying that if the men of Asante would not go forward, the
women would.

The rising besieged the British in the fort at Kumasi for some months. It was put
down with troops brought from elsewhere in the empire, Yaa Asantewaa was captured
and exiled to the Seychelles herself, and Asante was formally annexed in 1901.

The Golden Stool was not found. It had been buried in the forest, and it stayed
buried for twenty years.

## What was fought over

That is the part worth dwelling on, because the war has an obvious reading and
the obvious reading is wrong.

Britain had already won. Asante had been beaten in the field repeatedly, its king
was on an island in the Indian Ocean, its empire had gone, its capital was
occupied and its trade was controlled. There was nothing left to defend
militarily and the rising had no prospect of succeeding.

What the demand for the stool did was make submission mean something it had not
meant before. The Asante had accepted defeat, occupation, deportation and
annexation. What they would not accept was the physical transfer of the object
their nationhood was held in, to a man who thought it was furniture.

## Afterwards

Prempeh came back in 1924, after twenty-eight years, and was permitted to return
as private citizen and then recognised as head of the Kumasi division. The
Asante Confederacy was formally restored in 1935, under his successor, with the
Golden Stool at the centre of it.

The stool itself was accidentally rediscovered by road workers in 1920 and
partly stripped of its gold ornaments before it was recovered; the men
responsible were tried by the Asante themselves and would have been executed had
the British not intervened.

It is still there. The Asantehene remains one of the most substantial traditional
authorities in Ghana, the Odwira is still kept, and the stool is still not sat
on.

## The name, again

One last thing, which belongs at the end of the region rather than of this
record.

When the Gold Coast became independent in 1957 it took the name Ghana — after an
empire eight hundred kilometres to the north-west that had ended seven hundred
years earlier and had never held any of this ground. Asante, which had actually
governed most of the new country's territory within living memory, did not supply
the name.

A country named itself after the medieval empire it had no connection to, rather
than after the one it was largely made of, because the first had been destroyed
long enough ago to belong to everybody and the second was still a going concern
with a king in it.
