The Asante tradition has the priest Okomfo Anokye calling a golden stool down out
of the sky, in a cloud of white dust, into the lap of Osei Tutu, and declaring
that it held the soul of the nation and that the union of the Akan states was
bound up in it.

Nobody sits on it. It is not a throne. The Asantehene is enstooled beside it and
lowered towards it three times without touching it, and it has its own regalia,
its own attendants and its own chair to rest on.

In 1701 the confederacy that had been assembled around it destroyed Denkyira at
Feyiase and stopped paying tribute to anybody.

## Gold

This record's `revenue_basis` reads `mining, trade-toll, tribute`, and the first
value is the reason Europeans called the country the Gold Coast.

The Akan goldfields had been supplying the trans-Saharan trade for centuries
before this record starts — this corpus's Ghana, Mali and Songhai records live on
that traffic at its northern end, and the Ghana record's chapter on al-Bakri
describes the royalty on nuggets that funded three empires in succession.

Asante sat on the source, and by the eighteenth century the gold was going south
to the Atlantic as well as north across the desert. Gold dust was the currency,
weighed against brass weights — geometric ones and figurative ones showing
animals, proverbs, tools and scenes, made in their thousands and now among the
most collected African objects anywhere.

The state took a share of production, taxed the roads in both directions, and
assessed tribute on the conquered states in gold, and Wilks's book reconstructs
the accounting from the surviving correspondence.

## And the other export

Asante's wealth in the eighteenth century came substantially from selling people
to the Atlantic trade, and its wars produced the captives.

This corpus's Mali, Songhai and Kanem-Bornu records all carry `slave-soldier` or
describe the same circuit from the Saharan side, and Gomez's book — cited on two
of them — argues that categories of servility in West Africa were formed in that
economy rather than by later European contact. Asante is the Atlantic-facing
version and the largest single supplier on that coast for much of the century.

None of it is in a coded field on this page, because `revenue_basis` records
where a state's money came from and this was a trade the state taxed and
supplied rather than an instrument it operated. The Kilwa record makes the same
observation about the Indian Ocean traffic in the other direction.

Britain abolished its own slave trade in 1807, which removed Asante's largest
market, and the nineteenth-century wars this record's turning points are about
begin in the years immediately afterwards.

## A state with a civil service

The reason this record can code three of its four institution fields with
confidence is Wilks, whose eight hundred pages are about how the thing actually
worked.

A capital with wide swept streets and a stone palace. Great roads radiating from
Kumasi with a courier service, stages, and fixed travel times the treasury used
for accounting. A treasury with its own weights and its own officials. Provincial
administration by appointed residents rather than only by tributary chiefs. A
council at Kumasi with named offices and recorded factional politics —
Wilks reconstructs an argument between an imperialist and an accommodationist
party over policy towards the British that runs for decades.

That is a nineteenth-century African state described in the terms this corpus
usually reserves for Byzantium or the Ottomans, and it is why the one empty field
on this page is empty for a reason that has nothing to do with the evidence.
