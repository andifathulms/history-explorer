---
title: A king too gentle for his own nobles
drafted_from: reindorf-1895

The fifty years between Opoku Ware's death and the accession of Osei
Bonsu passed through four kings whose reigns, read together, show a
kingdom whose real check on royal power was not law but its own
nobility's willingness to depose a ruler who governed the wrong way -
too harshly, or not harshly enough.

## A humane king his own chiefs came to despise

Opoku Ware's nephew Kusi Obodom succeeded him around 1750 and
reversed his uncle's methods almost immediately: he restored
constitutional powers to the great chiefs that Opoku Ware had
concentrated in the stool, forbade human sacrifice, and ruled, by the
kingdom's own later memory, as the most humane monarch Asante had yet
had. It cost him the nobility's respect rather than earning it. Chiefs
who had grown used to a court capable of real severity needled him
deliberately, testing how much defiance so gentle a king would
tolerate - and when his eyesight began failing with age, the same
nobles used it as pretext to remove him from active rule entirely,
building him a separate residence at Kyeremade and governing without
him until his death around 1770.

## Osei Kwadwo's campaigns, and an administration to match them

His successor, Osei Kwadwo, survived a scandal at his own accession - a
sexual affair with several of the old king's wives, covered up by a
loyal official who took the blame and was executed in his place - and
went on to become one of Asante's more capable rulers in the field. He
crushed a revolt by Worosa, a regional ruler who had been killing
Asante traders on his territory, in a campaign that turned after
Worosa's own forces mistook a grove of flowering trees for an
impossibly large Asante encampment and panicked. He conquered Wasa
after its ruler boasted of what he would do to Osei Kwadwo if captured,
and forced Yani's king Nakawa into a written vassalage contract paying
annual tribute in slaves after defeating him in battle. Osei Kwadwo
matched this run of conquests with genuine administrative innovation,
becoming the first Asante king to appoint dedicated officials
responsible for public sanitation and the maintenance of roads and
paths across the kingdom - an office with its own insignia and its own
enforceable fines. He died around 1781 after Asen, Akuapem and Akyem
took advantage of his final illness to rebel.

## A boy king who lost the throne to his own absence

Osei Kwadwo's nephew Osei Kwame, still a child of about twelve,
inherited a kingdom already in revolt and opened his reign by sending
an army to crush the rebels who had troubled his uncle's last days.
As an adult he governed capably enough for years, but eventually moved
his own residence away from Kumasi to Dwaben and, while there, took a
wife from a lineage the kingdom's own constitutional law barred him
from marrying. Two of his generals, backed by the Kumasi nobility,
marched on Dwaben and deposed him rather than tolerate a king who had
abandoned the capital and broken the kingdom's own marriage law in the
same reign. Osei Kwame poisoned himself around 1799 rather than live
as a deposed king. His brother and immediate successor, Opoku Fofie,
reigned only two months before dying suddenly - Asante tradition held
that his dead brother's ghost had come for him in his sleep - clearing
the way for a much younger brother, Osei Tutu Kwamena, better known by
his own surname Osei Bonsu, whose accession opened the era in which
Asante's dealings with Britain became the central fact of its own
foreign policy.
