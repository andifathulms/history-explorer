The Asantehene succeeded through the *abusua* — the matriclan. The eligible
candidates were the sons of the royal women, not the sons of the reigning king,
and a king's own son belonged to his mother's clan and could not inherit his
father's stool.

The Asantehemaa — the queen mother, who was not necessarily the king's mother but
was the senior woman of the royal matrilineage — nominated. The council of chiefs
confirmed or refused, and could refuse repeatedly. A king who was confirmed could
later be *destooled* for cause, by the same council, and several were.

This record's `succession_rule` reads `matrilineal, election`, and it did not
when this chapter was written.

## Why it was empty

The seven values were primogeniture, tanistry, appanage, nomination, election,
acclamation and factional.

`primogeniture` is a rule about birth order in a line of descent and this is a
different line. `tanistry` is the closest and describes selection from a pool of
eligible kin, which is the confirming step here and not the rule that defines the
pool. `nomination` describes a predecessor designating, and the nominator here is
not the predecessor. `election`, `acclamation` and `factional` describe the
council's part and leave out the descent rule entirely.

Coding rule 7 takes the rule in force. The rule in force has two components — a
matrilineal definition of who is eligible, and a confirmation by council — and the
vocabulary can express the second and not the first.

## Where the count now stands

This corpus has met matrilineal or avuncular succession five times, and this is
the first case where it is documented in institutional detail by a modern
scholarly account rather than reported by a foreign visitor or inferred from a
titulature.

**Ghana**, before 1068. Al-Bakri states the rule outright and gives the reason he
was given: a man can be certain of his sister's child and not of his wife's.

**Mali**, 1352. Ibn Battuta records it as a general rule of inheritance, with
disapproval, and the royal succession shows the same mixed shape.

**Elam**, in the third and second millennia BC, on this corpus's Ur III record's
last chapter: brother to brother and then to a sister's son.

**Makuria**, seventh century to fourteenth, attested in the Arabic accounts and
in the royal titulature.

**Asante**, 1701 to 1901, in Wilks.

And a sixth in doubt: this corpus's Kush record's third chapter sets out
brother-to-brother succession with formally prominent queen mothers and ruling
kandakes, and declines to count it, because whether that is matrilineal descent
or a royal kin-group of both sexes with a senior woman is genuinely argued.

Five confirmed, one contested. Three regions. Something over three and a half
thousand years between the earliest and the latest, with no possibility of
contact between the Elamite case and the four African ones.

## The argument the rulebook asked for

The Makuria record's chapter said a fifth case would make the argument on the
merits that a vocabulary change requires. This was the fifth, and the argument
below is the one that was made and accepted. `matrilineal` is in the succession
vocabulary now, this field carries it, and so do the Ghana and Makuria fields.

The argument is kept in the present tense it was written in.

**The gap is not an awkward case.** Five records cannot all be edge cases of a
list that was supposed to cover succession generally.

**The gap is systematic rather than accidental.** The seven values are built out
of European, Islamic and steppe practice — primogeniture and appanage from Latin
Europe, tanistry from the Celtic and Turkic worlds, nomination and acclamation
from Rome and the caliphates, election from the Italian republics and the
Empire. Descent through women is not a marginal arrangement globally; it is
absent from this list because of where the list came from.

**The field currently lies by omission.** A null on this site means the question
was not answered. Five of these records have a clear, documented answer, and a
reader sees the same empty field on them as on a page where nobody has opened a
source.

**And the cost is the standard one.** Adding a value means auditing every record
in the corpus for it, as the `investiture` change did across a hundred and fifty
pages.

What a value would be called is a separate question, and the obvious candidate —
`matrilineal` — describes the descent and not the selection, which is the same
half-answer `tanistry` gives from the other side.

## What was settled

That last objection turned out to answer itself, and the answer was already in
the schema.

These fields take a *set* of values, not one. Four records were already carrying
more than one succession value before this change — the Rashidun record has
three. So `matrilineal` names the rule of eligibility, a selection value sits
beside it where one is known, and this record reads `matrilineal, election`
because the abusua defined who could be chosen and the council of chiefs chose.

The audit that followed found three records to fill and, more usefully, one to
leave alone. Ghana codes the value because al-Bakri states the rule. Makuria
codes it because the Arabic accounts and the royal titulature attest it. This
record codes it with `election` beside it. Mali stays null, because its throne
went to brothers, to sons and to sisters' sons in no order any source states —
which is a pattern and not a rule.

Kush stays null too, on the ground the third chapter of that record gives, and
the coding rules now say in terms that the prominence of royal women is not on
its own enough: queen mothers with formal office, ruling queens and matrilineal
inheritance of property are all compatible with a patrilineal throne.

Before the change, Ghana's field and Mali's field were both empty and looked
identical. That was the strongest argument for making it and it is only visible
now that they differ.
