After Wolseley burned Kumasi in 1874 and left, the Asante council spent twenty
years arguing about what to do, and the argument is recoverable.

Wilks reconstructs two standing positions from the correspondence and the
recorded debates. One — he calls it the imperialist party — held that the empire
should be reassembled by force, the seceded provinces brought back, and Britain
faced down. The other, the peace or accommodationist party, held that the
tributaries were gone, that the coast trade mattered more than the northern one,
and that an accommodation with Britain was the only way to keep anything.

Neither is a faction in the sense of a family or a region. They are positions,
argued in council, by named men, across changes of ruler.

## Why that is unusual

Because most states before the modern period are visible only as outcomes.

Songhai's kings were deposed at a rate of about one a decade and nobody can say
what the factions were arguing about. Mali had two usurpations and no account of
the reasoning. First Bulgaria's internal politics survives as a Byzantine
chronicler's summary.

Here the reasoning survives. The Asante state wrote letters, kept them, and had a
literate staff — the Muslim clerks at Kumasi, and later Africans educated at the
coast — and enough of that correspondence was preserved on both
sides of the frontier for a historian to reconstruct a policy debate.

It makes Asante one of very few pre-colonial African states where the question
*what did they think they were doing* has an answer that is not an inference.

## The twenty years

They were bad. The indemnity Wolseley imposed was larger than the state could
pay, and paying any of it meant squeezing provinces that were already leaving.

Dwaben seceded and was reduced by force. The northern tributaries stopped
sending. The Brong states went. Gyaman and Nkoranza went. By the mid-1880s the
empire had been reduced to its metropolitan core, and the argument in council had become about whether there was anything
left to accommodate with.

There was also a civil war over the succession, fought through the machinery the
next chapter describes: the Asantehemaa nominating from the royal matrilineage,
the council of chiefs confirming or refusing, and — when the council could not
agree — no mechanism at all.

## Prempeh

Prempeh I was enstooled in 1888 at about sixteen, after that war, and spent seven
years doing what the accommodationist party had argued for: recovering the
seceded provinces by negotiation where he could, sending an embassy to London in
1895 to argue Asante's case directly, and declining to accept a British
protectorate.

The embassy was refused an audience. In 1896 a British expedition came to Kumasi
without a battle, and Prempeh — who had been advised to fight and did not —
submitted, was arrested along with his mother and much of the court, and was
deported to the Seychelles.

He was there for twenty-eight years.

## The judgement the record does not make

Whether the accommodationists were right is not answerable, and it is worth
saying why rather than leaving the silence to look like neutrality.

The imperialist party's programme required beating Britain, and Asante had done
that once, in 1824, and killed a British governor doing it. The accommodationist programme required Britain to
accept a client state, and Prempeh's embassy establishes that it would not.

Both parties were arguing from a correct premise and both programmes were
unavailable. That is the ordinary situation of a state in the path of an
industrial empire.
