Osei Tutu's own reign after Feyiase did not slow down. Around 1730 a
rebellion by the tributary province of Akyem brought him back into the
field one last time, and the campaign that followed gave Asante both a
new king and the most solemn oath in its whole vocabulary.

## A death the army was not told about

Crossing the Pra during the Akyem campaign, Osei Tutu died - by one
account killed in an ambush while fording the river, by the Asante
court's own preferred version simply overtaken by age and illness on
campaign. His nephew Opoku Ware, already leading the army in practice,
kept the death concealed from the ordinary soldiers: the king's body
was coffined in secret and carried in the rear of the marching column
so the campaign could finish before the news broke it. Only after the
army had punished Akyem and started home did Opoku Ware tell the
generals what had happened. The place of the crossing gave its name to
Asante's most binding oath, Koromante - sworn, still, in memory of the
king soldiers in camp had not been told was already dead.

## A contested succession settled by execution

Osei Tutu's own funeral rites completed, the stool passed to Opoku
Ware, the nephew who had commanded the winning campaign and whom the
old king had named as his intended heir. Two other nephews, both named
Dako, pressed rival claims and drew enough support among the Kumasi
nobility that the dispute nearly became a civil war. Opoku Ware first
tried to buy them off, offering to share the estate while keeping the
stool itself - an offer both men refused. When their faction would not
back down, Opoku Ware had both claimants and their followers rounded up
and beheaded, sparing only one girl from the household. He was
proclaimed Osei Tutu's successor in 1731.

## Three decades that pushed Asante's borders outward

Opoku Ware's reign, from 1731 until his death around 1750, was the
period in which conquest turned the polity Osei Tutu had founded into
something closer to an empire. He broke a renewed Akyem revolt and
extended tribute obligations over it more firmly than before; used a
period of peace with Akyem, secured by a diplomatic oath, to conquer
territory to the north, west and east instead; and absorbed the
Techiman kingdom after its king Amo Yaw sheltered a rival claimant to a
neighboring Asante-installed throne, marching on Techiman's own capital
once the diplomatic option was exhausted. His armies reached as far as
Wasa and pushed Asante's frontier to the edges of the Gyaman kingdom in
the west. Opoku Ware's own policy toward each new conquest became
Asante's standing law for the rest of its existence: conquered
provinces were formally naturalized as citizens with full rights, and
naming a fellow Asante's conquered origin - reminding anyone that their
family had once been subjects of a defeated kingdom - was made a crime
punishable by death. It was this policy, binding new territory into the
kingdom as citizens rather than as a permanent subject class, that let
Asante absorb conquest after conquest for the rest of the century
without the resentments a purely tributary empire would have stored up
tearing the kingdom back apart.
