The Asante succession is the best-documented matrilineal succession anywhere, and
it worked like this.

The Asantehene belonged to the royal *abusua*, the matriclan, and eligibility ran
through the female line: a candidate had to be descended from the royal women, so
the reigning king's own sons were not eligible and his sister's sons were.

Within that pool, the *Asantehemaa* — the queen mother, herself a senior woman of
the matrilineage rather than necessarily the king's mother — nominated. Then the
council of chiefs confirmed or refused. If they refused, she nominated again. In
principle she had three attempts, after which the choice passed to the council.

So the office had two gates: descent through women decided who could be
considered, and a council of men decided which of them it would be.

## Why that is a good design

It solves both halves of the succession problem at once, which very few systems
manage.

The first half is legitimacy: who has a claim. Descent answers that, and
matrilineal descent answers it with something demonstrable — a man's sister's son
is certainly of his blood and his wife's son is a matter of trust. Al-Bakri had
been given the same reasoning in eleventh-century Ghana by people who practised
it.

The second half is competence: which of the eligible candidates. A rule of
descent alone cannot answer that, and every strictly hereditary monarchy
eventually gets a child, an incompetent or a lunatic and has no lawful way to
avoid him. Asante's council could simply decline, and did.

The cost is the one every elective system pays. When the council could not agree,
there was no mechanism at all — and the civil war of the 1880s was fought in
exactly that gap.

## Where else the female line ran

Five times, on two continents, across four thousand years, with no possibility of
contact between the earliest and the rest.

**Elam**, in the third and second millennia BC, where the throne passed from
brother to brother and then to a sister's son.

**Ghana**, before 1068, where al-Bakri states the rule and supplies the reasoning.

**Makuria** on the middle Nile, from the seventh century to the fourteenth,
where the king's mother and sister held formal office and appear in the
cathedral paintings at Faras beside the kings.

**Mali**, in 1352, where Ibn Battuta found inheritance running to the sister's
son and was scandalised by it.

**Asante**, into the twentieth century.

The convergence is not transmission. It is several societies arriving separately
at the view that maternity is a fact and paternity is a claim.

## What it did not mean

It did not mean women ruled, and the distinction gets blurred constantly.

The Asantehene was a man. The Asantehemaa was not a co-ruler; she was the person
who named the candidate and who held her own court, her own stool and her own
jurisdiction, particularly over cases involving women. Descent through the female
line and rule by women are different things, and Asante had the first without the
second.

What it did mean is that the most consequential political act in the state — the
naming of a candidate for the highest office — was performed by a woman, in
public, and could be refused by a council, in public, and everybody knew whose
turn it was to do what.
