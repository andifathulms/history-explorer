Britain and Asante fought five wars between 1824 and 1900.

The first is this record's second turning point and Asante won it: at Nsamankow
in 1824 an Asante army destroyed a British force and killed the governor of the
Gold Coast, Sir Charles MacCarthy, whose skull was taken to Kumasi and kept as a
royal object. It is one of very few occasions in the nineteenth century on which
an African state beat a European force in a pitched engagement, and the only one
in this corpus besides Ethiopia's Adwa seventy-two years later.

The last is the War of the Golden Stool in 1900, and the country was annexed the
following year.

## What happened in between

1874 is the hinge and it is this record's third turning point: Wolseley's
expedition reached Kumasi, found it evacuated, looted the palace and burned the
city, and imposed an indemnity larger than the state could pay. The previous
chapter is the twenty years that followed — the secessions, and the argument in
council between the party that wanted accommodation with Britain and the party
that wanted the empire reassembled by force.

Prempeh I was arrested in 1896 and deported to the Seychelles with his mother and
much of the court. He was allowed home in 1924 as a private person and recognised
as head of the Kumasi division in 1926; his successor was recognised as
Asantehene in 1935, and the office exists.

## The stool

In 1900 the governor, Frederick Hodgson, came to Kumasi, demanded the Golden
Stool, and announced that he intended to sit on it.

The country rose. The rising is remembered under the leadership of Yaa
Asantewaa, the queen mother of Ejisu — which is not incidental on this page,
since the office she held was one of the two that controlled the succession, and
this record's second chapter is about that. The British were besieged in the
Kumasi fort for months and relieved; the rising was put down; Yaa Asantewaa was
deported to the Seychelles and died there.

The stool was not found. It had been hidden in the forest and stayed hidden for
twenty years. In 1920 African road-builders came across it by accident and
stripped the gold ornaments; they were tried by the Asante and would have been
executed had the colonial administration not commuted it to exile.

It exists. It is not on display.

## `by` is empty

This record's `ended.type` is `conquest` and `ended.by` is empty, because the
British empire is not in this corpus.

This corpus's Kanem-Bornu record's last chapter counted six of nine before this
pass. It is now nine of twelve: this record, Alodia ending against the Funj and
Great Zimbabwe ending against nobody at all were added together.

The count is across Africa rather than across a region, and since those records
were written the one region has become three — West Africa, the Nile and the
Horn, and the Zimbabwe plateau with its coast. Splitting it changed nothing
about this number, which is the strongest evidence that the number is about the
corpus and not about any particular stretch of ground.

The Kanem-Bornu chapter's conclusion holds and can be stated more sharply now.
Three of the twelve end against a power this site can name, and only one of
those three — Ghana, ending against Mali — has that power on an adjacent page.
The rest end against Portugal, Britain, the Funj, a Sudanese warlord, three
colonial empires, a committee of officers, and nobody at all.

That is where the boundary of this project currently runs. It is not the Sahara
or the Red Sea. It is the point at which the polity on the other side of an
African record's ending is one this corpus has not written.
