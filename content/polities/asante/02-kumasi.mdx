In 1817 Thomas Edward Bowdich came to Kumasi with a mission from the African
Company of Merchants and published an account of it two years later. It is the
first detailed European description of the Asante capital and it is not what its
readers expected.

He describes streets — wide, straight, swept daily, some of them named — and
houses of two storeys with polished red-clay walls, carved façades and open
courtyards. He counts twenty-seven of the principal streets. He describes a
market, a palace complex, and a cleanliness he compares favourably with London.

And he describes the reception, which had something over thirty thousand people
in it: massed umbrellas the size of small tents, gold ornaments on a scale he
found difficult to convey, horns and drums, and the king's own party arriving
last after four hours.

## Reading a witness who wanted to be impressed

Bowdich was negotiating a treaty and had reasons to make his hosts sound
formidable, which is the opposite of the bias this corpus usually has to correct
for.

This site's Mali record's chapter on Ibn Battuta argues that a hostile witness is
worth more, because a traveller who objects to unveiled women and matrilineal
inheritance is recording things his own assumptions made him notice. Bowdich is
the other case: a man with an incentive to be dazzled, whose account has to be
read down rather than up.

What survives that discount is checkable against other things. The street plan
matches the archaeology and later descriptions. The scale of the gold matches the
weights, the regalia and the indemnities Britain later demanded. The population
estimates for the city — twenty to thirty thousand in the nineteenth century —
are consistent across several independent visitors.

## Odwira

The festival Bowdich saw part of is the Odwira, held annually, and it is the
state's own account of itself performed for a week.

The stools of the dead Asantehenes were carried out and fed. The chiefs of the
metropolitan and provincial divisions attended in person or sent
representatives, and their attendance was the visible measure of whether they
were still in the union. Tribute was presented. Cases were heard. The new yam
could not be eaten until the ceremony released it.

This record's `legitimation` reads `divine-sanction, descent` and the Odwira is
where both were operated. The Golden Stool held the soul of the nation and the
blackened stools of the ancestors held the dynasty, and a chief who did not come
was making a statement everybody could count.

That is also why the collapse this record's fifth chapter describes is legible in
attendance records before it is legible anywhere else.

## The Muslims at court

One detail from Bowdich that the general accounts leave out.

There were Muslim scholars resident at Kumasi — from the northern trading towns,
serving as clerks, as writers of Arabic correspondence with the northern states,
and as makers of protective amulets that Asante officers and the king himself
wore.

Asante was not a Muslim state and did not become one. This corpus's Ghana record
describes a comparable arrangement eight hundred years earlier and a thousand
kilometres north — a royal town with the ancestral cult, and a Muslim merchant
town six miles away with twelve mosques and salaried imams — and observes that
the arrangement worked because the state did not propagate anything.

Kumasi is the same shape at smaller scale, and this record's `religions_carried`
count is zero for the same reason Ghana's is.
