Eight great roads ran out of Kumasi. They were numbered in the administrative
usage, cleared and maintained, and the government kept a schedule of how many
days each stage took.

That last is the detail Wilks's book turns on and the reason this record can code
what it does. A state that records travel times in days is a state that plans
around them: the treasury used the figures for accounting, the council used them
for deciding whether a report could have arrived, and the courier service — the
*nkwansrafo*, road-wardens, and the runners who carried messages — worked to
them.

Kumasi to the coast was about ten days. Kumasi to the northern trading towns
about twenty.

## A treasury with its own weights

Gold dust was the currency and it was weighed.

The brass weights are the objects most people have seen without knowing what they
were for. Geometric ones in graded series for ordinary transactions; figurative
ones showing animals, tools, birds, people, scenes from proverbs. They are made
by lost-wax casting in their tens of thousands, they are one of the most
collected classes of African object anywhere, and they are a monetary
instrument.

The treasury — the *Gyaasewa* — kept the state's gold, its own standard weights,
and officials to run both. Wilks reconstructs a working fiscal administration
from the surviving correspondence: assessments on the provinces, arrears chased,
death duties on officeholders, tolls on the roads in both directions, and the
king's share of the goldfields.

This record's `revenue_basis` reads `mining, trade-toll, tribute` and all three
values come out of that material.

## What a nineteenth-century African state looks like when somebody has looked

This is the point of the chapter, and it is a point about the corpus rather than
about Asante.

Eight hundred pages by Wilks on how one state actually worked — the bureaucracy,
the roads, the treasury, the succession, the named offices, the factions in
council arguing about policy — is the fullest institutional account of any
sub-Saharan polity in this site's bibliography. It is why this record codes three
of its four institution fields with confidence and why the fourth is empty for a
reason that has nothing to do with the evidence.

Compare what this corpus has for its neighbours. Ghana is one Andalusian
geographer who never went. Mali is Arabic writers, one visitor and an epic
transcribed in the twentieth century. Great Zimbabwe has no text at all and its
three null institution fields are null because there are no sources. Kanem-Bornu
has a king list and one court historian's account of one reign.

Asante is not better documented because it was better organised. It is better
documented because it survived into the nineteenth century, kept written
correspondence, and was studied by a historian who spent a career on it.

That is worth saying plainly on a site that ranks polities on how many of their
fields are filled. The coded fields measure the evidence at least as much as they
measure the state.
