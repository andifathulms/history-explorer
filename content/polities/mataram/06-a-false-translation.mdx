The division of 1755 did not stop the war. A third prince, Mas Said, was still
in the field, and kept fighting for more than two years after Mangkubumi had
been proclaimed sultan by the Dutch governor.

He was hunted, his camp burned, a price put on his head, and his force finally
broken; and he still would not submit, because his name alone was enough to
bring men back to him. In the end he asked for districts to live on, and was
told the partition of the lands had already happened but that something might
be found for him if he came to court. He came, on the stipulation that the
Susuhunan would meet him half an hour's walk outside Surakarta, threw himself
at his feet, was raised up, and was given a bench to sit on.

The settlement made him Pangeran Adipati Mangkunagara with four thousand
*cacah* in Kaduwang, Malesa and the southern mountains. The kingdom that had
been divided in two was now divided in three.

Raffles, who governed Java sixty years later and had the company's papers,
summarises it like this: the war lasted twelve years, the finest provinces of
the island were laid waste, thousands were killed on both sides, and "the
independence of the empire" was finally annihilated. He gives the Dutch bill
for it too — 4,286,006 florins, 12 stuivers, 8 pennies, from 1746 to the peace
— and the return on that outlay, which was, if not the acknowledged sovereignty
of Java, at least effectual control over how it would be administered in
future.

## The shape the island was left in

The 1758 settlement drew a line the rest of the colonial period kept.

The Dutch took the direct administration of every province on the north coast,
from Cirebon to the eastern end of Madura. The inland and southern provinces
were restored to the Javanese princes and divided between them in nearly equal
shares, counted in *cacah* — households — rather than in territory, which is
how a Javanese kingdom had always measured what it had.

So the coast that Mataram's kings had spent a century and a half failing to
hold was formally taken away from them, and the interior they had always
actually lived on was handed back, subdivided, to be held on terms.

## 1808, and the argument about the words

Those terms lasted fifty years and were then declared void by the man who
governed for Napoleon's Netherlands.

Marshal Daendels announced in 1808 that the clauses under which the princes
held their lands in fee from the Dutch no longer applied, and that he would
henceforth treat them as independent princes, standing in no relation to the
European government except the one that necessarily exists between a weaker and
a stronger state next door to each other.

Yogyakarta took him at his word. Its court, Raffles writes, was conscious of
owing its existence chiefly to the military success of its founder and to the
weakness of the Dutch and of Surakarta, and had never fully accepted the terms
of 1755 — which, it was said there, had been imposed on the sovereign by a
false translation into Javanese.

That claim deserves to be recorded exactly as it stands: a court arguing, half
a century on, that the treaty which created it had been mistranslated to the
man who signed it. Whether or not it was true, it is what a dynasty says when
the document that made it is also the document that bound it.

Daendels marched on Yogyakarta with a considerable force. The treaty that
followed had the reigning sultan resign the administration to his son as regent
and cede certain provinces.

## What the British found, and did

None of it had been carried out when the British landed in August 1811. The
sultan had ostensibly resigned, but he still took his usual place on the
throne, and not one of the ceded districts had actually changed hands.

In 1812 the British government deposed him and put his son on the throne. That
son died, and in 1815 was succeeded by a boy of nine.

Two hundred and twenty-four years after a settler's son in a newly walled town
stopped going to court to render homage, the court he founded had its occupant
chosen by a European government that had been on the island for four years.
