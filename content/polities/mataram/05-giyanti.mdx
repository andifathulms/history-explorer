In 1755, after nine years of war, the kingdom was divided in two. Pakubuwana
III kept Surakarta. Mangkubumi took Yogyakarta and the title Hamengkubuwana I.

<Figure id="kraton-solo" />

The Dutch company brokered the treaty and guaranteed both halves.

No foreign power conquered it. The division was made between two Javanese
princes, in a war fought by Javanese armies, and the company's part was to
underwrite the outcome and take payment for doing so.

## The ending that is still there

Most endings end something. Both halves of this one are still running.

Two courts, two dynasties in unbroken descent, two palaces, and two divergent
traditions of Javanese court music, dance, dress and language, two hundred and
seventy years later. The sultan of Yogyakarta is the governor of a province of
Indonesia by hereditary right, which is the only such arrangement in the
republic.

The two courts are in competition, and always have been. Each presents itself
as the continuation of Mataram,
and the divergence between their court
traditions is partly the product of two centuries of each declining to do
things the way the other does them. A gamelan tuning, a way of folding a
headcloth and a dance posture are all, in this context, arguments about
succession.

<Figure id="kraton-yogya" />

## What Surakarta and Yogyakarta were after 1755

What the two courts kept was ritual authority, revenue and an enormous cultural
production. What neither had again was the disposal of its own external
relations: they were courts inside somebody else's empire — the company's, then
the Dutch state's, then the republic's.

That is the sense in which 1755 is an ending. The line did not stop, and has
not stopped. What stopped was Java being governed from a Javanese court's own
decisions, and the seven hundred years of kingdoms in the interior that ran
from Kediri through Singhasari, Majapahit, Demak and Pajang stop being that
kind of sequence here.
