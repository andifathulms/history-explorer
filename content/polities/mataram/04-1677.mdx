Trunajaya was a Madurese prince. His allies were Makasar exiles — men who had
left South Sulawesi after the fall of Makassar in 1669, and who had spent the
intervening years as fighting men for hire in the Java Sea.

In 1677 they took the Mataram capital and drove Amangkurat I out; he died on
the road. His son appealed to the Dutch company for help, and got it, and was
restored.

That is the hinge. Before 1677 the company is a coastal power that Mataram
alternately fought and traded with. After it, the company is a participant in
Javanese succession, is paid for participating in territory and in trade
rights, and is in the field for every subsequent dispute over the throne —
three of them, in 1704, 1719 and 1746, each of which cost the winner more than
it gained.

Nothing in the sources describes a rule that was supposed to settle a Javanese
succession, and three wars over the throne in forty-two years is what the
absence cost.

## What a diaspora did

The Makasar who came with Trunajaya, and the Bugis who took power at Riau after
1699, both came out of the settlement imposed on South Sulawesi in 1667 and
1669, when the Dutch company and Arung Palakka's Bone destroyed the
archipelago's last open port.

Makassar's most consequential export turned out to be its own defeated. One
half of them landed here, and what they did was pull the company into the
centre of Javanese politics eight years after it had finished with Sulawesi.
