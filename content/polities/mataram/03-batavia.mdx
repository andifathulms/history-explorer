Sultan Agung had taken every city in Java that mattered. In 1628 and again in
1629 he besieged the Dutch company's capital at Batavia, and failed both times.

<Figure id="batavia-1628" />

He did not fail to Dutch arms. He failed to rice.

The armies he sent were large enough; the problem was feeding them four hundred
kilometres from the granary, and his solution was to position depots along the
north coast in advance. Company ships found them and burned them. The army
outside Batavia's walls starved in front of them, and what was left of it
walked home.

It is about as clean a demonstration as the century offers of what sea power was
actually for. Mataram could put more men in front of Batavia than the company could put
behind its walls, and could not keep them there, because everything on that
coast moved by water and the water was not his.

## What the sea was for

Mataram was the largest agrarian state in the archipelago and drew nothing from
the sea at all. Its whole revenue was the rice country. Melaka, three hundred
miles away, had no farmland whatever and lived on the traffic through the
strait — and bought much of its food from Java.

Those are the two ways a polity in these islands could be rich, and Java's
politics ran through the second one even though its greatest kingdom was built
entirely on the first. Every decisive thing that happened to Mataram happened
because of a coast it held as a dependency and could not use.

## And the title from Mecca

In 1641 an embassy came back from the Hijaz with the title of sultan, granted
by the Sharif of Mecca, and Agung used it for the rest of his reign.

The sharifs had little land and no army, and lived substantially on granting
recognition of exactly this kind; Banten had sent for the same title and got it
three years earlier. What a Javanese king wanted from the Hijaz was a standing
that no rival on Java could confer and none could deny, and it was for sale.
