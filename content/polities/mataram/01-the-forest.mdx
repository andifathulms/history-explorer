In the third quarter of the sixteenth century the sultan of Pajang sent one of
his commanders, the lord of Pamanahan, west into the neighbouring country of
Mataram, to bring it into the world of Islam and settle it.

The Javanese chronicles are detailed about Pengging and Pajang, where they can
name Muslim kings ruling in the first half of the century. About Mataram they
have nothing comparable, and the silence is the point: no local ruler is
defeated in these stories, and none is driven out. What the stories describe is
men clearing forest.

The best reading of that is the plain one. The core of Mataram in the 1560s was
largely unpeopled and largely uncultivated, and the sultan of Pajang was doing
what Javanese kings had done before and would do again — planting a settlement
in empty ground next door, because settled ground pays tribute.

## What was under the trees

Nothing in the tradition suggests that either the sultan who ordered the
clearing or the man who did it knew that the same valleys had been the seat of
kings five centuries earlier.

They were. The Opak and Progo country is where the great temple complexes
stand, and Kota Gede, the capital Pamanahan's son built, is about thirty
kilometres from Prambanan. But tropical growth had covered the ruins of the old
palaces completely, and the kingdom that took the name of Mataram in 1588 took
it as the name of a district, not as an inheritance. The colonists were opening
a forest. That the forest had a floor made of somebody's capital is a fact
about the place that had to be dug up again before anyone could use it.

## A coconut, and a woman in the sea

The chronicle tradition preserves two stories that seem to be about older
rights.

The first is the palm-tapper of Giring, in the limestone hills of Gunung Kidul,
who possessed a coconut whose water, drunk at a single draught, would make the
drinker the ancestor of the family that would rule all Java. Ki Gede Giring let
it stand. Ki Pamanahan drank it by accident, and the prophecy came true.

As a story it is slight. As evidence it is not, because it concedes that the
authority of Giring in that country was older than the authority of the
newcomers from the east, and the tradition took the trouble to reconcile the
two by making them friends.

The second is Nyai Loro Kidul, the goddess of the Southern Ocean, who becomes
the king's wife. Along the whole south coast there are remains of pre-Islamic
religious observance in the stories and in the customs, and the myth attaches
to the dynasty probably in the seventeenth century, once there was a dynasty
for it to attach to.

A newly cleared district, a settler family with no ancestral claim to it, and
two sets of powers already in residence — one holding a coconut, one living in
the sea. The house of Mataram made terms with both, and went on making them:
the *labuh* offerings its kings ordered at four places in southern central Java
— Merapi, Lawu, the south coast, and Dlepih — were still being made in the
twentieth century.
