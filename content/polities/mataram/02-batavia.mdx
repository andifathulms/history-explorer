Sultan Agung had taken every city in Java that mattered. In 1628 and again in
1629 he besieged the Dutch company's capital at Batavia, and failed both times.

He did not fail to Dutch arms. He failed to rice.

The armies he sent were large enough; the problem was feeding them four hundred
kilometres from the granary, and his solution was to position depots along the
north coast in advance. Company ships found them and burned them. The army
outside Batavia's walls starved in front of them, and what was left of it walked
home.

It is the clearest demonstration on this site of what a sea power was actually
for. Mataram could put more men in front of Batavia than the company could put
behind its walls, and could not keep them there, because everything on that coast
moved by water and the water was not his.

## The two ends of the archipelago

Set this record's `revenue_basis` beside Melaka's.

Melaka: `trade-toll` alone, no farmland at all, importing its rice largely from
Java, coded that way because a city on a strait lives on what passes it.

Mataram: `land-tax` alone, the largest agrarian state in the archipelago, with no
revenue from the sea whatever.

Three hundred miles apart, at the two extreme ends of how the archipelago's
records are coded. They used to be in the same region on this site and are not
any more, and the split is partly for this reason: a single blurb saying these
polities lived on controlling straits instead of territory was accurate about
Melaka and false about this record, and no one sentence could cover both.
This record is now in Java and Bali, whose blurb is about rice. Java is an island, its politics ran
through ports it could not hold, and every decisive thing that happened to this
kingdom happened because of the sea it did not control.

## And the title from Mecca

In 1641 an embassy came back from the Hijaz with the title of sultan, granted by
the Sharif of Mecca, and Agung used it for the rest of his reign.

This record's `legitimation` carries `investiture` for it, and this corpus
carries the Sharifate of Mecca as a record of its own — so for once both ends of
the transaction are on the site.

That value was generalised out of `caliphal-investiture` because naming a
specific grantor was the anomaly rather than the rule — most values in that
vocabulary do not name a counterparty, and the one that did was the odd one out.
Four kinds of grantor have turned up since: a caliph, a Ming emperor at Melaka, a
Chinese court granting Dai Viet the title of King of Annam, and the guardian of
the Hijaz here. Every record added since the change has made the generalisation
look more necessary.
