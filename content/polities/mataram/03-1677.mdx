Trunajaya was a Madurese prince. His allies were Makasar exiles — men who had
left South Sulawesi after this corpus's Makassar record ended in 1669, and who
had spent the intervening years as fighting men for hire in the Java Sea.

In 1677 they took the Mataram capital and drove Amangkurat I out; he died on the
road. His son appealed to the Dutch company for help, and got it, and was
restored.

That is the hinge. Before 1677 the company is a coastal power that Mataram
alternately fought and traded with. After it, the company is a participant in
Javanese succession, is paid for participating in territory and in trade rights,
and is in the field for every subsequent dispute over the throne — three of them,
in 1704, 1719 and 1746, each of which cost the winner more than it gained.

`succession_rule` on this record is null for that reason. Three wars of
succession is a pattern and a catastrophic one, and coding rule 7 asks for the
rule.

## What a diaspora did

Two records on this site have their turning points set in motion by people who
left a third one.

The Makasar who came with Trunajaya are on this page. The Bugis who took power at
Riau after 1699 are on this corpus's Johor record's last chapter. Both groups
came out of the settlement imposed on South Sulawesi in 1667 and 1669, when the
Dutch company and Arung Palakka's Bone destroyed the archipelago's last open
port.

The Makassar record's own last chapter makes the observation from that end: its
most consequential export was its own defeated. This is where one half of them
landed, and what they did was pull the company into the centre of Javanese
politics eight years after it had finished with Sulawesi.

No measure on this site registers that. There is no edge from Makassar to
Mataram — nothing succeeded anything — and no influence count for supplying other
people's wars with veterans. It is three chapters on three records pointing at
each other, which is what this site does when the thing that happened has no
field.
