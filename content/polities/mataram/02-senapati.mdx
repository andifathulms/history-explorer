Ki Pamanahan never called himself anything grander than Ki Gede Mataram, the
lord of Mataram. His son was given a name and a title together when he was
installed at the court of Pajang: Senapati-ing-Alaga, commander in war. It
stuck to the dynasty, and the kings of Mataram carried it afterwards as a
matter of course.

He had it from the sultan he would depose.

## A wall

The first thing Senapati did with Mataram, from about 1584, was build a wall
around his palace.

That sounds unremarkable and was not. Walls were a new thing in Mataram. The
older courts of east Java and the north coast had had them for a long time, and
the work at Kota Gede was done on the advice of one of the *wali* — Sunan
Kalijaga, or whoever had succeeded him as the religious authority at Kadilangu
— and the stone fort of white and red brick was finished in 1592 or 1593 by a
refugee from the ruler of Surabaya. A frontier settlement's ruler was importing
fortification from the older centres, at the same time as he was importing
their religious sanction, and both of them arrived from the coast he would
spend his life fighting.

## Everything else he did before the war

He also stopped going to court.

The annual *sowan*, the appearance before the sultan to render homage, was the
act by which a vassal was a vassal. Senapati stopped performing it. When Pajang
ordered a punishment carried out on his brother-in-law's family, he gave the
man asylum instead. And he worked on the tribute-paying lords of Kedu and
Bagelen, who were persuaded to acknowledge him rather than carry their annual
dues east to Pajang. One of them, the lord of Bocor, tried to kill him, failed,
and became a supporter.

Beside those, the chronicles set the other kind of preparation. At the flat
stone of Lipura, southwest of what is now Yogyakarta and the sacred centre of
the Mataram country, he slept and received a prophecy of his descendants'
greatness. From there he went down the Opak to the dwelling of the goddess of
the Southern Ocean, while his uncle and adviser Juru Martani went north, up
Merapi, to make contact with the powers there.

The chroniclers were writing for his descendants, and they arranged the
insubordination and the visions into a single sequence in which each authorises
the other. It is worth reading them the other way round as well: a man in a
newly walled town on the edge of the settled country needed every kind of
standing he could get, and took it from a saint at Kadilangu, a goddess in the
sea, and the tribute of Bagelen, in whatever order they became available.

## 1588

The old sultan marched, his army broke up near Prambanan when Merapi erupted,
and he died the same year. His successor from Demak lasted a year in Pajang
before Senapati and the dead sultan's son threw him out.

Only when the son formally handed Pajang over could Senapati regard himself as
an independent king in southern central Java. From that point the chronicles
give him the title *panembahan*. The date is 1588, and what he assumed with it
was not just a kingdom but a claim: as the lawful successor of the sultan, he
considered that every ruler who had been subordinate to Pajang was now
subordinate to him.

He spent the rest of his life on the difference between considering that and
achieving it.
