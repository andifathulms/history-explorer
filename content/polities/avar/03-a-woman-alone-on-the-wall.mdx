In the summer of 626 the Chagan brought the entire Avar horde to
Constantinople itself, coordinating his attack with a Persian army
under Shahvaraz that had advanced to Chalcedon on the opposite
shore of the Bosporus. The Chagan himself reached the wall with
the whole of his horde on 29 July; two days later he arrayed his
forces for battle from the gate of
Polyandrion to the gate of the Pempton, fighting from dawn until
the eleventh hour with Slavic infantry in the front rank and his
own armoured troops behind them. By evening he had begun
positioning siege engines along the wall, and within days he had
built twelve towers, covered in hides against fire, advanced
almost to the city's outworks.

## An embassy answered with contempt

The Chagan tried negotiation as well as siege-craft. He brought
Persian envoys into view of his own ambassadors' conference with
the Romans and boasted that Salbaras had promised him three
thousand men in alliance, telling the Roman officials bluntly that
unless they left their city and property behind, "it is impossible
for you to be saved, unless you can become fish and depart by sea,
or birds and ascend to the sky." The Roman envoy George refused
him flatly, and when Roman ships intercepted the Persian
ambassadors attempting to cross the Bosporus to join the Chagan
by night, the defenders beheaded two of them and sent their heads
back with a message naming exactly what had happened to the third.

## The flotilla destroyed, the Chagan's own vision

The climax came on Thursday 7 August, when the Chagan tried to
ferry the Persian army across the Golden Horn using canoes
crewed by his Slavic subjects. The Roman fleet, forewarned,
destroyed the flotilla and killed the Slavs who reached shore,
some at the Chagan's own furious order once he realized what their
failure meant for his plan. He is recorded as saying, at the
moment of the defeat, that he had seen "a woman in stately dress
rushing about on the wall all alone" - taken by the chroniclers as
a vision of the Virgin, whose shrine at Blachernae stood near
where the flotilla went down. The following night he burned his
own siege towers, palisade and engines and withdrew, insisting he
retreated only for want of supplies and not from fear, and
promising to return and finish what he had begun. He never did.
Within the year several of the Slavic peoples who had fought under
him in the siege had risen in revolt, and the Avar reach into
Roman territory that the summer of 626 represented was never
matched again.
