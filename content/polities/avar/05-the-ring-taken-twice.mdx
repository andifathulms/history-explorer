Weakened by their own civil wars and by revolts among their
Slavic subjects, the Avars nonetheless kept to their old habits,
and in 788 they raided the Lombard march of Friuli and the vassal
duchy of Bavaria at once - drawing down on themselves a reckoning
that two years later arrived in full. Charlemagne marched down the
Danube in person with an Austrasian and Saxon army, broke through
the long line of fortifications the Avars had built along their
own border, and wasted their territory as far as the river Raab,
while a separate Lombard army pushed up the Drave valley into the
heart of Pannonia, beat the Avars in open battle, and stormed
their great circular camps.

## The Ring, and Avaric trophies sent as far as Mercia

A Saxon revolt pulled Charlemagne away before the conquest could
be finished, and he never again took the field against the Avars
in person, but the war continued under his son Pippin and Eric,
duke of Friuli. Between them they captured the Ring - the great
circular stronghold between the Danube and the Theiss that served
as the Avars' central treasury and seat of power - not once but
twice, and sent its plunder back to Aachen in such quantity that
Charlemagne was able to give Avaric spoils as gifts to distant
kings, Offa of Mercia among them.

## Submission at Aachen

The plunder of the Ring broke what was left of Avar cohesion. Their
chiefs, known as tuduns, travelled to Aachen of their own accord to
do homage to Charlemagne and offered to accept Christianity; he
accepted their submission, appointed one of them to rule the
remaining Avars in his name, and had him baptized under the name
Abraham while he kept the old title of Chagan for the office. The
new vassal Chagan paid regular tribute to the Franks and kept his
people clear of the Lombard and Bavarian borders that his
predecessors had raided for two and a half centuries. Avar power on
the Danube, which had once besieged Constantinople itself, ended at
Aachen not in battle but in a baptism and an oath of tribute.
