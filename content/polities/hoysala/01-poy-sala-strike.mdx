Every Hoysala inscription that tells the dynasty's own origin story
tells some version of the same scene. A hill chief named Sala was
worshiping at a forest temple to the goddess Vasantika when a tiger
burst out of the trees. The temple's holy man, seeing the danger, threw
Sala a slender rod and shouted a single command in Kannada: "Poy,
Sala" — strike, Sala. Sala struck, and the tiger died on the spot. From
the muni's own exclamation came the name Poysala, later softened by
ordinary phonetic drift into Hoysala, and the dynasty took the tiger as
the device on its flag.

## A story that kept growing

The earliest surviving version of the story, inscribed at Belur in
1117, tells it in a plain, unadorned style. Later inscriptions could
not leave it alone. A slab of 1190 has Sala not merely killing the
tiger but raising its body on the point of his rod, which then became
his banner outright. Another version replaces the tiger with a hare
chasing a tiger; another identifies the tiger as the goddess Vasantika
herself, taking animal form to test the man who would found her
line; a fourth drops the tiger for a boar and a lion entirely.
Whichever version an inscription tells, it exists to answer the same
question every origin story of a new dynasty has to answer: why this
family, and not another, deserved to rule.

## A name in the record, at last

Legend aside, the first Hoysala ruler who can actually be dated is
Nripa Kama Hoysala, whose own inscriptions place his reign between
1022 and 1027. Whether he and the tiger-slaying Sala of the origin
story were the same man is a question the source itself cannot settle
— every genealogical inscription that survives begins with Nripa Kama
Hoysala rather than naming Sala directly, which is itself part of the
puzzle. What is certain is that from this obscure hill chieftaincy on
the slopes of the Western Ghats, ruling as a subordinate of the greater
Chalukya power to the north, grew the dynasty that would one day
govern most of Karnataka in its own right.
