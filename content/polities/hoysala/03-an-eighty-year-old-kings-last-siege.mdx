Ballala III reigned for over fifty years, from 1291 to 1342, and spent
much of that reign leading the Hindu resistance to the Delhi
Sultanate's advance into South India. By 1341 he was already
campaigning as far south as Setu, close to the Pandya country's own
southern tip, erecting a pillar of victory there before turning to the
target he had wanted all along: Madurai, held since around 1330 by an
independent Sultanate under Ghias-ud-din.

## A siege ten months in the making

Ballala marched on Madurai in 1342 with an army the Muslim chronicler
Ibn Battuta — who was a guest at the Sultan's own court not long
afterward and left a detailed account — put at a hundred thousand men,
against a garrison of only six thousand. The two armies met near a
fortress called Kabban, and Ballala's forces won outright, driving the
Sultan's men back to Madurai itself. Ballala then settled in for a
siege, and after ten months the defenders were ready to surrender the
town in exchange for safe passage — a proposal they forwarded to the
Sultan himself for his consent.

## A Friday sermon that changed everything

The Sultan's answer was not surrender but a sermon, delivered the next
Friday, that moved his starving subjects to swear they would die for
him rather than give up the city. The following morning six thousand
cavalry rode out under the Sultan's own personal command. Ballala's
army, which had grown complacent through ten quiet months of siege and
had sent its horses out to graze, mistook the advancing force for a
band of raiders rather than the garrison's whole strength, and broke
in confusion. Ballala himself, eighty years old, tried to mount and
flee but was captured.

## A body treated as a warning

He was kept alive only until his captors had extracted everything of
value from him; then he was killed. Ibn Battuta, who saw the aftermath
with his own eyes, recorded that his skin was stuffed with straw and
hung from the walls of Madurai, still visible there when he passed
through the city himself. Ballala's son, Ballala IV, held a nominal
crown for a few more years, but his own power, the source notes,
"could not have been much more than that of an ordinary chieftain."
The military leadership of the wider Hindu coalition Ballala III had
spent decades holding together passed instead to a rising power at
Vijayanagara, which absorbed what remained of the Hoysala kingdom
within a few years of his death without any recorded resistance —
early Vijayanagara inscriptions describe its own kings simply as
ruling "the Hoysala kingdom" outright.
