title: A kingdom and a conversion in one year

Vishnuvardhana ruled for thirty-seven years, from 1104 to 1141, and the
source describes his reign as packed with military campaigns from
start to finish. He never did manage to throw off the Chalukya
overlordship his family had served for generations — that ambition
failed repeatedly — but he raised his own territory from a subordinate
chieftaincy to something his successors could build into an empire in
its own right.

## A capital lost for two centuries, retaken in one campaign

The centerpiece of that transformation was Talakad, the old Ganga
capital that the Cholas had held since conquering it around 1004.
Vishnuvardhana sent a large army under his general Ganga Raja, so
confident of the outcome that he granted Ganga Raja land in
anticipation of a victory not yet won. The campaign delivered: in
1116, the Hoysala army routed the Chola garrison and took Talakad
outright, ending two centuries in which the old Ganga territory of
Gangavadi had passed between Chola and Chalukya hands without ever
returning to a power that could call it home. In the same year,
Vishnuvardhana's forces sacked forts deep in Pandya territory as well,
a double campaign that announced the new kingdom's reach in both
directions at once.

## A change of faith in the same breath as a change of fortune

The year of the Talakad victory was also the year Vishnuvardhana
himself converted from Jainism, the faith of his predecessors, to
Vaishnavism. The source finds no sign of fanaticism in his character,
but notes that his old rivals — the Changalvas and Kongalvas to the
south, still Jain, and the Cholas, intolerant Saivites — gave his new
religious zeal an edge that fed directly into his military ambitions.
Conversion and conquest, in Vishnuvardhana's own reign, were not two
separate stories but one.

## An inheritance built to last

Vishnuvardhana did not live to see the Chalukya yoke broken outright,
and later Hoysala kings would spend generations doing what he could
not. But the kingdom he built at Belur and later at Dwarasamudra
outlived the Chalukyas who had once ruled over it, and every Hoysala
king who followed him ruled a state Vishnuvardhana's own campaigns had
made, not one he had merely inherited.
