`military_basis` on this record reads `conscript`, and it is the largest
peacetime military obligation any polity on this site imposed.

Every registered adult male owed two years: one of training and service in his
home commandery, one on the frontier or in the capital guard, with a liability to
be recalled until he was in his fifties. The registers that made it possible are
the same registers the poll tax was assessed on, and a census of 2 CE survives
that counts fifty-seven million people in something over twelve million
households — the earliest population figure of that scale anywhere, and one this
site does not enter in `peak_population` because it is a tax document rather than
a demographic estimate and the difference matters.

## What it was spent on

The Xiongnu, mostly. This corpus's Xiongnu record is the confederation on the
other side of that frontier, and the two records are a matched pair: a settled
empire that could raise enormous numbers of infantry cheaply and could not
force a battle, against a mobile confederation that could always decline one.

Wu's answer was to buy cavalry — horses from Ferghana, and eventually Xiongnu
cavalry who had surrendered — and to push the frontier west along the Gansu
corridor, which is what put Han garrisons in the oases and opened the route this
record's first chapter is about.

## The failure mode

Conscription of that kind depends on a registered smallholding peasantry, and the
Han did not keep one. Land accumulated: by the first century CE much of the
population was tenants of large estates, off the registers and unavailable to the
state, and the militia system decayed accordingly.

What replaced it, by the end, was provincial commanders raising their own troops
to suppress the rising this record's third turning point describes. They did
suppress it, and then would not disband, and the empire was divided between them.

It is the same sequence this corpus's Tang record carries seven hundred years
later, with different names: a militia resting on an allotted-land system, the
land system eroding, professional regional armies replacing it, and one of their
commanders taking the capital. Two of the four longest Chinese dynasties on this
site, ended the same way by the same mechanism, and the second one had the first
one's history written down and available.
