Two later states claimed to be the Han, and the second of them is one of the
strangest claims of legitimacy anybody has ever made.

## Shu Han

When the empire broke into three in 220, the ruler of the western kingdom, Liu
Bei, was a member of the Han imperial clan by a distant line, and named his state
Han. He was not claiming to succeed the dynasty; he was claiming to *be* it,
continuing, with the other two kingdoms as usurpers.

Whether the claim was any good is a separate question from whether it was made.
Chinese historiography spent the next fifteen hundred years arguing about which
of the three kingdoms held the legitimate line — Chen Shou put the Wei first,
Zhu Xi put Shu Han first, and the argument was still live in the Qing — which is
about as clear a sign as there is that people took it seriously.

## Liu Yuan

In 304 a Xiongnu leader declared a dynasty in Shanxi and called it Han.

His argument was this. The Han had bought peace on the northern frontier for
generations with the *heqin* treaties — a Chinese princess sent north to marry a
Xiongnu ruler, with silk and grain. His line descended from one of those
marriages. He therefore stood in the maternal line of the Han imperial house, and
when the Chinese dynasty then reigning collapsed, he was as entitled to the
mandate as anyone.

He sacrificed to the Han emperors, took a Han reign style, and his successors
held both Chinese capitals within a decade.

The Han and the Xiongnu are usually read as a frontier: the empire and the
confederation, the wall and the steppe. What this claim says is that the
frontier was also a family relationship, created deliberately by the Han as an
instrument of policy, and that four hundred years later somebody presented the
bill.

It is about the only claim to an empire ever built out of a treaty rather than
out of a conquest or a line of rulers — and the treaty was one the claiming side
had been paid to sign.
