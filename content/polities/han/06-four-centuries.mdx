The Han inherited the unified state the Qin had built by force and made it
durable, which is the harder achievement. Over four centuries they established
the machinery that Chinese empire used until the twentieth: a salaried
bureaucracy recruited partly on merit, a state monopoly on iron and salt,
official histories, and Confucian doctrine as the training and the justification
of officials.

## The machine

The list is worth being specific about, because every item on it outlived the
dynasty by a millennium and a half.

A salaried civil service, paid in grain and cash rather than in land, appointed and
transferable by the centre and therefore not hereditary. A territory divided into
commanderies and counties with a governor in each, appointed from the capital. A
census of households and individuals, taken regularly, on which the tax and the
conscription were assessed — the earliest such counts anywhere that survive as
figures. A uniform written law. A standing granary system for price stabilisation.
A state monopoly on salt and iron. An imperial academy teaching a fixed canon. And
an official history of the preceding dynasty, compiled by the succeeding one, which
became the obligation of every dynasty afterwards and is the reason China has a
continuous documentary record that no other civilisation can match.

## What the Han added to the Qin

The Qin had built most of the apparatus and had lasted fifteen years.

What the Han added was a theory of why the apparatus should be obeyed, and it was
explicitly a theory about the Qin's failure: that an empire taken by force cannot be
held by the same means. The doctrine that emerged — a Confucian ethics of
government grafted onto Legalist machinery — made the official's education, rather
than his birth or his troops, the ground of his authority, and made the ruler
answerable to something other than his own will.

It was frequently a fiction in practice. It was still the language in which every
subsequent dynasty had to justify itself, including every foreign one.

## The dividing line

The dynasty is conventionally split in two, and the break is real.

The Western Han ruled from Chang'an until AD 9, when the regent Wang Mang took the
throne outright and declared a new dynasty. He held it for fourteen years, attempted
a radical programme — land nationalisation, abolition of slavery, currency reform —
and was destroyed by rebellion, famine and the Yellow River changing its course.
A Han claimant restored the dynasty in AD 25 and ruled from Luoyang.

The Eastern Han was never the same state. The great landholding families that had
raised the restoration were the price of it; they were never brought back under
control; and by the second century the provinces were run by magnates with private
armies and a court paralysed by the struggle between eunuchs and officials.

The dynasty fell apart through that century into warlordism and ended in 220, pulled
to pieces by its own generals and provincial families. The three kingdoms that
followed each claimed to be its legitimate continuation, and one of them took the
throne by an abdication ceremony rather than a conquest — which is itself a Han
legacy, and the form every later usurpation would use.
