In 200 BC the founder of the Han dynasty took an army of three hundred and twenty
thousand men north to deal with the Xiongnu and spent seven days surrounded,
cut off from his own army, in a fort near modern Datong.

## Pingcheng

It was a bitter winter and about a quarter of the Chinese host had lost fingers to
frostbite before the fighting started.

Modu used the standard Xiongnu method — feigned defeat, best troops out of sight,
and a long enticing retreat northwards — and Gaozu, who had outrun the bulk of his
own infantry, walked into it. Three hundred thousand Xiongnu horse closed round
him.

Parker preserves the detail that makes it real: the white, grey, black and chestnut
horses were massed in four separate bodies, one at each point of the compass. It
must have been, he says, a very picturesque affair.

Then Modu hesitated, suspecting a trap, and lost his chance — as Attila would at
the Catalaunian Plains six centuries later. How the emperor got out is not
recorded, but it is clearly hinted that discreditable negotiations were conducted
with the shanyu's consort, and that one corner of the encircling line was left
unguarded. Gaozu rode out through it.

## The price

What followed was the settlement that governed the next seventy years, and it is
worth stating in its own terms because later Chinese history is careful not to.

A princess of the imperial blood was sent to the shanyu in marriage. With her went
an annual subsidy: silk piece-goods, waste silk for wadding garments, rice wine and
choice foodstuffs.

The man who proposed this policy was made the envoy who carried it out, and the
official rationale was long-term — the children of a Chinese consort would one day
be shanyu, and the empire would have a claim on them. Parker points out what
actually happened to that reasoning five hundred years later, when Xiongnu princes
descended from Han consorts claimed the throne of China as the only surviving
legitimists, and got it.

The subsidy moderated the raiding. It did not stop it.

## The letter

Gaozu died. His widow, the empress dowager Lü, put her own son on the throne, had
her rival killed with great cruelty, and then ruled for nearly a decade in her own
name as a recognised monarch.

Modu — prompted, Parker suspects, by one of the many Chinese renegades in his
service — wrote to her offering his hand and heart.

The letter caused a tremendous flutter in the imperial council, and the question
was whether to send back a civil answer or the envoy's head. There were braggart
generals ready to fan the dowager's fury. What cooled her was one cautious old
statesman reminding her that boys in the streets were still singing a popular song
about her late husband's narrow escape from the beleaguered town.

So a very diplomatic reply went north. It thanked the shanyu for the honour, and
pointed out that the condition of the dowager's teeth and hair was unequal to the
task of securing his affections. Two royal carriages and teams of horses went with
it.

## Why this is the beginning of the record and not an anecdote

Because it fixes what the Han was for the next seven decades: a state that had
unified China, inherited the Qin's machinery, and could not defend its own northern
frontier except by payment.

Every institution the dynasty is remembered for was built in the reaction against
that. The state monopolies were created to finance the war that ended the subsidy.
The road west was opened to find allies against the Xiongnu. The conscript armies,
the frontier colonies, the horse-breeding programmes and the enormous deficits all
begin with the seven days at Pingcheng.

A great deal of Chinese imperial history is the working out of one bad afternoon in
the snow.
