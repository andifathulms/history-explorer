From 117 BC the Han state took the production and sale of salt and of iron, and
later of liquor, out of private hands. It is the most thoroughly argued fiscal
institution anywhere in the ancient world, and the reason we can argue about it
is that both sides of the argument survive.

## What was taken over

Emperor Wu had been at war with the Xiongnu for twenty years by then, and the
ordinary taxes did not cover it. The land tax stood at a thirtieth of the harvest
and was deliberately light; the poll tax fell on people who could not pay much
more.

Salt and iron were different. Everybody needs salt, nobody can make it at home,
and it is produced at a small number of identifiable places — coastal pans and
inland wells. Iron is the same shape of commodity. A state that controls a few
hundred production sites can tax an entire population without knowing where any
of them live.

That is the whole idea, and it worked well enough that versions of it were still
being used in China two thousand years later.

## The transcript

In 81 BC, six years after Wu died, the court held a debate on whether to keep the
monopolies.

On one side were the officials who ran them, arguing fiscal necessity and
frontier defence. On the other was a delegation of Confucian scholars, arguing
that the state had gone into business, was competing with its own people, and had
made profit a respectable motive at court. A version of the proceedings was
written up a generation later as the *Discourses on Salt and Iron*, and it is a
substantial book.

The scholars lost on salt and iron and won on liquor.

It is worth appreciating how unusual that record is. For most of the states on
these pages, we know what was taxed and can only infer why. Here the fiscal
policy of an empire was put on trial by its own officials, in front of the
throne, and somebody wrote down what both sides said.
