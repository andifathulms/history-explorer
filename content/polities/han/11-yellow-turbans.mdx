The Han ended twice: once in fact, in 189, and once on paper, in 220, and the
thirty-one years between the two are the useful part.

## What was already broken

By the middle of the second century the court was a three-way fight between the
emperor's consort families, the eunuchs of the palace, and the scholar-officials
who had been trained to think of themselves as the empire's conscience.

The officials lost. In the proscriptions of the 160s and 170s hundreds were
banned from office, imprisoned or killed for factional association, and a class
that had been the state's administrative backbone was taught that the throne was
its enemy. That lesson did not unlearn itself.

Underneath the court, the fiscal base had been eroding for a century in the way
these systems erode: land accumulating in great estates, the households on them
disappearing from the tax registers, and the registered population — the people
who paid and served — shrinking while the actual population did not.

## 184

The Yellow Turbans were a religious movement, which is the part usually skipped
over.

Zhang Jue's Way of Great Peace was a healing cult with a doctrine, an
organisation of thirty-six divisions across eight provinces, and a date: the
*jiazi* year, 184, at which the blue heaven would die and the yellow heaven be
established. Hundreds of thousands rose in the same season across half the
empire.

It was suppressed within about a year, and suppressing it destroyed the dynasty.

To fight a rising on that scale the court gave provincial governors military
authority and let commanders raise their own troops locally. The armies that
resulted belonged to the men who had raised them. Within five years those men
were the real government, and in 189 one of them marched into the capital,
massacred the eunuchs, deposed one emperor, installed another and burned
Luoyang.

## The emperor who lasted thirty years

Emperor Xian was put on the throne as a child by a warlord and spent his entire
reign in the custody of one commander or another. From 196 he was held by Cao
Cao, who used him exactly as such a person is used: every campaign Cao Cao
fought was fought under an imperial commission, against rebels, on behalf of a
dynasty he was in the process of replacing.

That is worth being clear about, because it explains why the formal ending is
so late. The Han name was worth having. A general who held the emperor could
issue lawful orders to men who would not have obeyed him otherwise, and the
whole point of the mandate doctrine — that legitimate authority is a real thing
and not merely a possession — made the puppet valuable.

In 220 Cao Cao died and his son stopped bothering. Emperor Xian abdicated in
his favour, in a carefully staged ceremony with three ritual refusals, and was
given a fief and allowed to live. He outlived the man who took his throne by
several years.

## What ended

Not the country, not the institutions and not the idea. The commandery system,
the registers, the examinations, the canon and the mandate doctrine all went
straight into the states that fought over the wreckage, and all of them claimed
to be continuing something.

<Figure id="hn-banquet" />

What ended was four hundred years of one family, and the population registers.
The census of 2 CE counted fifty-seven million people. The next one anybody was
in a position to take, in 280, counted about sixteen million.

Almost nobody now reads that as a death toll of forty million. The state that
did the counting had come apart, and the great estates that had been absorbing
households for two centuries had no interest in reporting them. Uncounted is
not dead. But the fact that the number could fall that far, and that no
government could establish what had actually happened to its own people, is a
better measure of what the end of the Han was than any battle in it.
