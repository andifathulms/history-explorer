`successor_claims` on this record read null until this pass — a gap, meaning
nobody had opened a source. It reads two now, and the second is one of the
strangest entries in this column anywhere on the site.

## Shu Han

When the empire broke into three in 220, the ruler of the western kingdom, Liu
Bei, was a member of the Han imperial clan by a distant line, and named his state
Han. He was not claiming to succeed the dynasty; he was claiming to *be* it,
continuing, with the other two kingdoms as usurpers.

Coding rule 1 counts a later polity asserting an earlier one's authority and does
not ask whether the assertion is any good. Later historiography spent centuries
arguing about which of the three kingdoms held the legitimate line, which is
about as clear a sign as there is that a claim was made and taken seriously.

## Liu Yuan

In 304 a Xiongnu leader declared a dynasty in Shanxi and called it Han.

His argument was this. The Han had bought peace on the northern frontier for
generations with the *heqin* treaties — a Chinese princess sent north to marry a
Xiongnu ruler, with silk and grain. His line descended from one of those
marriages. He therefore stood in the maternal line of the Han imperial house, and
when the Chinese dynasty then reigning collapsed, he was as entitled to the
mandate as anyone.

He sacrificed to the Han emperors, took a Han reign style, and his successors
held both Chinese capitals within a decade.

This corpus carries the Xiongnu as a record of its own, and the two pages are
usually read as a frontier: the empire and the confederation, the wall and the
steppe. What this claim says is that the frontier was also a family relationship,
created deliberately by the Han as an instrument of policy, and that four hundred
years later somebody presented the bill.

It is the clearest instance on this site of a successor claim built out of a
treaty rather than out of a conquest or a genealogy of rulers — and the treaty
was one the claiming side had been paid to sign.
