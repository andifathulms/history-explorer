The heir the Han court settled on in 74 BC had spent his childhood not knowing
who he was.

## A prince kept hidden

Wu's own great-grandson, the boy who would become Emperor Xuan, had been raised
in obscurity by an official named Bingji, entrusted with the secret of the
child's birth during years too dangerous to reveal it. When the throne fell
vacant a second time in a matter of months - the boy emperor Zhao dead without
an heir, and the uncle briefly installed after him deposed within weeks for
disgracing the office with his own coarse conduct - it was Bingji who finally
produced the right heir for the regent Huo Guang to raise to the throne, at
seventeen.

## A kingmaker who declined to be king

Huo Guang had already made and unmade one emperor in a matter of months and had
every means available to make himself more than a minister. He did not take it.
He devoted himself instead, in the record's own phrase, to perfecting the new
emperor in the science of government, and within a short time had ceded actual
rule back to Xuan rather than governing through him. It is a restraint the same
record treats as genuinely rare - a minister content to be the power behind a
throne he could as easily have simply taken.

## The troops of justice

The reign's early foreign policy showed what that restored authority could do
without conquest. The Xiongnu confederation, already splitting into rival
chieftains fighting each other as bitterly as they had ever fought China,
began sending princes to surrender at the frontier rather than face it as an
enemy. Xuan's reputation for fair dealing with those who submitted did the rest:
for the first time, Chinese troops became known among the peoples of eastern
Asia as "the troops of justice" - not conquerors but a police force backing
the weaker side against the strong. By 51 BC every people between Shaanxi and
the Caspian Sea, in the record's own claim, acknowledged Han supremacy, and
Xuan marked the moment by building a hall of portraits - the Qilin Pavilion -
honouring the generals and ministers who had achieved it, Huo Guang's own image
foremost among them.

## What one woman's ambition cost

The one shadow across the reign came from inside Huo Guang's own family. His
wife, unsatisfied that her husband held real power without the title, worked to
put her own daughter on the throne as empress - and when Xuan refused to set
aside his first wife for her, arranged to have that first wife poisoned by a
physician in her own pay. Huo Guang, told the truth only after the murder had
already succeeded, chose to protect his family's name rather than expose his
wife, and the daughter was duly proclaimed empress.

Xuan's own sense of justice did not bend to it. When he named an heir apparent
two years later, he passed over Huo Guang's grandchildren and chose the murdered
wife's own son instead. The discovery of a further plot to poison that boy
finally brought the whole affair into the open: Huo Guang's wife and family were
executed or ordered to take their own lives, a stain the record says fell across
Huo Guang's own reputation for a crime that was never his.

## What twenty-five years built

Xuan died in 49 BC at forty-two, after a reign the record credits with
simplifying the law and governing with a rigor his predecessors had not
managed - though it adds, fairly, that much of his success rested on reforms
Wu himself had set in motion decades earlier and never lived to see repaid.
Between the frontier crisis Wu's own wars had left unresolved and the palace
intrigues that would eventually deliver the throne to Wang Mang two generations
later, Xuan's twenty-five years are the one clear stretch of the late Western
Han in which the machinery Wu built and the treasury Wu emptied were both, for
a while, put back in working order.
