The Han went west for a military reason and got a trade route by accident, and
the accident lasted longer than the reason.

## Zhang Qian

In about 139 BC Emperor Wu sent an envoy to find the Yuezhi, a people the
Xiongnu had driven out of the Gansu corridor a generation earlier, and propose
an alliance against them.

Zhang Qian was captured by the Xiongnu almost at once and held for about ten
years. He escaped, went on west anyway, found the Yuezhi in Bactria — settled,
prosperous and entirely uninterested in a revenge war three thousand kilometres
behind them — and came home after thirteen years with two of the hundred men he
had left with.

He had failed. What he brought back instead was a description: Ferghana and its
horses, Bactria, Parthia, Sogdiana, a settled and wealthy world west of the
desert that nobody at Chang'an had known was there, and — the detail that
mattered most to the court — Chinese goods on sale in Bactrian markets, which
meant a route already existed.

## What was actually being bought

The Han did not go west for silk. It went west for horses, and for the flank.

The horses are literal. Ferghana bred an animal larger than anything in China,
and Wu wanted them badly enough to send two expeditions across the Taklamakan —
the second with sixty thousand men — to get them, in a war fought over
bloodstock. The strategic argument was better: the Gansu corridor separated the
Xiongnu from the Qiang peoples to their south, and holding the Tarim oases cut
the Xiongnu off from the settled communities whose grain and manufactures a
chanyu had to distribute in order to be obeyed.

Wu took the corridor in the 120s BC and planted four commanderies across it.
Everything else follows from that: garrison colonies, a chain of posts and
beacon towers running out into the desert, and a permanent contest with the
Xiongnu for the oases.

## The cost

It nearly bankrupted the state, and the record of it is unusually good because
the Han argued about the bill in public.

The campaigns ran for decades and consumed the treasury. The horse losses were
catastrophic. To pay for it the government took over the production of salt and
iron, sold offices and titles, debased the coinage, and imposed a property tax
on merchants that was enforced by inviting informers to denounce them for a
share of the proceeds.

By the end of Wu's reign the empire was larger, the frontier was quieter and
the fiscal system had been rebuilt around monopolies. In 89 BC the emperor
issued an edict of repentance, abandoning further expansion.

## What was left when the reason went away

The Xiongnu split in the first century BC and stopped being the threat the road
had been opened against. The road stayed open.

Silk went west along it in quantity, through Parthian and Sogdian
intermediaries, to Rome, where the elder Pliny complained about the drain of
gold. The two empires knew of each other at several removes and never made
contact: an envoy sent west in 97 got as far as the Persian Gulf and was talked
out of going further by Parthian intermediaries with every interest in keeping
the trade indirect.

Buddhism came the other way, along the same route, into an empire that had
opened it to buy cavalry.
