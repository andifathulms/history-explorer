The Han spent its first seventy years paying a neighbour it could not beat, and
the man who built that neighbour into an empire did it by teaching his guard to
shoot whatever he shot at.

## Tuman's mistake

North of the Ordos the Xiongnu were ruled by a *shanyu* — Parker renders it
*Jenuye* — called Tuman, who in his old age fell under the influence of a much
loved young queen and agreed to make her son his heir in place of the legitimate
one.

That legitimate son was Modu, and he was an able captain. To be rid of him, Tuman
sent him as a hostage to the Yuezhi and then attacked them, expecting them to kill
the boy in their indignation. Modu took one of their fastest horses and rode home.

His father was so pleased with the feat that he gave him command of ten thousand
troopers.

## The arrow

Modu was not pleased. He invented a new kind of arrow — a whistling arrow, of the
sort the early Japanese called *nari-kabura* — and made a rule: whatever he shot at
with it, every man of his was to shoot at instantly.

He tested them on his own best horse. Several troopers who did not shoot were
executed. He tested them on his favourite wife. Several more were executed.

Then he waited until his father was out hunting, and fired a whistling arrow at
him. Tuman fell dead, pierced through and through, and Modu was proclaimed in his
place; a general massacre of his father's family and household followed.

## Rope

What he did next is the part worth reading, because it is not the behaviour of a
berserker.

The Donghu — Tungus, on the other side of a three-hundred-mile strip of neutral
desert — heard about the parricide and sent to demand his finest horses as the
price of leaving him alone. Modu publicly rebuked the members of his council who
wanted war and sent the horses.

The Donghu, encouraged, demanded one of his favourite wives. The council was
appalled. He sent her.

Then, having concluded that he had no stomach for a fight, they massed troops and
demanded the neutral strip itself. Some of his councillors were inclined to give up
what they called a useless piece of land.

He had them beheaded, declared war, and announced that any able-bodied man who did
not report to headquarters at once would be executed. The Donghu, who had taken no
precautions because they had decided he was a coward, were broken in a single
campaign; their herds were driven off and most of the people enslaved. A remnant
escaped north-east of what is now Peking, where — Parker notes, because he is
writing a thousand years of this — they became a formidable power of their own in
due course.

## What Modu had made

An empire of the steppe with a chain of command that reached from the shanyu to
the individual trooper, held by terror and drilled to act on a signal.

Parker's observation about how such things are assembled is worth having, because
it explains why the same ground produces one of these every few centuries. After a
great battle the captured young men became warriors, usually under their own
chiefs but subject to the conqueror; the old men were turned out to tend the flocks;
and the flocks changed masters for a few years until the next revolution. Master
and slave lived much the same way. Languages mixed, tribes merged, and no horde can
be given a fixed location or an exclusive nationality.

That is the thing the Han now had to deal with on a frontier two thousand miles
long, and it had just finished its own civil war.
