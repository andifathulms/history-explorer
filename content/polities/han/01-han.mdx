---
title: Four centuries, and a road west
drafted_from: loewe-twitchett-1986
phase: peak
---

The Han inherited the unified state the Qin had built by force and made it
durable, which is the harder achievement. Over four centuries they established
the machinery that Chinese empire used until the twentieth: a salaried
bureaucracy recruited partly on merit, a state monopoly on iron and salt,
official histories, and Confucian doctrine as the training and the justification
of officials.

Under Emperor Wu the empire pushed west into the Tarim basin, against the Xiongnu
confederation on this site's inner-asia page, and in doing so opened the routes
that carried silk to Parthia and eventually to Rome. The Han and the Romans knew
of each other's existence, vaguely and at several removes, and never made
contact; a Han envoy sent west in 97 got as far as the Persian Gulf and was
talked out of continuing by Parthian intermediaries with an interest in the trade
staying indirect.

The dynasty fell apart through the second century into warlordism and ended in
220. The site types that `fragmentation`: the state was pulled apart by its own
generals and provincial magnates, and the three kingdoms that followed each
claimed to be its legitimate continuation.
