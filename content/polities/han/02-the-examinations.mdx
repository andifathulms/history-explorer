---
title: Recruiting a bureaucracy
drafted_from: loewe-twitchett-1986
phase: aside
---

The Han began the practice that separates Chinese imperial administration from
every other polity on this site: recruiting officials by examination on a body of
texts, rather than by birth, purchase, military service or personal following.

Under the Han it was partial — recommendation by local officials mattered more
than examination, and great families dominated office in practice — and the
system only took its mature form centuries later. But the principle was
established, along with the Imperial Academy to teach the canon and the idea
that the texts an official had studied were the qualification for governing.

Set that against the rest of this corpus. The Abbasids ran on a Persian
secretarial class and then on Turkish slave soldiers. The Umayyads ran on Syrian
tribal levies. The Mamluks were governed by their own purchased troops; the
Ottomans by a slave-recruited administration; the Mongols by whoever could count.
Every one of those solved the problem of finding reliable servants by controlling
the servant's origin — buy him young, take him from a tribe, import him from
elsewhere.

China solved it by controlling the servant's education. It is the only durable
alternative anyone in this corpus found, and it is why the Chinese state survived
conquest by the Khitan, the Jurchen, the Mongols and eventually the Manchus with
its administrative machinery substantially intact: the conquerors on this site's
Liao, Jin and Yuan pages all had to decide what to do about a bureaucracy that
regenerated itself from books.
