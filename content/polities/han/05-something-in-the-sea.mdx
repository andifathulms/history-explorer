In AD 97 the general Ban Chao, who had spent thirty years making the Tarim basin
Chinese, sent an officer named Gan Ying west to find the country the Chinese called
Da Qin. He reached the Persian Gulf, asked about the crossing, and turned round.

## What he was told

The account is in the *Hou Han Shu*, and Hirth translates it without softening
anything.

Gan Ying arrived at Tiaozhi, on the coast of the great sea, and was about to take
his passage. The sailors of the western frontier of Parthia told him:

> The sea is vast and great; with favourable winds it is possible to cross within
> three months; but if you meet slow winds, it may also take you two years. It is
> for this reason that those who go to sea take on board a supply of three years'
> provisions. There is something in the sea which is apt to make man home-sick, and
> several have thus lost their lives.

> When Kan-ying heard this, he stopped.

## Who was telling him

Parthian sailors, on the Parthian shore, about a voyage Parthian middlemen made
their living from.

The route from Tiaozhi to the Syrian port of Aelana at the head of the Gulf of
Aqaba was regular traffic; Gan Ying was talking to the very men who made it. A
crossing described as three months with a fair wind and two years with a foul one,
requiring three years' stores, is not a description of that voyage. It is a
description designed to stop somebody making it.

The Parthians had every reason. Chinese silk reached Rome through their hands and
was taxed on the way, and a direct Han embassy to the Roman east would have made
that unnecessary. The one sentence about something in the sea that makes men
homesick, and has killed several, is the flourish of a man who knows his audience
is a long way from home.

Hirth's judgement is that Gan Ying, having talked to the sailors who actually made
that journey, had the best opportunity anyone had for collecting information about
the object of his mission. He collected it, and it was wrong on purpose.

## What the Han knew anyway

Enough to want to go.

The Chinese histories record an embassy in AD 87 offering lions and a hornless
unicorn, and in 101 the Parthian king sending lions and large birds from Tiaozhi
which were thereafter called Parthian birds — ostriches. They record the road west
in stages with distances, from Parthia to Aman to Sibin to Yulo on the extreme
western frontier, and then south by sea to Da Qin.

They also record, later, that Da Qin was governed from a great city, that its
people were tall and honest and resembled the Chinese — *which is why it is called
Da Qin*, the great Qin — that they made money of gold and silver, and that they had
always wanted to send embassies to China and been prevented by the Parthians, who
wanted to keep the silk trade in their own hands.

That last claim is in the Chinese record, made by the Chinese, about the people who
had just talked their envoy out of crossing.

## What did not happen

The two ends of Eurasia were never joined.

An embassy arrived at the Han court in AD 166 claiming to come from the king of Da
Qin, Andun — Marcus Aurelius Antoninus — and brought ivory, rhinoceros horn and
tortoiseshell, which the Chinese officials noted were ordinary tropical goods and
not the treasures a great empire would send. The likeliest explanation is a party
of merchants improving their standing at Canton.

So the record of contact between Rome and Han China amounts to a mission turned
back by a shipping line's sales talk, and a probable fraud sixty-nine years later.
Two empires of comparable size, each of which knew the other existed, each of which
had a name for the other, neither of which ever heard from the other directly.

*When Kan-ying heard this, he stopped.* Very few sentences in any record cost so
much.
