The Han put a peacetime military obligation on its whole adult male population
that almost no pre-modern state has matched.

Every registered adult male owed two years: one of training and service in his
home commandery, one on the frontier or in the capital guard, with a liability to
be recalled until he was in his fifties. The registers that made it possible are
the same registers the poll tax was assessed on, and a census of 2 CE survives
that counts fifty-seven million people in something over twelve million
households.

That is the earliest population figure of that scale from anywhere on earth, and
it should be read for what it is: a tax document. It counts households the state
could reach, not people who existed, and the difference between those two numbers
is the entire fiscal history of the dynasty.

## What it was spent on

The Xiongnu, mostly, and the two states are a matched pair: a settled empire
that could raise enormous numbers of infantry cheaply and could not force a
battle, against a mobile confederation that could always decline one.

Wu's answer was to buy cavalry — horses from Ferghana, and eventually Xiongnu
cavalry who had surrendered — and to push the frontier west along the Gansu
corridor, which is what put Han garrisons in the oases and opened the road
west.

## The failure mode

Conscription of that kind depends on a registered smallholding peasantry, and the
Han did not keep one. Land accumulated: by the first century CE much of the
population was tenants of large estates, off the registers and unavailable to the
state, and the militia system decayed accordingly.

What replaced it, by the end, was provincial commanders raising their own troops
to suppress the Yellow Turban rising. They did suppress it, and then would not
disband, and the empire was divided between them.

The Tang ran the same sequence seven hundred years later with different names: a
militia resting on an allotted-land system, the land system eroding,
professional regional armies replacing it, and one of their commanders taking
the capital. Two of the longest dynasties in Chinese history, ended the same way
by the same mechanism — and the second one had the first one's history written
down and on the shelf.
