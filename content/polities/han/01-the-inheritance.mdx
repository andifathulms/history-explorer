The Han did not build the Chinese empire. The Qin did, in about fifteen years,
by force, and then fell apart within four years of its founder's death. What the
Han did was work out how to keep one — which turned out to be the harder problem
and took them a century.

## What was on the table in 206 BC

When the Qin collapsed, the obvious thing to do was go back. The pre-Qin order
had been a set of hereditary kingdoms under a nominal Zhou king, and most of the
men fighting over the wreckage wanted that restored with themselves in it.

Liu Bang — a village headman of no family, which is worth stating plainly
because nothing like it happens again in Chinese history for fifteen hundred
years — won the war and then did neither thing. He kept the Qin apparatus of
commanderies and counties under centrally appointed officials for about half the
empire, and handed the other half out as kingdoms to his relatives and his
generals.

That was a compromise made under duress and it contained its own crisis. The
kingdoms grew, the generals were purged one by one, the kingdoms passed to Liu
relatives instead, and in 154 BC seven of them revolted at once. The revolt was
crushed inside three months, and after it the kings kept their titles and their
revenues and lost their governments. The commandery system quietly ate the rest
of the country.

## The argument about the Qin

The other thing the early Han had to do was explain why it was not the Qin, and
the explanation became the political theory of Chinese empire.

The verdict the Han settled on was that the Qin had failed through harshness:
too much law, too much labour service, too much punishment, no benevolence, and
therefore the loss of Heaven's mandate. Jia Yi's essay on the faults of Qin, in
the 170s BC, is the canonical statement, and its argument is that an empire
taken by force cannot be held by the same means.

That verdict is a piece of dynastic self-justification and it is also the
foundation of a doctrine with teeth. If Heaven withdraws its mandate from a
ruler who governs badly, then rebellion against a bad ruler is not treason but
evidence, and the dynasty that wins was owed the throne. Every Chinese dynasty
afterwards ruled on that proposition and every one of them was eventually
overthrown by it.

## The apparatus

Underneath the theory, what the early Han kept was Qin machinery: the
commandery and county administration, the standardised script, the registers, a
uniform law, weights and measures, and the roads.

What it added was people to run them who were not soldiers. From about 130 BC
the state established an Imperial Academy, began recruiting officials partly on
recommendation and examination in a canon of texts, and made a body of learning
the qualification for office.

That is the invention. The Qin had an administrative machine and staffed it
with clerks and officers. The Han built a class whose claim to govern was that
they had read the right books, and who therefore had views about how the empire
ought to be run and a vocabulary for arguing about it with the throne.

The argument started almost immediately, and eighty years later it produced the
most fully documented policy debate in the ancient world.
