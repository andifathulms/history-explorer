Ala-ud-Din Hasan's son Muhammad Shah inherited a kingdom the Rajas of
Vijayanagara and Warangal still hoped to roll back. Early in his
reign he found Hindu bankers in both kingdoms melting down Bahmani
gold and silver coin so that only Hindu currency would circulate in
the Deccan; he put a number of them to death and restricted the mint
and the banking trade to a few families descended from Delhi
emigrants. When the two Rajas then demanded the return of territory
his father had won, Muhammad Shah stalled through eighteen months of
embassies while he shored up his own treasury and purged officers he
distrusted, then went to war and won a quick victory that forced
tribute and elephants from both rivals.

## A jest, an oath, and a war without mercy

The peace did not last. A drunken order for a lavish gift drawn on the
Vijayanagara treasury, and the Raja's contemptuous refusal to honor
it, gave Muhammad Shah his pretext. After the Raja's own troops
massacred every inhabitant of the frontier town of Mudkal, the sultan
swore never to sheathe his sword until a hundred thousand "infidels"
had died in revenge, crossed the Krishna with a picked force, and
routed the Hindu camp in a night attack that Firishta - a court
historian writing two centuries later, and no neutral witness by his
own account's admission - says left seventy thousand dead in a single
morning. The war dragged on with such severity that Firishta records
the sultan halting for three days of celebration each time his own
armies had killed another twenty thousand, until the Raja finally sued
for the peace that ended it. It was, by Firishta's own aside, the
first time a Delhi-descended Muhammadan king had led an army across
the Tungabhadra and into Vijayanagara's own territory in person.

## A war fought at arm's length over Warangal

Muhammad Shah's dealings with Warangal followed the same rhythm as
his war with Vijayanagara - alliance when it suited both kingdoms
against a common Delhi threat, and open war when it did not. A raja's
son, Vinaik Dew, seized horses being carried to the Bahmani court and
provoked a punitive campaign that plundered deep into Telangana before
a large indemnity bought peace again. Warangal itself would not fall
to the Bahmanis outright for another sixty years, but Muhammad Shah's
reign fixed the pattern - punishing raids answered by tribute, never a
lasting conquest - that would define the frontier for a generation
after him.
