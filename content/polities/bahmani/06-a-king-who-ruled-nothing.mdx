The provincial governors who had drifted from court after Mahmud
Gawan's execution never fully returned to Bahmani obedience. Over the
following decades their own governorships hardened into outright
kingdoms: Bijapur under a line calling itself Adil Shahi, Ahmadnagar
under the Nizam Shahis, Golconda under the Qutb Shahis, Berar under the
Imad Shahis, and Bidar itself, the Bahmani capital, eventually under a
line of Barid Shahis descended from the sultanate's own last powerful
ministers. None of them formally deposed the Bahmani throne outright;
they simply stopped answering to it while continuing, for a time, to
issue documents in a Bahmani king's nominal name.

## A minister who kept his king as a prisoner

By the time Kalimullah Shah came to the throne, styled king in name
only, real power at Bidar belonged entirely to the minister Amir Barid,
who kept the young sovereign confined to his own palace and permitted
him no part in government at all. When Kalimullah once tried to gain
more freedom of movement, Barid tightened his confinement rather than
loosen it.

## An appeal to Delhi's new master

In 1526 the Mughal prince Babur conquered Delhi, and the rulers of
Bijapur, Ahmadnagar and Golconda — former Bahmani governors turned
independent kings in every practical sense — sent their own embassies
to the new power, seeking recognition on equal terms. Kalimullah, still
nominally their sovereign, sent an appeal of his own: a secret petition,
carried by a disguised companion, describing his kingdom as usurped and
his own person confined by rebellious servants, and offering to cede
Daulatabad and Berar to Babur in exchange for rescue. Babur, still
occupied subduing Malwa and Gujarat, did not act on it.

## An exile that closed the dynasty

Amir Barid learned of the mission and answered it by confining
Kalimullah more rigidly still. The king escaped his own captivity and
fled to his uncle, Ismail Adil Shah of Bijapur, hoping for shelter from
family rather than a minister's mercy; dissatisfied with his reception
there, he moved on to the court of Burhan Nizam Shah at Ahmadnagar,
who at first paid him the courtesy of standing in his presence before
concluding that the gesture only advertised a defect in his own claim
to kingship, and dropping the practice. Kalimullah remained at
Ahmadnagar until his death, in exile from a capital he had never
actually ruled — and with him, the source states plainly, the Bahmani
dynasty came to its end, in a Deccan already divided, in substance if
not yet fully in name, among the five kingdoms his own governors had
built out of its fragments.
