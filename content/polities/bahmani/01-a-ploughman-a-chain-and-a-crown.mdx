Zafar Khan, a Delhi-born officer known to history as Hasan, served in
the Deccan under Muhammad bin Tughluq, sultan of Delhi, a ruler whose
capricious punishments had made his own governors afraid to obey his
summons. When the sultan tried to recall his Deccan officers to
Gujarat, the messenger sent to fetch them treated them with such open
contempt that they chose open revolt over returning "like sheep to the
slaughter." The rebellion spread quickly, drawing in disaffected local
rajas along with the officers themselves, and by the time it reached
its climax the rebels held Daulatabad and much of the Deccan outright.

## A throne offered rather than seized

Nasir-ud-din Ismail, the rebels' own interim king, noticed that his
officers and soldiers deferred to his general Zafar Khan more readily
than to himself, and rather than contest it, called a meeting and
proposed that Zafar Khan be elected king in his place. The assembly
agreed unanimously. On a Friday judged auspicious by the court
astrologers — 3 August 1347 — Zafar Khan was crowned Ala-ud-Din Hasan
Bahman Shah, taking a black canopy in imitation of the Abbasid
caliphs and making Gulbarga his capital.

## A pedigree the chronicler himself doubted

The new king's name drew on a story of his own rise: as a young servant
to a Brahmin astrologer named Gangu, he had once turned up a buried
hoard of gold coins while plowing a field, and reported the find
honestly to his master rather than keeping it for himself. Gangu is
said to have predicted great things for him and asked only that, if
those predictions came true, Hasan take the name Gangu for himself and
keep him as finance minister — a promise Hasan kept. Some court
genealogists later traced Hasan's own ancestry to Bahman, a king of
ancient Persia, and the dynasty took its name from that claim. Firishta,
writing centuries later, records having seen such a pedigree in the
royal library at Ahmadnagar, but doubts it was anything but a flattering
invention: Hasan's own origins, he judges, were too obscure ever to
trace with any confidence, and the name Bahmani more plausibly honored
Gangu the Brahmin than any Persian king.
