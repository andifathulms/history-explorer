Muhammad Shah III came to the throne at nine years old in 1463, and
for most of his reign the kingdom's affairs rested in the hands of two
ministers acting under the regency of the queen mother. Chief among
them was Mahmud Gawan, a Persian-born former merchant who had turned
down offers to serve as vizier to greater courts before settling in the
Deccan. By the time the kingdom's territory had grown large enough that
its old four-province structure no longer served it, it was Gawan who
persuaded the king to divide the realm into eight provinces instead,
redrawing administrative boundaries across the entire sultanate and
reserving several districts, including Bijapur itself, under his own
direct charge.

## A minister richer in reputation than in gold

Gawan's own integrity became, after his death, a matter the king could
verify for himself. Rivals at court, envious of his standing, had spent
years poisoning Muhammad Shah's confidence in him; when the king
finally had Gawan's private treasury examined, he found not the
concealed fortune his accusers had implied but two accounts, one for
official expenses and one, nearly empty of substance beside it, that
Gawan called the treasury of the poor. His household kept a modest
kitchen, a servant's mat for a bed, and a personal library of three
thousand volumes willed entirely to the college he had founded rather
than to his own family.

## A forgery, a drunken order, and an old man's dignity

None of this integrity was known to the king in the moment that
mattered. Rivals produced a letter, bearing what appeared to be Gawan's
own seal, addressed to a hostile raja and full of treasonous
implications. Warned that the king's fury made the charge dangerous to
answer in person, Gawan went to court anyway, telling his own household
that a beard "grown grey in the service of the father" would be no
less honorably spent in the service of the son. Confronted with the
letter, he acknowledged the seal as his but denied any knowledge of the
letter itself — a defense the intoxicated king, past reasoning, did not
pause to examine. Gawan was executed within the hour, kneeling and
professing his faith as the blow fell.

## A king's remorse, and an empire that would not forgive it

Muhammad Shah's own investigation of Gawan's estate came only after the
execution, and the discovery of his minister's poverty broke him with a
grief he could not undo. Provincial governors who had served under
Gawan's own administrative reforms began withdrawing from the royal
camp within days, accepting the king's belated apologies and further
concessions but never fully returning to court. Muhammad Shah died the
following year, his own epitaph recording, in a chronogram his court
poets composed, that his death spelled "the ruin of the Deccan" — a
judgment the years that followed would not contradict.
