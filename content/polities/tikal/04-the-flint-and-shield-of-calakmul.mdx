title: The flint and shield of Calakmul

Jasaw Chan K'awiil I came to the throne in 682, three years after a
serious defeat his father, Nuun Ujol Chaak, had suffered at the
hands of a Tikal exile ruling the rival city of Dos Pilas. Under his
direction Tikal underwent a reversal of fortune that restored much
of the standing it had held before the Hiatus, and Jasaw Chan
K'awiil himself framed his own reign as a deliberate return to the
city's Early Classic glory.

## The flint and shield brought down

The defining act of that reversal came in August 695, when Jasaw
Chan K'awiil "brought down the flint and shield" of Yich'aak K'ahk',
king of Calakmul - the formulaic Maya phrase for a decisive military
victory. Tikal's forces captured one of Calakmul's own patron
deities in the fighting; Maya armies carried effigies of their gods
into battle much as they marched their soldiers, and losing one to
an enemy was a defeat inflicted on the gods as much as on the
king. A month later Jasaw Chan K'awiil staged a triumph, timed to
coincide with an anniversary of Spearthrower Owl's death, in which
he let blood, conjured a god, and had the story of the battle
performed before his court on a great ceremonial litter beneath a
towering jaguar effigy.

## A monument to outlast the victory

The victory did more than restore Tikal's standing for a season: it
marked the beginning of Calakmul's wider decline as the dominant
power of the Petén. Jasaw Chan K'awiil's own tomb, when it was
built around 734 in what would become Temple I on Tikal's Great
Plaza, stood as the last of a line of royal interments that made the
North Acropolis a working necropolis for the dynasty for well over a
thousand years afterward - a fitting resting place for the king who
had reversed a defeat his own city had suffered more than a century
before he was born.
