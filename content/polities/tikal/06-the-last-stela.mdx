The turning of the tenth bak'tun in 830 - one of the great calendar
anniversaries the dynasty had always marked in stone - passed at
Tikal without a monument, part of a sixty-year hiatus that ended any
cohesive royal authority at the city. Tikal's own name and titles
did not vanish; a lord styled a "holy lord of Tikal" travelled to
witness a monument dedication at distant Seibal in 849. But by then
such titles guaranteed little in the way of actual kingship, and
Tikal's authority had splintered among a scatter of provincial
lords, several of whom began raising their own monuments at the
satellite towns of Ixlu and Jimbal after 859, using the once
exclusive royal title for themselves.

## A last name from better times

One attempt was made to restore royal power at the old capital
itself. The lord who commissioned Stela 11, raised for the
calendar-ending of 869, took a name that deliberately recalled
Tikal's earlier restoration - Jasaw Chan K'awiil II - and set his
monument in the Great Plaza before the North Acropolis, alongside
stelae from centuries of his predecessors' reigns. It was, fittingly,
the last dated sculpture the city ever raised. The scattered
lordships that had inherited Tikal's name outlived it by only a
couple of decades, with their own final stelae going up at Jimbal in
889, the same year a namesake king at distant Uaxactun raised his
own last monument bearing the same royal name - whether the same
man or simply an echo of it, no inscription says.
