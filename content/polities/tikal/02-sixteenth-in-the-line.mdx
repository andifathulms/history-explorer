Sihyaj K'ahk' did not take Tikal's throne for himself once the
entrada was complete. He installed instead a son of Spearthrower
Owl, a boy known to history as Yax Nuun Ahiin I, elevated in
September 378 while still young enough that Sihyaj K'ahk' continued
to loom over his reign as an acknowledged overlord. Yax Nuun Ahiin's
own monuments make no concession to Maya tradition - they show him
in Mexican dress, seated in an unusual foreign pose - while his wife,
by contrast, carried the titles of a local woman, a strategic match
built to fuse the new dynasty's foreign blood with the old ruling
lineage it had replaced.

## A grandson reclaims the old regalia

That fusion reached its fullest expression under Yax Nuun Ahiin's
son, Sihyaj Chan K'awiil II, who ruled from 411 to 456. His great
monument, Stela 31, is a deliberate act of political theater: carved
in a style consciously archaic, copying a stela raised a hundred and
fifty years earlier, it shows the king in full Maya regalia, wearing
a headdress crested with the emblem of the dynasty's founder, Yax Ehb
Xook - two accompanying vase texts even number him sixteenth in that
founder's own line. Only in the tiny Teotihuacan medallion worked
into that headdress, and in the flanking images of his own father
dressed as a Teotihuacano warrior, does the monument admit the
dynasty's foreign origin at all. The rear face records Tikal's own
history from before the entrada down to the king's accession, the
completion of the ninth bak'tun in 435, and the stela's dedication in
445 - Maya kingship, the whole monument argues, restored rather than
diluted by the blood that had interrupted it.

## Three kings in obscurity

Sihyaj Chan K'awiil's own successors left a far thinner record.
K'an Chitam followed him, ruling into the 480s, and then Chak Tok
Ich'aak II, attested from around 486 into the early sixth century.
Little beyond their names and approximate dates survives from either
reign - a silence that would only deepen further, a generation later,
into the far more dramatic and total silence that followed Tikal's
own defeat in 562.
