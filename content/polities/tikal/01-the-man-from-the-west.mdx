Tikal's ruling dynasty traced itself to a lord called Yax Ehb Xook,
"First Step Shark," but no inscription pins down when he reigned;
the city's first securely dated monument, Stela 29, was not raised
until 292. For nearly a century afterward Tikal's kings governed the
central Petén as one power among several, their portraits carved in
a style whose basic elements - a richly garbed ajaw crowned with an
ancestor looking down from the sky - would remain essentially
unchanged for six hundred years.

## Arrival at Tikal

That continuity broke completely on 15 January 378, when a lord
named Sihyaj K'ahk', "Fire Born," reached Tikal after a journey that
inscriptions at the city of El Peru trace back toward Teotihuacan
itself, then the greatest power in Mesoamerica. King Chak Tok
Ich'aak I died the same day, recorded in the glyphic record as
"entering the water." The timing was no coincidence: his entire
lineage was replaced by a new royal line with direct ties to the
Teotihuacan court, and nearly every monument the old dynasty had
raised was smashed or hauled off to peripheral sites around the
city.

## A new order across the Petén

Sihyaj K'ahk' did not rule Tikal himself but installed a client king
there the following year, and went on to preside over similar
installations at Bejucal and, by 393, at Río Azul - evidence of a
new political order that now reached across the central Petén. A
second Teotihuacan-linked figure, known today only as Spearthrower
Owl from the glyphs naming him, appears as overlord to at least one
other Maya lord in the same period. Whatever the precise mechanics
of the takeover, the effect at Tikal was unmistakable: a kingdom
that had governed itself now took its kingship, its regalia and much
of its political orientation from a city eight hundred miles to the
west.
