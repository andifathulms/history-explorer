By the late 1860s the war Moshoeshoe had been fighting to hold back
Free State encroachment on his own territory had gone on, with
intervals of truce, for the better part of a decade. Moshoeshoe had
already offered more than once to place himself and his people, with
their land, under the Queen's authority rather than the Free State's;
for years the offer went unanswered, colonial officials wary of the
tangle of obligations it would create with the republic and with
neighbouring tribes. By 1867 the calculation had changed. With
farms burning on both sides of the Caledon and no end to the fighting
in sight, the secretary of state gave his consent that December to
receiving the Basuto as British subjects.

The high commissioner, Sir Philip Wodehouse, moved as soon as the
despatch reached South Africa, informing both Moshoeshoe and the Free
State's president of the power now placed in his hands and urging a
suspension of hostilities while the arrangements were made.
Moshoeshoe's own reply was full of thanks. On the 12th of March,
1868, Wodehouse proclaimed the Basuto British subjects and their
country British territory outright.

## A protection that came with a smaller country

Peace did not restore the ground already lost. The months that
followed were consumed by boundary negotiations with a Free State
president unwilling to give up territory his own commandos had
already occupied, and it was not until 1870, after more than two
years of what the record calls near anarchy in the unsettled
territory, that a final convention fixed the frontier and let British
administration actually take hold. Moshoeshoe had secured his people
against conquest, but the kingdom that emerged from the settlement
was smaller than the one that had entered the war, its own western
lands permanently ceded to the republic that had fought it for them.
