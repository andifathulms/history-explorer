The final ratification of the boundary settlement dragged on for more
than two years past the 1868 proclamation, a delay the historical
record of the time describes as leaving the Lesuto in a condition of
near anarchy. By the time the arrangements were at last completed in
1870, the man whose own repeated appeals had brought British
protection in the first place had little left to do with any of it.
Moshoeshoe was seventy-seven, worn down by decades that had asked
more of him than of almost any other chief of his generation, and his
sons Letsie, Molapo and Masupha now governed their own followers
without troubling to consult him at all. No one came to him for
orders any longer, and no one asked his opinion on the affairs of the
kingdom he had spent fifty years building.

Four months before he died, he chose a fine kaross made of panther
skins and asked the resident agent Mr. Bowker to send it to Queen
Victoria in his own name, as a mark of gratitude and a plain request
that British protection not be withdrawn from his country. He died at
Thaba Bosiu, the mountain stronghold that had sheltered his kingdom
through every war fought against it, on the 11th of March, 1870. So
little was left of his former standing that hardly a colonial
newspaper printed more than a brief notice of his death.

## From helpless old man to guardian spirit

The Basotho judged him differently than the colonial press had.
Where, in his last months, even his nearest relatives had shown him
little regard, his death transformed Moshoeshoe at once into the
highest object of his people's own reverence - a spirit whose favour
could bring prosperity and whose displeasure meant ruin. The kingdom
he had spent fifty years building, narrowed by war but never
conquered outright, passed to his son Letsie as a British
protectorate rather than a prize divided among its neighbours -
the outcome the kaross sent to a distant queen had been asking for.
