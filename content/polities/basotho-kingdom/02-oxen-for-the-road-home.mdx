With cannibalism suppressed and the lesser threats of Pakalita and
Matiwane spent, one enemy remained: the army of Mzilikazi, the Zulu
general who had broken from Shaka's own service to build a kingdom of
his own to the north, and now turned his forces on Thaba Bosiu itself.
The Basotho barricaded every breach time had opened in their
mountain's natural defenses and waited. Mzilikazi's regiments attacked
two faces of the mountain at once, advancing in tight ranks that did
not break even as boulders came thundering down the slopes at them;
when the avalanche of stone and spears finally told, the attackers
fled faster than they had come, their own officers snatching off the
plumes of the routed and trampling them in fury as they tried, and
failed, to force a second assault.

## A gift instead of a boast

The attack broken, Mzilikazi's army withdrew the next day toward
home. As they marched, a lone Mosotho met the retreating column
driving a herd of fat cattle before him, with a message from
Moshoeshoe: supposing that hunger alone could have brought them into
his country, he sent these oxen so they might eat on the journey back.
It was not surrender dressed as generosity - the mountain had already
beaten them - but a calculated act of respect toward a defeated enemy,
delivered at the exact moment it would be remembered longest.

## A promise a beaten army kept

Years later, Casalis met envoys from Mzilikazi's own court at Cape
Town and asked whether they knew the chief of the Basotho. "Know him?
Yes!" they answered at once. "That is the man who, after having
rolled down rocks on our heads, sent us oxen for food - we will never
attack him again." They kept their word. Of all the powers that had
tested Thaba Bosiu in the chaos of those years, the most formidable
never returned - not because Moshoeshoe had merely defeated it, but
because he had made defeat cost the enemy nothing to remember but
gratitude.
