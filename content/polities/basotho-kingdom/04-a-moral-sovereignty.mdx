The peace of the mission's early years did not last. Boer families
from the Cape Colony, emigrating chiefly, Casalis writes, because they
had been made to free their own slaves, began crossing the frontier in
small numbers, asking only temporary leave to settle thinly populated
ground. Once their numbers grew, they judged themselves strong enough
to stop asking. War followed - Casalis declines to narrate its
incidents in detail, noting only that alarm and victory songs rang
around the mission stations by turns for nearly four years, during
which the Basotho won back some of what had been taken from them, and
might have vanished from the map entirely before the distant colonial
government grasped what was happening in its name.

## A war ended by withdrawal, not by settlement

A show of force - a handful of dragoons and the mere sight of colonial
troops crossing the Orange - was, Casalis writes, enough on its own to
awe both settlers and Basotho into laying down arms, and a diplomatic
congress opened to find some remedy for troubles it ought to have
foreseen. Before it finished its work, a newly arrived colonial
governor cut through the negotiations at a stroke, proclaiming the
sovereignty of the Queen of England over the whole territory - a
sovereignty framed as purely moral, protecting black and white alike,
guaranteeing that each should keep what he already held and encroach
no further. It did not hold. England, unwilling to fund a war
promising neither glory nor profit, withdrew her troops and
magistrates once she judged the danger past, leaving the settlers who
had caused the trouble exactly where they had been.

## A verdict from the man who watched it happen

Casalis's own judgment, written with the trouble still unresolved
around him, is blunt: nothing had actually changed. The two rival
populations remained face to face, English neutrality was "very often
merely nominal," and successive Cape governors had never pursued any
frontier policy beyond reacting to whatever the colonists' own
encroachments had already made unavoidable. The chiefs of his own day,
he writes, faced difficulties their fathers had never known - a
mountain kingdom that had outlasted cannibal bands, the Ndebele and
every rival Sotho chief now found itself measured instead against a
colonial frontier that kept moving regardless of what any single
victory or treaty achieved.
