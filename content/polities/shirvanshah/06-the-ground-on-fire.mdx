The dynasty ended in 1538 and the province was governed from Tabriz, then fought
over by Safavids and Ottomans, then taken by Russia in 1813. What happened to
Shirvan afterwards is not really a political story at all. It is about the thing
its own geographers kept mentioning in passing.

## What Istakhri thought worth a sentence

The Arabic geographers describe Baku as a port south of Derbend with naphtha
springs. Istakhri refers to them as well known. Yaqut and others go into detail:
the produce was worth a thousand dirhams a day, the naphtha flowed continuously,
and all the ground was on fire round about.

Mustawfi, later, mentions the castle of Bakuyah standing so high above the town
that it kept the town in shadow at midday.

That is the entire medieval notice of the place: a fortress, a shadow, and
ground that burned. It is filed between a bridge of boats over the Samur and a
district near the mouth of the Kura where they grew corn and cotton.

## A thousand dirhams a day

Take the figure seriously for a moment, because the geographers did.

A thousand dirhams a day is a revenue, from a commodity, being collected and
counted in the tenth century. The oil was gathered from seeps and hand-dug pits,
carried away, sold, burnt for light and for heat, used in war, and taxed. The
Shirvanshahs drew part of their income from petroleum for five hundred years,
and nobody at the time thought it remarkable enough to write more than a
sentence about.

What made Shirvan wealthy, in every account of it, was the silk of Shamakhi and
the tolls of the coast road. The naphtha is a footnote about a curiosity: fields
that were on fire, which travellers went to look at.

## The footnote takes over

In the nineteenth century somebody drilled. The fields of the Absheron peninsula
became one of the great oil regions of the world within a generation, and the
Baku most people have seen — the boulevard, the opera house, the mansions of the
oil millionaires — was built with the money.

The palace of the Shirvanshahs is still inside the walled town on the hill, in
its pale limestone, with the divankhana and the mosque and the mausoleum. It is
what visitors go to see. It was paid for by silk and tolls and a fertile
province, by a dynasty that had lasted seven hundred years by never fighting
anybody it could avoid fighting.

Underneath it was the asset that would eventually make the country a state, and
for the whole of that seven hundred years it was a line in a geography book
about ground that would not stop burning.
