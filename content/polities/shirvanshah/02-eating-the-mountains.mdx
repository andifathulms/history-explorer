Al-Mas'udi passed along the Caspian in the 940s and wrote down who owned what in
the eastern Caucasus, and his list is the only contemporary account there is of
how the Shirvanshahs got large.

The country he is describing is not a country. The Caucasus, he says, holds
seventy-two nations, every one of them with its own king and its own language,
and there are passes and valleys in it inhabited by peoples who have no
acquaintance with each other at all, because the communication between them is
impeded by the height and roughness of the mountains, by marshes and forests, by
the water coming down off the summits and by immense rocks. Anushirvan had dealt
with that by appointing a chief to each of them, and the titles had stuck: a
Layzan-shah at Layzan, a Khursan-shah, a Tabarsaran-shah, and so on down the
range, each a hereditary king of one valley.

<Figure id="lahij" />

Mas'udi's Shirvanshah is Muhammad ibn Yazid, and the first thing he records
about him is what the family had been a generation earlier: he and his father
held Layzan, and no other kingdom. What they had besides Layzan was a claim —
descent from Bahram Gur, which Mas'udi reports without objection and adds that
nobody who knows genealogy will contradict it.

Then the list of what Muhammad ibn Yazid had eaten. Layzan, taken recently. The
Muqaniyya, subjugated. Al-Lakz, whose king was his vassal, in a kingdom whose
mountain population Mas'udi calls innumerable. And, in summary: this man has
subjected many kingdoms in these mountains which had been given to various
chiefs by Anushirvan, and they are now all under his sceptre — among them Khursan
Shah and Rawan Shah.

He had also taken the Gate. The city of Bab al-Abwab, Mas'udi notes, had been
governed since the first Arab conquests by the descendants of a man of the
Ansar, and on the death of the last of them it passed into the Shirvanshah's
possession.

Two things in the account are worth holding on to. The first is the method,
which is not campaigning. It is one valley lordship absorbing the next over two
or three generations by inheritance, marriage, vassalage and the occasional
short war, in country where no outside power could reach far enough or fast
enough to object — the Abbasids having stopped being able to, and nobody having
replaced them yet.

The second is what the method could not do, which Mas'udi records in the same
breath. Among the peoples of al-Lakz are the Dudaniyya, who are pagans, who have
no king at all, and who do not acknowledge the Shirvanshah as their master. A
polity assembled out of mountain valleys is exactly as large as the number of
valleys that have agreed, and the ones that have not agreed sit inside it,
unreachable, for as long as anybody cares to look.
