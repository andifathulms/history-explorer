Ibrahim I took the throne in 1382, founded the line historians call the
Derbendids, and did the single most consequential thing any Shirvanshah ever
did: when Timur came into the Caucasus he went to him in person, submitted
before being attacked, and offered service.

He was confirmed in his own country and used as an ally against the Golden
Horde. Shirvan came through the Timurid period intact — no siege, no
deportation of craftsmen, no towers of skulls — while Isfahan, Baghdad, Delhi,
Damascus and Sarai were being destroyed. Submitting before being attacked is the
technique the dynasty had been practising for six hundred years, and this is the
occasion on which it paid the largest dividend.

What followed is the peak. Khalilullah I ruled from 1417 to 1465 and Farrukh
Yasar from 1465 to 1500, and between them they made Baku a capital and built the
complex that is still the thing visitors to the city go to see: the palace of
the Shirvanshahs, with its divankhana, its mosque and minaret, its mausoleum and
its bath, cut in the pale local limestone on the hill inside the walled town.

The money came from three things and no source ranks them. The land tax of a
fertile province. The tolls of the Caspian coast road and the Derbend pass,
which is the only practicable land route between the steppe and Iran. And
extraction: Shirvan had the silk of Shamakhi, which Genoese, Venetian and later
Muscovite merchants came for, and it had the Absheron peninsula, with its salt
pans and its naphtha seeps and hand-dug oil wells.

A medieval principality drawing revenue from petroleum is the kind of detail
that reads as an anachronism and is not one. The oil was collected, sold, burnt
for light and heat, used in war, and taxed, four hundred years before anyone
drilled for it.
