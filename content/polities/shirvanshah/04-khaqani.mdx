Shirvan in the twelfth century, under Manuchihr III and his son Akhsitan I, was
one of the significant literary courts of the Persian-speaking world, which is
not what a reader would expect of a small principality on the Caspian.

Khaqani Shirvani was its poet — born at Shamakhi, employed at the court,
imprisoned by it at least once, and one of the most technically difficult poets
in Persian, whose qasidas are still set as the hard case in the tradition. His
*Tuhfat al-Iraqayn* is among the earliest Persian verse travel accounts, and the
poem he wrote on the ruins of Ctesiphon — the arch of the Sasanian palace, seen
from the road — is one of the best-known meditations on fallen empire in the
language. It is worth pausing on which court paid for that poem: a dynasty that
claimed the Sasanians as ancestors, employing a poet who wrote about their ruins.

Falaki Shirvani and Mujir al-Din Baylaqani were of the same court. And Nizami
Ganjavi — the poet of the *Khamsa*, working just to the west at Ganja —
dedicated *Layli and Majnun* to Akhsitan I in 1188, at the shah's request and,
by his own account in the poem's introduction, with some reluctance about the
subject.

<Figure id="layli-majnun" />

Political weight and cultural output come apart constantly, and the Caucasus and
upper Mesopotamia are full of examples — the Artuqids, the Marwanids, Trebizond,
the Kara Koyunlu. What distinguishes Shirvan is duration. This was not one
generous ruler in one generation. It was a court doing this while its neighbours
were being conquered, and still there afterwards.

An earthquake destroyed Shamakhi in 1191 and the seat moved to Baku for a time.
Then the Mongols came, and Shirvan submitted, and continued.
