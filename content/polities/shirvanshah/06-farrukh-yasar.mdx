The Safavid order's move from Ardabil into politics went through Shirvan twice,
and both times it went badly.

Shaykh Junayd led his Turkmen followers north on a raid in 1460 and was killed
in battle against the Shirvanshah Khalilullah I. Twenty-eight years later his
son Shaykh Haydar did the same thing, and was killed in battle against
Khalilullah's successor Farrukh Yasar, who had Ak Koyunlu help. Two generations
of the Safavid house died fighting the same dynasty in the same province.

Haydar's son Isma'il came out of hiding in 1499 with the Qizilbash behind him,
and the first campaign he fought — before Tabriz, before the proclamation of
Shi'ism, before any of the things the Safavid empire is remembered for — was
against Shirvan. In 1500 he
defeated Farrukh Yasar and killed him. The accounts of what was done to the
Shirvanshah's body, and to the tombs of the dynasty at Baku, are not pleasant
and are not in dispute.

<Figure id="mausoleum" />

Seven centuries of holding the corridor by submitting early to whoever was
coming ended against an opponent for whom the country was not a corridor. It was
a debt.

The dynasty continued for another thirty-eight years as Safavid vassals, and in
1538 Shah Tahmasp deposed the last of them and made Shirvan a province governed
from Tabriz. Baku's walls and palace stayed where they were; the province was
fought over by the Safavids and the Ottomans for the next century and a half,
lost to Russia at Gulistan in 1813 along with the rest of the eastern Caucasus,
and is the capital of a state today.
