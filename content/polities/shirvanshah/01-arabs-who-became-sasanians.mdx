title: Arabs who became Sasanians
drafted_from: minorsky-1958

Yazid ibn Mazyad al-Shaybani was an Abbasid general of the Banu Shayban, sent to
govern the Caucasus frontier around 799 and to hold the pass at Derbend against
the Khazars. His descendants held Shirvan afterwards, and by 861 — with the
caliphate in the middle of the collapse at Samarra that this site's Abbasid page
describes — they had stopped behaving like governors and taken the old local
title of Shirvanshah.

What happened to them over the following two centuries is the interesting part
and it is what Minorsky's book is for. An Arab tribal family in a Persianate
country, ruling a population that was neither Arab nor entirely Persian, wrote
itself into the landscape it had been sent to hold. The names go first: by the
eleventh century the shahs are called Manuchihr, Kavus, Farrukhzad, Faridun —
Sasanian and Shahnama names, in a family whose founder was a Shaybani Arab. Then
the genealogy: descent from Bahram Gur, and from Khusraw Anushirvan, asserted
publicly and carried in the titulature.

Almost nobody believes it. The Arab descent is well attested and the Sasanian
one is a construction. This site's `legitimation` field records `descent`
anyway, because what the field asks is on what ground the right to rule was
publicly asserted, and this ground was asserted for half a millennium by people
who had every opportunity to choose a different one.

Minorsky's history exists because he reconstructed the local chronicle of
Shirvan and Derbend out of a later compilation. Without it this dynasty would be
a column of names and dates in a genealogical manual — which is worth saying on
a page about a polity whose distinction is that it lasted, because lasting is
precisely the thing that leaves no dramatic record.
