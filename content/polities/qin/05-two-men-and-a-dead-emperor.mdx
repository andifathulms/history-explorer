The First Emperor died on the road, in the fifth month of a provincial
inspection tour, and the empire's entire chain of authority passed into the hands
of two men who had no legitimate claim to move it at all.

## The concealment

He died at Shaqiu, in the summer of 210 BC, far from the capital and with his
eldest son Fusu absent on the northern frontier, attached to the army of the
general Meng Tian — the same general who had built the Great Wall and who
commanded the empire's most capable field force.

Fusu was the obvious heir: the eldest son, associated in the sources with the
Confucian scholars the burning of the books had targeted, and by implication a
critic of his father's harsher methods. He was not present, and the two men who
were — the chancellor Li Si and the palace eunuch Zhao Gao, who controlled the
imperial seals — chose not to send for him.

They concealed the death. The body was carried back toward the capital in the
imperial carriage as though the emperor were still alive, meals still brought to
the curtained compartment, reports still received and — falsely — answered in his
name. Zhao Gao is reported to have justified the deception on the practical
ground that the empire's outlying garrisons were not yet secure enough to risk
the shock of an unexplained succession.

## The forged order

Then, before the concealment could no longer hold, Zhao Gao and Li Si acted.

A document went out under the imperial seal, addressed to Fusu and to Meng Tian
on the frontier, accusing both of failure in their duties and ordering them to
take their own lives. Fusu, given no reason to doubt a sealed order from his
father, obeyed. Meng Tian hesitated and asked to have the order confirmed — and
was imprisoned and then forced to suicide regardless.

The throne went instead to a younger, more pliable son, who took the name Qin Er
Shi — the Second Emperor. Li Si had calculated that a compliant emperor and his
own continued high office were worth more than honesty to a prince he did not
control. He was wrong about which of the two conspirators would end up holding
the compliant emperor's confidence.

## What it cost the state

Within roughly a year, Zhao Gao had moved against Li Si himself, engineering his
arrest, torture and execution — the same instrument the Qin legal system had
perfected for suppressing dissent among its own subjects, turned on the man who
had helped build it.

By the time Qin Er Shi himself was killed, in the final collapse two years later,
the throne had already been reduced to a formality: the eunuch who had forged the
first succession simply arranged the second one, then contemplated a third before
a resurgent claimant of the old royal line and then the rebel armies overtook the
capital entirely.

## Why one act of concealment did so much damage

Because the entire Qin system depended on a single, unambiguous chain of command
running from the throne down through the commanderies to every registered
household — the same structure the earlier chapters describe standardising a
script and an axle width. That structure had never been tested by a contested
succession, because there had only ever been one emperor.

The first test broke it in the space of a single funeral journey. Two men with
physical control of a dying man's seal and a closed carriage were able to redirect
the succession of the largest state on earth, execute the empire's most capable
general and its most legitimate heir, and install a ruler neither of them
ultimately controlled — and no institution anywhere in Qin's elaborately
standardised government was positioned to stop them, because the system had been
built to enforce the emperor's will, not to verify whose will was actually being
enforced.
