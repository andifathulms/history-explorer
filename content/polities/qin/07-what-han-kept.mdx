Rebellion broke out within a year of Qin Er Shi's accession, spread faster than
the dynasty's armies could contain it, and the state that had unified China by
force could not hold it for even fifteen years. What survived the collapse is
the more interesting story than the collapse itself.

## The fall

The rising that began it is remembered for the exchange attributed to its first
leaders, conscript labourers who found themselves unable to reach their assigned
post on time and facing execution under Qin's own uncompromising law for the
failure: if death awaited them either way, they reasoned, they might as well die
attempting something larger. Within months the revolt had spread across the
territories of the old six states, each of which still had families and local
memory of its own former ruling houses.

Two rival leaders emerged from the chaos — Xiang Yu, a general from the old
state of Chu, and Liu Bang, a minor local official of no particular distinction.
Xiang Yu took the Qin capital and had the surrendered royal family and the last
Qin ruler put to death; Liu Bang, who had reached the capital first and treated
it more moderately, spent the following four years defeating Xiang Yu in the
civil war that decided who would actually inherit what the Qin had built. He
founded the Han in 206 BC.

## What he did not tear down

This is the fact worth holding onto through the whole of the Qin's brief and
violent record: the dynasty that overthrew it kept almost the entire structure
that dynasty had built.

The commanderies and counties stayed, administered by appointed officials rather
than restored to the old feudal nobility. The standardised script stayed. The
standardised weights, measures and axle-width stayed. The road network Qin's
conscripted labour had built stayed, and carried Han's armies and Han's taxes the
same way it had carried Qin's. The legal codes were revised and softened in their
penalties, but their basic administrative machinery — registration, conscription
assessed against a household record, a bureaucracy answering to the centre —
survived essentially intact.

What Han rejected was not the apparatus. It was the harshness with which Qin had
run it, and the political theory that had justified the harshness. Where Qin's
legitimacy rested on naked conquest and a Legalist doctrine that treated the
population as an instrument of state power, Han's rested increasingly on a
Confucian framework of moral government and a claim to Heaven's favour that could,
in principle, be withdrawn from a ruler who governed badly.

## The verdict every later dynasty repeated

Han court writers spent the following four centuries elaborating a single
argument: that the Qin had shown what a unified, bureaucratic Chinese empire
could achieve, and had then shown, in fifteen years, what happens to a government
that rules such an empire by naked force and calls it virtue.

That argument became the working political theory of every dynasty that followed
Han in turn — the machinery is legitimate and necessary, and the moral framing
around it is what determines whether the state that runs it survives its own
founder. Sui, eight centuries later, would fall in almost the same shape - a short,
violent, administratively brilliant reunification exhausted by its own
exertions - and Tang would explain it in exactly the terms Han had used for
Qin. The verdict outlived every dynasty that had to give it, repeated with the
names changed for the next two thousand years.
