The First Emperor began building his own tomb the moment he took the throne of a
unified China, and the fullest account of what went into it was written a
century later by the historian Sima Qian — close enough to the event that people
who remembered the construction were still alive when he wrote it down.

## The description

Giles gives Sima Qian's account entire rather than summarising it, and it is
worth reading the way he chose to present it: as a document rather than as an
anecdote.

> In the 9th moon the First Emperor was buried in Mount Li, which in the early
> days of his reign he had caused to be tunnelled and prepared with that view.
> Then, when he had consolidated the empire, he employed his soldiery, to the
> number of 700,000, to bore down to the Three Springs, and there a firm
> foundation was laid and the sarcophagus placed thereon.

Seven hundred thousand men, on Sima Qian's figure, working across the whole of
the reign — begun when the future emperor was still a young king of one state
among seven, finished only after he had become ruler of all of them.

## What was inside

Rare objects and jewels were collected from the palaces and from every official
in the empire and stored there in huge quantity. Craftsmen built mechanical
crossbows rigged to fire on anyone who entered uninvited. And the centrepiece:

> With the aid of quicksilver, rivers were made — the Yangtsze, the Yellow River,
> and the great ocean — the metal being made to flow from one into the other by
> machinery. On the roof were delineated the constellations of the sky, on the
> floor the geographical divisions of the earth.

A working model of the empire itself, its two great rivers running in mercury,
under a ceiling painted with the stars above it and a floor mapped with the land
below — built to scale, inside a mountain, for an audience of one dead man. Modern
soil surveys around the mound have found mercury concentrations well above the
natural background level in exactly the pattern the text describes, which is the
closest thing to independent confirmation an account like this ever gets.

## What happened to the workmen

The Second Emperor, taking charge of his father's funeral, first ordered that the
childless concubines of the harem accompany the dead monarch into the tomb rather
than be released — *and accordingly he ordered them to accompany the dead
monarch to the next world, those who thus perished being many in number.*

Then came the workmen. Someone pointed out that the men who had built the
mechanism and hidden the treasure knew exactly where it was and what it was
worth, and that the secret would leak out through them.

> Therefore, so soon as the ceremony was over, and the path giving access to the
> sarcophagus had been blocked up at its innermost end, the outside gate at the
> entrance to this path was let fall, and the mausoleum was effectually closed,
> so that not one of the workmen escaped. Trees and grass were then planted
> around, that the spot might look like the rest of the mountain.

An entire labour force — the same conscripted, corvée-obligated men whose service
had built the wall and the roads and the tomb itself — sealed inside the thing
they had just finished building, so that the mountain could be made to look, from
the outside, like nothing had ever been dug into it at all.

## Why the tomb belongs beside the axle and the fire

Because it is built on the same theory as the rest of the reign, taken to its
natural end.

Standardising the axle and the script made the empire legible to one government.
Burning the books tried to make its intellectual life begin again with one man.
The tomb is the same ambition turned inward and downward: not a monument seen by
the living, but a complete second empire — its rivers, its stars, its geography,
its guards, its labour force — built in secret, for an audience of nobody, and
then sealed shut around the men who had made it, so that even the fact of its own
construction could be erased.
