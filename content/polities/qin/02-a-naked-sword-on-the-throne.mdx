From 235 BC the king of Qin sat his own throne with a naked sword
across his knees - a plain announcement of how the next nine years
were going to go. One by one, the six rival states that had divided
China among themselves for two centuries were going to stop existing.

## Han, first

Han fell first and easiest. Its ruler, watching Qin's power grow and
hoping timely surrender would buy better terms than resistance, gave
up his seat and retired into private life inside the conqueror's own
territory - a state removed from the board without a battle worth
recording.

## An assassin's severed leg

It was in the middle of the next campaign, against the state the
sources call Chow, that the king of Qin came closer to dying than he
ever would again. Insulted at court, Crown Prince Dan of Yan fled home
and set an assassin on him - a man named Jing Ke, who had first
persuaded the fugitive general Fan Yuqi to kill himself, so that his
severed head could be presented to the Qin king as a gift and buy the
assassin access close enough to strike. The plan very nearly worked.
Jing Ke was admitted into the king's presence and was on the point of
drawing his dagger when the movement caught the king's own eye; in the
scuffle that followed, the king got the better of his attacker and cut
off Jing Ke's leg with one stroke of his own sword. It is hard to find,
in any history, a stranger measure of desperation than Fan Yuqi's: a
man killing himself on the calculation that his own death would arm
his avenger.

## What the failed plot bought Yan

The king's response was immediate. He sent his general Wang Ben to
overrun Yan outright. The ruler of Yan, trying to avert the coming
storm, executed his own son Dan and sent the head to Qin as an
apology; he himself fled into the wilds of Liaodong. It did not save
him. The same year, Wei's capital was stormed and its ruler sent to
the Qin capital for execution - two states destroyed within a single
campaigning season, one of them undone by the very plot meant to save
it.

## Two hundred thousand men, and then six hundred thousand

Only Chu remained among the great principalities, and subduing it
proved the hardest task of the whole war. Asked how large an army the
campaign required, the general Li Xin - eager to please - offered to
do it with two hundred thousand men. The veteran commander Wang Jian
said nothing less than six hundred thousand would serve. The king,
preferring the answer he wanted to hear, gave Li Xin his two hundred
thousand and called Wang Jian a dotard for asking more.

Li Xin's army broke through Chu's border defences and then walked into
a surprise attack that destroyed it - more than forty thousand men
lost in the battle and the retreat that followed, the whole force
driven back across its own frontier in confusion. History does not
record what became of Li Xin afterward, only that he never appears
again in it.

There was nothing left to do but recall the man the king had just
insulted. Wang Jian agreed to take the command, on the one condition
he had stated from the start: six hundred thousand men, no fewer. When
he raised the question of how such an army would be supplied, the
king's answer was direct: "Do not let that disquiet you, I have
provided for everything. I promise you that provisions shall rather be
wanting in my own palace than in your camp." Wang Jian broke the Chu
army in a single decisive battle, garrisoned its cities, and sent its
ruling family to the Qin capital to share the fate of every other
royal house the war had already ended.

## What was left to mop up

Chu's fall cleared the way for the rest. Yan and the small remaining
territories of Dai and Qi were annexed in turn, Wang Ben - the same
general who had overrun Yan - assisting his own father in the final
campaigns. By 221 BC every one of the six rival kingdoms that had once
parcelled out China between them had been destroyed, their ruling
houses exterminated or exiled, and the king who had sat nine years
with a sword across his knees had nothing left standing against him.
He changed his own name and style to mark it - from his birth name to
Qin Shi Huangdi, the First Sovereign Emperor of Qin - and turned, with
the war finally over, to the work of ruling what the war had won him.
