Fourteen years before Qin became an empire, a minister from a rival state argued
its king out of expelling every foreigner at his own court — including himself —
and the argument he made is the clearest statement anywhere of what kind of state
Qin was about to become.

## The proscription

Li Si was from Chu, one of the six states Qin would eventually destroy, and he had
risen to high office in Qin's service. Around 237 BC the native-born nobles at
court combined to persuade the king to expel every foreign adviser and hanger-on
on the grounds that such men served their home states' interests rather than
Qin's. The edict would have included Li Si himself. He sent up a memorial instead
of leaving.

## The argument

He starts with luxury, because it is the easiest case to make.

> From the mountains of Tibet your Majesty receives jade; from elsewhere, jewels.
> Bright pearls, good blades, fine horses, kingfisher banners, triton-skin
> drums — of such rarities not one is produced at home, yet your Majesty delights
> in all. But if nothing is to be used in future save local produce, then will
> rich pearls shine no more at Court.

If the king is willing to prize foreign jade and foreign horses and foreign music
because they are good, Li Si asks, why does the same logic not apply to foreign
men?

> In the choice of men, however, this principle is not to prevail. There is to be
> no question of capacity or of incapacity, of honesty or of dishonesty. If he be
> not a native, he must go: all foreigners are to be dismissed. Surely this is to
> measure men by a lower standard than music and gems!

Then the argument turns from luxury to power, and this is the part that
anticipates the empire Qin was about to build.

> As broad acres yield large crops, so for a nation to be great there should be a
> great population; and for soldiers to be daring their generals should be brave.
> Not a single clod was added to T'ai-shan in vain: hence the huge mountain we now
> behold. The merest streamlet is received into the bosom of Ocean: hence the
> Ocean's unfathomable expanse. And wise and virtuous is the ruler who scorns not
> the masses below. For him, no boundaries of realm, no distinctions of
> nationality exist.

And finally the practical threat, stated as plainly as a memorial to a king could
state it:

> It is proposed to deliver over the black-haired people into the power of the
> foe. For if strangers are expelled, they will rally round the feudal princes...
> Many things that are not produced here are nevertheless highly prized. Countless
> men who were not born here are nevertheless loyal of heart. Therefore to dismiss
> all foreigners will be to make our enemies strong; for those who suffer
> expulsion will go to swell the hostile ranks.

## What it won

The king rescinded the order. Li Si stayed, rose to be chancellor, and was still
at the king's side thirty years later when the last of the six rival states fell
and his master proclaimed himself the First Emperor.

The principle Li Si had defended — capacity over origin, the state's advantage
over any noble's claim of birth — is the same principle Qin then applied to
everyone inside its own new borders. The old feudal titles were abolished in
favour of an administrative hierarchy staffed by appointment rather than descent.
A man's standing under Qin law depended on what he did, registered and taxed and
conscripted by the state directly, not on which lord he had been born to serve.

Li Si had asked a king not to throw away useful men because they came from
somewhere else. Within a generation the same logic, applied at the scale of a
continent, had abolished the entire class of men whose only qualification was
where they had been born at all.
