In 221 BC the king of Qin finished destroying the last of six rival states and
did something no Chinese ruler had done in living memory: he made the whole
country answer to one government at once, down to the width of a cart wheel.

## The man Giles compares to Napoleon

Herbert Giles's judgement, writing in 1911, is that the First Emperor's *best
historical counterpart is Napoleon*, and the comparison is doing real work: a
military genius who ended a fractured continental order by force, remade its
institutions from the ground up, and left a legal and administrative framework
his successors relied on for far longer than his own dynasty lasted.

He called himself Shi Huangdi — the First Emperor — a title invented for the
occasion, because no existing word fit a ruler who had ended not just a war but
an entire political system.

## What he actually did in his first years

Giles's list is worth taking at face value, because each item is a fact about the
new empire's reach rather than a claim about its style.

He sent an army of three hundred thousand men north against the Xiongnu — *an old
and dreaded enemy on the north, recently identified beyond question with the
Huns.* He sent a fleet to search for mysterious islands off the coast, which some
later took to be Japan. He built the Great Wall, largely with convict labour,
malefactors condemned to long terms of penal servitude on the works. And he
issued a copper coinage of such consistent quality that the old cowry-shell
currency disappeared from commerce entirely during his reign.

## The axle

The detail that explains the rest is the one that sounds smallest: Qin fixed a
single standard width for cart axles across the whole empire.

Before unification, each of the seven states had built its roads to its own
gauge, so that a cart from one state's ruts did not run smoothly on another's.
Qin's decree made every cart in the empire interchangeable with every road in the
empire — which sounds like a detail of engineering and is in fact a description
of what an empire is. A single government that cannot move an army, a tax
shipment or an official down every road at the same speed does not yet control
the country it claims.

The same principle was applied everywhere at once: one system of weights and
measures, so that a bushel meant the same thing in the old Chu lands as in the
old Qin heartland; one form of the written script, replacing the regional
variants the six states had each developed over centuries of separation; and the
abolition of the hereditary feudal titles in favour of a uniform hierarchy of
commanderies and counties, each answering directly to the throne rather than to
a local lord.

## What that cost the old order

Every one of those reforms took power away from somebody specific — the regional
nobility whose titles vanished, the local scribes whose script was declared
obsolete, the merchants whose local weights no longer applied — and gave it to a
single administrative machine with its centre at Xianyang.

That is the same trade every later Chinese dynasty would make in its own way: a
government legible enough to tax, conscript and command as one body, purchased at
the cost of every local arrangement that had made the old feudal patchwork
livable for the people running it. Qin made the trade first, made it completely,
and made it in about a decade — which is also why enforcing it took every
instrument of coercion the new state had, including the one the next chapter is
about.
