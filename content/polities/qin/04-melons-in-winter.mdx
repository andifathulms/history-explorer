In 213 BC the same Li Si whose memorial had once saved his own place at court
proposed destroying almost every book in the empire that was not his own
government's, and the decree that followed has defined the Qin's reputation for
over two thousand years.

## The proposal

Giles gives the shape of it plainly: existing literature was to be destroyed, with
exceptions only for works on agriculture, medicine and divination, so that
*history was to begin again with the ruling monarch, thenceforward to be famous
as the First Emperor.*

<Figure id="qn-burning" />

The penalty for concealment was branding and four years of forced labour on the
Great Wall — then still under construction, so that a scholar caught hiding a
book joined the same conscripted workforce the wall itself was being built by.

> This plan was carried out with considerable vigour. Many valuable works
> perished; and the Confucian Canon would have been irretrievably lost but for the
> devotion of scholars, who at considerable risk concealed the tablets by which
> they set such store.

## What survived and why

Three categories were exempted by design: the practical knowledge a state needs
regardless of politics — how to grow food, how to treat the sick, how to read the
signs the court's own diviners depended on. Everything else — history, poetry,
the philosophical schools that had argued with each other through the whole of
the Warring States period — was proscribed.

<Figure id="qn-rishu" />

It did not work as completely as the decree intended. Copies survived buried in
walls, hidden in the countryside, memorised by scholars who staked their lives on
recitation over possession. The recovery of those texts, and the argument over
which recovered versions were genuine, became one of the central scholarly
projects of the following dynasty.

## The tradition Giles reports and flags as a tradition

The scale of the human cost attached to the burning has its own legend, and Giles
is careful to mark it as one rather than as established fact.

> So many, indeed, of the literati are said to have been put to death for
> disobedience that melons actually grew in winter on the spot beneath which their
> bodies were buried.

*Said to have been* is doing real work in that sentence. Giles is not claiming
that hundreds of Confucian scholars were executed and their mass grave produced a
horticultural miracle; he is reporting that later Chinese tradition told the story
that way, and telling his reader so. The image — melons ripening out of season
over buried scholars — is a folk verdict on the event rather than a chronicle
entry, the kind of detail a culture invents when a plain death toll will not carry
enough horror by itself.

## What actually made the burning consequential

Not the fire itself, which — however extensive — did not erase Chinese
literature; the Han recovered enough of the canon within a generation that
Confucianism became the state orthodoxy it would remain for two thousand years.

What made it consequential was the precedent: that a Chinese government could
attempt, by decree, to sever the country's relationship with its own past and
begin the calendar of ideas over with the reigning monarch. Every subsequent
dynasty that wanted to be seen as legitimate had to define itself against that
possibility — by patronising scholarship, endowing academies, compiling the
histories of the dynasties before it, and making a public show of continuity with
everything Qin had tried to discard.

A decree meant to erase the past instead became the reason every later Chinese
government felt obliged to be seen protecting it.
