In 862 Rostislav sent envoys to Constantinople with a request to the
Byzantine emperor Michael: "Our people have rejected paganism and
already observe Christian law. But we have no teachers who can in our
own language teach us the true Christian faith." The plea was sincere,
but Lützow judges it carried an equally real political purpose -
Rostislav and his uncle wanted a Moravian Church free of the Frankish
hierarchy, since the German priests already active in Moravia served
their own nation's interests as much as their faith's. Michael chose
two brothers from Thessalonica, Constantine and Methodius, native
speakers of a Slavic dialect, and sent them to Moravia the following
year.

## An alphabet and a liturgy

Constantine arrived with a Bible he had already translated into
Slavic, written in a new alphabet of his own invention that would
later take his monastic name, Cyril, as its own - the first time a
Slavic language had been rendered in writing, and the point at which
it took its place beside Latin and Greek as a language fit for
worship. Churches multiplied and congregations came eagerly to
services conducted in a tongue they could actually understand, and the
brothers' reputation spread beyond Moravia's own borders into
neighbouring Pannonia. German clergy, and especially the Archbishop of
Salzburg whose own diocese the mission encroached on, denounced the
brothers to Rome as heretics, made easier by their Eastern origins and
their past association with the Byzantine Patriarch Photius - though
both brothers had in fact sided consistently with Rome throughout.
Constantine died in a Roman monastery in 869, but Methodius continued
the work, eventually securing from the Pope himself the title
Archbishop of Moravia and Pannonia and formal recognition of the new
script.

## Betrayal repaid with betrayal

The religious contest ran alongside a political one that never really
paused. German armies attacked Rostislav's domains again in 864 and
868, without lasting success, and the resulting truce did nothing to
secure Rostislav's own position: his nephew Svatopluk, who governed
the district of Neutra under his uncle's authority, allied himself
with the Germans instead, captured Rostislav, and handed him over to
the Frankish prince Carlomann in 870. Carlomann's forces then occupied
the whole of Moravia, imprisoned Methodius and delivered him to hostile
German bishops, and - mistrusting the loyalty of the man who had just
delivered them their victory - arrested Svatopluk as well when a
Moravian revolt broke out shortly after. Svatopluk would answer that
betrayal in kind.
