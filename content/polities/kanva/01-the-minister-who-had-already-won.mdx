By the time Vasudeva Kanva had the last Shunga king killed, the throne he
was taking had already, in every practical sense, been his for some time.

## A pattern with a name

Smith reaches for a comparison from much later Indian history to describe
what the Shunga-Kanva transition actually was: the relationship of the
later Maratha rajas to their own Peshwas, nominal kings governed in
practice by hereditary chief ministers who eventually held all the real
power the royal title implied. The later Shunga kings, on this reading,
had already stopped being rulers in anything but name well before Devabhuti
died — Vasudeva simply removed the last formality standing between his
existing control of the state and the title that matched it.

## What the coup actually changed

Very little, on the evidence available. The capital stayed at Pataliputra.
The territory Vasudeva inherited was whatever remained of the already
contracted Shunga holdings in Magadha and the middle Gangetic plain — no
new conquest accompanied the change of dynasty, because none was needed;
the state Vasudeva took over was one he had already been administering.
What changed was a name on a throne that had, for some years before that,
belonged to him in every respect except the formal one.

## The silence that follows

That is close to everything that can be said about how the dynasty began.
No inscription of Vasudeva's own reign survives to describe his own view
of the transition, no coinage can be attributed to him with confidence, and
the sources that preserve the story of the coup itself — the Puranas, and
Bana's drama composed centuries later at an entirely different court — have
nothing further to say about what the new king actually did with the power
he had just formally claimed.
