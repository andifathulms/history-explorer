Nobody knows who actually killed Susarman, the last Kanva king, beyond the
Puranic statement that it was a king of the Andhra dynasty — and even that
answer opens more questions than it closes.

## The wrong king, ruled out

The Puranas treat the Andhra dynasty as following directly on from the
Kanvas, and on that basis identify Susarman's killer with Simuka, the
Andhra line's own founder. Smith rejects the identification outright: the
independent Andhra dynasty, ruling across the Deccan tableland from coast
to coast, had already existed since roughly 220 BC — more than a century
and a half before the Kanvas fell in 27 BC. Whoever killed Susarman cannot
possibly have been the Andhra dynasty's own founder, however neatly the
Puranic sequence would have it.

## What a coin might settle

The best candidate available rests on numismatic evidence rather than any
narrative source: coins of northern type carrying the legend Sata, which
may preserve the name of the actual king responsible. Smith had earlier
proposed treating the Kanva and early Andhra dynasties as partly
contemporary rather than strictly sequential — a theory he explicitly
abandons by the time he writes this account, on the strength of both the
Puranas' and Bana's independent agreement that Devabhuti, the last Shunga,
was the one Vasudeva Kanva killed, which fixes the sequence more firmly
than the alternative allowed for.

## An empire with no monument at the scene of its own conquest

No coin or inscription directly ties any Andhra king to Pataliputra itself.
What can be said is only that Andhra coinage from first to last shares
affinities with northern Indian mintages — a pattern that makes sense if
the dynasty held Magadha as a dependency for some period, but proves
nothing about the manner of conquest, its extent, or how long the arrangement
actually lasted. A dynasty that killed the last king to rule from
Pataliputra as a paramount power has left less physical trace of the act
than either of the two dynasties it displaced.
