By the time Rome's own civil war reached Africa, six decades after
Bocchus handed Jugurtha to Sulla in chains, the single Mauretanian
throne he had ruled no longer existed. Two kings, both named in the
record only as Bocchus and Bogud, now held the country between them -
and both, unlike their Numidian neighbor Juba I, backed Julius Caesar
rather than the Pompeians who had made Africa their own refuge after
Pharsalus.

## The safer bet, twice over

The choice made sense on its own terms. Juba's Numidia could field
four legions and cavalry beyond counting, resources the two smaller
Mauretanian kingdoms could not match alone - but Caesar's own cause in
Africa was reinforced by Sittius, a Roman adventurer who had spent
years already operating in the region with a private army of his own,
giving Bocchus and Bogud a patron whose interests ran alongside
theirs rather than a neighbor whose ambitions, like Jugurtha's before
him, might eventually reach for their own thrones next. Where a
Mauretanian king had once married into Numidia's royal house and
fought Rome at its side, his successors now stood against Numidia
and beside Rome instead - the same calculation of self-interest,
running in the opposite direction.

## Two names outlasting one kingdom's memory

Whether Bocchus and Bogud divided a kingdom once ruled by a single
line, or represented two houses with older claims of their own, goes
unexplained in the war's own narrative. What that narrative shows
plainly is a Mauretania no longer capable of acting as one power even
when its interests aligned - two kings making the same choice
separately, rather than one making it for both.
