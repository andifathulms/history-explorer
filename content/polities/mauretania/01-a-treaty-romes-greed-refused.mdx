Bocchus ruled all the Mauritanians, the westernmost of Africa's
peoples, a king who knew the Roman name and nothing more of Rome
itself until Jugurtha's own war brought the two into contact. At the
very outset of that war, Bocchus had sent an embassy to Rome asking
for a treaty of friendship - an alliance that would have served Rome's
own interest in the war then just beginning. It came to nothing. The
same senators whose greed had already sold Jugurtha half a kingdom and
a king's own silence let the offer die rather than trouble themselves
over a distant ruler with nothing yet to offer them.

## A marriage that meant less than it looked

Bocchus had also, some time before, married one of Jugurtha's own
daughters - a tie the text notes was held cheaply among both
Numidians and Mauritanians, where kings kept as many wives as they
could afford and no single marriage carried the weight it might
elsewhere. It was a slight bond, but it was there when Jugurtha, cast
off by Rome's rejected friendship and needing every ally he could
still find, came to Bocchus with an offer of his own.

## A speech about a hunger that never stops

The two kings met with their armies at an agreed place, exchanged
pledges, and Jugurtha made his case in a single speech: the Romans
were unjust and boundlessly greedy, the common enemy of every people,
and had no more real quarrel with Bocchus than they had once had with
Carthage, or with King Perses of Macedon after that - each simply the
richest victim within reach at the time. Whoever they had not yet
attacked would be attacked eventually; it was only a matter of whose
turn came next. Persuaded, Bocchus took up arms against Rome
alongside his new son-in-law, marching with him toward the Roman
spoils gathered at Cirta.
