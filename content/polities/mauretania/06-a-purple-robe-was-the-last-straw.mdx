Three generations after Bocchus and Bogud backed Caesar's winning
side, the Mauretanian throne belonged to Ptolemy, grandson of Mark
Antony through his mother, Cleopatra Selene, and son of Juba, a king
whom the emperor Caligula counted among his own cousins and invited to
Rome with every mark of honor. The reception did not last. Caligula
had the young king put to death for no greater offense, by the
historian Suetonius's own account, than that Ptolemy had drawn every
eye in the theatre at a public exhibition by the splendor of his own
purple robe - a display of royal magnificence the emperor could not
forgive in a client king who was supposed to owe his own position to
Rome's favor.

## A death with an empire attached

Ptolemy's execution, probably in AD 40, left Mauretania without an
heir Rome recognized, and the emperor who had ordered the killing
simply absorbed the kingdom rather than name a successor. The
Mauretanian throne Bocchus had first brought into the Roman historical
record a century and a half earlier, through an embassy Rome's own
corrupt senators had once refused to answer, ended the same way most
of the ancient world's smaller kingdoms eventually did: annexed by an
emperor who wanted the throne more than he wanted any reason to
justify taking it.
