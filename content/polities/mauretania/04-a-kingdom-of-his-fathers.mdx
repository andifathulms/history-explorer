Not long after Actium, Augustus settled the fate of a boy who had
been paraded through Rome as a child in his father's triumph and then
raised at the emperor's own court. Juba, son of the Numidian king
Juba I, was given both a bride and a throne: Cleopatra Selene,
daughter of Antony and Cleopatra, and "the kingdom of his fathers" -
Numidia, the realm his father had lost to Caesar's own conquest a
generation before. It was restoration by favour rather than by right,
granted to a man who had spent his whole life among the people who
had destroyed his father's kingdom in the first place.

The arrangement did not last as first made. In 25 BC Augustus moved
Juba again, trading his hereditary Numidian throne - by then already
folded largely into Roman provincial territory - for Mauretania and
the former domains of Bocchus and Bogud. For the first time since
Caesar's civil war split the kingdom between two rival kings,
Mauretania answered to one ruler alone, and that ruler owed the whole
of his throne, twice over now, to Roman favour rather than Mauretanian
succession.

## A revolt Rome had to finish for him

Favour did not buy loyalty from the kingdom's own subjects. Around AD
6 the Gaetulians, a people within Juba's own territory, rose against
him, unwilling to accept rule at one remove by a king who was himself
little more than a Roman appointee. They ravaged the country
bordering their own lands and killed a number of the Roman soldiers
sent to help put them down, gaining enough ground that it took a
dedicated Roman commander, Cornelius Cossus, to finish the campaign -
a service substantial enough that Cossus returned to Rome with
triumphal honours and a new title, Gaetulicus, taken from the very
people he had subdued in another king's name.
