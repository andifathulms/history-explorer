A geographer writing in the last years of his own life, revising an
account of the western world he had been building for decades, paused
over Mauretania long enough to note what had become of the king who
ruled it. Juba, he records, had held the kingdom "having received it
from Augustus Caesar, in addition to his paternal dominions" - the
same double grant of Numidia and then Mauretania that had defined the
whole of his reign. And then, in the same breath, the geographer
closes the account of him: Juba "died lately, and was succeeded by
his son Ptolemy, whose mother was the daughter of Antony and
Cleopatra."

<Figure id="ma-selene" />

Nothing in the geographer's own words marks the succession as
contested, delayed, or violent. Ptolemy took his father's throne as
plainly as the sentence that records it - the son of Cleopatra Selene
inheriting a kingdom his grandmother had lost and his father had spent
a lifetime being given back, piece by piece, at another empire's
discretion.
