The Sulu tradition has a foreign holy man arriving and marrying into the local
ruling house. Sayyid Abu Bakr, a *sarip* — a descendant of the Prophet —
reached Jolo around 1450 by way of Palembang, married the daughter of the
ruling datu, and became the first sultan.

Before him, in the same tradition, comes Karim ul-Makhdum, a preacher who
arrived around 1380 and whose mosque at Tubig-Indangan on Simunul is claimed as
the oldest in the Philippines; and Rajah Baguinda, a Sumatran prince, some
decades later.

## The shape of the story

It is the same story Melaka tells about Parameswara, and Ternate and Pasai tell
in their own versions: a stranger of prestigious descent arrives by sea,
marries the local house, and the polity dates itself from the marriage.

The consistency is not evidence that it happened the same way five times. It is
evidence about what these courts thought a founding was, and the two components
are always the same — descent from somewhere important, and a local woman.
Neither on its own would do.

Note what it implies about the local house: it was there, it had something
worth marrying into, and the chronicle does not pretend otherwise. What
preceded the sultanate was not a blank but a polity of datus that the sultanate
was grafted onto.

## And the datus stayed

That is the substance rather than the etiquette.

Sulu was a sultanate over an archipelago of communities each under its own
datu, who held their own followings, their own boats and their own share of
what was taken. The sultan's authority over them was real, negotiated, and
variable; the sultanate's periods of weakness are periods when particular datus
were acting on their own account, and Spanish and later British officials
dealing with Sulu found repeatedly that an agreement with the sultan was not an
agreement with the datus.

Brunei was built the same way, on ranked noble houses whose senior officers
decided which member of the ruling family held the throne and for how long. So
was Melaka. So was Johor. Asking what Sulu's rule of succession was is asking
the question in the wrong shape: the sultan's position was an outcome of a
standing negotiation with the datus, and the negotiation was the constitution.
