In 1899, with the Spanish gone and the Americans arriving, the sultan of Sulu
wrote to his *panglimas*:

> The Americans have come here in exchange for the Spaniards; they are a
> different people from the Spaniards, and it will not be good to "juramentado"
> against them. They did not come to take our lands, religion, or customs. They
> leave us our laws, and if you love yourselves and your country avoid coming to
> blows with the Americans, because they are like a matchbox — you strike one and
> they all go off.

Three centuries of resistance to Spain, and the instruction the sultanate
issued about its next occupier was: do not.

## What he was disowning

The *juramentado* was the Spanish name for the *mujahid* — a fighter prepared
by ritual, by *panditas*, for an attack he was not expected to survive. It had
been one of the standing instruments of the Moro wars, and Spanish officers
occupying Jolo had threatened any pandita suspected of conducting the rites.

Under the Americans it became the sultan's official policy to disown them. His
chief minister went further and argued publicly that the practice had no basis
in the religion at all.

The panditas then produced the legal position to match. There were no longer
any real juramentados, they held, and those so described were not genuine —
because the condition that had made the thing meaningful no longer obtained. As
Majul puts it: for all practical purposes the Sulu sultanate had ceased to
exist as a political entity.

That is what an afterlife looks like from the inside. Not a defeat, but a body
of scholars quietly reasoning their way to the conclusion that a category no
longer applies.

## The same argument, in the same decade, elsewhere

It was not only happening at Jolo.

In the First World War the Ottoman sultan issued a universal call to jihad and
very little answered it. The forces working against him — nationalism,
secularisation, the modern state — were the same ones working on the panditas
of Sulu, and they produced the same result in both places within a few years of
each other: an old claim on universal obligation, issued in the proper form,
and met with a shrug.

The word *juramentado* survived the thing it named. It came to mean any Muslim
who died fighting bravely for any cause at all, personal or otherwise, and the
panditas whose predecessors had prepared such men now reported anyone who asked
for the rites to the police.

## What was left

A great deal, and none of it a state.

Jamal ul-Kiram II remained sultan from 1884 until his death in 1936, having
surrendered his political powers in the middle of it, in 1915. The office
continued as the religious headship of the Muslims of Sulu, and his successors
hold it still. So does the claim descending from the word *padjak* in the 1878
contract, which two modern countries have been arguing over ever since.

The sultanate stopped being a polity and did not stop existing - a state
dissolved by signature while the office and the family that held it carried
on regardless, outliving the very sovereignty it had signed away.
