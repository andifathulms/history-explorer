Spain attacked Jolo in 1578, in the same season it took Brunei, and did not
take it. What followed is the longest continuous resistance to Spanish rule
anywhere in the Philippines — three centuries of it, which the Spanish called the
Moro wars and never finished.

What ended the sultanate's position was not an army. It was steam.

Spanish steamers destroyed the Balangingi settlements in 1848 and deported the
survivors to Luzon. A steam vessel did not depend on the monsoon, which meant it
could reach a raiding base in the season the raiders were at home — something no
sailing fleet in three hundred years had been able to do. The raiding stopped
being viable within a generation, and with it went both the sultanate's revenue
and the labour that produced its export.

## The document

In 1915 the sultan signed the Carpenter Agreement with the United States,
renouncing temporal sovereignty and retaining his authority as head of the Muslim
community of Sulu, which his successors hold.

It is the latest ending anywhere in this region, twelve years after this
corpus's Aceh record. And it is the only ending on this site delivered by the United
States, which is not in this corpus and is unlikely to be: a site organised
around polities with spans, capitals and successions has no obvious place for it.

`ended.type` is `gradual absorption` and `ended.by` is empty. Fourteen of this
region's twenty-two records have an empty `by` and this is the last of them by
date, and it is emptier than most — the polity on the other side is not merely
absent from this corpus, it is a kind of thing the corpus is not a list of.

## The count

`religions_carried` reads one, and it is the northernmost of the ten counts of
Sunni Islam in this region.

This corpus's Pasai record traces the chain: a throne on the north Sumatran
coast in 1267, a marriage into Melaka in 1414, and from there outward through the
ports. Sulu is one of the ends of it. Brunei's count covers Borneo and Manila bay
and this one covers the archipelago between Borneo and Mindanao and the coasts
beyond — and the line those two records mark is still the boundary of the Muslim
south of the Philippines.

Between them and the Spanish coming the other way across the Pacific in 1571,
this is where two of the world's expansions met. Neither record has a field for
that either.
