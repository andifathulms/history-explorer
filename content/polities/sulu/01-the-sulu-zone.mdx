Sulu's `revenue_basis` reads `trade-toll` and `plunder`. One other record on this
site carries that pair and it is this corpus's Champa record — a string of ports
on the coast of what is now Vietnam, six thousand kilometres away and eight
hundred years earlier.

Two polities on two coasts of the same trade route, both selling what their
hinterland produced and both taking what passed when selling was not enough, and
in both cases the same fleets and the same crews did both. The Champa record says
that trade and raiding on that coast were one skill applied in two directions.
It is the better sentence and it fits here exactly.

## What was sold

The China trade in the eighteenth century wanted things that could be gathered
rather than grown: tripang, the sea cucumber, dried and shipped to Canton; pearl
shell; birds' nests from limestone caves; wax; shark fin; tortoiseshell.

Sulu sat in the middle of the richest gathering grounds in the world for most of
that list, and British country traders out of India wanted a place to sell opium
and cloth for goods that Canton would take. Jolo became the exchange. The
sultanate taxed it.

## What was taken

The labour that gathered the tripang and dived the pearl shell was largely
captured, and the capturing was done by the Iranun and the Balangingi — maritime
peoples who were clients and allies of the sultanate rather than its subjects,
who kept most of what they took, and whose fleets ranged from Luzon to the
Moluccas and across to the Malay peninsula.

The scale is large. Tens of thousands of people were taken from the coasts of the
Philippines and eastern Indonesia over the century in which the trade was at its
height, and the Spanish coastal watchtowers still standing in the Visayas were
built against it.

`military_basis` here reads `client-levy`, and it is the same relation this
corpus's Srivijaya, Melaka and Johor records carry with the orang laut of the
straits — sea peoples attached to a polity that could not command them. What
differs is what the arrangement was for. Those three fleets made a strait safe,
which is why those records are coded `trade-toll` and the tolls were a protection
charge. These ones made a region dangerous, on the sultanate's behalf and for a
share.
