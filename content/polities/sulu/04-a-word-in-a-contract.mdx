In 1878 the sultan of Sulu signed an agreement with two European merchants over
the territory Sulu claimed on the north-east coast of Borneo. The rights passed
to a chartered company, and from the company to Britain, and from Britain into
the settlement that made that coast part of Malaysia.

The agreement was written in Jawi. The operative word is *padjak*.

Sulu's position, and that of the sultan's heirs since, is that *padjak* means
lease — that the territory was rented for an annual payment. The company's
translation, and the position of the states that inherited its rights, rendered
it as cession. The annual payment was made for well over a century by
successive administrations, which is a fact both readings can accommodate and
neither can settle.

## Why a word matters this much

Not for the modern dispute, which is outside this period and outside what a
page like this can settle. It is here because it is the clearest thing in these
islands about what the ending of a polity actually consists of.

The sultanate did not fall. It signed.

In 1878 it signed away the Borneo coast in a text whose central word was
contested at the moment of writing. In 1915 it signed away temporal sovereignty
itself, by another document, to another power. Between those two signatures
there is no siege to describe, no battle, no dynasty replaced. There is a
sultan with a diminishing set of things left to concede, and a succession of
counterparties arriving with drafts.

That is how a great many polities in this part of the world ended, and it is
harder to narrate than a conquest because there is no moment to point at. A
treaty has a date but not a fall. The Attalid kingdom was left to Rome in a
will. Venice voted itself out of existence. The Holy Roman Empire was dissolved
by its own emperor. Brunei was given away in three pieces to two companies and
a private Englishman.

None of those is a defeat in the sense a siege is. All of them are endings, and
what they have in common is a signature.
