The 1878 agreement over North Borneo did not end with the nineteenth century.
Every year since, the Malaysian embassy in Manila has paid a sum - now 5,300
ringgit - to the heirs of the Sulu sultanate, the same figure the original
contract fixed once its coverage was extended to the coastal islands. A
payment meant to close a transaction has instead kept the transaction legally
alive for a hundred and fifty years, because nobody on either side has ever
agreed on what the payment is for.

## Two words, one cheque

Britain always read the 1878 agreement as a cession: North Borneo transferred
outright, the annual sum a fixed tribute rather than rent. Sulu's own reading,
carried down through the sultanate's descendants, was that the territory had
only been leased - and a lease implies a landlord who can eventually ask for
the land back. North Borneo passed from British hands into the Federation of
Malaysia in 1963 and was renamed Sabah; the payments continued regardless,
inherited along with the territory itself, and so did the disagreement about
what they meant.

## A claim that outlived every government that might have owned it

The Philippines itself pressed the Sabah claim on Malaysia's behalf when
Sabah joined the federation in 1963, then formally agreed in 1977 to stop
pursuing it. That did not settle anything for the sultanate's own heirs, who
had never been a party to either government's negotiations and had no reason
to consider themselves bound by an agreement made without them. In 2008 a
former Moro guerrilla leader tried to bring the claim before the
International Court of Justice; Malaysia's own authorities in Sabah dismissed
it as a non-issue.

## When the claim stopped being theoretical

In February 2013 it stopped being theoretical. Some two to three hundred
armed men loyal to Jamalul Kiram III, who held the sultanate's now-ceremonial
title, landed at a village in Lahad Datu and refused to leave, describing
themselves as returning to land their own family had only ever rented out.
Malaysia's counter-offensive that March spread the fighting to nearby towns
and left sixty-two dead, Malaysian security forces among them, before the
occupation was broken.

Nothing was resolved by it. The claim rests on a documentary disagreement
over a single word in an 1878 contract, and neither the death toll at Lahad
Datu nor a century and a half of annual payments has settled which reading of
that word was correct - which is exactly the condition the claim has existed
in since 1878, made briefly violent rather than made any clearer.
