Few rulers of any early modern African kingdom have been claimed, redrawn
and reinterpreted by as many different traditions after their death as
Nzinga Mbandi. Her name itself survives in a dozen or more spellings across
the sources - Njinga, Nzinga, Jinga, Singa, Zhinga, Ginga and others - partly
an accident of transcribing Kimbundu into Portuguese and partly a product
of the Queen's own habit of signing her correspondence differently
depending on who she was addressing.

## A queen reimagined for two centuries of readers

The Italian missionary Giovanni Antonio Cavazzi's own 1687 account of her
1622 meeting with Governor Correia de Sousa - the meeting where she famously
supplied her own chair rather than accept none - became the founding image
for everything written about her afterward. In 1769 the French writer
Jean-Louis Castilhon turned her into the subject of the first anti-colonial
historical novel written about Africa, and in 1830 the French illustrator
Achille Devéria produced an imagined portrait of her that Europe
subsequently treated, with no better evidence behind it than an artist's
own imagination, as her official likeness.

## An icon claimed by an independence movement she never knew

Twentieth-century Angola picked up her memory for a very different purpose
than any nineteenth-century novelist had. Nationalist leaders of the
Angolan war of liberation invoked her explicitly as a symbol of resistance,
and when independence was finally proclaimed in 1975, a statue of Nzinga
was raised in Luanda - the city her own great-grandfather had once
received Portuguese visitors near, and the city her own kingdom fought a
century-long war never to lose control of.

## A presence beyond Angola's own borders

Her memory did not stop at Angola. In Brazil, where many of her own former
soldiers were shipped as slaves after her death, she is honored in the
Congada processions that blend African and European tradition, and
portrayed in Candomblé as a warrior spirit called Matamba, after the
kingdom she built her resistance from. In Haiti, a variant of vodou
represents her among its own spirits. Across three continents and four
centuries, the queen who spent her own reign refusing to accept that
Ndongo's throne was lost has been remembered, again and again, as proof
that it does not have to stay that way.
