Nzinga Mbandi arrived in Luanda in 1622 as her brother's envoy, sent to
negotiate an end to a war that had already ruined the country. She was, by
then, already unusually prepared for the task: taught to read and write by
Portuguese missionaries and merchants who had passed through Ndongo since
her own childhood, and fluent enough in Portuguese to deal with the
colony's governor directly, in his own language, without an intermediary.

## A seat claimed rather than given

When she entered the reception room for her audience with Governor João
Correia de Sousa, no chair had been provided for her - a deliberate
slight, meant to seat the Portuguese governor above a mere envoy from a
defeated kingdom. Nzinga's response, recorded by the Italian missionary
Giovanni Antonio Cavazzi and repeated by nearly every writer on her life
since, was to order one of her own attendants to kneel on all fours and
serve as her chair. The governor had offered her no seat of honor. She
supplied her own, and negotiated the peace from it as an equal rather than
a supplicant.

## An education put to use against the people who gave it

The peace Nzinga negotiated at that meeting did not survive her brother's
own death two years later, and the war she would spend the next four
decades fighting drew directly on what her time among Portuguese
missionaries had taught her - not just the language, but a working
understanding of how the colony's own governors thought and negotiated.
She would go on to send spies regularly into Luanda to track troop
movements and study Portuguese fighting methods, training her own forces
in the tactics she had learned about from the very people she was
fighting. The gesture with the chair, more than thirty years before her
death, was the first public sign of a mind the Portuguese Crown would
never manage to outmaneuver.
