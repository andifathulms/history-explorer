Ndongo took shape around 1500 out of a merger of many small chiefdoms in
the Kwanza River valley, south of Kongo - a very different foundation from
the alliance of large existing provinces that had built Kongo two centuries
earlier. Where Kongo's founding had joined established powers together,
Ndongo's joined fragments, and the result was a state whose military
system had to work harder than Kongo's own ever needed to, because Ndongo
had no layer of provincial government above the level of the petty
chiefdom to fall back on. What held Ndongo together in its first
generation was less an inherited administrative hierarchy than an army
built specifically to compensate for not having one.

## Ndongo in Kongo's shadow

For at least the first two decades of the sixteenth century, Ndongo's own
independence was only partial. As late as 1520, the kingdom was still
partly subordinate to Kongo - part of a wider pattern in which Kongo's own
hegemony reached south along the coast well beyond its own core provinces.
Ndongo's rulers bore the title ngola, a word Portuguese sailors would later
mangle into the name of the entire region: Angola.

## A title that outlived the kingdom that coined it

The kings who held that title left almost no trace in written sources
before the middle of the sixteenth century, when the first Portuguese
expeditions began working their way down the coast south of Kongo. What
would make Ndongo's own name famous, in the end, was not the kingdom's
founding or its early independence from Kongo, but the war of conquest it
would spend most of the following century fighting - one that outlasted
the kingdom's own sovereignty by several decades, carried forward by a
queen who refused to accept that the war had already been lost.
