Exiled from the kingdom her own family had ruled, Nzinga spent the next
thirty-seven years fighting a war she had, by any ordinary measure, already
lost - and came closer to reversing it than anyone in Luanda expected a
deposed queen ruling from a neighboring kingdom to manage.

## Alliances built out of a losing position

She forged strategic partnerships with Kongo, with the Kassanje and
Kissama peoples, and, once the Dutch seized Luanda from Portugal in 1641,
with the Dutch Republic itself - the same pattern of playing European
rivals against each other that Kongo's own king Garcia II pursued at
exactly the same moment, against exactly the same colonial power. Her army
fought a pitched battle against twenty thousand Portuguese soldiers at
Kavangaem in the 1640s and won a further victory at Ilamba, and she
authorized her own slave trade between Matamba and Luanda to fund the
decades of warfare her resistance required - the same trade her ancestors
in Ndongo had fought a war against Portugal to control, now run on her own
terms from a kingdom that was not, strictly, the one she had been born to
rule.

## Outlasting the men sent to defeat her

Portuguese governor after Portuguese governor arrived determined to finish
the war Nzinga's own exile was supposed to have already ended, and each in
turn found her impossible to capture, outmaneuver, or intimidate into
surrender. When Governor Salvador Correia de Sá arrived to find her still
in the field and still commanding real loyalty past the age of seventy, he
concluded there was nothing left to do against a sovereign of her
reputation. On 24 November 1657, the Portuguese Crown formally renounced
its own claim to rule in her place, in a treaty ratified in Lisbon - not a
restoration of the kingdom she had lost in 1626, but the closest
acknowledgment Portugal ever made that the queen it had spent three decades
trying to remove could not be removed by force.

## A name that outlived the throne it named

Nzinga died on 17 December 1663, at the age of eighty-two, having ruled
Matamba and claimed Ndongo for thirty-seven years without ever setting foot
in Kabasa as its queen again. Ndongo's own remaining territory, whatever
of it still answered to a Ngola after her death, fell to Portugal for good
in 1671. Matamba, the kingdom she had actually built her long resistance
from, endured under her own successors for generations afterward. Of the
two crowns she claimed until her last day, the one she was born to inherit
was the one that did not survive her - and the one she had to conquer for
herself was the one that did.
