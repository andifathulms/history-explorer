Nearly four decades into the war, Portugal was still struggling to
translate its coastal foothold into control of Ndongo's own interior. What
finally tipped the balance was not a Portuguese army at all, but an
alliance struck in 1612 with the Mbangala - nomadic warrior bands, called
Jaga by the same sources that used the name for the invaders of Kongo two
generations earlier, who had been living in the region by pillage since
before 1600. With Mbangala fighters, and especially those under a leader
named Kasanje, doing much of the actual fighting, the Portuguese occupied a
large part of Ndongo between 1617 and 1621.

## A country laid waste by its own war

Ndongo's king fled east ahead of the advance, and the Mbangala allies did
not spare the country they had helped conquer - they sacked it as
thoroughly as any enemy, leaving the land devastated behind them. Even the
slave trade that had driven so much of the preceding decades of warfare
came to a standstill amid the destruction, and famine spread through a
kingdom that could no longer feed itself while fighting off a war on this
scale.

## Peace bought at a price already too high

By 1622 the devastation had become severe enough that both sides needed a
settlement, and a peace treaty was concluded the following year. Ndongo's
own king had lost the ability to negotiate that peace directly. It was his
sister who represented the kingdom instead, sent to Luanda as an envoy -
and the diplomatic performance she gave there would make her a far more
consequential figure to Ndongo's own future than the treaty itself turned
out to be.
