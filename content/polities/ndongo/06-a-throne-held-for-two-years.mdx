Ngola Mbandi, the king Nzinga had represented at Luanda in 1622, never
matched his own father's authority or his sister's evident intelligence,
and the years after the peace treaty did not go well for him. Fearing a
plot from within his own circle, he had Nzinga's only son - a small child -
put to death, a decision that cost him his sister's trust without buying
him any lasting security. In 1624 Ngola Mbandi himself died, by some
accounts murdered and by others a suicide, and Nzinga took power, first as
regent and then, in 1626, as queen in her own right.

## A war that never paused for the succession

The Mbangala leader Kasanje was still occupying the richest part of
Ndongo's own territory when Nzinga took the throne, and Portugal, rather
than treat the new queen as a partner for the peace her own diplomacy had
once secured, moved instead to unseat her. War broke out again in 1626,
against Nzinga's own government and against Kasanje both, and this time
Portugal did not stop at reoccupying territory - it moved to install a
rival ruler of its own choosing in Nzinga's place.

## Driven out, not defeated

Within three years, the Portuguese-backed rival held Kabasa and the
Ndongo throne Nzinga's own family had ruled from for at least three
generations. Nzinga herself was not captured and did not surrender. She
withdrew instead into the neighboring Kingdom of Matamba, conquered it,
and built there a new base from which to keep fighting a war Portugal
believed, at that point, it had already won. She never accepted the loss.
For the rest of her life she signed her own letters and treaties as Queen
of Ndongo and Matamba both - a title claiming a throne she no longer held
in a kingdom that, from that point on, no longer had a Ndongo dynasty
ruling it at all.
