Paulo Dias de Novais came back to Ndongo in 1575 with a fleet of soldiers,
not the allied army he had been asked for a decade earlier. Ndongo's own
people, caught unprepared by a mission they had every reason to expect was
friendly, defended their homeland but were beaten back by superior
Portuguese firepower. The Portuguese seized the whole coastal strip in a
single rapid campaign, renamed it Angola after Ndongo's own royal title,
and founded a new port there that same year: São Paulo da Assunção de
Loanda, known ever after simply as Luanda.

## A colony built to feed a different kind of demand

The gold and silver the Portuguese Crown had hoped to find during Dias de
Novais's earlier stay at Kabasa never materialized. Deprived of the mineral
wealth that had justified the colony in the first place, Luanda's founders
turned instead to a trade Ndongo's own territory could supply in abundance:
captives, sold in large numbers to labor on Portugal's plantations in
Brazil. Every subsequent stage of the war against Ndongo would be driven
as much by the demand for slaves to fill Luanda's own export quotas as by
any remaining ambition to find precious metal at all.

## A border that only moved one way

Portuguese migrants - missionaries, farmers, merchants and independent
adventurers alike - poured into the new colony behind the soldiers, taking
land as they found it. Ndongo's own borders contracted steadily eastward
under the pressure, a war that had begun as a betrayed alliance settling
into a slow, grinding annexation that would not stop until the kingdom
itself was gone.
