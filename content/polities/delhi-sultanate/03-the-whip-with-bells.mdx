Ibn Battuta travelled from Multan to Delhi in the 1330s and reports that the
journey takes fifty days. He then reports that the sultan's despatches make it
in five.

## How

Two relay systems, running in parallel.

The horse couriers are stationed every four miles, mounted from the sultan's
own cavalry. The foot couriers are closer: one every mile, in stations of
three, so that every three miles there is an inhabited village and, outside it,
three sentry-boxes where the runners sit with their loins girded, ready to move.

Each of them holds a whip about two cubits long with small bells fixed at the
head of it. A courier leaving a city carries the despatches in one hand and
shakes the whip continuously in the other, and as he approaches the next station
the bells announce him, so that a fresh runner comes out and takes the packet
without either of them stopping.

*For this reason it is,* Ibn Battuta concludes, *that the Sultan receives his
despatches in so short a time.*

## What that buys

Ten times the speed of a traveller, over a thousand kilometres, on foot.

A state that can do that can govern provinces it cannot see. It can hear about a
revolt in Sind while the revolt is still small, order a response, and have the
order arrive before the situation has changed beyond recognition. Everything the
Delhi sultans did at distance — the Deccan campaigns, the transfers of governors,
the suppression of the recurring rebellions of the far provinces — rests on it.

It is also, unusually for a medieval institution, described in a way that lets
you check the arithmetic. Foot relays a mile apart, run flat out, will move a
packet somewhere between five and eight miles an hour continuously through the
day and night. Fifty days' travel in five is exactly what that produces.

## The rest of the machine

The sultanate's expansion in the fourteenth century was the largest thing any
Indian state had attempted since the Mauryas, and it was built on that kind of
apparatus rather than on a larger army.

Ala al-Din Khalji, in the two decades either side of 1300, took Gujarat, broke
the Rajput fortresses at Ranthambore and Chittor, and sent his armies as far as
the far south — Madurai — while simultaneously holding the Punjab against
repeated Mongol invasions.

<Figure id="ds-alai" />

He paid for it with a fiscal apparatus that was closer to a command economy than
anything else in the medieval world: land revenue assessed at half the produce
and taken in kind, the intermediary chiefs stripped of their exemptions, and
prices of grain, cloth, horses and slaves fixed by decree in the Delhi markets
with an inspectorate to enforce them.

<Figure id="ds-alacoin" />

The point of the price controls was military. If grain and horses cost a known
fixed amount, a soldier can be paid a known fixed salary — which meant a large
standing army maintained in cash rather than by land assignment, which meant
troops who belonged to the sultan rather than to whoever held the district.

## And the direction of the conquest

There is one thing about the southern campaigns worth stating plainly. They were
not annexations.

The armies went south, took an enormous quantity of treasure, imposed tribute,
and came home. Malik Kafur's expedition to Madurai in 1311 is the deepest, and
it left no garrison and no administration.

Which is why the empire built on the courier relay had, within a generation of
its greatest reach, produced independent sultanates in the Deccan and Bengal
that the couriers could no longer reach.
