Iltutmish had grown sons, and named none of them his heir. When his
courtiers objected that a daughter had no business inheriting a throne
with capable princes available, he told them plainly that his sons
were absorbed in youthful pleasure and fit to manage nothing, and that
after his death none of them would prove worthier of it than she was.
Raziyyat - Razia - became his declared successor by that judgment
alone, and when the throne came to her in 1236 the nobles who had
expected otherwise rose against her immediately, mustering before the
gates of Delhi itself. She broke the coalition through the same tools
her rivals were using against her: she turned two of the rebel
commanders secretly to her own side, had the ringleaders seized in the
night, and watched the rest scatter and flee once the trap closed.

## A woman who rode as a man

Razia governed capably enough that the court chronicler Minhaj-i-Siraj
- who held office under her directly, first as qadi of Gwalior and
then, at her own appointment, as head of a college she founded at
Delhi - records her leading armies in person: putting down a governor
of Lahore's rebellion in the Punjab, campaigning into Gwalior and
Malwa, riding out at the head of her own forces rather than governing
from behind a curtain. In the last year of her reign she set aside
female dress, put on a man's tunic and headdress, and began appearing
before her subjects openly on an elephant rather than shielded from
public view - a break with convention Minhaj records without
apparent alarm, though it gave the Turkish nobles a grievance they had
been looking for.

## An Abyssinian's favor, and a revolt with two names

What the nobles actually resented, on Minhaj's own telling, was her
elevation of Jamal-ud-Din Yakut, an Abyssinian who held the office of
master of her horse, to a prominence that cut the old Turkish
aristocracy out of her inner circle. Later writers embroidered this
into an accusation of scandal; Minhaj, who was there, states only that
the Turk maliks grew envious, and Raverty's own footnotes to the
translation dismiss the more lurid later versions as unsupported
invention layered onto a contemporary's plain account. When Malik
Altunia, the governor of Tabarhindah, rose in revolt in 1240, the
resentment gave his rebellion the backing it needed: the Turkish
amirs at her own camp killed Yakut, seized Razia, and imprisoned her
in Altunia's own fortress.

## A marriage, a betrayal, a death shared

Altunia, out of calculation as much as anything Minhaj can vouch for,
married his prisoner and turned his army to restoring her rather than
replacing her - marching on Delhi together to unseat the half-brother,
Bahram, whom the nobles had placed on the throne in her absence. The
combined force was routed near Kaithal, and the troops who had joined
them melted away in the retreat. Razia and Altunia, left without an
army, fell into the hands of local Hindu chiefs and were killed in
1240 - a reign of three years, six months and six days, over her own
father's sons, for the one child he had judged fit to rule after him.
