Timur crossed the Indus in 1398, took Delhi in December, and left in January.
The sultanate never recovered, and it took another hundred and twenty-eight
years to stop existing.

## The sack

He came, on his own stated reasoning, because the sultans of Delhi had been too
tolerant of their Hindu subjects — an argument advanced by a man who had spent
his career sacking Muslim cities, and which is best read as what it was.

The city was taken and given over to three days of massacre and plunder. The
craftsmen, in the usual Timurid practice, were carried off to Samarkand, where
they built the great congregational mosque. The chroniclers give figures for
the dead that cannot be relied on and describe towers of skulls, which Timur
built elsewhere as well and which are not in doubt as a practice.

Then he went home. There was no attempt to hold anything.

## What that did

Delhi was reported deserted for two months and depopulated for years. More
importantly, the fiction that the sultanate was the paramount power of northern
India had been publicly destroyed, and the provinces drew the conclusion.

Jaunpur, Malwa, Gujarat, Bengal and the Deccan all consolidated as independent
sultanates in this period, each with its own court, its own architecture and its
own literary patronage. Several of them were richer than what was left at Delhi.

The Sayyid dynasty that emerged in 1414 governed a territory a contemporary
verse described as running from Delhi to Palam — about ten miles. That is a
joke made at the time and it is roughly accurate.

## The Lodis

The Afghans who took over in 1451 did better, and their difficulty is the more
interesting one.

Bahlul Lodi and his successors were Afghans among Afghans, and the Afghan tribal
convention was that a chief is first among equals. Bahlul ate with his nobles and
sat with them. His grandson Ibrahim tried to govern as a sultan in the old
Turkish manner — requiring the nobles to stand in his presence, disciplining
them, executing some — and they went and found somebody else.

The somebody else was Babur, who had been raiding into the Punjab for years and
had been invited by Ibrahim's own governor of Lahore and his own uncle.

At Panipat in April 1526 a smaller force with field artillery and a fortified
centre destroyed an army several times its size. Ibrahim was killed on the field
— the only sultan of Delhi to die in battle — and the sultanate ended.

## Three hundred and twenty years

Five dynasties, thirty-odd sultans, and the arithmetic of how they left is worth
setting down: assassinated, deposed, blinded, or dead of natural causes in
roughly equal proportions, with a great many of the transitions decided by the
slave officers, the Afghan amirs, or the sultan's own household.

That instability is usually treated as the sultanate's defining weakness, and it
coexisted with something remarkable. This is the state that held the Mongols on
the Indus when nothing else between the Pacific and the Mediterranean did. It
governed most of the subcontinent for a generation. It ran a courier relay from
Sind to Bengal.

A polity can have no working rule for replacing its ruler and still be the most
effective administration of its age. The two facts sit side by side for three
centuries and neither cancels the other.
