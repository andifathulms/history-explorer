The Delhi Sultanate's largest and least intended legacy is a language that
about half a billion people now speak.

## Where Hindustani came from

The sultanate ran on Persian. The chancery was Persian, the poetry was Persian,
the histories were Persian, and Persian remained the language of administration
in northern India until the British abolished it in 1837 — which means the
official language of the Indian state was Persian for six hundred years.

But an army, a court and a bazaar in Delhi contained Turkic soldiers, Persian
secretaries, Afghan amirs and a local population speaking the Khari Boli dialect
of the upper Doab, and what came out of that was not Persian. It was a language
with an Indic grammar and an enormously enlarged vocabulary — Persian, Arabic
and Turkish words carried in wholesale, on top of a Sanskritic base.

Nobody decreed it. It grew where the languages met, spread along the routes the
sultanate's armies and administrators used, and reached the Deccan with the
southern campaigns, where it acquired a literature earlier than it did in the
north.

That language is Hindustani, and its two modern standardised forms are Hindi and
Urdu — separated in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries by script and by
which reservoir their formal vocabulary is drawn from, and mutually intelligible
in ordinary speech to this day.

Amir Khusrau, at the court of five sultans between the 1280s and 1320s, wrote in
Persian and also in the vernacular, and is the earliest poet anybody can name in
it.

## The buildings

The Qutb Minar was begun by Aibak in the 1190s and is still the tallest brick
minaret in the world.

What is around it is more informative than the tower. The Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque
beside it was built out of the columns of demolished temples, reused whole, with
the figural sculpture defaced but the carving otherwise intact — so a
congregational mosque of the first years of Muslim rule in India stands on
recycled Hindu and Jain pillars that are visibly what they are.

Over the following three centuries that hybridity stopped being improvised. The
Indo-Islamic architecture of the sultanate — corbelled arches learned from local
masons, then true arches; Indian materials and Indian craftsmen working Persian
plans; the Tughluq fortresses, the Lodi tombs — is the direct ancestor of what
the Mughals built, and the Taj Mahal is at the end of an unbroken line that
starts here.

## The Sufis

The other transmission is religious and it did not travel through the state.

The Chishti order established itself in India in this period — Muin al-Din at
Ajmer, Nizam al-Din Auliya at Delhi, whose shrine is still one of the busiest
pilgrimage sites in India — and its practice was deliberately unofficial:
shaykhs who refused court appointments and royal gifts, whose lodges fed anybody
who came, and whose devotional music, *qawwali*, was open to non-Muslims.

Nizam al-Din outlived seven sultans and made a point of not visiting any of
them. When Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq sent word that Delhi was not big enough for
both of them and that the shaykh should leave before he returned from campaign,
Nizam al-Din is said to have answered *Dilli hanuz dur ast* — Delhi is still far
away — and the sultan died on the road.

<Figure id="ds-nizam" />

The conversions that made the Indian subcontinent's Muslim population, which is
now the largest in the world outside Indonesia, happened overwhelmingly through
that channel rather than through the sultans, and mostly in the two regions
furthest from Delhi's control: Bengal and the Punjab.

Which is the shape of the whole afterlife. The sultanate's deliberate
projects — the campaigns, the price controls, the capital moved to Daulatabad and
back — mostly failed. What lasted came out of the sides of it: a language nobody
planned, a building style nobody theorised, and a religious movement that made a
point of keeping away from the court.
