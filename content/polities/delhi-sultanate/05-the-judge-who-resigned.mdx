Ibn Battuta was appointed qadi of Delhi by Muhammad bin Tughluq, held the post
for some years, was ruined by debt, was rescued by the sultan, came within four
days of being executed by him, and resigned to become a wandering ascetic. His
account is the closest look anybody has at how the sultanate worked at the top.

## The appointment

The exchange at the appointment is the whole system in six lines.

The sultan warned him the office would require the greatest attention. Ibn
Battuta, who understood the warning perfectly, did not give a good answer: he
pointed out that he was of the Maliki school, that the people of Delhi followed
the Hanafi, and that he did not speak the language.

The sultan — who knew Arabic and was not pleased — replied that he had appointed
two learned men as deputies who would advise him, and that *it will be your
business to sign the legal instruments*.

A foreigner who could not read the local law or speak to the litigants was made
chief judge of the capital, with the actual work delegated, because the point of
the appointment was not the law. It was that a Moroccan scholar with no local
kin, no faction and no property owed everything to the man who had appointed
him.

## The debt

Then the finances, which are equally instructive.

He was given twelve thousand dinars. Within a short time he was fifty-five
thousand in debt, because attending on the sultan during his campaigns cost more
than the office paid.

His solution was to compose a panegyric in Arabic and read it out. The sultan
translated it himself, was wonderfully pleased — *the Indians are fond of Arabic
poetry* — and paid the debt from his own treasury with the advice not to exceed
his income in future.

A court where a senior officer's insolvency is normal, and where the remedy is a
poem, is not badly run by accident. It is a system in which nobody's position is
secure and everybody's continuation depends on the ruler's favour, refreshed
personally and frequently.

## The four days

The other side arrived when the sultan changed his mind about a shaykh he had
previously trusted and visited — a holy man living in a cave outside the city.

He had the shaykh imprisoned and then interrogated his children about who had
been visiting him. Ibn Battuta had.

He was put under guard. He spent four days reciting a Quranic formula — *God is
our support, and the most excellent patron* — thirty-three thousand times, and
was released.

> the Sheikh, and all those who had visited him, except myself, were put to
> death.

He resigned the judgeship immediately, gave everything he owned to the faqirs,
put on their tunic and attached himself to a shaykh for five months, during
which he fasted five days continuously and then broke his fast on a little rice.

## What this is a portrait of

Muhammad bin Tughluq is the most argued-about ruler in Indian medieval history,
and Ibn Battuta is why. He is also the reason the arguments are possible: no
other Indian sultan is described from the inside by somebody who held office
under him.

The picture is not of a madman. It is of an extremely capable and extremely
literate ruler with an enormous apparatus, an ambitious programme — the
transfer of the administration to Daulatabad in the Deccan, the token currency,
the Deccan and Khurasan campaigns — and no working restraint on the exercise of
his own judgement.

Every one of those projects was defensible in principle. Daulatabad is better
placed than Delhi to govern a southern empire. A fiduciary coinage had worked
in China. What none of them survived was being ordered by one man, executed by
officials who could be killed for advising against them, and reversed by the
same man when they went wrong.

By the end of his reign Bengal and the Deccan were independent and the
sultanate had lost most of what the previous fifty years had gained.
