Al-Mansur died in 1002 and the arrangement he had built survived him by six
years. Al-Maqqari records what the Andalusis called those six years afterwards,
and the name is the whole of the tragedy: *al-usbu*, the week, because they were
like the first week after a marriage.

## Abd al-Malik

The succession was managed as though it were a throne, which in substance it was.
Al-Mansur died at Medinaceli on campaign and was buried where he fell. His son
Abd al-Malik came back to Cordoba, and the caliph Hisham II — who had by then
been kept in his palace for a quarter of a century — clothed him with the robe of
honour himself and signed his appointment as chamberlain, exactly as he had done
for the father.

Al-Maqqari notes, in the middle of the ceremony, what it cost: this was not
achieved without *some alteration among the eunuchs of the palace*, but at last
those who leaned were put straight and the ill-disposed became loyal. A dynasty
of chamberlains changing hands required a purge of the household staff, and the
chronicler mentions it the way one mentions the weather.

Abd al-Malik then did what his father had done. Eight campaigns against the
Christian north; Leon taken and destroyed in 1004; the titles Sword of the State
and the Victorious. The Maghrawa ruler of Fez acknowledged Hisham as his liege
and was granted the investiture of western Africa on condition that he proclaim
Hisham's name and Abd al-Malik's from every pulpit and send Cordoba one horse,
one shield and a sum of money each year, with his son as a hostage.

Then Abd al-Malik died on the way back from a campaign in 1008, and the week
ended.

## The one thing the fiction could not survive

His brother Abd al-Rahman took the chamberlainship and kept Hisham as secluded as
before. And then he asked for the succession itself.

Hisham complied. The counsellors of state and the notaries were assembled to
witness it and authorise it by their presence, and a contemporary writer told
al-Maqqari it was a very solemn ceremony.

It was also the end. The arrangement had worked for thirty years precisely
because it never said out loud what it was. An Umayyad caliph reigned; a
chamberlain governed. The moment the chamberlain's family was written into the
succession, every Umayyad in Cordoba and everyone who had accepted the fiction
had a reason to act, and they did. Abd al-Rahman was assassinated. Muhammad ibn
Abd al-Jabbar was proclaimed as al-Mahdi.

## What twenty years of it looked like

After that the caliphate stopped being a thing anyone held for long.

Al-Mahdi turned against the Berber regiments his predecessors had recruited; they
were driven out of Cordoba, camped outside it, and proclaimed a rival, Sulayman.
Sulayman beat al-Mahdi and took the capital; al-Mahdi beat Sulayman and restored
Hisham. Then the Berbers came back and took Cordoba by storm. There was a general
massacre; Hisham was secretly put to death; houses were sacked, and men
distinguished for piety and learning died with everyone else.

Then the Hammudids, who claimed descent from Ali. Ali ibn Hammud killed Sulayman
and was proclaimed at Cordoba, and for eighteen months governed strictly and
well — al-Maqqari has an eyewitness story of him meeting a Berber trooper riding
out of the Gate of Amir with a load of grapes, asking where he had got them,
being told *I seized them like a man*, and having him beheaded on the spot, with
the head set on the grapes and paraded through the streets as a warning.

Then he learned that the Andalusis had raised an Umayyad claimant in the east,
and he changed entirely. He let the Berbers loose again. The great buildings of
the Umayyads were demolished or stripped, the rich were imprisoned until they
ransomed themselves — and when their families came to the prison gate with led
horses to carry them home, he confiscated the horses too, and the prisoners
walked.

Ali was assassinated. His brother was elected by the army, expelled by the
people, shut out of Seville, and ended a prisoner of his own nephew. Umayyad
claimants were proclaimed and put to death in succession.

The caliphate was not conquered. It was used up — by men who each needed it for
about a year.
