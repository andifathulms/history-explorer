Abd al-Rahman III came to the throne of Cordoba in 912 as ruler of Cordoba and
not much else. The two decades in which he made that into a state worth calling
a caliphate are the expansion of this polity, and almost all of it was fought
against people who were already his subjects.

## The rebel who killed an emir

The measure of how bad it had become is Umar ibn Hafsun, a lord of Christian
descent who raised the southern uplands and held them for forty years against
three successive rulers of Cordoba.

Al-Maqqari preserves the episode that made his name a byword. The emir al-Mundhir
had him besieged in one of his strongholds and pressed so hard that he could not
get out. So Ibn Hafsun offered to surrender — to come and live in Cordoba, on
terms: his life spared, his property untouched. Al-Mundhir agreed, issued the
letters of pardon and safe conduct, received him in his tent and treated him with
great kindness. Ibn Hafsun then asked for pack animals to carry his baggage, and
was given a hundred mules and ten companies of cavalry to escort his family to
the capital.

The judges and theologians who had come out with the army, seeing the war
finished, travelled home with the same convoy. Ibn Hafsun followed at a distance
with a band of outlaws — *like the hawk hovering over his prey*, in al-Maqqari's
phrase — fell on the column, killed the guards, took back his family and his
treasure, and returned to the mountains.

Al-Mundhir fought him for the rest of his short reign and was killed in a
skirmish against him in 888, two years and fifteen days after his accession. A
reigning emir of Cordoba died fighting a rebel inside his own country. That is
the state Abd al-Rahman III inherited a generation later.

## Taking it back

What followed was two decades of reduction: strongholds, valleys, factional lords
and rebel towns, one at a time, until al-Andalus was governed from Cordoba again.
Only when it was did he take the caliphal title, in 929 — the order that matters,
because it meant the claim described something rather than announcing an
intention.

Then the direction of effort turned outward, and the instrument was not primarily
an army.

An Idrisid prince of Morocco, with the Fatimids pressing him, swore vassalage to
Cordoba and asked for troops. Abd al-Rahman III would not send any unless he was
given the ports of Tangier and Ceuta as hostages. The prince refused; a Cordoban
fleet went and ravaged his coast until he accepted. Holding the two ports, the
caliph extended his authority over the country behind them until, al-Maqqari
says, he was obeyed from Tahert to Sijilmasa, and the Zanata chiefs and the
Idrisids and the other great families of the Maghreb came to court to kiss his
hand and hold their lands in his name.

A caliphate that had begun the reign as one city was now the arbiter of North
Africa, and it got there by demanding collateral from a man asking for help.

## The limit

It stopped in 939, in a place the Andalusis afterwards called simply the year of
al-Khandaq — the ditch.

Al-Masudi, whom al-Maqqari quotes, describes an army breaking into the outer
defences of Zamora and then being caught between them: forty thousand dead, some
say fifty, many of them drowned in the water-filled moats between the walls. He
calls it one of the most signal defeats ever inflicted on the Muslims of
al-Andalus. The figures are his, and figures of that kind in this period are
never independent of the telling; what is not in doubt is what the caliph did
afterwards.

He never led an army in person again. He went on making war on the Christian
north for another twenty years, every year, by ordering his frontier governors to
do it.

The Christian kingdoms sent embassies to Cordoba to seek his friendship anyway,
which tells you the defeat had not changed the balance. But the caliph had drawn
a conclusion about himself, and the state he left to his son was one that had
found its edges.
