---
title: The one who got away
drafted_from: kennedy-1996
phase: formation
---

When the Abbasids hunted down the Umayyad house in 750, one grandson of the
caliph Hisham escaped. Abd al-Rahman crossed North Africa with a price on his
head, reached al-Andalus in 755, exploited the feud between the Arab factions
governing it, and took Cordoba in 756.

He did not claim the caliphate. For 173 years his descendants ruled as *amirs*,
governing a province they had detached from the caliphate without ever formally
denying that a caliphate existed — the same posture, in reverse, that the
Samanids and Tahirids would adopt in the east. What they had that the eastern
governors did not was legitimacy of their own: they were the Umayyad house, and
the men in Baghdad were the ones who had taken it.

Abd al-Rahman III ended the ambiguity in 929, taking the titles caliph and
*amir al-mu'minin*. The immediate provocation was the Fatimids, whose new
caliphate across the strait was recruiting Berber clients in the Maghreb and
whose claim, if unanswered, would have left al-Andalus a province of somebody's
empire. By 929 there were three caliphs in the Islamic world.

It is also, precisely, a claim on the Umayyads of Damascus. Not a new caliphate
but the old one, resumed a hundred and eighty-four years after it fell and four
thousand kilometres from where it fell, by the descendants of the one man who
got out alive.
