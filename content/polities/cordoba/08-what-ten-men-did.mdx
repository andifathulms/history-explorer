The historian Ibn al-Raqiq set down the arithmetic of the day Cordoba changed
hands, and it is the arithmetic that shocked him rather than the violence.

Between noon on Tuesday the fifteenth of Jumada II in the year 399 and the
following Wednesday — one day — Cordoba was taken, Madinat al-Zahra was
destroyed, one caliph was deposed and another appointed in his place, the power
of the house of al-Mansur was overthrown and its vizier killed, citizen
militias were raised, and the whole succession of viziers was swept away and
replaced.

And then the sentence he thought most remarkable of all: that it was
accomplished by ten men, who were charcoal-sellers, butchers and dung-carriers
serving in the new caliph's army.

## The man they raised

The new caliph was al-Mahdi, and the way he had survived to be available is the
best joke in the story.

When al-Mansur had taken the state over and killed or exiled every Umayyad who
stood between him and it, he had left this one alone — on the grounds that he
was feeble-minded and of no consideration among the people, and therefore not
worth the trouble. That man destroyed everything al-Mansur built, and inherited
his wealth and his power.

He was, on the account preserved here, dissolute, cruel, vindictive and without
administrative talent, and a tolerable poet. There is a verse of his about a
sprig of myrtle handed to him by a page while he was drinking with guests. It
compares the boy's walk to a lion's and the myrtle's fragrance to the boy's,
and it is a perfectly pleasant piece of work, and it was composed by the man in
whose name Cordoba was being burnt.
