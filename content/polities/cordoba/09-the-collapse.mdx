Cordoba in the tenth century was probably the largest city in western Europe,
with a library the sources credit with hundreds of thousands of volumes, a
translation and scholarly culture that later fed Latin Europe, and a caliph
whose recognition was sought from Byzantium and the Holy Roman Empire.

<Figure id="aljaferia" />

It came apart in about thirty years, by a mechanism that was not unique to
Cordoba. A chamberlain ruling through a caliph too young or too secluded to
rule himself is the same arrangement as the Buyid amirs in Baghdad and the
Fatimid viziers in Cairo — arrived at independently, three times over,
because a palace and an army in different hands has only so many stable
configurations.

There is no agent to name for the ending. No foreign army, no rival dynasty, no
conquest. A caliphate that had recently been the strongest state in the western
Mediterranean was dismantled by the mechanism it had used to make itself
strong, which is the same story as the Abbasids at Samarra a century and a half
earlier.
