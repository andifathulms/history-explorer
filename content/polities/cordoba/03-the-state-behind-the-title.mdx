---
title: The state behind the title
drafted_from: kennedy-1996
phase: peak

Abd al-Rahman III took the caliphal title in 929, after two decades spent
retaking a country the Ibn Hafsun revolt and the Arab factional lords had
fragmented — the title following the state rather than announcing it, which
is why it stuck. What he built behind that title is the thing worth examining
in detail.

It was a salaried machine. The army was deliberately not an Arab tribal levy:
it was recruited from *saqaliba*, slaves of European origin, and from Berbers
brought over from the Maghreb, and it was paid from the treasury. Troops with
no local kin had no local faction, which is precisely why a caliph who had
just spent twenty years fighting local factions wanted them. The same
reasoning produced the same instrument in Baghdad, Cairo and Ghazna, and the
reader who has been through those pages already knows what it costs.

From 936 he built Madinat al-Zahra, a palace-city outside Cordoba, and moved
the government into it. The caliphate was now a place as well as a claim.

The Maghreb policy the first chapter gave as the provocation for the title
turned into a thirty-year proxy war once he had it. Ceuta was taken in 931 and
Melilla before it, and both caliphates then fought through Berber clients
rather than armies, buying the same tribes away from each other. The Umayyads
were paying for troops in Cordoba and for allegiances in the Maghreb out of the
same treasury, and the second bill is the one the sources are quietest about.
