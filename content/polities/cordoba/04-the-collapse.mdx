title: Thirty years, and then taifas

Cordoba in the tenth century was probably the largest city in western Europe,
with a library the sources credit with hundreds of thousands of volumes, a
translation and scholarly culture that later fed Latin Europe, and a caliph whose
recognition was sought from Byzantium and the Holy Roman Empire.

It came apart in about thirty years. Al-Mansur, chamberlain to a caliph who
succeeded as a child, took effective power in 976 and ruled through him for a
quarter of a century — the same arrangement as the Buyids in Baghdad and the
Fatimid viziers in Cairo, arrived at independently for the third time in this
corpus. He held it together by continuous campaigning against the Christian
north and by recruiting Berber troops from the Maghreb who had no local ties.

After his sons, the arrangement collapsed into a civil war between the Berber
regiments, the Cordoban population and rival Umayyad claimants. Cordoba was
sacked by its own troops in 1013. The caliphate was formally abolished in 1031
and al-Andalus divided into the *taifa* kingdoms — Seville, Zaragoza, Toledo,
Granada and a couple of dozen others.

This site records the ending as `fragmentation` with no agent, and there is
none: no foreign army, no rival dynasty, no conquest. A caliphate that had
recently been the strongest state in the western Mediterranean was dismantled by
the mechanism it had used to make itself strong, which is the same story as the
Abbasids at Samarra a century and a half earlier.
