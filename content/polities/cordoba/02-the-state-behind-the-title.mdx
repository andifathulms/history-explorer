---
title: The state behind the title
drafted_from: kennedy-1996
phase: peak
---

Abd al-Rahman III took the caliphal title in 929, seventeen years after
inheriting an amirate that barely existed. In 912 Umayyad authority ran to
little more than Cordoba and its hinterland: the long revolt of Umar ibn Hafsun
held the southern uplands, and the Arab factional lords held most of the rest.
He spent roughly two decades taking al-Andalus back, town by town, and only then
called himself caliph. The title followed the state rather than announcing it,
which is the opposite of the usual order and the reason it stuck.

What he built behind it was a salaried machine. The army was deliberately not
an Arab tribal levy: it was recruited from *saqaliba*, slaves of European
origin, and from Berbers brought over from the Maghreb, and it was paid from the
treasury. Troops with no local kin had no local faction, which is precisely why
a caliph who had just spent twenty years fighting local factions wanted them.
The same reasoning produced the same instrument in Baghdad, Cairo and Ghazna,
and the reader who has been through those pages already knows what it costs.

From 936 he built Madinat al-Zahra, a palace-city outside Cordoba, and moved the
government into it. The caliphate was now a place as well as a claim.

The Maghreb policy the first chapter gave as the provocation for the title
turned into a thirty-year proxy war once he had it. Ceuta was taken in 931 and
Melilla before it, and both caliphates then fought through Berber clients rather
than armies, buying the same tribes away from each other. The Umayyads were
paying for troops in Cordoba and for allegiances in the Maghreb out of the same
treasury, and the second bill is the one the sources are quietest about.

He also lost. Ramiro II of Leon broke an Umayyad army at Simancas in 939, and
Abd al-Rahman III never took the field in person again. The strongest state in
the western Mediterranean was not the strongest army on the peninsula, and the
sources this site reads do not pretend otherwise.
