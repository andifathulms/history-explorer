title: What the jurists were asked

Al-Andalus is usually introduced through *convivencia*, and this chapter is not
about that. The interesting evidence is not that three communities coexisted —
they did, as they did across the whole Islamic world on the same legal terms —
but that Andalusi jurists spent two centuries answering questions about where
one community ended and the next began, which is not a question that arises
where the boundary is obvious.

Safran's material is the jurists' own: legal opinions, court practice and
handbooks from the ninth and tenth centuries, read for the problems they were
actually brought. They are strikingly practical. What is the status of a child
whose parents are of different faiths. Whether a Muslim may buy meat butchered
by a Christian, share a bathhouse, attend a funeral, accept a gift at a
festival. What happens when a convert's family does not convert. What to do
about a Christian who converts, is married, and now has a marriage the law
reads differently. Whether the son of a convert may inherit from a non-convert
grandfather.

Those questions arise because the boundary was dissolving in exactly the ways
that make boundaries hard to police: conversion running one way in large
numbers over generations, intermarriage, and Arabic becoming the language
everybody used — including Christians, whose own bishops complained that young
men read Arabic poetry and could not construe Latin. A ninth-century Cordoban
current went further and sought martyrdom by public blasphemy, which the
authorities were markedly reluctant to grant and which the Christian
community's own leadership was divided about; the episode is legible as a
protest against assimilation rather than against persecution.

Two things follow from it. It supplies the texture that a political history of
amirs and campaigns cannot: the state in these chapters was administering a
population that was becoming Muslim and Arabic-speaking underneath it, and the
caliphal claim of 929 was made over a society in the middle of that change
rather than after it.

And it comes at the problem from a direction the political narrative cannot. A
history of amirs and campaigns is written from chronicles; this one is written
from law, from cases about what a boundary between communities actually was
when somebody had to rule on it.
