Seyyid Hamid bin Thuwaini died suddenly on August 25, 1896, and within
the hour Khaled bin Barghash - who had already tried and failed to
seize the throne once before, in 1893 - marched fifty armed followers
into the palace square and broke down the doors. General Sir Lloyd
Mathews, who had known Khaled since boyhood, tried to talk him down as
he had three years earlier; this time Khaled would not be talked to
at all, and within minutes a mob of five hundred Arabs held the
palace behind him. Mathews's own impulse was to shoot Khaled on the
spot - he later reflected it might have averted everything that
followed - but he judged himself not justified in taking so extreme a
step alone, and withdrew.

Khaled spent the next two days fortifying the palace with roughly two
thousand armed Persians, Comorians and slaves, seven Hotchkiss and
Krupp guns, two Maxims that had been gifts from Queen Victoria
herself, and the sultanate's one remaining warship, the Glasgow. He
ordered a salute fired to announce his own accession and demanded the
foreign consuls recognise him; they kept their flags at half-mast and
waited for Britain to act first. By the second night, residents later
agreed, Zanzibar Town had never known a stiller one.

## Nine o'clock

At seven on the morning of August 27, Rear-Admiral Rawson sent Khaled
an ultimatum: haul down his flag, disarm his followers, and surrender
himself at the Custom House by nine o'clock, or the ships in the
harbour would open fire. Khaled's envoy asked for a parley and was
refused. At five minutes to nine the Admiral signalled his three
bombarding ships to prepare for action; the Palace clock struck the
hour, and the Thrush, Racoon and Sparrow opened fire on the palace
guns.

Half an hour later the palace and its clock tower were in flames, the
middle palace a ruin, five hundred of Khaled's own men dead or
wounded in and around the square, and the Glasgow - which had opened
fire of its own accord and been sunk by a six-inch shell from the
flagship St. George - at the bottom of the harbour. Thirty-seven
minutes after the first shot, the red flag came down from the palace
staff and the cease-fire sounded. Khaled himself had already fled
through the town to the German consulate, from which he was carried
aboard a German warship to exile at Dar-es-Salaam. Within hours Seyyid
Hamoud bin Mohammed was conducted to the Custom House and proclaimed
Sultan amid the salutes of the same ships that had just destroyed his
predecessor's palace.

Lyne's own history, written less than a decade later by a man who had
served the government it describes, draws the plainest conclusion
available: for a century Britain had "imposed her treaties upon
[Zanzibar's] rulers without having to fire a shot," and the
bombardment was less a conquest than confirmation of an authority
Zanzibar's sultans had, in every practical sense, already stopped
being able to refuse.
