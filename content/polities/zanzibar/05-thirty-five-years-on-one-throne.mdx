The bombardment did not end the sultanate; it settled, by force, an
arrangement Britain had already been building by treaty for years.
France and Germany had waived their own claims to the islands in
1890, in exchange for Madagascar and Heligoland, and on the 4th of
November that year Zanzibar and Pemba were formally proclaimed a
British Protectorate. A constitutional government followed within
the year, with a British Representative sitting as First Minister
beside the sultan. Seyyid Hamoud bin Mohamed, installed within hours
of Khaled's flight, proved an eager partner in this new order rather
than a resentful one: intensely English in his own sympathies, he
completed the abolition of slavery by decree the year after his
accession, and reigned in comparative calm until his death in 1902.

## Three sultans in fifteen years, then one for decades more

The throne that had briefly changed hands within a single hour in
1896 kept changing hands quickly for a decade and a half afterward.
Hamoud's son Ali, educated at Harrow, succeeded him as a minor with a
British-appointed Regent managing the state until his majority in
1905; six years later, en route to the coronation of King George V,
Ali left his ship at Naples, travelled on to Paris, and abdicated from
exile rather than return to Zanzibar at all. The throne was first
offered to Seyyid Khaled bin Mohamed, Hamoud's brother, who declined
it on grounds of failing health; it was then offered by the British
Government to Khalifa bin Harub, a nephew of the dynasty who had
himself witnessed the 1896 bombardment as a young man newly arrived in
Zanzibar. Khalifa ascended the throne on the 9th of December, 1911.

He did not leave it again for decades. Administrative control passed
from the Foreign Office to the Colonial Office in 1913, an Executive
and a Legislative Council replaced the old advisory Protectorate
Council in 1926, and through every one of these changes Khalifa
remained the sultan they were made around - his own son and heir
apparent eventually sitting beside him on the Executive Council he
presided over. In December 1936 Zanzibar marked the twenty-fifth
anniversary of his accession; in November 1944, the two-hundredth
anniversary of the Al-Busaid dynasty's own founding was commemorated
under the same sultan's reign. The throne that three men had held
between them for fifteen turbulent years after the bombardment had,
by then, been occupied by one man for longer than any of his
predecessors combined.
