Sayyid Said died at sea off the Seychelles in 1856, sixty-five years
old, leaving behind an empire that had never been one state so much
as one man's personal rule over two: Muscat, poor and mountainous, and
Zanzibar, rich on cloves and the slave trade and governed, in Said's
last years, by his son Majid. No will settled who would inherit which
half, or whether the two should even stay divided. Majid's elder
brother Thuwaini, already ruling Muscat as their father's governor,
expected to inherit the whole - or failing that, an annual tribute
substantial enough to prove Zanzibar still answered to him.

Majid paid the first year's forty thousand crowns and then stopped,
pleading an empty treasury even as his own revenue ran past two
hundred thousand a year. Thuwaini, convinced the debt was less the
issue than the principle, prepared in 1859 to send an expedition to
Zanzibar to collect it by force. Neither brother had the fleet or the
appetite to fight the matter out to a finish, and both, in the end,
turned to the one power both trusted more than they trusted each
other: Great Britain, whose Political Agent at Zanzibar, Colonel
Hamerton, had already spent years managing exactly this kind of
family quarrel.

## An arbitration neither brother could refuse

The case went to Lord Canning, Governor-General of India, whose
commission examined Arab custom on inherited sovereignty and found
that succession in Oman's history had never been settled by
primogeniture alone, but by the "concurrence of the principal
tribes" - and that the people of Zanzibar had already, in effect,
elected Majid their ruler by accepting his government for years
without dispute. Canning's Award, dated April 2, 1861, declared Majid
sovereign of Zanzibar and its African dominions in his own right,
bound only to an annual subsidy to Muscat rather than to any claim of
subjection. Thuwaini accepted it on May 15; Majid, on June 25. For the
first time since Said had built it, the empire existed as two
separate states rather than one man's unequal inheritance waiting to
be fought over - and Zanzibar, the richer half, now answered to no one
outside its own island but the British whose arbitration had made it
sovereign.
