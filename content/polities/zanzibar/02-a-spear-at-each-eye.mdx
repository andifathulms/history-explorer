Barghash inherited Zanzibar in 1870 already the most consequential
sultan his line would produce, and the demand that defined his reign
arrived within his first three years on the throne. In January 1873
Sir Bartle Frere reached Zanzibar as Queen Victoria's special envoy,
carrying a treaty that would prohibit the export of slaves from the
coast entirely - even between two ports of Barghash's own dominions,
a trade the sultanate's economy had never operated without.

The timing could not have been worse for Barghash to hear it. A
hurricane had flattened plantations and houses across the island the
previous August; cholera had already reduced Arab families across the
coast to poverty. "It will require years to recover the losses," he
told Frere's staff, "and now the prospect of being obliged to give up
the supply of slave-labour will absolutely ruin us." When Dr. Badger,
Frere's interpreter, pressed him on the hardship inflicted on the
slaves themselves, Barghash did not deny it - he simply insisted his
own people's survival came first, and refused to sign.

## Blockade, not persuasion

Frere's mission left without a treaty, but Britain had never actually
needed Barghash's consent to act - only his acknowledgment that it
would act regardless. Dr. John Kirk, the British Agent who had stayed
behind after Frere's departure, spent months working on the fears of
the Arab chiefs whose own support Barghash could not rule without,
describing a blockade in terms alarming enough - no mail, no fresh
meat from the mainland, no safe passage even for the Sultan himself -
that the chiefs began urging Barghash toward the treaty their own
resistance had helped him refuse in the first place. "A spear is held
at each of my eyes," Barghash told his advisers, "with which shall I
choose to be pierced?"

He signed on June 5, 1873: the total cessation of the slave export
trade, the closing of every public slave market in his dominions, and
British protection for slaves freed under the treaty's terms. "Now my
head is safe on my shoulders," Barghash told Kirk when it was done;
"it is your head that is in danger." He was not entirely wrong.
Zanzibar's economy had been built for a century on the trade the
treaty ended, and the sultanate would spend the rest of Barghash's
reign discovering exactly how much of its own power over its own
mainland had depended on Britain's continued goodwill rather than on
anything it still controlled itself.
