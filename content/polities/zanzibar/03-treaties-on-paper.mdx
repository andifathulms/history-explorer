Barghash's own offers of British protection over Zanzibar's mainland
possessions had gone unanswered for years - Britain, committed since
1862 to respecting Zanzibar's independence alongside France, had no
wish for burdensome new territory. Germany had no such hesitation. In
1884 a "Society for German Colonization" sent Dr. Karl Peters into
the interior to collect signatures from local chiefs on documents
most of them cannot have understood: one Bondei headman, asked
outright, declared he was "neither a subject of the Sultan Seyyid
Barghash nor of any person" and that the Sultan "has no possessions
or fortresses" anywhere near his village - which did not stop the
same document from surrendering, on his behalf, "all the rights
which according to European ideas are comprised in the sovereign
rights of a Prince."

The German Emperor accepted these treaties as grounds for a Charter
of Protection over Usagara, Nguru, Uzeguha and Ukami on February 17,
1885. Barghash, who received the proclamation from the German
Consul-General on April 27, protested by telegraph and had to be
talked out of travelling to Berlin in person. He sent General Mathews
to secure declarations of loyalty from the chiefs around Kilimanjaro
before the Germans could reach them - and succeeded, on paper, only
to have a German agent named Jühlke arrive days later and undo it,
also on paper. As one Chaga chief who had signed for both sides put
it to Mathews: "bring with thee a better flagstaff."

## A fleet, not a negotiation

Paper contests were not what settled the matter. On August 7, 1885,
Commodore Paschen's German fleet entered Zanzibar harbour, and on the
11th the Commodore demanded Barghash recognise the Kaiser's
protectorate over Usagara, Uzeguha, Nguru, Ukami and Witu outright,
and withdraw the garrisons he had stationed there. Barghash had no
fleet capable of answering Paschen's, and no ally willing to
intervene on his behalf; his formal submission went to the Commodore
on August 14, a week after the ships had arrived.

What followed over the next two years was less a negotiation than a
formal accounting of what Zanzibar had already lost. A joint
British-German-French delimitation commission spent 1885 and 1886
determining what coastline the sultanate still controlled, with the
German commissioner refusing to recognise any stretch the others
could not prove beyond dispute. The Anglo-German Agreement of October
and November 1886 confirmed Zanzibar's sovereignty over the islands
and a coastal strip ten miles wide - and assigned everything behind
that strip, from the Rovuma river to the Tana, to German and British
spheres the sultanate would never again administer. Barghash accepted
the arrangement on December 4, 1886. The mainland empire his own
diplomacy, treaties and garrisons had spent two decades assembling
now existed almost entirely on paper of a different kind: European
paper, drawn up in London and Berlin, that no Zanzibar delegation had
been present to negotiate.
