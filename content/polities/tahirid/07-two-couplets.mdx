The Tahirids governed Khurasan for half a century and policed Baghdad at the
same time, and what survives of them in the language of the country they
governed is four lines of verse by one man.

## Hanzala of Badghis

When Muhammad Awfi compiled the first anthology of Persian poets in the
thirteenth century, he could find exactly one belonging to the Tahirid period,
and he quotes two couplets of him. Browne, going through the whole early
material a century ago, could not improve on that.

Hanzala of Badghis appears to have lived more or less under Tahirid patronage.
Here is a quarter of everything that is left of him, in Browne's rendering:

> Though rue-seed in the fire my sweetheart threw
> Lest hurt should from the Evil Eye accrue,
> I fear nor fire nor rue can aught avail
> That face like fire and beauty-spot like rue!

Wild rue is burned as a fumigation against the evil eye, which is held to fall
particularly on whatever is perfect of its kind. The poet's point is that his
sweetheart is too perfect for the remedy to work, and that her bright face and
dark beauty-spot are themselves the fire and the seed.

It is a good joke, well made, in a language that had not been written for two
centuries. And it is the whole of the Tahirid contribution to it.

## Why there is so little

The comparison that makes the point is not with some later golden age. It is
with the dynasty that had the same province eighty years afterwards.

Awfi's chapter on the early poets lists three names from the Tahirid and
Saffarid periods together. The remaining twenty-eight are Samanid. Something
changed at Bukhara that had not changed at Nishapur, and it was not the
availability of poets.

Browne's judgement is that the Tahirids, unlike the Saffarids and Samanids after
them, cannot really be said to have sought to foster the Persian revival at all
— and he cites, without vouching for it, the story Dawlatshah tells: a man
brought Abdallah b. Tahir at Nishapur an old Persian book, the romance of Wamiq
and Adhra, said to have been compiled by wise men for King Anushirvan. The amir
answered that they were men who read the Qur'an, that the book was the work of
Magians and accursed in their eyes, had it thrown into the water, and gave
orders that any Persian book of Magian authorship found in his territories
should be destroyed.

Browne will not attach much historical weight to the anecdote, and neither
should anyone else. What he thinks it does represent, fairly, is the attitude.

## The shape of the loss

So the record stands like this. A Persian family, ruling a Persian province,
loyal to an Arab caliphate, whose court language was Arabic and whose surviving
Persian is two couplets about a girl and a superstition.

There is no need to make them villains for it. Being a good governor of Khurasan
in the ninth century meant being intelligible in Baghdad, and the Tahirids were
better at that than anybody. But the thing the Iranian Intermezzo is remembered
for did not begin with them, and Hanzala's four lines are the measure of how
nearly it did not begin at all.
