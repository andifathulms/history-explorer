In 873 Ya'qub ibn al-Layth, a coppersmith's apprentice turned warlord out of
Sistan, marched on Nishapur and took it. Muhammad ibn Tahir, the last Tahirid
governor, was captured. There was no significant battle.

Ya'qub had no appointment. He had asked for one, and Baghdad had refused, and
he had taken the province anyway. Where the Tahirids had spent fifty years
demonstrating that formal subordination could contain real independence, Ya'qub
demonstrated in a season that it could also be dispensed with.

The Tahirid collapse is what opens the space this whole thread runs through.
Khurasan was, from 873, a province with no settled claimant: the Saffarids held
it by force without a title, the Samanids would take it from them and receive a
title afterwards, and for the next three hundred years it would keep changing
hands between houses that had each acquired it by some variation on the same
move.

The family did not vanish. Tahirids held the governorship of Baghdad itself for
some years after Nishapur fell, which is a strange coda: the dynasty outlived
its own state as officials of the power it had spent fifty years quietly
outgrowing.
