---
title: The march from Merv
drafted_from: ibn-khallikan
phase: expansion
---

The man al-Ma'mun sent west in 811 was not a Khurasani grandee. Tahir ibn
al-Husayn belonged to the tribe of Khuzaa by adoption rather than by blood: his
great-grandfather Ruzayq had been a freed slave of a celebrated Khuzai patron,
and the family rose as secretaries and small governors in the east. Tahir was
born at Bushanj, and years later, at the height of everything, someone in
Baghdad congratulated him on a rank no rival in Khurasan had ever reached. He
answered that he could not enjoy it, because he could not see the old women of
Bushanj climbing onto their roofs to watch him ride past.

Al-Amin sent Ali ibn Isa ibn Mahan out from Baghdad to stop him, and the two
armies met at Rayy. Ali was killed. Ibn Khallikan spends a full page trying to
fix the day and cannot: one authority puts the death on the seventh of Shaban,
al-Tabari gives the same date for Ali's *departure* from Baghdad and says the
news of the death reached the city on the fifteenth of Shawwal, and the
likeliest reconstruction has a copyist writing one month for another. What
survives the argument intact is the ride. Tahir's messenger covered the two
hundred and fifty parasangs from Rayy to Merv between Friday evening and Sunday
morning, and al-Ma'mun learned in two nights that his brother's army no longer
existed.

After Rayy the campaign stopped being a battle and became an absorption. Tahir
moved on Baghdad taking possession of every place he passed through, and then
sat down in front of the capital. The siege was long enough that he ran out of
money and had to write east for more; the banker al-Ma'mun directed him to
refused the loan, and when the city fell Tahir had the man brought before him
and promised him the worst of deaths, then let him go because he recited a good
poem about a falcon sparing a sparrow. He was one-eyed, and a court poet got
away with the line *O thou ambidexter, thou hast an eye too little and a hand
too much.* Another he compelled to satirise him, read the result, warned the
poet never to say it again, and tore up the paper.

Al-Amin was killed in Safar 198 — October 813. Tahir had written asking leave to
do as he liked with his prisoner, and al-Ma'mun sent back a shirt with no
opening for the head. The head went east to Khurasan, and its owner's brother
was proclaimed caliph.

Then came the check that shaped everything after. Towards the end of 814
al-Ma'mun wrote from Merv instructing Tahir to hand over every country he had
subdued — the two Iraqs, Fars, al-Ahwaz, the Hijaz and Yemen — to al-Hasan ibn
Sahl, and to take himself off to Raqqa and govern Mosul, the Jazira, Syria and
the west. The soldier who had won the empire was moved sideways out of it. The
government of Khurasan, when it came seven years later, was what was left.
