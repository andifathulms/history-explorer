The Lithuanian tribes that Ryngold and his son Mindaugas set out to
unify in the early thirteenth century were pagan, scattered along the
Neman and Neris rivers, and squeezed between two forces that would
otherwise have absorbed them piecemeal: the Teutonic and Livonian
military orders pressing in from the Baltic coast, and the fragmented
principalities of Rus' to the east and south.

## A father's consolidation, a son's crisis

Ryngold moved the seat of his growing authority from Kernavė to
Novogrudok in the heart of Black Ruthenia and brought most of the
scattered Lithuanian settlements under one rule; his son Mindaugas
continued the work, both by further consolidating Lithuanian territory
and by drawing Rus' lands into it through the same campaigns.
Consolidation bred resistance. By the late 1240s a coalition had formed
against Mindaugas that included his own banished nephew, the prince of
Galicia, the Yotvingians, the Samogitians, and - most dangerously - the
Livonian Order itself.

## Baptism as a weapon

Mindaugas answered the coalition with diplomacy rather than only with
arms. He offered to accept Christian baptism, an offer the Livonian
Order's own local commander seized on as a chance to present his
crusading order as bringing a pagan ruler to the faith rather than
merely conquering him. Baptised in 1251, Mindaugas was crowned king two
years later on the authority of a papal bull - the only time in
Lithuanian history the country's ruler would hold that title rather
than the more usual grand duke.

## A conversion abandoned as readily as it was made

The peace bought Mindaugas time to eliminate the rest of the coalition
through alliance rather than war, trading control of Black Ruthenia to
the prince of Galicia in exchange for his support. But his
accommodation of the Teutonic Order - including a 1259 grant of
Samogitia meant to buy its goodwill - triggered exactly the popular
uprising it was meant to prevent. When the Samogitians and Yotvingians
rose and broke a combined Teutonic-Livonian force at Durbe in 1260,
Mindaugas abandoned Christianity as quickly as he had adopted it and
placed himself at the head of the revolt he had, in effect, provoked.
Three years later his own domestic rivals, not the crusading orders,
killed him along with two of his sons - and Lithuania spent the rest of
the thirteenth century as the one pagan state in Europe still capable of
defending itself.
