By the 1560s, more than a century of shared kings had not produced
agreement on what a union of Poland and Lithuania actually meant. When
the Sejm convened at Lublin in 1569 to settle the question once and for
all, the two delegations arrived with fundamentally incompatible plans.

## Two proposals, one dominant partner

The Polish plan, drafted with Poland's own advantage in mind, called for
a single common monarch elected at a Warsaw assembly and crowned only at
Krakow, a single Sejm resolving every matter without exception, and a
shared currency and customs border - terms that would have left Lithuania
answerable to Polish institutions in everything. The Lithuanian delegation
countered with a plan preserving separate coronations at Krakow and
Vilnius, a joint Sejm limited to genuinely shared business while separate
Lithuanian and Polish assemblies handled everything else, Lithuania's own
currency, and continued Lithuanian control over Podolia, Volhynia and
Livonia. Poland rejected every one of these terms outright.

## A walkout that backfired completely

Rather than compromise, the Lithuanian delegates left Lublin in protest
at the end of February, hoping the gesture would pressure King Sigismund
II Augustus into softening the Polish position. It had the opposite
effect: with no Lithuanian delegation left to object, the Poles who
remained drafted the union entirely on their own terms and had the king
formally detach Volhynia and Podolia from Lithuania and reattach them to
Poland, backed by threats of confiscation against any Lithuanian
Rus' noble who refused to swear the required oath.

## The magnates who made resistance pointless

Two of Lithuanian Rus''s most powerful figures, the voivodes of Kyiv
and Volhynia, accepted the reannexation and swore the oaths demanded of
them; the rest of the region's upper nobility followed their lead, and
the lesser nobility, already drawn toward Polish szlachta privileges,
welcomed the change outright. With Podlachia, Volhynia, Podolia and Kyiv
already gone and its own leading families reconciled to the outcome, the
Lithuanian delegation that returned to Lublin in June had no remaining
leverage.

## What was surrendered, and what was refused to the end

On 27 June the Lithuanians accepted nearly every Polish term, holding
out only on a handful of points that touched Lithuanian dignity rather
than substance: they asked that a clause branding their earlier walkout
as rebellion be struck, and that the Grand Duchy's own state seal
continue to be used alongside Poland's. Even these were refused. On 1
July 1569 the assembled delegates and senators swore to the union as
Poland had written it, creating the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth as a
single elective monarchy under one crown. The Grand Duchy that Ryngold
and Mindaugas had built out of scattered pagan tribes, that Gediminas
had extended through marriage and Algirdas through conquest, and that
Vytautas had brought to its widest reach without ever being crowned its
king, ended its separate existence not on a battlefield but in a
negotiating hall its own delegates had briefly, and fatally, walked out
of.
