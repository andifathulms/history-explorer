Gediminas, who came to rule Lithuania in 1316 as the first of a dynasty
that would hold the office for the rest of the Grand Duchy's existence,
expanded Lithuanian territory mostly without armies. Marriage did the
work instead.

## Princes who needed a stronger patron

The fall of Kyiv and other Rus' cities to earlier Mongol devastation had
left a scatter of weakened principalities - Smolensk, Turov, Pinsk among
them - unable to stand alone. Their princes accepted Gediminas's
authority not as conquered subjects but as clients seeking protection
from Tatar overlordship, and many welcomed Lithuanian rule as a
deliverance rather than a subjugation. Gediminas reinforced these
arrangements through marriage alliances that reached as far as the
Polish crown itself, turning a former enemy into a partner against their
shared adversary, the Teutonic Order - an alliance that produced a joint
victory over the Order at Płowce in 1331.

## A rivalry with Moscow that Lithuania lost

Not every expansion succeeded. When Gediminas moved to add Pskov and
Novgorod to his own sphere, he ran directly into Ivan Kalita, the prince
of Moscow, who was building exactly the same kind of tribute-based
authority over the Rus' lands from the opposite direction. Lithuania and
Moscow emerged as rival centres of the same project - gathering the
scattered Rus' principalities under one authority - and where they
clashed directly, over both Pskov and Novgorod, Kalita's Moscow came out
ahead.

## A government reshaped by what it had absorbed

By Gediminas's reign, Rus' territory already made up roughly two-thirds
of the Grand Duchy's lands, and its influence transformed Lithuania's own
government and army. Rus'ian regiments under Rus'ian commanders became
central to a military that had once relied on marshes and quick raids
and now fielded organised units capable of sustained sieges. Gediminas
founded Trakai in 1320 and, in 1323, Vilnius itself on the Neris - a
capital built, unlike the older seat at Kernavė, specifically to govern
a state that had grown far larger than the Lithuanian tribal lands it
started from.

## Sons who divided the labour

Gediminas left seven sons, and two of them, Algirdas and Kęstutis,
divided his work between them rather than fighting over it. Kęstutis
held the western frontier against the Teutonic and Livonian Orders, prized
across Europe for a chivalry his own enemies respected. Algirdas pushed
south and east, annexing Chernigov in 1356 and drawing Kyiv itself,
already leaning on Lithuanian support since Gediminas's own reign, fully
into his authority. In 1362, at the Battle of Blue Waters, Algirdas's
forces broke three Tatar commanders and freed Podolia and the steppe down
to the Black Sea from the Golden Horde's control - extending Lithuania,
within two generations of Gediminas's death, from a Baltic duchy into a
power bordering the Black Sea itself.
