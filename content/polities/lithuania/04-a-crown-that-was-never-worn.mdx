Vytautas died without the royal crown he had spent his last years
chasing, and the six decades that followed brought Lithuania a civil war
over the succession, a durable but subordinating peace with Poland, and
the first serious losses of territory to a Moscow the grand duchy could
no longer treat as a lesser neighbour.

## A duke who tried to walk away from Poland

The Lithuanian-Rus' nobility, still smarting at the union's terms,
pressed Vytautas's cousin Švitrigaila onto the throne and got exactly
the ruler they wanted: within months of his accession he declared his
intent to separate Lithuania from Poland outright. War followed, then an
inconclusive truce, then a Polish-organised uprising that drove
Švitrigaila from Vilnius before he could even attend the peace congress
meant to settle the dispute for good. His replacement, Vytautas's own
brother Sigismund Kęstutaitis, immediately signed the 1432 Union of
Grodno, renewing Lithuania's subordination to the Polish crown and
promising that after his own death the grand duchy itself would pass
to the Polish king's line. Švitrigaila did not accept the loss quietly:
he fought on from his refuge in Lithuanian Rus' for years, and was only
broken for good at the Battle of Wiłkomierz. Sigismund did not long
survive his own victory - a faction of the Lithuanian nobility,
resentful of his rule, assassinated him in 1440.

## A fourteen-year-old, and terms the nobility set themselves

The nobles who might have restored Švitrigaila found him delayed too
long on his own return journey, and settled instead on Casimir, the
fourteen-year-old brother of the new Polish king, sent to Vilnius
initially only as a deputy governor. The Lithuanian nobility saw the
opening this created and took it: rather than accept Casimir as a
subordinate administrator, they offered him the grand ducal crown
outright, on the condition that he swear never to surrender Volhynia,
Podolia or Kyiv to Poland. Casimir accepted, and in 1440 the crown once
associated with Gediminas's own line passed to a teenager who owed his
throne to Lithuanian, not Polish, initiative.

Casimir's early reign put down a Smolensk rebellion and a renewed
Samogitian separatist movement, and when his own brother Władysław III
was killed at Varna in 1444, the Poles - eager to restore the personal
union rather than risk electing a rival to their own throne - offered
Casimir the Polish crown as well. Lithuanian nobles bitterly opposed any
new union, but Polish pressure, and the threat of electing a rival
candidate instead, won out: Casimir was crowned king of Poland in 1447,
without ever formally reconciling the competing claims the two
nobilities pressed on him for the rest of his reign.

## The first ground lost to Moscow

What Casimir's near half-century on the throne could not prevent was
the rise of a Grand Principality of Moscow no longer content with its
own borders. Moscow's absorption of Novgorod gave it a frontier
directly against Lithuania's own, and its rulers spent the following
decades courting the Orthodox princes of Lithuanian Rus' away from a
grand duchy whose Catholic nobility had begun pressing Catholic
missionary campaigns into Orthodox territory. Casimir answered by
granting Orthodox nobles the same privileges Catholics already held -
his 1447 Privilege - but by the 1480s Crimean Tatars allied with Moscow
were raiding the grand duchy's southern frontier, and in 1482 the
Crimean khan sacked Kyiv itself. By the time of Casimir's death in 1492,
Moscow's own forces had already crossed the border and burned Lithuanian
towns directly, a pattern of loss his son and successor, Alexander,
would inherit rather than reverse.
