Alexander Jagiellon and his brother Sigismund I held the grand ducal
throne between them for over half a century, and neither could do more
than slow a Muscovite advance that, by the sources' own reckoning, cost
Lithuania more territory under Alexander alone than in any other reign
in its history.

## A marriage that bought no peace

The Lithuanian-Rus' nobility, wary of a restored personal union with
Poland, elected Casimir's son Alexander grand duke on his own in 1492,
independent of the Polish crown he might otherwise have shared. It made
little difference to Moscow. Ivan III and his Crimean Tatar allies
seized several Lithuanian border towns within a year of Alexander's
accession, and when Lithuanian officials arranged Alexander's marriage
to Ivan's own daughter Elena in the hope that family ties would restrain
him, the marriage restrained nothing: Ivan's armies broke Lithuania's
forces at the Battle of Vedrosha and annexed Smolensk and Chernigov
territory regardless. Alexander, concluding that only a revived union
with Poland could give Lithuania the military weight to hold its ground,
pursued and won the Polish crown himself in 1501, confirming the Union
of Mielnik that bound the two thrones together under one ruler chosen by
a joint assembly. The union bought him a peace treaty with Moscow in
1503, but only at the price of ceding Chernigov, Starodub, Bryansk and a
dozen other towns outright - Lithuania kept Smolensk and Kyiv, and lost
more territory under Alexander's single reign than in any other period
the sources record.

## Sigismund I, a Statute, and a war fought to a draw

Alexander's younger brother Sigismund succeeded him in 1506, elected
grand duke by the Lithuanian-Rus' nobility before Poland had any say in
the matter - a deliberate assertion of Lithuanian independence that
Poland answered simply by electing him its own king days later, keeping
the union intact regardless. Sigismund's reign was shadowed by his own
former favourite Michael Glinski, who, having fallen from favour,
attempted to detach Lithuania's Rus' territories and hand them to Moscow
outright; the plot collapsed for want of support even among the Orthodox
nobility it was meant to benefit, ending the last serious attempt by
Lithuanian Rus' to break from the grand duchy on its own initiative. The
war against Moscow that followed reached no decisive verdict either way:
a crushing Lithuanian victory at Orsha in 1514 could not undo the loss
of Smolensk, and a series of truces - 1522, 1526, 1537, 1542 - left the
frontier fixed roughly where the fighting had stalled rather than where
either side wanted it.

Sigismund's domestic reign proved as contested as his foreign one. The
lesser szlachta pressed for the same rights the great magnates already
held, a campaign that culminated in the 1529 Lithuanian Statute -
Lithuania's first written legal code, replacing a patchwork of custom
with a single reference every judge in the grand duchy was bound to
follow. Sigismund transferred rule to his own son, already elected grand
duke years before, in 1545, and died in 1548 at eighty-eight, the
longest-lived grand duke Lithuania had yet had. His son
took the Polish throne in the same year as Sigismund II Augustus,
carrying the union neither of his predecessors had been able to either
dissolve or make equal into the crisis that would end Lithuania's
separate existence altogether.
