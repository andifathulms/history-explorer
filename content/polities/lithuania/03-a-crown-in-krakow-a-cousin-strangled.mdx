Algirdas's son Jogaila and Kęstutis's son Vytautas were first cousins,
raised as allies against the same Teutonic threat their fathers had
divided between them. What each of them would do to the other over the
following decade shows how thoroughly Lithuania's own future - as a
kingdom, a duchy, or something else entirely - hinged on family
politics rather than foreign war.

## A secret treaty and a strangled uncle

Jogaila, having succeeded his father as grand duke, secretly allied with
the Teutonic Order against his own uncle Kęstutis without informing
Vytautas, whom he still treated as a close friend. When the Order's own
commander at Osterode betrayed the arrangement to Kęstutis, the old duke
struck first, seized Vilnius, deposed Jogaila, and took the grand duke's
throne himself - though he still granted Jogaila his father's former
lands at Vitebsk and Krewo. Jogaila did not accept the loss. While
Kęstutis campaigned against a rebellious nephew elsewhere, Jogaila and
his Teutonic allies lured both Kęstutis and Vytautas into a meeting
under false pretences. Kęstutis, eighty years old, was chained and
strangled at Kreva Castle within days. Vytautas escaped the same fate
only by fleeing disguised as one of his own wife's servants.

## An enemy who became the ally who mattered most

Vytautas spent years afterward in exile and open war against his
cousin, at one point allying with the same Teutonic Order that had
helped kill his father. Reconciliation came in 1384, and it proved
durable: the following year Jogaila negotiated the terms that would make
him king of Poland, and Vytautas, rather than opposing the union, became
the figure who actually ran Lithuania on Jogaila's behalf and, from
1392, in his own right as sovereign grand duke.

## A queen of fourteen and a crown that came with conditions

Poland's own crisis - a depleted treasury, an exhausted nobility, and a
fourteen-year-old queen, Jadwiga, with no husband - made the match
possible. Jogaila accepted baptism, married Jadwiga, and was crowned
Władysław II in Krakow in March 1386, having already signed terms
committing him and his Lithuanian subjects to Catholic conversion and
joint diplomacy with Poland before the wedding itself took place. The
Union of Krewo ended Lithuania's standing as Europe's last pagan state
at a stroke, and tied its foreign policy to Poland's for the first time.

## The reign that made Lithuania an empire

Under Vytautas, recognised as sovereign grand duke in 1392, Lithuania
reached the furthest extent it would ever hold. He crushed a Samogitian
revolt, secured a Baltic outlet at Palanga through the 1422 Treaty of
Melno, extended religious tolerance to the Orthodox Christians who made
up much of his Rus' territory, and - allied with his cousin's Polish
crown rather than opposed to it - broke the Teutonic Order for good at
Grunwald in 1410. He never held the royal title his grandfather-in-law's
family had claimed; twice, at Lutsk in 1429 and Vilnius the following
year, Polish opposition blocked his path to a crown of his own, and he
died within months of the second refusal. No later grand duke would rule
over as much territory as Vytautas did without ever being crowned king
of it.
