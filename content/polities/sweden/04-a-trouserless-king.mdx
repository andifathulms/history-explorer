Birger Jarl's own line did not outlast his grandsons. King Birger
Magnusson starved two of his own brothers to death in a dungeon after
luring them to a Christmas banquet at Nyköping in 1317, and died in
exile in Denmark still hated for it - the only Swedish king ever
buried on Danish soil. In the confused aftermath, all Sweden elected
his three-year-old great-nephew Magnus king in 1319; the same year,
Magnus's own grandfather's death in Norway brought him that crown too,
though the two kingdoms' councils continued to govern independently
of each other under a union that existed, for decades, in name alone.

## A king too poor, too indebted, too absent

Magnus's own long reign proved ruinous rather than glorious. He
mortgaged himself deeply into debt buying Scanian and Hallandic
territory from Denmark, then watched Denmark's own King Valdemar
Atterdag seize it back within a generation by treachery and open war.
Neglecting Norway so completely that its council eventually installed
his younger son Håkon there as a separate king in his place, Magnus
alienated the Swedish nobility as well, who in 1364 invited Albrecht
of Mecklenburg - Magnus's own nephew by his sister - to take the
Swedish crown outright. Albrecht's father seized Stockholm, had his
son elected king at the Mora stones, and touched off years of civil
war in which Albrecht captured Magnus in battle and mockingly styled
himself king over a "trouserless" rival. The Swedish nobility on both
sides eventually settled the war at their kings' expense in 1371,
reducing Albrecht to a puppet of a Council empowered to appoint its
own members - ending, after half a century, the union with Norway,
and leaving Magnus to drown off the Norwegian coast a few years later.

## A regent from Denmark answers a Swedish appeal

Albrecht's own puppet kingship did not survive contact with an
ambitious foreign widow. When Bo Jonsson - the powerful justiciary who
had come to hold two-thirds of Sweden and all of Finland in mortgage
- died in 1386, the state councillors managing his estate appealed
for help against Albrecht's own attempts to claim it, offering
Margaret, already regent of Denmark and Norway, the Swedish regency in
exchange for her support. Albrecht responded by mocking her openly,
claiming the title king of all three kingdoms at once, and bringing a
German army over from Mecklenburg - only to be defeated and captured
outright by Margaret's combined Danish, Norwegian and Swedish forces
at Falköping in 1389. German burgesses held Stockholm itself for
years afterward, murdering and burning out their own Swedish
neighbors, until Albrecht, imprisoned and unable to raise his own
ransom, surrendered the city to Margaret in 1398.

## A triple crown, and a union that never worked as written

The formal union Margaret built from that victory was sealed at Kalmar
in 1397, when her grand-nephew and chosen heir was crowned with a
single crown representing all three kingdoms at once - a coronation
held, pointedly, in neither Copenhagen, Oslo nor Stockholm, so that no
one capital's claim to precedence would be honored over the others.
The union's own written terms promised each kingdom continued
independence and equal standing. Margaret never actually promulgated
those terms, ruling instead through Danish officials placed in
Swedish and Norwegian offices alike, and it was that daily reality of
Danish predominance - not the theory the nobles had signed at Kalmar -
that would define Sweden's own experience of the union for the
century that followed.
