Gustavus Adolphus, born at Stockholm Castle on 9 December 1594, was
by every account a prodigy: fluent in Swedish and German as mother
tongues and conversant in Latin, Italian, Dutch, Russian, Polish and
Spanish besides, he discussed state affairs with foreign ambassadors
at thirteen, opened parliament with a speech from the throne at
fifteen, and at sixteen took the fortress of Christianopel by
surprise with a handful of men during a war with Denmark. His father
Charles IX is said to have remarked of him, "he will do it" -
accomplish everything the father himself had not managed - never
imagining how completely the words would come true. When Charles IX
died in October 1611, his heir was not yet seventeen and inherited a
kingdom under attack on three fronts at once: at war with Russia,
threatened by a Poland that was then the largest kingdom in Europe,
and barely holding its own against Denmark, which already occupied
two of Sweden's own chief fortresses. Declared of age at seventeen
by a Parliament that set aside the full legal age of eighteen, he
swore, in exchange for the nobility's support, never to make war,
peace, alliance, tax or law without the consent of the Council, the
Estates and the people - and won broad loyalty across every class of
Swedish society in return.

## A prayer before Breitenfeld

Two decades of consolidation and war against his three neighbours
prepared the ground for Gustavus's intervention in the wider war
convulsing Germany. When the Catholic general Tilly's army stormed,
plundered and burned Magdeburg in May 1631 after Gustavus was denied
safe passage to relieve it, the destruction of North Germany's
wealthiest city forced hesitant Protestant princes off the fence and
into alliance with him. He met Tilly's army that September on the
plain of Breitenfeld outside Leipzig, and before the battle he rode
bareheaded along his own lines and prayed aloud: "From a distant
land, from beloved homes, are we come here to battle for freedom,
for truth, for Thy Gospel." His tactical innovations - shallow
six-deep lines instead of the fifty-man-deep Spanish squares still
standard in Imperial armies, light flintlock muskets in place of
Tilly's cumbersome matchlocks, and mobile "flying artillery" moved
with the troops rather than fixed in position - proved decisive.
Pappenheim's cavalry charged the Swedish right seven times and was
beaten back each time; Gustavus stormed the hill holding Tilly's own
guns and turned them on the Imperial centre, breaking the army
outright. Seven thousand Imperial troops died and five thousand were
taken prisoner, against seven hundred Swedish dead. Catholic
contemporaries, refusing at first to credit the defeat of the
supposedly invincible Tilly, said it was as if God himself had
suddenly turned Lutheran - and the victory, in Stefánsson's own
judgment, raised Sweden at a stroke to the rank of a European great
power.
