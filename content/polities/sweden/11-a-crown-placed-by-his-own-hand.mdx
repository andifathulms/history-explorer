Charles XII, born 17 June 1682 and rigorously educated in mind and
body from childhood - he shot his first bear at eleven and showed a
genuine talent for languages and mathematics - inherited a regency
government that lasted only seven months before the Estates, in
November 1697, asked him to take full sovereign power himself. He
did more than accept: at his own coronation he omitted the
traditional oath entirely and placed the crown on his own head, a
deliberate declaration of untrammeled autocratic rule. One of his
first acts as king, against his own Council's advice, was to abolish
judicial torture. He had little time to settle into peacetime rule.
By autumn 1699 Denmark, Saxony and Russia had secretly agreed an
alliance to partition Sweden's possessions among themselves, and war
opened on multiple fronts at once.

## Narva

Charles answered the Danish threat first, exploiting a channel of the
Sound sailors considered too dangerous to navigate to unite his fleet
with an Anglo-Dutch squadron, gain naval superiority, and land troops
within marching distance of Copenhagen - forcing Denmark to a quick
peace at Travendal that August. He then turned east, where Tsar Peter
of Russia had laid siege to Narva with forty thousand men. Charles
marched to its relief with fewer than eight thousand, capturing a
mountain pass held by six thousand cavalry along the way, and on 20
November 1700 threw his exhausted, half-frozen army straight into the
Russian entrenchments during a raging snowstorm the moment they
arrived. Peter had already left the camp the night before; by
morning the Swedes held more prisoners than they had soldiers in
their own ranks. The victory made Charles's name famous across Europe
- and convinced him, wrongly as it would prove, that Russia was an
enemy not worth taking seriously.

## A cane against a crown

Charles spent the following years clearing Livonia and Courland of
Russian and Saxon forces before turning against Poland, where he
deposed the Elector of Saxony from the Polish throne, defeated the
combined Polish-Saxon army at Kliszów in 1702, and took the fortified
coronation city of Kraków carrying nothing but a walking cane, through
sheer audacity rather than force of arms. A rigged assembly elected a
Swedish client, Stanisław Leszczyński, king of Poland in the deposed
elector's place in 1704, though Swedish troops spent years afterward
propping up his unstable throne. When Charles invaded Saxony itself in
1706, the Duke of Marlborough was sent by England to determine whether
he intended to join the War of the Spanish Succession on France's
side - and found instead that Charles's actual target was Russia. The
Elector of Saxony was forced to the Peace of Altranstädt, resigning
the Polish crown and abandoning every alliance against Sweden. At the
height of his reputation, with Poland cowed and Denmark and Saxony
both neutralized, Charles turned his army east for the invasion that
would undo everything he had built.
