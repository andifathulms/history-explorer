Two reformers, Olavus Petri and Laurentius Andreae, had done much of
the intellectual work of bringing Lutheran teaching to Sweden under
Gustav's own patronage, and paid for their independence with a 1540
trial before a court packed with foreigners on the flimsy charge of
concealing, under seal of confession, knowledge of a plot against the
king's life. Both were condemned to death and then, their sentences
commuted to heavy fines, survived to die naturally years later. It was
a measure of how thoroughly Gustav had come to identify his own
authority with the reformed church he had spent two decades building
that even the men who built it with him were not safe from his
suspicion once he thought his own rule threatened.

## Hereditary at last

With the last of the Dalecarlian revolts finally suppressed, Gustav
summoned the Estates to Västerås in 1544, and in gratitude to the man
who had freed them from Danish rule, the assembly declared the crown
of Sweden hereditary in Gustav's own family and its male descendants
- ending, at a stroke, the centuries-old tradition by which Swedish
kings, from Birger Jarl's own son onward, had needed election by an
assembly to reign at all. The same Diet abolished what remained of
Catholic ceremony and completed the Lutheran church's establishment:
bishops became crown-appointed superintendents rather than officials
chosen by their own cathedral chapters, and by the end of Gustav's
reign more than twelve thousand church and monastic estates had
passed into the crown's possession.

## Three things, epoch-making

Gustav had, by the Diet of Västerås, accomplished what a later Swedish
poet called the work of "God's miracle-worker who built up the kingdom
of Sweden from basement to roof and gave his people a Protestant
fatherland against their will": he had freed the country from Danish
overlordship, forced a reformation on a people who mostly did not want
one while genuinely cleansing the church of real abuses along the way,
and made the crown hereditary in a single family strong enough to keep
peace where election by assembly had so often produced only rival
factions. He died in 1560, having ruled since his own election at Strängnäs in
1523 as a hunted fugitive turned king, and left the Vasa name no
longer merely one noble family among Sweden's many, but the hereditary
House that would rule the kingdom for more than a century to come.
