The Dalecarlian peasants who had put Gustav Vasa on the throne knew
exactly what they had done, and proved just as ready to threaten
taking it back. In a letter dated May Day 1525, they reminded him
bluntly how they had sheltered him as an outlaw and fought to seat
him on his throne, only to watch him "make light of good Swedish men"
by bringing Germans and Danes into government, levy taxes on churches
and monasteries against his own coronation oath, and strip chalices
and treasures from parishes that had begged him for better terms.
"We see that you mean wholly to destroy us poor Swedish men," they
wrote, "which with God's help we will prevent." Gustav refused to
believe they meant to break their allegiance outright, but offered
his own abdication at an assembly in Västerås rather than force the
issue - and the Estates nearly went down on their knees begging him
to stay. When he visited Dalecarlia himself that autumn, his old
comrades in arms asked forgiveness, claiming they had been misled.

## "Swine and goats"

A second revolt broke out in 1527, this time over religion: Gustav's
growing sympathy for Lutheran reform convinced the Dalecarlians,
egged on by an impostor claiming to be a son of the Sture family,
that failed harvests were divine punishment for the king's own
ungodliness. Gustav's reply mixed genuine bewilderment with open
scorn - he had only ordered that scripture be preached honestly
rather than used to deceive simple people, he wrote, and was
astonished the Dalecarlians troubled themselves over matters that
did not concern them. When they complained about foreign fashions at
court and demanded he execute anyone who ate meat on fast days, his
patience ran out entirely: he preferred to dress his household like
other kings and emperors, he told them, "that they may see that we
Swedes are no more swine and goats than they are." The impostor fled
once the Dalecarlians realized they had been deceived, but a 1528
campaign into the Dales still ended with rebel ringleaders executed
despite a promise of safe conduct.

## A dog barking, a cock crowing

A third rebellion followed in 1531 over an order that every parish
surrender a church bell, or buy it back at half its value, to help
pay Sweden's war debt to Lübeck. The Dalecarlians refused outright,
offering a cash payment instead - which Gustav accepted only because
an invasion threat from Norway under the exiled Christian II left him
unable to spare troops for a fourth confrontation. Once that danger
passed, he marched into Dalecarlia anyway, and made the assembled
peasants kneel, surrounded by his own men-at-arms, through an entire
day's furious speech: he would no longer be their plaything, he told
them, and if the Dales were not obedient from then on he would lay
the country waste so thoroughly that "one could not hear a dog bark
or a cock crow" there again.
