Charles XII's invasion of Russia drove deep into hostile territory
before disaster began compounding disaster. A Cossack ally, Mazepa,
joined him as a fugitive with thirteen hundred followers after the
Tsar burned his own capital and country to a wasteland rather than
let it support the Swedish army, and the Russian scorched-earth
strategy left Charles's forces stranded in a trackless wilderness
just as the winter of 1708-9 set in - the coldest in living memory.
By November firewood would not catch in the open air, and soldiers
warmed themselves over burning straw instead. Worse came in January
1709 on the exposed steppes of Ukraine: wine and spirits froze solid,
birds dropped dead out of the sky in flight, and men lost hands,
feet, ears and noses to frostbite by the hundreds. Even so, the army
followed a king many of them had come to regard as divinely inspired,
and Charles twice personally led a few hundred men to victory against
Russian forces ten times their number, holding his starving,
freezing soldiers together through sheer force of will. By spring his
army had been cut in half, down to barely twenty thousand men, and
seasonal floods halted any further march for months.

## A litter in the hottest fighting

Charles laid siege to the fortress of Poltava that May, hampered from
the start by a shortage of gunpowder, while Peter waited across the
Vorskla river with eighty thousand Russian troops, unwilling to cross
until word reached him that Charles had been shot through the foot -
at which point the Tsar dug in on the Swedish side of the river
instead. Charles decided to attack the Russian entrenchments on 27
June 1709 regardless of his wound, handing field command to Rehnskiöld
while he himself was carried into the thick of the fighting on a
litter. Swedish troops broke through on both wings at first, but a
miscommunication sent the elite Guards regiment forward alone into
the massed fire of Peter's French-made artillery, which could fire
five shots for every one the powder-starved Swedes managed in
return. Cut down before they could close to hand-to-hand range, the
army collapsed; Charles escaped with fifteen hundred men into Turkish
territory, while fourteen thousand exhausted, starving Swedish
soldiers surrendered at Perevolochna on the Dnieper days later.

## What Poltava undid

News of the disaster brought Sweden's old enemies back into the field
at once - Saxony renewed its alliance with Denmark, Poland rose
against the puppet king Charles had installed, and Russia began
methodically seizing Sweden's Baltic possessions one by one, invading
Finland and taking Vyborg outright. A Danish invasion of Scania was
thrown back at Helsingborg in March 1710 by hastily gathered peasant
levies under Count Magnus Stenbock - a rare bright spot - but the
wider war had turned decisively against Sweden. Charles himself,
sheltered by the Ottoman Sultan in considerable honour despite
Russian and Swedish agents both bribing the Grand Vizier to give him
up, still managed one last stroke of strategic influence: it was his
own plan of campaign that let Ottoman forces trap Peter and thirty-
eight thousand Russians against a hundred and ninety thousand Turks
on the Pruth River in 1710, though the resulting peace let the Tsar
escape the encirclement that Poltava itself had denied Sweden the
chance to inflict on him first.
