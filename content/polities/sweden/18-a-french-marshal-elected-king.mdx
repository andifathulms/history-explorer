Sweden's first choice of heir to the childless Charles XIII, the Duke
of Augustenburg, died suddenly at a troop review in May 1810 - so
suddenly that a mob rioted at his funeral and beat a suspected
poisoner to death in his own carriage while the guard looked on. Into
the vacancy stepped an unlikely candidate: Jean Bernadotte, a French
marshal and Prince of Ponte Corvo who had risen from the ranks to
command armies under Napoleon, and whose generous treatment of
captured Swedish soldiers had made him popular in a country still
smarting from the loss of Finland. A Swedish officer in Paris offered
him the crown on his own initiative; Bernadotte laughed off the
proposal but said he would accept if properly elected, and once
Sweden's own government judged that Napoleon favoured the match and
might help recover Finland, the Estates elected him Crown Prince
unanimously in August 1810.

## A new policy

Bernadotte, who took the name Karl Johan, arrived that autumn and
quickly concluded what Sweden's own government had refused to admit:
Finland could never be retaken from Russia, but Norway could plausibly
be had from Denmark instead. He secured Tsar Alexander's guarantee of
Norway in exchange for thirty thousand Swedish troops against Napoleon,
then led that army into Germany in 1813, fighting at Leipzig while
deliberately sparing Swedish lives wherever he could. From there he
marched not toward France but north into Holstein, and Frederick VI of
Denmark, with little left to resist, ceded Norway to Sweden by the
Peace of Kiel on 14 January 1814 - keeping Iceland, the Faroes and
Greenland, and receiving Swedish Pomerania in exchange.

## Eidsvoll and Moss

Norway itself had not been consulted, and did not accept being
transferred like a parcel of territory. Released from their old
allegiance to the Danish crown, Norwegians rallied instead around
their popular viceroy, Christian Frederik, who summoned a national
assembly at Eidsvoll that adopted a constitution modelled on the
American, French and Spanish examples and elected him king in his own
right on 17 May 1814. Bernadotte answered with an invasion of southern
Norway; after a fortnight of fighting he offered generous terms rather
than press his advantage, and the Convention of Moss ended the brief
war on the understanding that Christian Frederik would hand his crown
to Norway's own parliament, the Storthing, which could then choose
whether to accept union with Sweden. It did, nearly unanimously, that
autumn - and on 4 November 1814 the Storthing elected Charles XIII
King of Norway under a constitution amended, but not replaced, to
accommodate the union. The Act of Union passed by both parliaments the
following August declared the bond one of free conviction rather than
conquest, with Norway to keep its own laws, army and Storthing under a
shared king.

Norway did not pay its assigned share of Denmark's war debt without a
fight, disputing a treaty it had never itself signed, and its
parliament spent the following decades pressing for the full equality
the Act of Union had promised on paper - a friction Karl Johan, who
hoped for a union as close as Scotland's with England, never fully
resolved before his own accession as Charles XIV John in 1818.
