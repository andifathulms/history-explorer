King Eric the Victorious earned his byname defeating a combined army
of Jomsborg vikings and Danes at the Battle of Fyrisvellen near
Uppsala around 983, commanded by his own nephew Styrbjörn the Strong,
who was killed in the fighting; tradition held that Eric secured his
victory by vowing to give himself to Odin within ten years, and he
died, as promised, around 993. His son Olof, called Skötkonung, made
peace with the Danish king Sven Forkbeard, whose mother Sigrid the
Proud he married, and the two allied kings together defeated and
killed King Olaf Tryggvason of Norway at the Battle of Svolder in
1000, ruling Norway jointly afterward until Olaf Haraldsson won it
back in 1015. Olof himself was baptized by an English missionary,
Sigfrid, at Husaby in West Götaland around 1008 - the first king of
Sweden to accept Christianity, even though his own new faith took
generations longer to displace the old religion of Uppsala among his
subjects than it had taken among the Danes or Norwegians.

## A speech no king could ignore

Olof's own ambitions ran up hard against the limits of Swedish
kingship when he refused to make peace with Olaf of Norway and marry
his own daughter to him as the bonder, the free landholding peasantry
assembled at the Thing, demanded. Thorgny, the old Lawman of
Tiundaland, rose to speak for them directly to the king's face: "the
Kings of Sweden minded now [are] otherwise than they were of yore,"
he said - once friendly and accessible to their people, but this king
wished only to hear what pleased him, and coveted Norway as no
Swedish king before him ever had. "This we bonder will stand no
longer," he warned, "but demand that you make peace with Norway's
king and give him your daughter in marriage. But if you will not do
as we say we shall attack and slay you, as our forefathers used to do
with self-willed kings. Now declare at once which you choose." The
assembly roared its approval, and Olof gave way at once. Snorri
Sturluson, writing two centuries later, called Tiundaland - the
district around Uppsala, seat of both the king's own estates, known
as the Wealth of Uppsala, and the archbishop's see - the best and
most nobly peopled part of Sweden, each of the country's own regions
keeping its own law-assembly and its own lawman answerable, in the
end, to the very peasants a king defied at his own peril.
