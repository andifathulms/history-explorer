Earl Birger of Bjälbo, head of the Folkung family, rose to rule
Sweden in all but name after helping his brother-in-law, King Erik
"the Lisping and the Lame," survive a period of dethronement and
suppress the revolts that followed. When Christian settlements around
the Finnish city of Åbo came under pressure from Karelians and
Russians in the 1240s - Alexander Nevsky himself defeated Swedish
forces there - the Pope called for a crusade, and Birger led it in
person in 1249, conquering and Christianizing Tavastland and building
the fortress of Tavastehus to hold it. News of King Erik's death that
same year called him home before the campaign was finished.

## "A king from under my cloak"

He arrived to find his own young son Valdemar already elected king in
his absence - Birger himself had no royal blood, but his wife did,
and that was enough to make Valdemar eligible. Furious at not being
consulted, Birger demanded to know how the nobles had dared elect his
son without his knowledge. The chieftain Joar answered him bluntly:
if Birger was dissatisfied, they could just as easily elect someone
else. "Whom will you then choose for king?" Birger asked. "From under
my cloak here," Joar replied, "I, too, might easily let a king come
forth" - a plain warning that the nobility's own power to make kings
did not depend on any one candidate, Birger's son included. Birger
accepted the fait accompli and ruled the kingdom himself as regent
for his still-underage son, crushing rival claimants to the throne as
they appeared.

## Laws, a city, and the first Swedish duke

Birger's regency built more than it fought. He struck a commercial
treaty with Lübeck that helped German immigrants bring mining and
manufacturing skills into the country, fortified and walled in a
rising town on Lake Mälaren that grew, under his own patronage, into
Stockholm, Sweden's chief city. At his son's own wedding he
promulgated a law giving daughters an equal share of inheritance
alongside sons, abolished trial by ordeal and certain forms of
slavery, and declared that anyone who broke the peace of the home, of
women, of the Church or of the assembly would be outlawed. He married
the widowed queen of a Danish king and made his own son Magnus Duke
of Södermanland - the first time the title of duke had ever been used
in Sweden. Birger died in 1266, remembered, in Stefánsson's own
judgment, as both the last and the greatest of Sweden's earls, and
the first of its rulers who genuinely deserved to be called a
statesman.
