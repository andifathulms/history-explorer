A year after Breitenfeld, Gustavus Adolphus met the Imperial general
Wallenstein's own army near Lützen on 16 November 1632. His old
wounds no longer let him wear armour, so he rode along his lines in a
plain leather doublet as king and army knelt together in prayer
before the assault; the autumn mist that hung over the field cleared
only slowly as the Swedes pressed forward against entrenched Imperial
musketry and artillery. The infantry, thrown back once with heavy
losses, gave way to a cavalry advance Gustavus led across the ditch
himself, and by the time word reached him that Pappenheim's cavalry
was overwhelming his own left wing, only three attendants and the
Duke of Lauenburg could keep pace with him as he rode to the rescue
through fog thick enough to hide an approaching column of Austrian
horse until it was already upon him.

## Shot through the back

In the hand-to-hand fighting that followed, Gustavus's horse was
wounded and a musket ball shattered his arm; in pain, he asked the
Duke of Lauenburg to lead him out of the melee, and was shot through
the back as he turned to go. His page tried to help him onto another
horse when Croatian cavalry reached them and finished him with shot
and sword where he had fallen. It was his own riderless horse,
galloping down the Swedish ranks with its saddle soaked in blood,
that told the army its king was dead - and the news drove exhausted
soldiers into a furious final charge that broke what remained of
Wallenstein's line and forced his retreat under cover of darkness,
leaving his own artillery behind on a field littered with more than
twelve thousand dead and wounded. Gustavus's own body was found
afterward buried under a heap of the fallen, stripped naked, marked
by nine wounds, and trampled by horses almost past recognition.

## What outlasted him

Vienna, Madrid and Rome celebrated Lützen as an Imperial victory with
formal Te Deums, but even hostile contemporaries could not entirely
withhold their admiration for the man they had lost the field to. He
died, in Stefánsson's own words, thirty-three days short of his
thirty-eighth birthday, at the height of a fame and power whose full
ambitions - Chancellor Oxenstierna's letters describe a plan to make
Gustavus a Protestant, Scandinavian emperor of a Baltic empire
centred on Sweden - he did not live to complete. When Oxenstierna had
once warned him against exposing himself so recklessly in battle, his
own answer was characteristic: "God the Almighty lives, though I
die." Napoleon would later credit him with revolutionizing the art of
war outright, and Scottish and English volunteers - eighty-seven
British officers alone, most of them Scots - had flocked to serve
under a king who shared his soldiers' own hardships and fought beside
them to the last battle of his life.
