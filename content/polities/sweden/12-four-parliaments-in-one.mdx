Charles XII's sister Ulrica Leonora abdicated in 1720 in favour of her
husband, Frederick of Hesse, and a new constitution stripped the crown
of almost every power it had held. The king could not even choose his
own Council; the four Estates - nobles, clergy, burghers and peasants
- each sat as a separate parliament, but the House of Nobles ran the
country through a Secret Committee that always held a majority
against the other three. Poorer nobles regularly sold their votes to
the highest bidder. Real power belonged to Count Arvid Horn, who kept
Sweden at peace for nearly twenty years and modelled his government on
England's. A rival faction that mocked Horn's caution as timid took
the name Hats, proud to restore Sweden's lost greatness by force if
needed, against Horn's own party, nicknamed the Caps.

## Hats and Caps

The Hats took power in 1738 through French subsidies and superior
organisation, and openly sought revenge on Russia. A convenient
pretext - Russian soldiers murdering a Swedish envoy, Major Sinclair,
in 1739 - let them declare war in 1741, but the campaign in Finland
collapsed through incompetence and secret dealing, and by the
Convention of Helsingfors in 1742 Sweden had evacuated the whole
province. Empress Elizabeth of Russia agreed to restore it only on
condition that her own cousin, Adolphus Frederick of Holstein, be
elected heir to the Swedish throne - a bargain the Hats accepted
readily, and the Peace of Åbo in 1743 gave Finland back on those
terms. Adolphus Frederick proved as powerless as his predecessor: the
Estates had a name-stamp made with his signature so the Council could
issue orders whether he agreed or not, and dismissed his own tutors
and household appointments over his objections. When his queen,
Louise Ulrica, tried to organise a court party against this
"intolerable tyranny" in 1756, the plot was betrayed and its noble
conspirators executed.

## An oligarchy exhausts itself

A dragged-out campaign in the Seven Years' War (1756-62) bankrupted
the Hats' government, and the Caps took power in 1765 on a platform of
retrenchment, paying down debt and introducing freedom of the press -
but their austerity left them just as unpopular, and just as
dependent on foreign money, this time Russian rather than French.
Catherine II of Russia had secretly agreed with Denmark and Prussia to
guarantee Sweden's weak constitution indefinitely, judging factional
paralysis the surest path to eventual partition, the fate she was
already engineering for Poland. When the Caps moved to punish public
criticism of the Estates in 1768, King Adolphus Frederick abdicated in
protest and left the country ungoverned for six days before the
Council backed down and summoned a new assembly - which the Hats,
bribed by six million French francs in exchange for a promise to
restore real monarchy, went on to dominate without ever honouring that
promise.
