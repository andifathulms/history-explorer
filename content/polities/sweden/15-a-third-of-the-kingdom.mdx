Gustavus IV, who came of age in 1796, inherited his father's
suspicion of revolutionary France without his father's brilliance, and
put off his own coronation for years rather than summon the Estates.
His reign turned on a personal obsession: convinced Napoleon was the
Beast of the Apocalypse and himself God's appointed instrument against
him, he broke relations with France after a French newspaper mocked
him for inheriting "nothing from Charles XII but his folly and his
boots," then squandered the resulting war through inaction - remaining
idle in Pomerania through two successive coalition collapses, and once
even placing the visiting British general Sir John Moore under arrest
in a fit of pique. When Russian troops invaded Finland without
warning in February 1808, the mighty fortress of Sveaborg, holding two
thousand guns and six thousand men, surrendered to a besieging force
half its size - betrayed, many believed, by a commandant who thought
Finland would fare better under Russian rule. The outnumbered Finnish
army fought on alone for six months under leaders whose "bare presence
was equal to whole regiments," but dwindling reinforcements from
Sweden arrived too late and in too small numbers to save it, and by
the Convention of Olkijoki in November 1808 Sweden's forces had
evacuated Finland entirely.

## The King is arrested in his own palace

With Denmark invading from the west and Russia's Tsar Alexander I
threatening to partition Sweden outright, a group of senior officers
conspired to depose the king before he could drag the country further
into ruin. On the morning of 13 March 1809, Adlercreutz - hero of the
Finnish campaign - entered the king's apartments with six other
officers to stop him fleeing to join loyal troops in Scania; Gustavus
drew his sword and was disarmed, escaped briefly through a secret
passage, and was recaptured in the courtyard. Not a drop of blood was
shed. The Estates went further than the conspirators had asked,
declaring Gustavus IV and his entire line permanently forfeit of the
crown rather than simply installing his son; he lived out his life
abroad under an assumed name and died in poverty in Switzerland in
1837. His uncle, proclaimed regent and then crowned Charles XIII in
June 1809, signed a new constitution that made Sweden the limited
monarchy it remains.

## Fredrikshamn

Deposing the king could not undo what his war had already cost. Three
separate Russian columns - one crossing the frozen Gulf of Bothnia on
foot at its narrowest point, Cossacks raiding within sight of
Stockholm on the ice - forced Sweden to the negotiating table, and on
17 September 1809 the Treaty of Fredrikshamn ceded more than a third
of the kingdom's territory outright: all of Finland, the Åland
Islands, and Swedish Lapland to the Tornea river. It was, by the
source's own judgment, the hardest peace in Swedish history. A
separate peace with Denmark that December restored the prewar
boundary in the west, but the eastern half of the kingdom Sweden had
governed since Birger Jarl's own thirteenth-century crusade was gone
for good.
