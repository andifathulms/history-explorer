Gustavus III was twenty-five and in Paris, where French had become his
second language, when news reached him that his father had died in
February 1771. He returned to Sweden that June determined to save his
country from becoming, in his own words, a second Poland - a state
paralysed by factions living on foreign gold - and opened parliament
with what many judged the finest oratory the Swedish throne had ever
produced, urging the Estates to set party quarrels aside for the
common good. It did not work: the Caps controlled the Secret
Committee, and their new Coronation Oath bound the king to permanent
rule with no right of abdication and to appointments made strictly by
merit rather than favour, stripping the nobility of one of its last
privileges. Gustavus signed it in February 1772, weary and disgusted,
and began planning a revolution of his own.

## The coup

Two officers, Magnus Sprengtporten in Finland and J.-C. Toll in
Scania, agreed to seize garrison towns and march on Stockholm while
the king's brother pretended to suppress their revolt. Word of the
plot reached the Council on 16 August, and Gustavus - alone in the
capital, hundreds of miles from his co-conspirators - resolved to
strike immediately rather than wait. He spent the night drafting a new
constitution by hand, wrote a letter asking his brother not to avenge
him if he died, and took the sacrament at dawn. At ten the next
morning he rode to Arsenal Square, where some two hundred officers
joined him; after a review he remarked that since the men would be
walking back, he might as well too - the prearranged signal that the
coup would proceed. He won the Guards over with an appeal to the
memory of Gustav Vasa and Gustavus Adolphus, sent thirty men to arrest
the State Council in the middle of their own meeting, and tied a white
handkerchief around his arm as a royalist badge that the whole city
soon adopted. By evening Stockholm was his without a shot fired.

## A constitution read aloud once

Four days later the frightened Estates filed into their hall between
rows of soldiers with lit matches beside their cannon, and Gustavus,
crowned and enthroned, delivered an address accusing them of having
turned liberty into aristocratic tyranny and mangled their own
fatherland for foreign gold. He had the new constitution read aloud
once, gave the deputies no time to deliberate, and demanded a yes or
no - which they gave, three times over, unanimously. The Age of
Liberty was over. The new constitution restored the crown's control of
appointments, foreign policy and the armed forces while leaving the
Estates the power of taxation and consent to offensive war, and
Gustavus spent the years that followed abolishing judicial torture,
freeing the press, reforming the courts and personally presiding over
the prosecution and dismissal of half a corrupt supreme court's own
judges.
