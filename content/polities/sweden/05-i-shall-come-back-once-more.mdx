Eric of Pomerania, Margaret's own chosen heir and the boy crowned with
Kalmar's triple crown in 1397, ruled the union for decades afterward
without ever much bothering to visit Sweden himself. The Danish
officials he left in charge there governed as they pleased: Eric
provoked the Swedish church directly by naming a dissolute Danish
cleric archbishop of Uppsala, and his own tax collectors treated the
Swedish peasantry, in Stefánsson's own account, as something close to
property - one official, after seizing the horses of the Dalecarlians
for unpaid taxes, is said to have harnessed the men themselves to
ploughs and their wives to carts.

## "I shall come back once more"

A Dalecarlian noble named Engelbrekt Engelbrektsson carried his
countrymen's complaints to Eric in person, repeatedly, until the king
lost patience and told him never to appear before him again with his
endless grievances. "I shall come back once more," Engelbrekt is said
to have answered, "but only once." When the Swedish state council
answered the Dalecarlians with nothing but empty promises, the region
rose at midsummer 1434, and stronghold after stronghold fell to a
rebellion determined to drive Eric's officials out of the country
entirely - a campaign so disciplined, tradition insists, that no
peasant lost so much as a single hen's worth of property to it.

## A parliament that deposed a king

Engelbrekt did not stop at the battlefield. He confronted the state
councillors gathered at Vadstena directly, demanding they depose Eric,
and when they hesitated he seized several of them by the collar and
threatened to hand them over to the armed Dalesmen waiting outside -
persuasion effective enough that every councillor present signed the
deposition on the spot. Within four months nearly all of Sweden was
free of Eric's officials. In January 1435 Engelbrekt summoned a
parliament at Arboga - the first in Swedish history to include
burgesses and peasants alongside nobles and clergy - which elected him
regent, and Eric was formally deposed by a further parliament the
following year.

## Murdered by the men he had just saved

Engelbrekt's own triumph did not survive him long. The nobility,
uneasy at how popular their own liberator had become, passed the
regency to Karl Knutsson instead, and on 27 April 1436, while
traveling to Stockholm ill and exhausted from years of campaigning,
Engelbrekt was ambushed and killed with an axe by the son of a state
councillor - his body, pierced afterward with arrows, buried by
weeping peasants rather than by the nobles he had just spent two years
fighting for. In under two years he had, in Stefánsson's own
judgment, made the Swedes a nation: reviving a national feeling that
crossed every class and province, restoring the old independence of
the Swedish peasantry, and setting a precedent of armed provincial
revolt against a distant, extractive Danish crown that a Dalecarlian
peasant army would rise to repeat, under a very different leader,
less than a century later.
