Charles XII spent his last years still fighting, besieging the
Norwegian fortress of Fredrikshald and its approach works at
Fredriksten in the winter of 1718 while his minister Baron Görtz
negotiated secretly with Russia, not yet daring to let either Charles
or the Swedish people learn how unfavourable Russia's actual terms
were. Charles, as always, exposed himself freely during his daily
inspections of the siege lines. On 11 December 1718, looking over a
trench parapet, he was struck by a cannon shot that passed through
both temples and died instantly - found afterward still standing
upright, his sword gripped in his hand. A British minister, on
hearing the news, wrote to a colleague that the death of the King of
Sweden was "a plain declaration that our Cause is a just one, since
God has so visibly espoused it." He was thirty-six.

## What the Lion left behind

Stefánsson's own verdict on Charles is double-edged: a man with all
the virtues and vices of the old Viking temperament, who had the
sagas read aloud to him in camp, whose stubborn sense of honour and
faith in the ultimate triumph of justice were also the root of the
obstinacy that ruined him - a king who, born seven centuries earlier,
might have founded an empire with the same fierce will that instead
lost one. His death broke the pent-up frustration of a Swedish people
who had endured one continuous campaign for eighteen years: Baron
Görtz, the diplomat who had upheld his master's crumbling position
for three years on verbal authority alone, was arrested the very next
day - "The King's death is my death," he said when the news reached
him - and was beheaded within two months, the first victim of a
reckoning Charles's own death had set loose.

## Nystad

Peace followed in stages. Sweden made terms with England-Hanover and
Prussia in 1719 and 1720, ceding Bremen, Verden and Stettin, and with
Denmark by the Peace of Frederiksborg, which restored Danish conquests
in exchange for a cash payment and Sweden's surrender of both its
alliance with Holstein-Gottorp and its old exemption from Sound tolls.
Russia proved the hardest reckoning of all: a British fleet sent into
the Baltic stood by while Russian raiders burned five Swedish towns
and hundreds of villages and farms along the coast, and Sweden was
finally forced to the Peace of Nystad on 30 August 1721, ceding
Ingria, Livonia, Estonia and Karelia with Vyborg to Russia outright in
exchange for a modest cash payment and free Baltic trade - though the
rest of Finland was restored. As Stefánsson puts it, the same bullet
that killed the Lion of the North also killed autocracy in Sweden: the
absolute personal rule that had replaced the old noble oligarchy under
Charles XII's own house did not survive the king who had exercised it
to its final, ruinous extreme.
