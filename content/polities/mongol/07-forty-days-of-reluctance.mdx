For two years after Chinggis Khan's 1227 death, the empire had no
supreme ruler at all. His youngest son Tolui, who by Mongol custom
inherited his father's own home territory, governed as regent while
the wider family scattered to their own appanages, and it was Tolui
himself who finally summoned the quriltai that met in the spring of
1229 to choose an heir. The assembly's own preference leaned toward
Tolui; Chagatai, the eldest surviving son, held the stronger claim by
birth order. Neither mattered against Chinggis Khan's own prior
choice: he had named Ögödei before his death, and it took forty days
of the candidate's own reluctance before the family's insistence
overcame it. Chagatai and an uncle led him to the throne themselves;
Tolui presented him the ceremonial cup; the assembled princes bared
their heads and prostrated themselves nine times in the old Chinese
manner, then swore an oath binding the succession to Chinggis Khan's
own line for as long as a scrap of his descendants' flesh remained.

## A brother's bargain with a shaman's cup

Ögödei's own reign nearly ended almost as soon as it began. Campaigning
against the Jin dynasty in north China, he fell seriously ill, and
Tolui - by then his brother's own trusted lieutenant in the
campaign - took up the shamans' consecrated liquor at his bedside and
offered himself in Ögödei's place: naming his own greater share of
killing and cruelty in war as grounds enough that any punishing god
should take him instead. Ögödei recovered. Tolui died months later,
in October 1232, still only forty years old - from the illness he had
prayed to inherit, the Persian chronicler Juvaini says, or simply from
drink, depending on which account is believed. Mongol custom forbade
speaking a dead man's own name afterward; Tolui's name meant "mirror"
in Mongolian, and even the Turkic word for mirror was struck from
use among his own people rather than risk saying it.

## A dynasty that had outlasted its enemy

The war Tolui had helped fight went on without him. In May 1234 the
last Jin emperor died by his own arrangement as Mongol troops broke
into his final refuge, ending a dynasty that had ruled north China
for 118 years across nine reigns. Ögödei marked the wider triumph the
following year by convening a kuriltai that authorized the empire's
next round of campaigns - including the western expedition under his
nephew Batu that would reach Kyiv, Hungary and the outskirts of
Vienna - and by building himself a permanent capital at Karakorum on
the Orkhon river, walled in the same year, fed by five hundred cartloads
of provisions daily, and linked to the rest of the empire by
thirty-seven relay stations of the postal system his own reign
formalized. When Ögödei inaugurated his new palace with a grand feast
in 1236, he had the chief toast proposed not to a general or a prince
but to Yelü Chucai, the Khitan minister who administered conquered
north China and who told his own khan plainly that governing a
country required trained, literate men the way a porcelain workshop
required skilled potters - and who won Ögödei's consent to open
administrative office to the very Chinese subjects the conquest had
just defeated. It was one of the clearest signs that the empire
Chinggis Khan had built by conquest was already, under his son,
starting to learn how to govern what it had conquered.
