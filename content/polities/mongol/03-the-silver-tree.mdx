In 1254 a Flemish friar reached the Mongol capital and found that the drinks
fountain in the great palace had been built by a goldsmith from Paris.

## Guillaume Buchier

The man's name was William, a native of Paris; his family name was Buchier, his
father was Laurent Buchier, and Rubruck learned from a woman at the court that
there was still a brother of his living on the Grand Pont in Paris. He had been
captured in the Hungarian campaign, probably around 1242, and taken four
thousand miles east.

At Karakorum he was the khan's silversmith.

## What he built

It was thought unseemly to carry skins of milk into the palace, so he made a
tree.

> Master William the Parisian had made for him a great silver tree, and at its
> roots are four lions of silver, each with a conduit through it, and all
> belching forth white milk of mares.

Four more conduits ran up inside the trunk to the top and bent over, each with a
gilded serpent whose tail twined round the tree. From one pipe flowed wine, from
another *caracosmos* — clarified mare's milk — from another honey mead, and from
the fourth rice mead. A silver bowl at the foot of the tree caught each.

Between the four spouts at the top he set an angel holding a trumpet, and under
the tree he built a vault a man could hide in, with pipes running up through the
trunk to the angel.

He tried bellows first. They did not give enough wind. So when drink is wanted,
the head butler calls up to the angel, and the man in the vault blows with all
his might into the pipe, and the angel raises its trumpet and sounds it loudly.
The servants in the cellar outside hear it, pour the liquors into the proper
conduits, and the butlers draw off the bowls and carry them in.

The tree had silver branches, leaves and fruit.

## What it is a picture of

Everything about the empire at its height is in that machine.

The materials came from everywhere and so did the drinks: grape wine from the
west, fermented mare's milk from the steppe, honey mead from the northern
forests, rice mead from China. Four liquors from four ecologies, arriving in one
room through one object, because one family now took a share of all four.

The craftsmanship is European, involuntary, and superb. The empire's most
characteristic asset was not its cavalry but its ability to take a skilled man
from anywhere on earth and put him to work at the centre — engineers from Persia
for the siege trains, Chinese for the administration, Uyghurs for the script,
Muslims for the accounts, and a Parisian for the plumbing.

And the machinery is a fake. The angel does not blow the trumpet; a man in a
hole underneath does, because the bellows failed. That is not a criticism of
Master William, who solved the problem he had. It is the thing itself: an
enormous apparatus of display, assembled out of the whole world's skills, with
somebody hidden underneath doing the actual work.

## What else Rubruck found

He is the best witness the empire ever had, mostly because he was not impressed.

He counts. He measures. He asks what things cost. He records that he would not
drink mare's milk and that this was noticed. He records that Möngke's palace was
laid out like a church, with a nave and two aisles beyond two rows of pillars —
and then, in the sentence that has probably done more than any other to fix the
scale of the place in European minds, that the city apart from the palace was
not as big as the village of Saint-Denis, and that the abbey of Saint-Denis is
ten times larger than the palace.

Inside that town were two quarters — one of Muslim traders, where the markets
were, and one of Chinese artisans — with the court secretaries' offices between
them, a mud wall, and four gates: grain sold at the eastern, sheep and goats at
the western, oxen and carts at the southern, horses at the northern. And there
were twelve temples of different nations, two mosques, and one Christian church
at the far end of the city.

Twelve temples, two mosques and one church, in a capital you could walk across
in an afternoon. The
khan's own family included Nestorian Christians; his mother had been one. In
1254 Möngke ordered a formal debate at court between Christians, Muslims and
Buddhists, with Rubruck arguing for the Christian side, and afterwards told him
that God had given men different fingers on one hand and different ways in the
same manner.

Rubruck went home having converted nobody and having been given no alliance.
What he brought back instead was the only detailed account by a European of what
the Mongol empire looked like from inside it while it was still one thing.
