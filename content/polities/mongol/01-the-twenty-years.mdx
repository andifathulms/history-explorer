Before there was an empire there was a man with no followers, hiding in a place
called Baljuna with a handful of friends and nothing to eat. The twenty years
between those two facts are the part of the story that is usually skipped, and
they are the part that explains the rest.

## What he inherited

Temüjin's father Yesügei had been a chief of the Borjigin, of no great consequence
on a plateau full of confederations — Tatars, Kereit, Naiman, Merkit, Tayichiut,
Onggirat, Jajirat — most of them larger and better placed. When Yesügei died his
following dispersed and his family was left on the steppe.

What he had instead of an inheritance was other people's obligations. Yesügei had
once rescued Toghrul, the Kereit chief whom the Jin emperor styled Wang Khan, when
his uncle drove him out for usurping a throne and killing his brothers; Yesügei had
chased the usurper into the country west of the Yellow River and put Toghrul back.
The two had become *anda* — sworn brothers. That was the capital Temüjin started
with, and it is why he addressed Wang Khan as father.

## The letter

The alliance broke. Jamukha, Temüjin's own sworn brother and closest rival, worked
on Wang Khan's suspicions until the Kereit turned on him; a feast at Sad was
arranged at which Temüjin was to be taken. Warned by two shepherds, Kishlik and
Badai, he got away — and was beaten anyway. Abandoned by most of his troops, he
fled to the desert of Baljuna with a few firm friends and was reduced to great
straits. Those companions were afterwards known as the Baljunas, and the two
shepherds were made *tarkhans*, exempt from tax and punishment for nine offences.

From there, a fugitive with almost nothing, he sent Wang Khan a letter. Howorth
prints it in seven numbered clauses, and it is the most revealing document about
Temüjin that survives from before his rise.

It is a reckoning of favours. Did my father not rescue you and drive out the Gur
Khan? Did I not bring your brother back when the Naiman drove you out, and beat the
Merkit who attacked him?

> When in your distress you came to me with your body peering through your tatters,
> like the sun through the clouds, and worn out with hunger, you moved languidly
> like an expiring flame, did I not attack the tribes who molested you, present you
> with abundance of sheep and horses? You came to me haggard. In a fortnight you
> were stout and well-favoured again.

Then the reproach: they had sworn on the river Kara that if a snake glided between
them and envenomed their words they would not listen to it until they had asked
for an explanation — *yet you suddenly left me without asking me to explain*.

And at the end, the argument, in the form of a cart:

> When one wheel of a cart breaks, and the ox tries to drag it, it only hurts its
> neck. If we then detach the ox, and leave the vehicle, the thieves come and take
> the load. If we do not unyoke it, the ox will die of hunger. Am I not one wheel
> of thy chariot?

With it he sent a request that a particular black gelding, with its embroidered and
plated saddle, lost on the day of the battle, might be sent back.

## What the letter is doing

It is not a plea. It is an indictment delivered in the correct form, with the
obligations enumerated, the oath cited and the breach named, from a man who at that
moment had a few dozen followers and a lake.

Read it beside what happened next — the Kereit destroyed, the Naiman destroyed,
Jamukha handed over by his own men and executed — and the sequence stops looking
like a conqueror's rise and starts looking like a legal case being enforced. The
steppe ran on sworn alliance, hospitality owed and returned, and the enumeration of
services. Temüjin was better at that than anybody, and when the debts were not paid
he collected them with an army.

Twenty years later there was nobody left on the plateau to owe anything to.
