The empire was never a single inheritance in Mongol practice. It was patrimony,
divided among a family, and the parts were assigned in Chinggis's lifetime:
Jochi's line the western steppe, Chagatai's line Transoxiana, Tolui's line the
homeland, Ogedei's line the succession.

It held together as long as the great khanate was worth having. Mongke's death
in 1259 produced a war between two of his brothers, Kublai and Ariq Boke, and
the winner moved the centre of gravity to China and founded a Chinese dynasty.
After 1260 the western khanates ran their own foreign policies, adopted
different religions, and fought each other — the Ilkhanate and the Golden Horde
over the Caucasus, the Ilkhanate and the Chagatais over Khurasan.

## Why it fell apart on that particular fault line

Not because the brothers quarrelled — royal brothers quarrel everywhere. Because of
what a Mongol inheritance was.

A settled empire has a throne and a rule for filling it. The Mongols had a
patrimony, an *ulus*, which meant a body of people rather than a territory, and it
was distributed among sons in the founder's lifetime the way a herd is distributed.
Every one of Chinggis's sons received real subjects, real commanders and real
pasture, held in his own right and not at the great khan's pleasure. The great khan
was senior, and the seniority was worth having while the conquests continued and
the loot was shared from the centre.

The succession compounded it. A khan was supposed to be raised by a *quriltai*, an
assembly of the family and the commanders, which meant that every succession was an
election among people who each had armies and none of whom could be compelled to
attend. Güyük's election took years to arrange. Möngke's was disputed. After 1260
nobody even tried: Kublai and Ariq Böke each held their own assembly, each declared
the other's invalid, and the machinery for deciding between them did not exist
because it had never been anything but a negotiation.

## The shape afterwards

Four states, all of them Chinggisid, all of them at war with at least one of the
others within a generation.

The Ilkhans in Iran and the Golden Horde fought over the Caucasus for sixty years,
and the Horde allied with the Mamluks of Egypt — the one power that had beaten a
Mongol army — against their own cousins. The Chagatais and the Ilkhans fought over
Khurasan. Kublai's line, ruling China as the Yuan, was acknowledged as senior by
the Ilkhans and ignored by everybody else.

They also diverged in religion, and fast. The Ilkhans went from Buddhist to Muslim
in a generation; the Horde and the Chagatais became Muslim; the Yuan patronised
Tibetan Buddhism. By 1320 the four branches of one family were praying in three
directions.

## What did not divide

The claim. Every one of the successor states, and every state in this region for
the next four hundred years, went on deriving the right to rule from descent from
one man — in the coinage, in the Friday sermon, in the titles, and in the rule that
only a descendant of Chinggis could be called khan.

That rule outlived the empire by centuries and did more work than the empire ever
did. It is why the Manghit ataliqs of Bukhara called themselves amir instead, why
Timur ruled through a puppet, why Edigü made khans he could not be, and why a
Qungrat inaq at Khiva in 1804 had to send to the Kazakh steppe for a figurehead
before he could bring himself to take the title.

Nobody defeated this empire. Its parts stopped taking instructions, and then spent
four hundred years insisting they were still its heirs.
