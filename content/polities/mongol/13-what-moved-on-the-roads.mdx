For about a century it was possible to go from the Black Sea to Beijing under one
set of passes, and the things that made that journey are the empire's most durable
consequence — including the one that killed a third of Europe.

## The people

The evidence is in the eyewitnesses themselves, and it is worth laying out as
evidence rather than as a generalisation.

A Franciscan from Italy is at a Mongol enthronement in Mongolia in 1246 and gets
his intelligence from Russian and Hungarian captives who have learned the language.
A Flemish friar reaches Karakorum in 1254 and finds there a Parisian goldsmith
called Guillaume Boucher, taken at Belgrade, building a silver drinking fountain for
the khan; an Englishman; a Frenchwoman from Lorraine married to a Russian; a
Nestorian from Damascus; Armenians; Chinese craftsmen. A Venetian family spends
seventeen years in the service of Kublai. A Moroccan judge travels from Tangier to
the Volga and on to Delhi and China, and is employed by rulers in three of them.

None of those journeys had been possible in the twelfth century and none was
possible again until the nineteenth.

## The techniques

What moved with them is harder to date precisely and more consequential than any of
it.

Persian astronomers worked at the observatory in Beijing and Chinese astronomers at
Maragha in Azerbaijan, where the Ilkhans built the best-equipped observatory in the
world and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi produced tables that were still being copied in
Europe three centuries later. Chinese medical texts were translated into Persian.
Rashid al-Din, the Ilkhanid vizier, wrote a history that tried to cover the Franks,
the Chinese, the Indians and the Turks in one book, because for the first time there
was a court where informants on all of them were sitting in the same room.

Gunpowder, printing and the blast furnace all existed in China before the Mongols
and all appear in the west after them. The routes by which they travelled are
argued about and the coincidence is not.

## The plague

And in the 1340s something else came along the same roads.

The Black Death appears in the Crimea in 1346, at the siege of the Genoese trading
post of Caffa by a Mongol army of the Golden Horde, and reaches Constantinople,
Genoa and Marseille in 1347 in the ships that left it. Behind it lies a reservoir
of infection in the rodent populations of Inner Asia, and in front of it lies a
third of Europe.

The mechanism that carried it is exactly the mechanism that carried the friars, the
merchants and the astronomers: a continuous, policed, well-supplied road network
across the whole of Eurasia, with ships at the western end. A world that can be
crossed in a year by a man with a pass can be crossed in a year by a disease.

The pandemic then helped destroy every state that had built the roads. The Yuan lost
China within twenty-five years of it. The Golden Horde entered the twenty years of
troubles from which it never fully recovered. The Ilkhanate was already gone.

## What was left

Three things outlasted all four khanates.

A rule about who may rule, which governed the politics of half of Asia into the
nineteenth century. A set of trade routes and city networks that were not the same
after the connection broke and never went back to what they had been before it.

And a demonstration, which every empire since has been measured against, that the
distance between Korea and Hungary is not a natural limit — that it can be crossed,
administered, taxed and reported on from one place, by people with no cities of
their own, in a single lifetime.
