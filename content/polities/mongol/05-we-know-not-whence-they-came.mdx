While Chinggis was finishing Khwarazm, two of his generals took twenty thousand men
round the south of the Caspian and kept going — through Azerbaijan, over the
Caucasus, into the steppe north of the Black Sea — and in 1223 they destroyed an
army of the Rus' principalities in a country no Mongol had ever seen.

It was a reconnaissance. The Novgorod chronicler did not know that, and his entry
is the best account in any language of what it was like to be hit by this empire
without warning.

## What they were

> The same year, for our sins, unknown tribes came, whom no one exactly knows, who
> they are, nor whence they came out, nor what their language is, nor of what race
> they are, nor what their faith is; but they call them Tartars, and others say
> Taurmen, and others Pecheneg people.

Others again, he notes, said these were the people Bishop Methodius of Patmos had
prophesied, who would come at the end of time out of the Etrian desert between East
and North, and subdue the whole land from the East to the Euphrates and from the
Tigris to the Black Sea, except Ethiopia.

Then the sentence that makes the chronicler worth reading:

> God alone knows who they are and whence they came out. Very wise men know them
> exactly, who understand books; but we do not know who they are, but have written
> of them here for the sake of the memory of the Russian Knyazes and of the
> misfortune which came to them from them.

He is a monk in a northern trading city admitting, in writing, the limits of what
he can find out — and recording it anyway so that it will not be lost.

## The warning

The Polovtsy — the Cuman Turks of the steppe — had been beaten first. Their
survivors fled into Rus'. Kotyan, one of their princes, was father-in-law to
Mstislav of Galich, and came to him and to all the Rus' princes with greeting and
many gifts: horses and camels, buffaloes and girls. And he said:

> Our land they have taken away to-day; and yours will be taken to-morrow.

The chronicler, who has no affection for the Polovtsy and calls them cursed and
godless and thinks God sent the Tartars to punish them for the Christian blood on
their hands, records the warning exactly and records that the princes acted on it.
They gathered the whole Russian Land and marched.

## The two embassies

Then the Mongols sent envoys, and what they said was their standard opening.

> Behold, we hear that you are coming against us, having listened to the Polovets
> men; but we have not occupied your land, nor your towns, nor your villages, nor is
> it against you we have come. But we have come sent by God against our serfs and
> our horse-herds, the pagan Polovets men, and do you take peace with us. If they
> escape to you, drive them off thence, and take to yourselves their goods.

*Our serfs and our horse-herds.* The Mongols had never been in that country and
had already classified its nomads as their own runaway property.

The princes killed the envoys. A second embassy came: since you have listened to
the Polovtsy and killed all our envoys and are coming against us — *come then, but
we have not touched you, let God judge all*. Those envoys were let go.

## The Kalka

They pursued for nine days, crossed the river Kalka, and sent the Polovtsy forward
as a screen. The Polovtsy broke, ran back through the Rus' camp before it could
form, and trampled it. There was a terrible and savage slaughter.

Mstislav of Kiev never moved. He had taken position on a stony hill above the river
and built a stockade of posts around himself, and fought from inside it for three
days while the rest of the army was destroyed and pursued to the Dnieper.

Then a man called Ploskynya, a commander of the auxiliaries with the Mongols, kissed
the holy Cross to Mstislav and the two princes with him, swearing not to kill them
but to let them go for ransom — and handed them over bound. The stockade was taken
and the people in it slaughtered.

> And having taken the Knyazes they suffocated them having put them under boards,
> and themselves took seat on the top to have dinner.

Six other princes were killed in the pursuit. Of the rest of the army, every tenth
man came home; some the Polovtsy killed for their horses and others for their
clothes. It was the thirty-first of May.

## The disappearance

Then the thing that had happened made no sense at all, because it stopped.

> And the Tartars turned back from the river Dnieper, and we know not whence they
> came, nor where they hid themselves again; God knows whence he fetched them
> against us for our sins.

Jebe and Sübedei had ridden something like eight thousand kilometres, destroyed the
Georgian army, crossed the Caucasus in winter, broken the Alans and the Polovtsy by
persuading them to abandon each other, annihilated the assembled forces of the Rus'
princes, and gone home to report.

They came back fourteen years later with the map.
