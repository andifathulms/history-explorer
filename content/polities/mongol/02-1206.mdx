Temujin spent twenty years destroying the other confederations of the Mongolian
plateau — Tatars, Kereit, Naiman, Merkit — and was proclaimed Chinggis Khan at a
gathering on the Onon in 1206. The significance is not the conquest. Steppe
confederations had risen and dissolved for a thousand years.

The significance is what he did to the tribes afterwards. He broke them up and
redistributed their fighting men into decimal units — tens, hundreds, thousands,
ten-thousands — commanded by men he appointed, frequently of low birth, who owed
their position to him alone. A soldier could not leave his unit on pain of death.
The tribal aristocracies that had made every previous steppe polity temporary
were dismantled as organising structures and replaced with an army that was
loyal to a person and, after him, to a lineage.

That is the innovation, and it is administrative rather than military. The
Mongol advantages usually listed — horse archery, mobility, feigned retreat —
were shared by every steppe army the settled world had faced. What was not
shared was an instrument that stayed assembled.

## What the decimal army destroyed

It is worth being exact about what was dismantled, because it had been the
permanent feature of steppe politics for a thousand years.

A tribal confederation is an agreement among aristocracies. Each chief brings his
own men, who are his men; he can take them away; his successor may or may not be
bound by his father's oath. Everything depends on the personal standing of the
paramount chief with a dozen other chiefs whose interests are their own. When he
dies, or is beaten once, the parts go home. Attila's empire, the Türk khaganates,
the Uyghurs, the Kereit and the Naiman that Temüjin had just destroyed — all of
them worked this way and all of them came apart the same way.

The decimal reorganisation cut across every one of those loyalties. A man's unit
was not his clan. His commander was not his hereditary lord. He could not transfer
to another unit, and the penalty for trying was death, as it was for the commander
who took him. Men from tribes that had fought each other for generations were put
in the same thousand.

And the commanders were made, not born. Several of the most important — Jebe, who
had shot Temüjin's horse from under him while fighting for the other side, and was
given a name meaning arrow; Mukhali; Sübedei — had no standing whatever in the old
order and everything to lose if the new one failed.

## The guard

The other half of the instrument was the *keshig*, the household guard, raised to
ten thousand men and drawn deliberately from the sons of commanders — which made
it simultaneously a bodyguard, a staff college and a hostage pool. The men who
governed provinces from Korea to the Caucasus for the next fifty years came out of
it.

## Why it mattered more than the cavalry

The Mongol tactical advantages usually listed — the composite bow, the string of
remounts, the feigned retreat, the ability to live off the herd — were shared by
every steppe army the settled world had faced since the Scythians, and the settled
world had always survived them. Cavalry that cannot be held together does not take
cities, and cities were where the wealth was.

What was new in 1206 was an instrument that stayed assembled after the man who
assembled it stopped watching it, and then after he died. That is why the empire
kept growing for forty years under three successors of quite ordinary ability, and
it is the single fact from which everything that follows was built.
