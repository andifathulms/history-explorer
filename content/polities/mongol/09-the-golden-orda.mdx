In August 1246 two Franciscan friars stood in a crowd on a plain in Mongolia and
watched an emperor being made. They were there because the pope had sent them to
find out what the Mongols were and what they intended, and they are the first
Europeans who saw the imperial government at work and came home to describe it.

John of Plano Carpini was over sixty and had ridden from Lyon.

## Two tents

The court moved between them and Carpini names both.

The first was the Sira Orda, where the electors had been sitting for four weeks.
He is careful about what he could and could not establish there: *I am under the
impression that the election was made there, though it was not proclaimed.* His
evidence is behaviour rather than announcement — whenever Güyük came out of the
tent they sang to him, and while he stood outside they inclined towards him fine
staffs tipped with tufts of red wool, *which was done to no other chief*.

Then the whole court rode three or four leagues to a plain by a river, where a
second tent stood which they called the Golden Orda. It rested on pillars covered
with gold plates fastened with gold nails, and the top and sides were hung with
brocade.

The enthronement had been fixed for the Assumption, 15 August. It was put off
because of hail.

## What he would not do

On 24 August a great multitude assembled and all turned to face the south, each
a stone's throw from the next, ranged further and further away, and made
genuflections southward.

Carpini's reaction is the most revealing sentence in the passage:

> As for us, not knowing whether they were making incantations or bending their
> knees to God or what else, we would not make any genuflexions.

Two friars stood upright in the middle of an empire's chief religious observance
because they could not identify it and would not risk idolatry. Then everyone
went back to the tent, Güyük was placed on the imperial seat, the chiefs knelt,
and the whole people knelt — *except ourselves who were not his subjects*.

After that they began drinking and kept on until evening, and carts of cooked
meat were brought in, without salt, a quarter distributed to every four or five
men.

## The oath he did not see

What Carpini could not report is what was said inside. Rockhill prints alongside
him a second-hand account, from a contemporary who had it probably from
Carpini's own companion, of the form of words used when a Mongol emperor is
raised.

The barons set a gilded seat in their midst, put a sword before him and said they
wanted, begged and commanded that he should have dominion over all of them. He
asked whether they were each ready to do whatever he commanded, to come whenever
he called, to go wherever he sent them, and to put to death whomever he named.
They said they were. Then:

> My command shall be my sword.

They then put a piece of felt on the ground, sat him on it, and told him to look
up and recognise God and to look down and see the felt he sat on — and that if he
ruled well he would reign in glory, and if he did otherwise he would be so poor
that the felt would not be left him. Then they lifted him and his wife up on the
felt and proclaimed them.

Whether those exact words were used at any particular enthronement is not
something the source establishes, and Rockhill says where he got it. What it
preserves is the shape: a ruler asked to accept unlimited power by men who make
the offer conditional on their own obedience, and then reminded, at the moment of
being lifted, that he is sitting on a mat.

## What the mission achieved

Nothing it was sent for. The pope had written asking the Mongols to stop killing
Christians and to explain themselves. Güyük's reply, which Carpini carried back,
declined both: the Latins were to come in person and submit, and the question of
why the Mongols had attacked Hungary and Poland was answered by pointing out that
God had given them the earth.

What the journey produced instead was a report on how these people fought, how
they treated a city that resisted, how their armies were organised in tens and
hundreds and thousands, and what a European force would have to do to survive
meeting one. That was the actual commission, and it is the part of the book that
was copied.
