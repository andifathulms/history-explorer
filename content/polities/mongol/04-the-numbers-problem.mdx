How many people did the Mongols kill? The honest answer is that nobody knows, and
the reason is worth setting out, because the figures get repeated as though somebody
did.

## The figures

The medieval accounts give casualty figures for the Khurasan sieges — 1.7 million
at Nishapur, comparable numbers at Merv — that cannot be reconciled with any
plausible population for those cities. Merv at its height was one of the great
cities of the Islamic world, and the highest estimates anyone has made for it from
its built area fall an order of magnitude short of the figure a Mongol army is said
to have killed there in a few days.

The arithmetic alone disposes of them. A million bodies counted in a week, at a
rate one chronicler gives as so many per Mongol soldier, is not a tally anybody
performed; the number of executioners required to do it in the time available
exceeds the army.

## What can be said

That the destruction in Khurasan was severe is not in doubt and does not rest on
the round numbers. It rests on the irrigation.

The cities of that country lived on *qanats* — underground channels, dug and
maintained by hand over centuries, which brought water tens of kilometres from the
foot of the mountains. A qanat is not damaged by an army passing over it. It is
destroyed by the death or flight of the people who clean it every year, and once
the gallery collapses the labour of restoring it is close to the labour of digging
it in the first place.

That is why the Khurasan figure is a real thing even when the numbers attached to
it are not. A massacre kills a generation; the loss of a hydraulic system that
nobody is left to maintain removes the basis of settlement for centuries, and the
records of what was under cultivation in the fourteenth century compared with the
twelfth show exactly that.

## Why the round millions survive anyway

Because they are doing work that has nothing to do with counting.

Ibn al-Athir wrote his account as a religious catastrophe and said so in the first
line. The Persian historians who followed served Mongol masters and had reasons of
their own for making the conquest total and irreversible — a ruler whose victory
was absolute cannot be blamed for the state of what he rules. Chroniclers writing
for a defeated audience, and chroniclers writing for the victors, both had uses for
a million dead.

A chronicler's round million is a statement about how the sack felt, not a count of
the dead, and it should be quoted with that said out loud. The alternative — a
modern writer silently substituting a smaller number they cannot source either — is
not an improvement.
