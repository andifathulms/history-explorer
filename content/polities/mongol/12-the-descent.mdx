Chinggis Khan's most durable creation was not a state. It was a qualification.

For roughly four centuries after his death, across an enormous stretch of
Eurasia, the answer to the question of who was entitled to rule was: a man
descended from him in the male line. Not the strongest man, not the best
Muslim, not the man the notables preferred — a Chinggisid.

## What the rule did to people who were not one

Timur is the clearest case, because he was the most powerful man in the world
for thirty years and could not have the title.

He was not a Chinggisid. So he ruled as *amir*, married a Chinggisid princess to
become *güregen* — son-in-law — and kept a puppet khan of Chinggis's line at his
court, in whose name documents were issued, for most of his reign. A man who
conquered from Delhi to Anatolia, sacked Baghdad and Damascus, and captured an
Ottoman sultan alive spent his whole career deferring to a figurehead because
the alternative was a claim nobody would accept.

His descendants did the same. So did the Uzbek and Kazakh khans, who were
Chinggisids and said so constantly. So, in a different way, did the Ottomans,
who had no Chinggisid descent and built their legitimacy out of ghaza and later
the caliphate instead — and who were still, in the fifteenth century, careful
about how they addressed Chinggisid rulers.

## Where it lasted longest

The Crimean khanate, founded in the 1440s by a descendant of Jochi, survived as
a Chinggisid state until Russia annexed it in 1783.

That is five hundred and seventy-seven years after the assembly of 1206, and the
Girays were still ruling on the same claim their ancestor had made. For most of
that period they were also the last state to which Muscovy paid anything
resembling tribute — payments to the Crimea continued into the eighteenth
century, long after Russia had become the larger power, because the arrangement
was old and the alternative was raids.

The Mughals in India are the other long survival, and they are a hybrid: Babur
descended from Timur on his father's side and from Chinggis on his mother's, and
used both. The dynasty called itself Timurid; everyone else called it Mughal,
which is simply the Persian word for Mongol.

## What it was actually worth

It is easy to treat this as a curiosity — a prestige name outliving its
usefulness. It was not. A rule about who may rule is the scarcest thing a
political order has, and this one solved, for a very large part of Eurasia, the
problem that had produced permanent civil war on the steppe before 1206.

It also had a cost, and the Mongol empire itself paid it first. A qualification
by descent does not narrow the field. Chinggis had four sons by his principal
wife and a great many descendants, all equally qualified, and no rule at all for
choosing among them — which is why the succession had to be settled by an
assembly, why the assembly stopped being held after 1260, and why every khanate
in this family spent its existence in dynastic war.

The most successful principle of legitimacy in medieval Eurasia contained no
procedure for applying itself, and its author, who thought about everything
else, does not appear to have noticed.
