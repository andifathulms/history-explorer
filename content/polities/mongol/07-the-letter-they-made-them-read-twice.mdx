In 1245 Pope Innocent IV sent a sixty-year-old Franciscan across Asia to find out
what the Mongols were and to ask them to stop. The answer he brought back is one of
the most revealing documents of the thirteenth century, and the man who edited his
account for English readers dismissed it in a footnote as containing nothing of
importance or interest.

## Getting there

John of Plano Carpini reached the camp where Güyük was about to be enthroned after
a journey of about a year, riding the Mongol post at a pace that nearly killed him.
He was kept there for months, hungry.

> We remained with him for quite a month, in such hunger and thirst that we were
> barely able to keep alive, for the allowances which they gave the four of us were
> scarcely enough for one; and we could find nothing to buy, the market being too
> far away.

What saved them was a Russian goldsmith named Cosmas, a favourite of the emperor,
who helped them a little. Carpini believed they would have died otherwise.

Cosmas also, before it was put in place, showed them the throne he had made for the
emperor, and the seal he had manufactured for him, and told them what was written
on it:

> God in Heaven, and Güyük Khan on earth. Might of God. The seal of the Emperor of
> all men.

That is the whole Mongol theory of world government in twenty words, engraved on an
object made by a captive European craftsman for a court in Mongolia.

## The information Carpini actually got

Not from the emperor — Carpini notes that the emperor of the Tartars never addresses
a stranger in person, however great, but listens and answers through an
intermediary, while his own subjects stay on their knees until the speech is
finished, however great they are.

He got it from the prisoners. Russians and Hungarians who knew Latin and French,
Russian clerks, men who had been with the Mongols twenty years and thirty years, in
war and in everything else, who had learned the language and knew how the whole
thing worked.

> From these we were able to learn about everything: they told us most freely of all
> things without our having to question them, for they knew of our desire.

The single best intelligence source on the Mongol empire available to Europe in
1246 was the Mongol empire's own deported population.

## The letter

Then the answer was drafted, and the care taken over it is the point.

Carpini was told to write down his business, and did. Later he was called in again
before the procurator of the whole empire and two prothonotaries and a crowd of
secretaries and told to say everything again. Then, on the feast of Saint Martin,
the letter was translated to them word for word, and as they turned it into Latin
the Mongol secretaries made them explain each phrase to check that no word had gone
wrong. When both versions were written they were made to read them together and
separately in case anything had been left out.

> Be sure you understand it all, for it must not be that you do not understand
> everything, when you have reached such very distant lands.

Then, so that the Pope might be able to find somebody able to read it, they wrote a
third copy in Arabic.

A chancery at the far end of Asia, producing a trilingual diplomatic instrument and
proof-reading the foreign version against its own, for a correspondent it had never
heard of two years earlier.

## The man

Carpini's portrait of Güyük is short and worth having. Forty or forty-five or more;
of medium stature; very prudent and extremely shrewd; serious and sedate, and never
seen to laugh lightly or show any levity.

Christians of his household assured Carpini that he was about to become one himself,
and offered evidence: he kept Christian clerks and paid them allowances, and the
Christian chapel stood in front of his great tent, where the priests chanted openly
at the appointed hours and beat the wooden semantron in the Greek fashion, however
many Tartars were about. No other chief had that.

He did not become a Christian, and was dead within two years. But the arrangement
Carpini is describing — a Nestorian chapel in front of the imperial tent, a Catholic
friar in the camp, a Muslim secretariat drafting in Arabic, and a Russian goldsmith
making the throne — is the empire at the top of its arc, and no state before it had
ever looked like that.
