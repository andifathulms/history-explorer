Güyük ruled less than two years and died on the road, and the empire
went three more without a Great Khan at all while the two most powerful
houses in it decided, by force rather than by the rule they had all
sworn to, who was allowed to inherit Chinggis Khan's own title.

## A reign that ended where it began

Güyük set out from his enthronement camp in the spring of 1248 for his
own appanage on the Imil river - ostensibly an ordinary progress,
though Sorkoktani, the widow of Tolui, warned Batu that the march was
aimed at him. He never arrived. Seven days short of Bishbalik he died,
forty-three years old, worn down by the gout that came with a reign of
steady drinking. He left two named sons, Khoja and Naqu, and neither
succeeded him.

## A regency, and a Kuriltai nobody agreed to hold in one place

Oghul Qaimish, Güyük's widow, took the regency with Batu's assent while
a new Kuriltai was arranged - the same office Töregene had held after
Ögödei's death, and just as unable to stop the empire's administration
sliding into the sale of tax farms and exemption warrants to whoever
paid for them. Batu, already on the road east to pay his own respects to
the new Khan, stopped at Alaqmaq and summoned the assembly there instead
of in Mongolia, where Ögödei's own house insisted it belonged. The house
of Ögödei sent a single representative to register a protest rather than
attend in force - a decision that cost them every argument they might
have won by numbers.

## The debt from 1246 called in

At Alaqmaq, the general Ilchikadai reminded the princes of their oath
never to take the succession from Ögödei's line while any of his blood
remained. Möngke's own brother Khubilai answered him directly: that oath
had already been broken twice over, once when Ögödei's daughter Altalun
was put to death without the trial the yasa required of any prince of
the blood, and again when Güyük was raised over Shiramun, the grandson
Ögödei himself had named as his heir. If the house of Ögödei's own
claim on the throne had already been set aside once by force, it could
be set aside again.

It was the general Möngkesar who first named Möngke, eldest son of
Tolui, citing his record in China and in the west under Batu's own
command. Batu backed him, offered him the ceremonial cup, and the
assembly proclaimed him khan on the spot - though the formal
installation was deferred to a second Kuriltai the following spring, in
the Mongols' own homeland, where the wider family could confirm what a
partial assembly at Alaqmaq had only begun.

## The house that would not come

Oghul Qaimish and her sons refused to recognise what had been done in
their absence. So did Yesü Möngke, the Chagatayid khan then ruling in
his father's line. Persuasion failed. Batu finally ordered his own
brother Berke to carry out Möngke's installation regardless, and warned
that anyone who kept disturbing the succession would answer for it with
his head.

Möngke was enthroned on the first of July, 1251, on a day the court
astrologers judged auspicious. Ten thousand warriors knelt outside the
tent as the princes inside bent the knee nine times; a general holiday
followed, in which no animal was to be hunted, worked or slaughtered and
no water disturbed, and a feast that consumed three hundred horses and
cattle, five thousand sheep and two thousand cartloads of wine and
kumis a day for seven days running. It was a throne the house of
Chinggis had agreed thirty years earlier would never leave Ögödei's
line, taken instead by the sons of the brother who had first proposed
electing Ögödei at all - settled not by the succession rule the whole
family had sworn to keep, but by whichever house still had an army
strong enough to make a rump assembly's vote stick.
