The thing the Mongols built that no previous steppe power had built, and that no
settled empire had built on that scale either, was a road system with horses on it.

## The yam

Marco Polo saw the finished article under Kublai, and described it with the
enthusiasm of a man who had used it.

From the capital the roads ran out to every province, each named for the province
it led to — *and it is a very sensible plan*. At every twenty-five miles along any
of them a messenger found a station, called a *yamb*. Each had a large and handsome
building to put up at, the rooms furnished with fine beds and everything else in
rich silk. *If even a king were to arrive at one of these, he would find himself
well lodged.*

At the larger stations four hundred horses stood ready; at others two hundred.
Where the road crossed country with no house or hostel on it the stations were
built anyway, further apart, so that a day's stage became thirty-five to forty-five
miles instead of twenty-five to thirty.

On all the posts together, Polo reckoned, more than three hundred thousand horses
were kept for the messengers alone, and the buildings numbered more than ten
thousand.

> The thing is on a scale so wonderful and costly that it is hard to bring oneself
> to describe it.

## The runners

Between the post-houses, every three miles, stood a little fort with about forty
houses round it, where the foot-runners lived.

Each runner wore a wide belt hung all over with bells, so that his approach could
be heard a long way off. Reaching the post he found the next man standing ready,
equipped the same way; he handed over whatever he carried and received a slip of
paper from the clerk who was always there for the purpose; and the new man set off
and ran his three miles.

By this arrangement, Polo says, the emperor had news from ten days' journey away in
a day and a night, and from a hundred days away in ten.

## What it was actually for

Not the fruit that Polo mentions being picked one morning and eaten in the capital
the next. The yam was the nervous system of a state that covered a quarter of the
land surface of the planet, and it did four things at once.

It moved intelligence. A campaign five thousand kilometres from the court could be
directed from it — which is precisely how the western invasions were run, with
orders arriving from a khan who had never seen the country.

It moved officials, and with them the census returns, the tax registers and the
orders. It moved envoys — which is why a friar from Lyon could be at the Mongol
court in sixteen months, and why Chinese doctors ended up in Tabriz and Persian
astronomers in Beijing.

And it moved the merchants, under passes, along roads that were policed because the
state used them. What the fourteenth century called the safety of the road from the
Crimea to China was a by-product of a military communications system, not a
commercial policy.

## What it cost

It was paid for by the districts it ran through, which had to supply the horses,
the fodder, the buildings and the men — an obligation that fell on settled farmers
and was, in every khanate that inherited it, one of the heaviest taxes in the
state. Rubruck, travelling on it, records being moved along at a speed that
exhausted him and the animals both.

An empire is not held together by cavalry. It is held together by knowing, sooner
than anyone else, what has happened.
