title: A founding story that kept changing

Four separate accounts of how Kongo began survive from the seventeenth
century, and no two of them agree - not on where the founders came from, not
on how they took power, and not even on which direction they arrived from.
Read side by side, the disagreement turns out to say less about a distant
past nobody recorded reliably than about the political moment each
storyteller was actually addressing.

## A blacksmith, or a killer

The earliest full account, recorded by the Jesuit Mateus Cardoso at Pedro
II's coronation in 1622, describes a hero-conqueror from the north who
crossed the Congo River and rapidly seized the land that became Kongo,
handing out provinces to his own followers as he went. A different Italian
missionary, Giovanni Antonio Cavazzi, recorded a version around 1687 in
which the same founding figure murders his own aunt in a dispute over a
river crossing - proof, in a version composed for a court that still claimed
near-absolute royal power, of a king's authority to enforce the law by force
if he judged it necessary.

By 1710 a third missionary, Francesco da Pavia, was recording a version of
the same founder as a wise and skillful blacksmith who settled disputes
rather than started them. Forty years of civil war lay between Cavazzi's
account and da Pavia's, and the shift in what the founding king was for -
punishing rivals, or reconciling them - tracks the shift in what Kongo's own
politics needed a founding story to justify.

## A mother of Kongo, in a province the official story skipped

Cavazzi's own account also preserves a second story, gathered by a fellow
missionary, Girolamo da Montesarchio, from people in the district of Mpemba
Kasi who had no reason to repeat the capital's version of events. A woman
there, describing herself as the "Mother of Kongo," told da Montesarchio
that the kingdom's rule had begun in her own district - not with an
invasion from outside at all, but earlier, before any of the conquest the
official story remembered. A cemetery da Montesarchio was shown nearby,
said to hold the earliest kings, matched nothing in Cardoso's account,
because Cardoso's account was the one written for the capital, and the
capital by 1622 had every reason to prefer a single decisive founder to a
province it had since absorbed and demoted.

## A third direction entirely

A fourth version, recorded in 1680 by an Angolan settler named António de
Oliveira Cadornega, does not even agree that Kongo was founded from the
north. Cadornega's informant, a Portuguese trader who had lived in Kongo's
eastern territories, traced the kingdom's origin instead to the east, to a
polity called Kongo dia Nlaza that had only been absorbed into Kongo itself
in the 1580s - a claim that made sense to somebody whose own knowledge of
Kongo's history came from its newest province rather than its oldest one.

None of the four accounts is a simple corruption of an original text passed
down and garbled. Each is a full, internally consistent story, shaped by
where its teller stood and what political question that teller's own
moment needed the past to answer. The country's real origin, pieced
together from provincial detail none of the four storytellers had reason to
invent, looks less like any single one of them - and more like a decades-
long alliance that each generation of retelling had its own reasons to
compress into a single dramatic act.
