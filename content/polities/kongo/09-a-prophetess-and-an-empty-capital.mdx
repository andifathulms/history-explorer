---
title: A prophetess and an empty capital

For twenty-six years after Mbanza Kongo's abandonment, no single ruler held
the town or the country it once governed. What ended that interval was not
an army but a prophetess: Dona Beatriz Kimpa Vita, a young noblewoman who in
1704 began preaching a reworked Christianity she called Antonianism,
built around the claim that Saint Anthony had entered her body and spoken
through her against the endless factional war consuming Kongo's own nobles.

## A saint invoked against the nobility's own war

Kimpa Vita's preaching rejected the European missionaries and the racial
hierarchy of the Portuguese-aligned church without rejecting Christianity
itself, and her core political message was simple and aimed squarely at the
crisis at hand: the rival claimants to Kongo's throne should stop fighting,
restore a single king, and repopulate Mbanza Kongo. It found a genuine
popular following - remarkable given that Kimpa Vita herself came from the
lesser nobility rather than a background that would ordinarily command
that kind of authority - and she used it to persuade one of the rival
claimants to actually occupy the abandoned capital, where she crowned him
herself with a black Antonian crown of her own design.

## Burned as a heretic, believed for years afterward

The claimant Kimpa Vita had crowned did not hold power for long. In 1706 a
rival claimant, Pedro II, captured her and had her burned as a heretic - a
verdict that ended her life without ending her movement, which continued to
find adherents for several years afterward despite having lost the
prophetess who had founded it.

## A king comes back, but not the kingdom

It was Pedro IV, not Pedro II, who eventually succeeded in restoring and
repopulating Mbanza Kongo, in 1709 - thirty-one years after the town had
been left empty, and five years after Kimpa Vita first began preaching that
exact outcome. A king once again occupied the capital his predecessors had
ruled from for three and a half centuries. But forty years of
fragmentation could not be reversed by one reoccupation. The kingdom Pedro
IV presided over was a titular unity laid over a country still governed, in
practice, as a collection of separate principalities, many of them still
contending with each other independently of whatever the king in Mbanza
Kongo claimed. The throne Afonso I's descendants had spent two centuries
fighting over had been returned to its old capital. The centralized state
that throne had once commanded was not.
