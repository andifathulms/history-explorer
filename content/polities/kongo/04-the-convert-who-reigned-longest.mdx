title: The convert who reigned longest

Nzinga Mbemba took the throne in 1506 the same way most of Kongo's kings
before him had - as one faction's candidate in a war between rival houses,
this one fought between lords of the north and lords loyal to the capital
and the south. What made his victory different from the ones before it was
what he did with it afterward: a reign of thirty-seven years, the longest in
the kingdom's recorded history, built around a religious and political
transformation no earlier king had attempted.

## A Christian since before he was king

Nzinga Mbemba had converted to Christianity in 1491, fifteen years before
he took the throne, and had protected the handful of missionaries then
working in Kongo while he was still only a provincial governor and a
faction leader rather than a king. Once he won the succession war, he took
the baptismal name Afonso and moved quickly to make the religion he had
already practiced privately into the religion of the state. The Catholic
Church became Kongo's official faith under his direction, and in 1518 his
own son Henrique was sent to Rome and consecrated a bishop - the first
sub-Saharan African to hold that office, presiding over the young church
Afonso had built until 1536.

## Opening a country, and paying for it

Afonso's conversion was never only a religious act. It opened Kongo
formally to Portugal, and with it to a wave of European technicians and
missionaries whose skills and knowledge he set out to acquire on his own
kingdom's terms. He paid for them with the country's own exports - ivory,
raffia cloth, and increasingly, from 1514 onward, captives sold into the
slave trade the Portuguese were building along the whole coast.

The revenue supported more than churches and technicians. It fed an
appetite for imported fabric, wine and other luxury goods that spread fast
through Kongo's nobility, until by the end of Afonso's own reign nearly the
entire income the trade generated was being absorbed by exactly that kind
of consumption rather than reinvested in the state. Afonso's own kingdom
had, by the time he died in 1543, grown dependent on a trade whose
long-term costs he had already begun to protest - a protest that is its own
story, and did not succeed.

## A house too large to hold together

The same reign that built Kongo's Christian state also planted the seed of
its later unraveling. Afonso, like the kings before him, practiced
polygamy on a scale that produced an enormous royal house - by his own
reckoning, three hundred grandsons and great-grandsons after twenty-five
years on the throne, and by his death a great many more. Every king who
followed him for the rest of Kongo's history descended from one or another
of his three principal daughters, and the ever-multiplying number of
descendants with a plausible claim to the throne turned the ordinary
factional politics of a Kongo succession into something considerably more
combustible. The convert who built Kongo's longest and most consequential
reign also built the royal house whose internal rivalries would eventually
help tear the kingdom apart.
