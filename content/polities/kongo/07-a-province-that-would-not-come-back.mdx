Kongo did not collapse after the Jaga invasion and the loss of its regional
hegemony - it recovered a working, sound state, and even expanded eastward
in the decades that followed. But recovery was partial, and the losses that
did not reverse were the ones that mattered most for the kingdom's long-term
position. The Island of Luanda, with its fishing grounds and its nzimbu
shells, was lost in 1622, along with territory in Luanda town itself, and a
Kongo army sent to recover ground in the south was defeated at Bumbe by a
combined force of Angolan and Jaga troops. Only a general mobilization
across the kingdom finally brought that particular war to a stop.

## Diplomacy in place of an army

What Kongo could no longer win by force, its kings pursued instead through
diplomacy on a genuinely international scale: an alliance with the Vatican,
a deliberate strategy of playing Portugal against Spain, and eventually
playing both of those against the Dutch Republic. It worked well enough to
hold off further Portuguese encroachment for decades - but it could not
prevent the kingdom's most economically vital province from simply walking
away.

## Soyo goes its own way

Soyo held the port of Mpinda, the maritime gateway that had linked Mbanza
Kongo to the Atlantic trade since 1491, and its prince had long held a
guaranteed voice in electing each new king of Kongo in exchange for that
strategic position. In 1636 a royal election went against Soyo's own
preferred candidate, and the province's electors, already weakened by the
loss, found themselves unable to stop what came next: Soyo declared
effective independence, keeping only its ceremonial vote in future
elections while answering to no one in how it actually governed itself.
Kongo's own maritime lifeline was now sovereign territory belonging to
someone else.

## An uneasy peace under a new king

Garcia II became king in 1641 in circumstances unusual even by Kongo's own
standards - the electors who would ordinarily have confirmed his succession
had been weakened enough by the Soyo affair that he took power without
needing their formal endorsement at all. That same year the Dutch captured
Luanda from Portugal, and Garcia II, like Ndongo's own Queen Nzinga to the
south, allied with the Dutch against the Portuguese Crown. Large numbers of
Italian Capuchin missionaries arrived in Kongo from 1645 onward, and their
presence helped Garcia II reach an accommodation with Portugal even as the
military standoff continued elsewhere in the region.

The accommodation did not hold. A Brazilian fleet drove the Dutch out of
Luanda in 1648, and the disagreements left unresolved by Garcia II's own
treaty with Portugal set the stage for the war that would follow his reign
- a war that, unlike the Jaga invasion seventy years earlier, Kongo would
not recover from at all.
