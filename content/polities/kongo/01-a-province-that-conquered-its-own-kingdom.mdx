For most of a century, the standard account of how Kongo began described a
single conquering hero: a chief from a small kingdom called Vungu, north of
the Congo River, who crossed south with his followers, defeated the peoples
he found there, and carved up the conquered land among his own men. It is a
clean story, recorded in the 1620s and repeated by every historian who used
it afterward, and it puts a spectacular founding at the start of Kongo's
history in place of something slower and less flattering to tell.

## A humble start in someone else's province

Working back through oral traditions gathered from outside the royal
capital, in provinces whose own ruling families kept memories the court
version had reason to drop, a different picture emerges. The kingdom's own
founders did not arrive as outside conquerors. They began as the rulers of
Mpemba Kasi, a small district inside the territory of a larger, older state
- a kingdom called Mpemba, which by some accounts had already stood for
generations before Kongo's own name meant anything at all.

From that modest starting point, the rise was built one alliance at a time
rather than in a single campaign. An arrangement with the ruling family of
Mbata, in the Inkisi valley, secured a partner whose own leaders kept their
throne and, in exchange, gained a permanent voice in choosing every king of
Kongo who came after. A separate arrangement, this one military, brought in
the province of Mpangu. A further concession gave a stake in royal
succession to Vunda, itself formerly a province of Mpemba. Only once these
pieces were in place did Mpemba itself - the state Kongo's own rulers had
started out serving - fall to the alliance its former subjects had built.

## A process, not an invasion

No source dates the fall of Mpemba itself. What can be dated, loosely, is
the alliance-building that surrounded it: a process reckoned to the middle
of the fourteenth century, and one that most likely took more than a single
generation to complete. By the time Kongo's own court began writing its
history down, in correspondence with Portugal in the early sixteenth
century, the state built this way already looked old, settled and
centralized enough that nobody thought to record it as anything other than
finished business.

The country's own name survived the process by an accident of politics
rather than scale: an alliance of provinces earlier and larger than the one
that swallowed them chose to be remembered by the name of the smallest
partner in it.
