Afonso I did not object to trade with Portugal. He had built his own reign
on it - the technicians, missionaries and imported luxuries that sustained
his court all arrived on Portuguese ships, paid for with Kongo's own
exports. What he objected to, in a letter written to Portugal's King João
III on 6 July 1526, was a specific and by then uncontrollable consequence
of that trade: Portuguese merchants and their local agents were seizing
Kongo's own free subjects, including noblemen and the sons of noblemen, and
selling them into the same slave trade Afonso's own treasury had helped
build.

## Depopulation, in the king's own words

Afonso's letter describes a country being stripped of its own people by men
he calls, in his own words, "thieves and men of bad conscience," working
under cover of night specifically so their victims would not be recognized
and rescued. "We cannot reckon how great the damage is," he wrote, "since
the mentioned merchants are taking every day our natives, sons of the land
and the sons of our noblemen and vassals and our relatives... our country is
being completely depopulated." It is not the language of a ruler managing
an economic policy. It is the language of a ruler watching a trade he had
once controlled slip entirely out of his hands.

## A total ban, asked for and refused

Afonso's proposed remedy was total rather than partial: he asked João III
to stop sending merchants and trade goods to Kongo altogether, keeping only
priests, a few teachers, and the wine and flour needed for the Catholic
mass his own court had adopted. "It is our will," the letter states plainly,
"that in these Kingdoms there should not be any trade of slaves nor outlet
for them." No source records a Portuguese reply granting the request, and
none was going to come. By 1526 the Atlantic slave trade was already the
economic engine linking Portugal, its colonies in the Americas, and every
port along the West African coast it controlled, and no single African
king's objection - however sincerely meant, and however senior the king -
carried the weight to stop it.

## A protest that outlived its own author

The letter did not end the trade Afonso protested, and it did not even end
his own kingdom's participation in it - Kongo's court continued exporting
captives for the rest of his reign and for two and a half centuries after
his death, under kings who inherited both his throne and the same
dependence he had tried and failed to break. What the letter did leave
behind was a written record, in a king's own hand, that the human cost of
the trade was visible and named by its victims' own sovereign from nearly
its earliest years - not a case of an African ruler failing to grasp what
the trade would do to his country, but of one who saw it clearly and could
not stop it.
