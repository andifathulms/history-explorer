Portuguese ships reached the mouth of the Congo river in the 1480s,
sent south by King João II in search of a route to the Indies rather
than a new ally. What followed was neither conquest nor a simple
trading arrangement: a king's own conversion, a rebellion it triggered,
and a succession war between his two sons that the Kongo court's own
storytellers would go on retelling, in increasingly elaborate form, for
generations.

## A prince converts before his king does

The first Portuguese to stay any length of time at the mouth of the
Zaire found a ready audience in the Mani Soyo, the provincial lord who
governed the river port and who happened to be uncle to the reigning
king, Nzinga a Nkuwu. Impressed by the Portuguese and persuaded by the
priests traveling with them, the Mani Soyo accepted baptism in 1491,
before the king himself had committed to anything - and carried the
new faith to court himself, urging his nephew to receive it too. The
king sent to Portugal for priests, vestments and everything else needed
to establish the church properly, and for a time the plan was to build
in stone. A rebellion among river peoples living along the Zaire's
upper islands forced a change of plan: with the king needing to lead
troops in person to put it down, the stone church was abandoned half
finished and a hasty wooden one raised instead, so the king could be
baptized without further delay. He took the name João, after Portugal's
own king, and his wife took the name Leonora; the rebellion was
suppressed shortly after, and the king's elder son - who had already
converted during the crisis, taking the name Afonso - returned to court
at the head of the victorious army.

## A second son who would not convert

João's own conversion did not hold every member of his family. His
younger son, governor of a separate province and known in the
Portuguese sources by the name Mpanzu a Kitima, refused the new
religion outright, along with the single-wife rule it demanded of a
polygamous court, and gathered around him nobles equally reluctant to
give up the old ways. Afonso, meanwhile, threw himself into the new
faith with a zeal that made him enemies of his own: rivals at court
convinced the aging king that his elder son's Christian enthusiasm was
cover for a plot to seize the throne early, and João stripped him of
his province before an ally's intervention won a reprieve. The
suspicion was never fully dispelled. When João died, his wife concealed
the death for three days, using the kingdom's own relay-runner network
to summon Afonso in secret so he could reach the capital before his
rival learned the throne was open at all - a message that, by the
account handed down at court, covered roughly two hundred miles in a
single day and two nights.

## A war decided in a matter of days

Mpanzu answered his brother's succession by marching on the capital
with, by the same account, nearly two hundred thousand men against a
defending force of barely ten thousand, many of them recent and
uncertain converts. Afonso's own supporters wavered before the fighting
even began, and it was the elderly Mani Soyo - by then, the tradition
held, a hundred years old and still willing to take up arms himself -
who talked the wavering defenders back into the city. The battle that
followed, as the Kongo court's own storytellers told it for generations
afterward, turned on something no army could supply: a vision that
appeared over the city at the height of the fighting, which defenders
and attackers alike described afterward as a blinding light and, within
it, a woman in white and a mounted knight bearing a red cross. Whatever
actually decided two clashes on consecutive mornings, Mpanzu's much
larger force broke and fled both times. On the third day he tried to
force a river crossing his own men had staked with poisoned spikes
during the earlier fighting - a trap set for the defenders that, in the
confusion of his own retreat, killed him instead.

## What the winning side built afterward

With his brother dead and his rivals at court either submitted or
fled, Afonso spent his first months as king consolidating what the war
had won him: he ordered a proper stone church raised on the site of the
wooden one his father had hastily built, and had every household in
the capital surrender its own religious images for burning in a single
bonfire on the same ground where the battle against his brother had
turned. The war itself, and the
vision the court came to credit with winning it, became the founding
story of a Christian kingship Afonso would spend the next thirty-seven
years building into something considerably more consequential than his
father's brief and contested conversion had managed.
