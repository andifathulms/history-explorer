The disagreements Garcia II left unresolved with Portugal broke into open
war under his successor, Antonio I, and this time an Angolan army, not a
band of Jaga raiders, was on the other side. Antonio I assembled his
nobles and gave battle at Mbwila in 1665 - a defeat total enough that
contemporary observers called it the greatest battle of the century.
Antonio I himself died on the field, along with a great many of his
nobility, in a single afternoon that cost Kongo more of its ruling class at
once than any earlier war in its history had.

## A second battle Portugal could not build on

Kongo's collapse was not immediate. A second Portuguese army, pressing its
advantage, was itself disastrously beaten at Soyo five years later, in
1670, and Angola abandoned any further attempt to conquer Kongo outright.
Mbwila was not followed by a Portuguese conquest of the kingdom. It was
followed by something that did more lasting damage: a Kongo with no clear
king and a royal house too large, after a century and a half of
descendants tracing their claim back to Afonso I, to settle the succession
peacefully among itself.

## A capital that emptied out

The disputed succession hardened quickly into open civil war between rival
noble houses, and the war's cost fell first on Mbanza Kongo itself. The
capital was destroyed in the fighting in 1666, the year after Mbwila, and
by 1678 it stood completely abandoned - the town that every institution of
the kingdom had been built around, from the royal council to the currency
to the cult of the territorial spirit, now simply empty. The nobility that
had lived there for generations scattered into the countryside to contend
for provincial power instead, a way of life most of them had never had to
learn. A number of noble families crossed the river northward entirely,
founding small principalities of their own in Mayombe and the country
between the Zaire and Niari rivers rather than compete for what remained
of the kingdom they had left behind.

Soyo, already independent since 1636, survived the collapse better than
anywhere else in the old kingdom - though even Soyo struggled to keep its
own territory from fragmenting further, losing several of its own outlying
districts in the process. Everywhere else, the centralized state Afonso I
had built a century and a half earlier had, by 1678, simply ceased to have
anywhere to be centralized in.
