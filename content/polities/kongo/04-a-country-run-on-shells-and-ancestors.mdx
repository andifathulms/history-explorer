Kongo's own capital, Mbanza Kongo, sat at the center of a state built on
three distinct legal categories of person - nobility, villagers, and slaves
- and on a language that made the distinction explicit: the same vocabulary
that meant "civilization" and "politeness" was tied to town life, while
"rusticity" described the countryside, and a single word served for both
"slave" and "war captive," naming exactly where the enslaved were expected
to come from.

## A nobility bound by marriage rather than land

Kongo's nobles held no hereditary claim to their offices. The king
appointed provincial governors and dismissed them at will, with two
standing exceptions: Mbata, whose own ruling family had negotiated a
permanent claim to govern itself in the alliances that built the kingdom,
and, after 1491, Soyo, which controlled the kingdom's Atlantic port. Every
other province's governorship was the king's to give and take away, and at
every succession the entire hierarchy of the nobility was redefined around
whoever held the throne.

What held that shifting nobility together was marriage rather than land.
Succession to village headship ran through the mother's line, and the
king's own principal wife had to come from the ruling family of Mbata - the
same family whose alliance had helped build the kingdom generations
earlier. Mbata's ruling house, in turn, married into the king's own kin,
and a branch of that same family, the Nsaku Vunda, supplied the kingdom's
senior religious official, the Mani Kabunga, who tended the spirit cult of
the capital itself. A royal council of twelve, four of its seats held by
women representing the clans of the king's own grandparents, formally
carried out the election that put each new king on the throne.

## A currency the king controlled

Kongo's economy ran on nzimbu, small shells gathered from the fishing
grounds around the Island of Luanda - itself part of the kingdom - and
issued as currency under direct royal control. When the Portuguese began
importing competing shell currencies in the early seventeenth century, the
nzimbu lost two-thirds of its value within a matter of years, and the king
lost a matching share of his own revenue with it. Kongo's kings treated the
currency as a lever of state power precisely because it was one: a
population that trusted and needed a shell only the king could authorize
was a population whose economic life ran, in the end, through the throne.

## A king who was himself sacred

Kongo's religious life centered on a concept called nkisi - a term the
Christian missionaries later adopted wholesale, translating "church" as
"the house of the nkisi" and "the Bible" as "the book of the nkisi" rather
than inventing new vocabulary for ideas Kongo already had words for. Three
cults structured the concept in practice: ancestor worship, centered on the
royal burial ground; the worship of territorial spirits, tended by the Mani
Kabunga at the capital; and the worship of royal charms.

The king himself stood at the center of all three. Addressed as Nzambi
mpungu, the Supreme Creator, he was credited with guaranteeing the
fertility of the land and the arrival of the rains through his own person,
and was himself understood, in the most literal sense the word carried, to
be the nkisi of the country. It was an office built to make an ordinary
succession dispute an argument over who held divine favor as much as
political power - and Kongo's history is, in no small part, the record of
how often that argument turned violent.
