Afonso I's successors inherited a kingdom already leaning on a trade he had
tried to stop, and Diogo I's own attempts to limit Portuguese influence
after him did nothing to reverse the trend. Kongo's relative position in
the region weakened steadily through the mid-sixteenth century, especially
against Ndongo to the south, which was gaining ground of its own through
trade its own rulers ran outside Kongo's control. In 1561 Kongo cut off
relations with Portugal almost entirely - and then, in 1566 and 1567, lost
two kings in quick succession to a war with the Tio kingdom of Pool. A
kingdom already destabilized by the loss of two rulers in two years had no
capacity left to absorb what came next.

## An invasion nobody can fully explain

Warrior bands the sources call Jaga - a name used broadly for nomadic
fighting groups rather than naming any single, identifiable people - swept
in from the direction of Mbata and the country south of Pool and routed
Kongo's royal army outright. The court fled to an island in the lower Zaire
river, and large numbers of Kongo's own people, taken as refugees or
captives in the chaos, were sold into slavery by the very invaders who had
displaced them.

No source establishes who the Jaga actually were. What can be reconstructed
is a plausible motive: the invasion followed closely on the near-total
collapse of the slave trade after 1561, and many of the fighters swelling
the Jaga ranks appear to have been ordinary villagers who had grown weary
of Kongo's own nobility and saw an opportunity to seize a larger share of
the wealth the trade generated for themselves. Once victorious, the
invaders were found selling captives on the coast just as the nobility they
had displaced once had.

## Rescued at the price of a rival kingdom

Kongo's own king had no army left capable of retaking the country, and
appealed to Portugal for help. A Portuguese expeditionary force reconquered
Kongo between 1571 and 1573, though the troops did not fully withdraw until
1575 or 1576 - and the price of that rescue changed the balance of power in
the whole region permanently. The same soldiers who had restored Kongo's
king used the campaign as the occasion to found a new Portuguese colony,
named Angola after the royal title of Kongo's own southern neighbor,
Ndongo. Portuguese traders began working the Loango coast in numbers that
same year. Kongo's regional hegemony - built by Afonso I's line over two
generations - did not survive the rescue that saved Kongo itself.
