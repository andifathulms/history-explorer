The Sejm's founding principle, that a diet's decisions required
unanimous consent, had existed on paper for decades before anyone
actually acted on its most extreme implication. That changed in 1652,
under King John Casimir, when a deputy named Sicinski left a session
before its business concluded, and his simple absence was accepted as
grounds to nullify everything the diet had done that year - since
unanimity, by definition, could no longer be reached without him.

## A precedent nobody could undo

The ruling was, by later description, an absurd one - but absurd
precedents that are easy to imitate rarely stay isolated. Once a single
deputy's walkout could dissolve an entire session's work, any deputy
bribed, pressured, or simply convinced of his own righteousness could do
the same, and the Commonwealth's legislature increasingly found itself
unable to pass anything its most stubborn member objected to. The
liberum veto, meant to protect each noble elector's own liberty, instead
protected every session against ever concluding one.

## A king's own warning, decades early

John Casimir himself, addressing the same body whose paralysis he had
watched take shape, delivered what later readers would treat as an
eerily precise prophecy: Russia would seize Lithuania as far as the Bug
and the Vistula, Brandenburg would press its own claim on Greater
Poland, and Austria would turn toward Kraków and its neighbouring
provinces. He hoped, he told the assembly, to be proven a false prophet.

## The first bite

He was not. In 1772, Russia, Prussia and Austria coordinated a single
agreement carving substantial territory from the Commonwealth on almost
exactly the lines Casimir had named more than a century before. The
Sejm that convened afterward, its members bribed, coerced or simply
outmatched, ratified the loss rather than resisting it - the same
institution whose own unanimity rule had once been designed to protect
Poland-Lithuania's sovereignty now formalising the first substantial cut
into it.
