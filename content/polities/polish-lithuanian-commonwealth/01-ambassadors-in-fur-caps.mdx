Sigismund II Augustus, the king under whom Poland and Lithuania had
merged into a single Commonwealth in 1569, died in 1572 without an
heir. For the first time, the nobility of both nations faced choosing a
monarch from nothing rather than receiving one by inheritance - and they
turned the crisis into a permanent constitutional principle rather than
a one-time emergency.

## A crown with conditions attached in advance

Before any candidate could take the throne, the assembled nobility
revised the coronation oath into a document known as the Pacta
Conventa, binding whoever was elected to convene the Sejm every two
years, to share power rather than rule independently, and - in the
accompanying Warsaw Confederation - to guarantee religious toleration
among the electors themselves, whatever faith the individual noble
professed. The king would remain Roman Catholic by requirement, but his
own subjects, for the first time in the region, were formally protected
from being compelled into religious conformity by each other.

## A choice that startled Paris

The nobility settled on Henry, Duke of Anjou and brother of the reigning
king of France, over a rival Habsburg candidate. When the Polish embassy
arrived in Paris to announce the decision, eyewitnesses could not stop
describing the spectacle: ambassadors in long robes, fur caps, sabres,
bows and quivers, their jewelled sword-scabbards and lavishly decorated
horses drawing crowds who had never seen anything resembling this
combination of Eastern dress and evident aristocratic wealth. Henry
himself would not stay in Poland long - he abandoned the throne within
months to claim the French crown instead - but the precedent he was
elected under did not depend on any individual king's staying power.

## An assembly that could bind its own king before he arrived

What the 1573 election actually established was that the Commonwealth's
throne belonged, in the most literal sense, to its electors rather than
to any dynasty. Every king from Henry onward would take the same
office on the same terms: chosen by a nobility that had already written
down, in advance, exactly how much power he would not be allowed to
hold.
