Real power in the kingdom never rested with its constitution but with
the tsar's own brother, Grand Duke Constantine, commander-in-chief of
the Polish army and, by every account collected soon after his fall,
a man given to "the most violent paroxysms of temper." He had a
respectable brewer named Woloski fettered and set to work with a
wheelbarrow in the public street over a servant's undisclosed
offence, and kicked the man's own daughter down a staircase when she
begged for mercy. A nine-year-old boy who chalked "The 3rd of May
forever" on a schoolroom bench - the anniversary of Kosciuszko's own
constitution - was sentenced by Constantine to lifelong service as a
common soldier, and the grand duke spurned the boy's mother from his
carriage with his own foot when she threw herself in its path.

## A weekly flogging, recorded for its own sake

Worse than either was reserved for Major Lukasiński, an officer
Constantine had once favored, drawn into confessing his own
membership in a patriotic association by a display of false
friendship and then convicted on that confession alone. A prison
informer manufactured a fabricated escape conspiracy and named
Lukasiński its leader without his knowledge; sentenced to death for a
plot he had never joined, he found the sentence commuted instead to
perpetual imprisonment and a weekly flogging, with written reports of
each beating's effect on his body kept for Constantine's own personal
information - a record later found and confirmed among the grand
duke's own abandoned papers.

## Ninety minutes from forty thousand men

On the night of 29 November 1830, a small band of army cadets and
conspirators moved on Belweder, Constantine's palace outside Warsaw.
They killed the Russian general Gendre in the corridors and came
within a closed secret door of the grand duke's own bedchamber before
his valet sealed it behind him, letting Constantine escape half-dressed
through a window to his guards. The attackers, falling back toward the
city, were caught by Russian fire near the barracks and lost three
hundred men in the retreat - but by then every state prisoner in
Warsaw had been freed, the theatres emptied with a cry of "women,
home - men, to arms," and the city's arsenal forced open. Within an
hour and a half of the first shot, forty thousand men were under arms;
by eleven that night the Polish regiments still nominally loyal to
Constantine had gone over to the rising as well, and the grand duke
withdrew from the kingdom altogether, dragging two reluctant guard
regiments with him.

## A dictatorship, and a demand no dictator could accept

What followed was less a settled revolution than a scramble for
leadership. Patriot leaders allowed Constantine to retire under a
truce they later regretted, having had the chance to capture his
whole army outright; General Chłopicki, "a man of stern character,"
declared himself dictator to head off the disorder that followed, sent
envoys to St. Petersburg demanding recognition, and received in
answer only a demand for unconditional submission. He resigned within
days rather than lead a war he had hoped to avoid, leaving the kingdom
briefly without any executive authority at all, until the diet named
Prince Radziwiłł commander in his place - a man honest and brave by
every account, but inexperienced enough that he handed the army on
almost immediately to the general who held it when this account of
events was written down, Skrzynecki.
