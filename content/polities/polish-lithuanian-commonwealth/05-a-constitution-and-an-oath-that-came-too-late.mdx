By 1791 the Commonwealth's reformers understood exactly what had
crippled their own state and moved, in a single dramatic session, to
dismantle it. Marshal Malachowski opened the diet by having a report on
Russia's own hostile designs read aloud in full, and when a deputy
called on King Stanislaw August Poniatowski, as the one figure not
bound by factional interest, to propose a remedy, the king revealed a
plan he had been quietly preparing for exactly this moment.

## An oath sworn on the spot

The diet did not merely debate the king's proposal - it adopted it
immediately, with a deputy calling for the king and the whole assembly
to swear allegiance to the new constitution before opposition could
organise. Stanislas took the oath from the Bishop of Kraków on the
spot, declared he would never swerve from it, and led all but twelve of
the assembled diet in procession to the cathedral to repeat it. The
liberum veto and the noble confederacies that had exploited it were
abolished outright; a majority vote, not unanimity, would govern the
Sejm from that day forward.

## Praise from every court but the ones that mattered

The new constitution drew congratulations from courts and reformers
across Europe - even the pope offered his approval, and English
politicians spoke of it in the same terms as their own most cherished
liberties. None of that admiration translated into protection. Russia
and Prussia, the two powers whose interests the reform most directly
threatened, saw a Commonwealth capable of governing itself as a
Commonwealth no longer safe to leave standing at all.

## An uprising fought under an honest oath

A national rising under Tadeusz Kosciuszko in 1794 delayed the end
without preventing it. Kosciuszko himself swore before his own
countrymen never to turn the authority entrusted to him against a
fellow citizen, using it only to recover the nation's independence - an
oath, this account insists, he kept in full even as the cause he led
collapsed under Russian and Prussian arms.

## A pensioner signs the end

The Third Partition in 1795 divided what remained of the Commonwealth
entirely among Russia, Prussia and Austria, leaving no Polish or
Lithuanian territory outside their control. Stanislas, already reduced
to dependence on the powers that had dismembered his own kingdom, was
compelled by the Russian ambassador to travel to Grodno, where on 25
November he signed a formal act of abdication in exchange for a pension
and a promise that his debts would be settled. He died three years later
in Saint Petersburg, the last king of a state that a constitution
written with genuine conviction had not been given enough time to save.
