---
title: A kingdom bound by its own constitution

Napoleon gave the Polish name back to the map without ever quite
giving Poles a country of their own. The Grand Duchy of Warsaw he
carved out in 1807 was, by a contemporary ambassador's own account,
a place of open wretchedness - an unpaid army, officers in rags, the
greatest lords too poor to keep their own tables - propped up as a
French vassal state too small to defend itself and dependent on a
patron who admitted, on at least one unguarded occasion, that his
interest in restoring Poland outright was "merely a whim." Polish
troops nonetheless followed him to the end: they stayed loyal through
the retreat from Moscow and fought on with him into France itself,
earning Dąbrowski's own legion an honorable capitulation few of
Napoleon's other allies received when Paris finally fell.

## A crown attached by its own charter

Loyalty of that kind was not wasted on Alexander I. Fresh from
touring free institutions abroad and mindful, in Madame de Staël's own
phrase, of how much an emperor could be "the creature of
circumstances," Alexander secured the Congress of Vienna's agreement in
1815 to re-erect a Kingdom of Poland - bound to Russia not by simple
annexation but, the treaty specified, "irrevocably... by its
constitution." He wrote to the Polish senate himself promising to found
the country's happiness on that charter; the grand duchy of Posen went
to Prussia with its own limited diet, Austrian Galicia kept a diet of
its own, and Kraków became a nominally free republic - but the kingdom
proper, the largest Polish-speaking territory left in existence, held
its first constitutional diet under Alexander's own presidency in 1818,
five years before the settlement's promises began visibly breaking
down.

## A charter honored in ever thinner letter

They broke down anyway. Press freedom guaranteed by the constitution
was nullified by decree in 1819; the diet of 1820 fought back hard
enough to reject a bill stripping ministers of responsibility and to
impeach two of them outright, and the government answered by starving
the treasury to punish the opposition and by silencing the diet's own
published debates in 1825. Alexander died that December; his brother
Nicholas, whose own heir-apparent brother Constantine had renounced the
succession years before, took the throne and swore before God to
preserve the constitution "without any changes." Two hundred Poles and
Lithuanians were swept up that same winter on suspicion of ties to a
conspiracy in Russia proper - eight were tried, and acquitted in 1828
with a single dissenting vote. On 24 May 1829 Nicholas was crowned King
of Poland at Warsaw in person, the last coronation the kingdom's own
constitutional forms would ever see performed in earnest.
