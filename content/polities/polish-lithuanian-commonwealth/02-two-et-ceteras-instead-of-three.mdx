Chmielnicki was a man of some standing in the Ukraine when a Polish
governor, resenting his objection to being stripped of a small tract
of land, abducted his wife and burned his house, killing his infant
child in the fire. He drew his sword to avenge them, joined the
Cossacks the Commonwealth's own nobility had spent generations
mistreating, and was made their leader - and what began as one man's
private vengeance became, through 1648 and after, a rebellion the
Commonwealth could not put down. King John Casimir, newly on the
throne and judging the Cossacks the wronged party, tried
conciliation first; the nobility's own confederated forces broke the
truce with the sword instead, and Chmielnicki's army answered by
advancing into Poland itself and besieging the king in his own camp at
Zborów.

## A treaty kept for less than a year

The 1649 settlement at Zborów granted the Cossacks free exercise of
their own privileges and religion - terms the Polish nobility resented
bitterly enough that the diet was already planning a fresh campaign to
undo them before the year was out. John Casimir turned the renewed war
into something close to a crusade, receiving a blessed helmet and
sword from Pope Innocent X and assembling well over a hundred thousand
men against a rebellion the Commonwealth's own nobles dismissed as a
few thousand malcontents. It was not so easily finished: Chmielnicki
allied with the Crimean khan and fought the Poles to a standstill
before accepting a new peace in 1651. Submission proved no more lasting
the second time. In 1654 the Cossacks placed themselves under the
protection of the Russian tsar Alexis instead, who found, in a
Polish diplomatic slight over his own titles, a pretext to march two
armies into Commonwealth territory at once.

## A war declared over a missing honorific

Poland's crisis compounded almost immediately. Sweden's new king,
Charles Gustavus, found his own pretext for war in a similarly minor
insult - a letter from John Casimir that abbreviated his royal titles
with two "et ceteras" where Swedish protocol expected three - and
invaded with sixty thousand troops swollen further by Poles willing to
join a conqueror rather than fight him. Warsaw fell, and John Casimir
fled to Silesia while Charles Gustavus held Poland outright. The
Commonwealth's own nobility, disillusioned with their new master
within months, confederated against him in 1656 and invited John
Casimir back; a wary Russia, alarmed at Sweden's own gains, withdrew
its support for the invasion, and the combined pressure forced Charles
Gustavus to retreat. What contemporaries called the Deluge receded as
suddenly as it had arrived, but not before it left the Commonwealth
fighting three separate wars - against Sweden, Russia and its own
former Cossack subjects - within less than a decade.

## What the peace actually cost

Peace came piecemeal and expensively. The 1660 Treaty of Oliva
settled accounts with Sweden - Livonia ceded, the disputed titles
finally arranged to everyone's satisfaction - but the war with Russia
dragged on for years longer, with the Ukraine changing hands
repeatedly between the two powers and between rival factions of
Cossacks themselves. Poland kept the Russians at bay through campaigns
that first made John Sobieski's own military reputation, but the
Commonwealth could not reverse what nearly two decades of continuous
war had already decided. By the 1667 peace, Severia and the whole of
Ukraine east of the Dnieper passed to Russia outright, the Cossacks
placed under the two powers' joint and uneasy authority - the same
Ukraine whose own mistreatment had started the entire chain of wars
two decades earlier, lost for good to the power the Commonwealth's
own nobility had once dismissed as no threat at all.
