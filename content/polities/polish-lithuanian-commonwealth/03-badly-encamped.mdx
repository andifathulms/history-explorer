In July 1683 an Ottoman army under the grand vizier Kara Mustafa laid
siege to Vienna with, by contemporary counts, well over a hundred
thousand men and more than three hundred pieces of artillery. The
Holy Roman Emperor Leopold fled the city with his pregnant wife, sleeping
one night in a wood on a bundle of straw, and left his capital's defence
to a garrison of barely more than ten thousand men and armed citizens
under Count Starhemberg.

## A siege the defenders expected to lose

By late August the garrison's own officers estimated they could not
survive a determined general assault more than three additional days.
Starhemberg, who had sworn not to surrender the city while a drop of his
blood remained, sent a message consisting of nothing but the words "no
more time to lose." The vizier, confident of forcing a surrender rather
than a costly storm, had not even confirmed that a relief force was
approaching until it was almost in his own camp.

## A king who read the ground before the battle

That relief force was led by John III Sobieski, king of Poland since
1674, who marched his own troops over a mountain ridge so difficult that
the accompanying German forces had to abandon their artillery rather
than drag it across; the Poles alone managed to bring twenty-eight guns
against the Ottomans' three hundred. From Mount Kahlenberg, looking down
on an Ottoman camp of extraordinary scale and evident disorganisation,
Sobieski dismissed the spectacle outright: this man, he told his own
officers, is badly encamped, and knows nothing of war - they would
certainly beat him.

## A battle won on both morale and terrain

Sobieski's read of the situation proved exact. Disease, a prolonged
siege, and a superstitious conviction among the Ottoman rank and file
that they had transgressed by acting as the aggressors had already
sapped the army's discipline before the relief force ever engaged; even
elite janizary units openly mocked their own commander's caution. The
combined Polish and imperial forces broke the siege decisively, and
Sobieski's own cavalry charge down from the heights settled the field.
Vienna survived, central Europe's frontier with the Ottomans shifted
decisively in the following campaigns, and Poland-Lithuania had
delivered the single most celebrated military feat of its own existence
- gaining, in return, remarkably little that outlasted the campaign
itself.
