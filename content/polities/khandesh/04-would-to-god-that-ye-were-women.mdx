Bahadur Faruqi's defiance began quietly. When Akbar's son Daniyal
arrived at Burhanpur in January 1600, Bahadur stayed shut in the
citadel of Asirgarh and refused to come out and receive him — a
provocation serious enough that Akbar himself abandoned his plans to
winter in Malwa and marched on Burhanpur in person. He arrived on 8
April and opened the siege of Asirgarh the following day.

## A siege both sides tried to win without fighting it

What followed was less a battle than a contest of patience. Bahadur
opened negotiations with Akbar while privately hoping that Khandesh's
own dwindling supplies would exhaust the besiegers before they exhausted
him; Akbar, for his part, pressed the siege only fitfully through the
spring and summer, distracted by rebellions in Bengal and by his own son
Salim's open defiance elsewhere in the empire. It was December before an
important outwork finally fell, and on 21 December 1600 Bahadur rode out
to Akbar's camp and surrendered himself in person — but not the
fortress.

## An order sent to be refused

Bahadur had planned that separation carefully. Before leaving Asirgarh he
instructed its commander, an African officer named Yaqut, to hold the
citadel to the last regardless of any order purporting to come from
Bahadur himself once he was in Akbar's power — a device meant to let
Bahadur present the garrison's continued defiance as a rebellion he no
longer controlled, and so escape the blame for it. Yaqut, though he had
argued bitterly against his king's decision to leave the fortress at
all, obeyed the order faithfully. When Bahadur's own son Muqarrab Khan
arrived from the imperial camp carrying Bahadur's command that the
fortress be surrendered, Yaqut refused even to hear him out. Muqarrab
returned, reported his father would never yield Asirgarh, and was
stabbed to death on Akbar's orders shortly afterward — a killing Haig
calls one of the darkest blots on Akbar's own name.

## Freedom offered, and refused

Inside Asirgarh, nearly fifty princes of the Faruqi royal house sat
imprisoned, kept there under the same jealous confinement of collateral
kin that had secured every Faruqi king's own succession for two
centuries — among them seven men closely enough related to Bahadur to
have a real claim to his throne. With the fortress's fall now only a
matter of time, Yaqut released every one of them and begged one to
ascend the throne and lead the defence in Bahadur's place. None of them
answered him. "Would to God that ye were women!" the old African is said
to have exclaimed, and turned away and took poison rather than surrender
what he had sworn to hold. Asirgarh capitulated on 6 January 1601, and
Khandesh — the smallest of the post-Timur sultanates, and the only one
that had never rebelled against Delhi at all — passed to the Mughal
empire, renamed Dandesh after the prince who was made its governor.
