By the later sixteenth century Khandesh's position between three
stronger neighbours had narrowed from inconvenience into outright
dependency. Raja Ali Khan accepted Mughal overlordship as a tributary in
1577, formalising a subordination that Gujarat's own earlier
interventions in Khandesh's succession had already made plain in
practice.

## A vassal's loyalty pointed the wrong way

Tributary status did not mean loyalty. When Akbar's campaign against
Ahmadnagar reached the Deccan in the 1590s, Raja Ali Khan was compelled
to march with the imperial army, but his sympathies lay unmistakably
with the kingdom under siege — he sent secret messages to Ahmadnagar's
defenders encouraging them to hold out even while his own troops stood
in the besieging camp, a small and clandestine act of resistance from a
ruler with no realistic option to refuse his overlord's summons outright.

## What forty years of tribute could not disguise

Khandesh's rulers after Adil Khan III never regained the freedom of
manoeuvre their predecessor Adil Khan II had briefly enjoyed. Boxed in
by a Gujarat that had once installed its own king by force and a Mughal
empire that now claimed formal submission, the dynasty's remaining
decades were a matter of managing dependence rather than exercising any
independent policy — the quiet, unglamorous prelude to a confrontation
its last king would eventually choose to have instead of continuing to
avoid it.
