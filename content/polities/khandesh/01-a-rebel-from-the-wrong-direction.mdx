Every other regional sultanate that broke away in the chaos after Timur's
1398 sack of Delhi broke away from Delhi itself. Khandesh did not. Its
founder, Malik Ahmad, had made his career in the Deccan, in the service
of the Bahmani dynasty at Bidar, and it was against his own Bahmani
masters that he rebelled in 1365, joining Bahram Khan Mazandarani's
uprising against Muhammad I, the second Bahmani king.

## A refuge on the Tapti

The rebellion failed, and Malik Ahmad fled north rather than face its
consequences, establishing himself at Thalner on the Tapti river — a
location chosen for distance from Bidar's reach rather than for any
particular strength of its own. By 1382 he had subdued the surrounding
country enough to rule it as an independent prince in fact, if not yet in
any grand style: he and his descendants for several generations were
content with the modest title of Khan rather than Sultan, and it was
from this restraint that the territory itself took its name — Khandesh,
"the country of the Khans."

## A caliph's name, claimed at a remove

The dynasty called itself Faruqi, tracing its descent from Umar
al-Farooq, the second Caliph of Islam — a claim of genealogy rather than
conquest, asserted the way founders of small and vulnerable states often
asserted legitimacy when they had no larger empire's investiture to point
to instead. It was a modest inheritance in every sense: wedged between
Gujarat, Malwa and the Deccan, Khandesh would spend most of its existence
not expanding at their expense but surviving between them, a rebel's
refuge that outlasted the rebellion which produced it by more than two
centuries.
