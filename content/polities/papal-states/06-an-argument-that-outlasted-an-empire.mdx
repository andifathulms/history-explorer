---
title: An argument that outlasted an empire

The confrontation at Canossa settled nothing permanently between popes
and emperors, and the conflict that had produced it recurred, in one
form or another, for a century and a half afterward - reaching its final
and most sustained expression in the long rivalry between the papacy and
the Hohenstaufen dynasty, above all in the person of Frederick II.

## A monarch the Church could not simply outlast

Frederick II held the imperial title alongside the kingdom of Sicily,
which he had inherited and had used as a laboratory for the kind of
centralized royal government no other European monarch of his era had
yet attempted - a regular code of law, a check on feudal privilege, an
early inclusion of townsmen in royal government. None of this innovation
made him easier for the papacy to deal with. If anything, it made him
more dangerous: an emperor who governed a kingdom of his own effectively
enough to fund a long contest with Rome on favorable terms, rather than
depending, as many of his predecessors had, on the goodwill of German
princes the papacy could turn against him.

## An empire that closed with its last strong ruler

Frederick's death in 1250 ended the contest not because either side had
won a decisive battle, but because no Hohenstaufen ruler after him
proved capable of sustaining it. His line's own claim to Sicily and the
imperial title outlived him by only a few years in any real strength.
Historians looking back on the collapse have generally credited Frederick
himself with closing an entire epoch of the old German imperial idea -
the notion, inherited from Charlemagne's own coronation, of a single
universal Christian empire standing alongside a single universal
papacy - even as he tried, in his own kingdom, to build something more
like a modern centralized state instead.

## A victory the papacy did not have to fight for twice

What the Hohenstaufen collapse handed the papacy was not merely victory
in one contest but a free hand in Italy that no earlier pope had
enjoyed for generations - a claimant line broken, its remaining heirs
scattered or imprisoned, and a kingdom in the south now available to
whichever new power the papacy judged fit to grant it to. That the
argument between empire and papacy did not survive Frederick's own death
in any form serious enough to threaten Rome again said as much about the
particular strength of one Hohenstaufen ruler as it did about any lasting
resolution of the underlying question. Fifty years later, it would be a
French monarchy, not a German empire, that took up the same argument
against papal authority - on entirely different, more explicitly national
terms.
