By 1870, the papacy governed only a fraction of the territory it had
once held, and the Kingdom of Italy - unified around the House of Savoy
over the previous decade - had made clear for years that its own
national project would remain incomplete without Rome as its capital.
What had held the Italian army back was less papal strength than French
protection: a French garrison had guarded Rome against Italian
annexation since 1849. The collapse of the French Empire at Sedan in
September 1870 removed that protection overnight.

## A last appeal, and a furious reply

King Victor Emmanuel II authorized the invasion on 6 September 1870, but
still sent Count Gustavo Ponza di San Martino to Pius IX two days later
with a proposed settlement - occupation "to protect the pontiff" rather
than outright conquest. Pius IX's reception of the count was, by a
contemporary account, openly hostile: he flung the king's own letter
onto a piece of furniture and denounced the Italian government as "a
nest of vipers, whitened tombs, and lacking in faith," before declaring,
more composed, that the Italians would never enter Rome. His own written
reply to the king was scarcely gentler in substance, if more restrained
in language - a refusal to accept any of the proposed terms, delivered
alongside a prayer that God grant the king deliverance from the dangers
his own actions invited.

## A battle both sides tried to keep symbolic

Italian troops crossed the frontier on 11 September, facing papal forces
under General Hermann Kanzler that were outnumbered roughly five to one.
Pius IX, unwilling to see the Church's own cause stained by real
bloodshed but equally unwilling to surrender without any resistance at
all, ordered Kanzler to offer only a token defense - enough to establish
that force, not consent, had taken the city, but no more than that. On
19 September, with Italian guns already massing outside the walls, the
pope made his own instructions explicit: resistance should amount to a
protest against the violence being done, "and nothing more."

## Five hours, sixty-nine dead, and a wall breached at Porta Pia

The final assault began before dawn on 20 September. Two divisions
staged diversionary attacks while the real breach was concentrated at
the Porta Pia and Porta Salara, on the northern wall. The battle lasted
five hours rather than the token exchange either side had originally
anticipated, in part because not every general held to the planned
restraint - one Italian commander shelled areas near a different gate
without authorization, putting civilians directly at risk. When the
guns fell silent, sixty-nine men were dead and a further hundred and
ninety-two wounded, on both sides combined. Italian troops entered Rome
through the breach that same day, and the eleven centuries of papal
temporal rule that had begun with a Frankish king's donation of a
conquered Byzantine province ended in an Italian one.
