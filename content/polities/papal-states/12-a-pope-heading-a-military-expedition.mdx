Julius II was elected in 1503 on the ruins of Cesare Borgia's failed
attempt to carve a private state out of the Romagna for himself, and
he drew the obvious lesson from it: the territory Borgia had briefly
assembled by force could belong to the Papacy outright, if the Papacy
itself were willing to take the field. In August 1506, at the age of
sixty-four, Julius left Rome at the head of a small armed column of
his own, and Italy watched in open astonishment at the unheard-of
spectacle of a reigning pope personally leading a military campaign.

## A tyrant who surrendered without a fight

His first target, the mercenary captain Giampaolo Baglioni, who ruled
Perugia as an outright tyranny, lost his nerve entirely at the Pope's
approach and surrendered the city without a battle - so thoroughly
overawed that Julius entered under arms with his own troops left
behind him, an arrogance Baglioni never punished. From Perugia the
column pressed on toward Bologna, where the Bentivoglio family had
ruled for generations from one of the finest palaces in Italy;
confronted with the Pope's advancing army, the Bentivogli fled and
Bologna too fell under direct papal government - two of the Papal
States' most substantial cities absorbed by intimidation rather than
siege.

## A war won and a cardinal murdered by the Pope's own nephew

The wider war against Venice and then France that followed proved far
costlier. Julius formed and broke alliances in rapid succession - the
League of Cambray against Venice, then the Holy League against
France - losing Bologna to a French-backed revolt in 1511 before
recovering it the following year. The recovery came soured by scandal:
the Pope's own nephew, the young Duke of Urbino, blamed the papal
legate Cardinal Alidosi for the city's loss, and when the two met by
chance in a street at Ravenna, the Duke dragged the cardinal from his
mule and stabbed him to death in public. Julius, informed of the
murder of a prince of the Church by his own family, was inconsolable -
he wept and cried out like a man struck, and left Ravenna within two
hours, lying prostrate in his litter the whole way to Rimini. Bologna
itself remained lost for months afterward, recovered only once the
French position in Italy collapsed under the weight of the wider
coalition Julius had assembled against it.

## What the wars actually built

By his death in February 1513, Julius had accomplished, through
outright warfare, what centuries of diplomacy and donation had only
partially secured: a Papal States that governed Rome, the Romagna, and
the cities of Umbria as a direct, continuous territorial bloc rather
than a patchwork of nominal overlordships contested by local tyrants.
The nickname that stuck to him - il Papa terribile, the terrible or
fearsome Pope - reflected a reign remembered as much for the Sistine
Chapel ceiling and the rebuilding of St Peter's as for the sight of
the Vicar of Christ riding at the head of an army through the
Romagna's winter mud.
