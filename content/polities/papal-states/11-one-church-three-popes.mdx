Gregory XI's death in Rome in 1378, only months after he had ended the
Avignon papacy, left the cardinals to elect a successor under conditions
no conclave had faced before. A Roman mob, remembering the wealth and
patronage the papal court had carried away to France for two
generations, surrounded the conclave chanting "Romano, Romano, lo
volemo, lo Papa" - a Roman, we want a Roman for Pope - and broke open
the bell tower of St Peter's to summon a larger crowd as the vote
approached.

## An election made twice, under two different pressures

The cardinals elected the Archbishop of Bari, who took the name Urban
VI - not a Roman, but at least an Italian, which the terrified conclave
judged near enough to satisfy the crowd outside. In the chaos of
breaking away from the mob, one cardinal blurted out that the elderly
Roman Cardinal Tebaldeschi was pope instead, and the crowd very nearly
tore the real Urban's hiding place apart searching for him before the
truth got out. Urban's own behavior in office - harsh, suspicious, and
soon openly abusive toward the cardinals who had elected him - drove
most of the same College to flee Rome within months and declare, from
the safety of Fondi, that his election had been made under duress and
was therefore void. In September they elected one of their own
number, Robert of Geneva, who took the name Clement VII and
established a rival court at Avignon. Neither pope would yield, and
for the first time Latin Christendom had two claimants to the papal
throne at once, each with his own college of cardinals, each
excommunicating the other.

## A council that solved nothing by adding a third

Thirty years of divided allegiance, competing taxation, and failed
diplomacy followed before a general council of the Church, meeting at
Pisa in 1409, took the drastic step of trying both reigning claimants
for schism and heresy and formally deposing them, to the pealing of
church bells relayed from village to village across Italy within
hours. The council then elected its own pope, Peter Philargi, a monk
of obscure Cretan birth who took the name Alexander V. It was a
council without an army: neither of the deposed popes recognized the
sentence or stepped down, and Christendom now had three popes
simultaneously claiming the tiara instead of two, each still commanding
the loyalty of different kingdoms and universities. Alexander himself
died within a year, succeeded at Pisa by Baldassare Cossa as John
XXIII - the line that eventually convened the far larger Council of
Constance in 1414, which finally secured the resignation or deposition
of all three claimants and elected a single, uncontested pope, Martin
V, in 1417 - closing a schism that had run for nearly four decades and
restoring, for the first time since Gregory XI's death, one Church
with one head.
