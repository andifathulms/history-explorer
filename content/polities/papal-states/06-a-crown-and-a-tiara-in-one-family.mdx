Alberic ruled Rome for twenty-two years without ever taking the papal
throne himself, content with the secular title of Prince and Senator
of all the Romans while a sequence of compliant popes he controlled
occupied the Lateran. He built a standing city militia loyal to his
own pay rather than to any noble faction, reorganized Rome's judicial
courts under his personal authority, and kept both the German king
Otto and the rival claims of Hugh of Provence's own family at arm's
length for the whole of his reign - the longest period of stable
government Rome had known in generations. On his deathbed in 954 he
extracted an oath from the assembled Roman nobility and clergy that
his teenage son Octavian would succeed him as prince, and, at the next
vacancy, be elected pope as well.

## A boy who became both prince and pontiff

Octavian kept both promises: he inherited his father's secular
authority immediately, and within a year of Alberic's death he was
elected pope, taking the name John XII - the first Pope to change his
name on election, a practice that became permanent afterward. Barely
past adolescence, John governed Rome with his father's political
instincts and none of his father's discipline, turning the Lateran
into what one contemporary account called a court of open
debauchery, while the German threat Alberic had held off for two
decades grew more pressing by the year.

## A privilege bought with a crown

Threatened by the ambitions of King Berengar of Italy, John did what
his own predecessor John X had once tried and failed to do: he invited
a foreign king to Rome as protector. Otto I of Germany crossed the
Alps, and on 2 February 962 John crowned him Emperor in St Peter's - a
revival of Charlemagne's title that had lain dormant for decades.
In return, Otto issued a charter confirming and extending the
papacy's temporal claims across a wide swath of central Italy, the
document later known as the Ottonianum. But the alliance bound John as
much as it protected him: the Romans and the Pope alike swore oaths of
fidelity to the new Emperor, and the independence Alberic had spent
his whole reign defending was folded back into imperial authority
within months of the coronation that was meant to secure it.

## A pope deposed, and a pope who would not stay deposed

John chafed almost immediately, intriguing with Otto's own Italian
enemies behind the Emperor's back. Otto returned to Rome, convened a
synod that deposed John for offenses ranging from sacrilege to
simony, and installed a layman, Leo VIII, in his place - the first
deposition of a pope by an emperor's own authority rather than by
Roman factional violence. The arrangement did not last: once Otto
withdrew, the Romans expelled his appointee and restored John, who
died within months in circumstances contemporaries were happy to
report as scandalous, whether by apoplexy or, as some claimed, at the
hands of an outraged husband. The precedent, though, outlived both
men - the German emperor, not the Roman nobility, now held the final
word over who wore the tiara, a relationship of tutelage and conflict
that would harden, within little more than a century, into the
Investiture Controversy.
