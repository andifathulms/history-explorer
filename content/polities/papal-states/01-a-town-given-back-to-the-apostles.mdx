In 727 or 728, the Lombard king Liutprand seized Sutri, a fortified town
on the Cassian road guarding the approach to Rome from the north. It was
one small piece of a much larger Lombard advance that had already taken
Ravenna and the surrounding Pentapolis from Byzantine control. Pope
Gregory II, unable to meet Liutprand's army with one of his own, met it
instead with money and persistent diplomacy - and it worked. Liutprand
gave the town back, but not to the Byzantine officials who had governed
it before his conquest. According to the papal register, he restored it
"to the apostles Peter and Paul" directly.

## A single town, and a question that would outlive it

Nothing about the restitution of Sutri looked, at the time, like the
founding of a state. It was a modest, single-town settlement between a
pope with no army and a king who had simply judged the town not worth
the trouble of a fight. Later historians would nonetheless argue over
exactly this transaction as the true beginning of papal temporal
sovereignty - the first recorded moment a territory was handed to the
papacy in its own right, rather than administered by the pope on behalf
of some other recognized authority. The argument has never been fully
settled, in part because Sutri itself set no durable precedent on its
own: the Lombards kept pressing south, and nothing stopped a future king
from seizing the same ground again whenever it suited him.

## A precedent too small to hold

That is exactly what happened. Within a generation, a far more
ambitious Lombard king would take not one town but the whole of the old
Byzantine Exarchate of Ravenna, forcing the question of papal territory
onto a scale Sutri's own modest restitution had never addressed. What
Sutri established, if it established anything at all, was only the idea
that a Lombard king could hand papal territory back directly to the
Church rather than to Constantinople - an idea the papacy itself would
need a considerably more powerful ally than its own diplomacy to make
stick.
