Three centuries after Charlemagne's coronation, the relationship between
pope and emperor had inverted almost entirely. Pope Gregory VII, elected
in 1073, pursued a program of reform that struck directly at the
practice of lay investiture - the right of kings and emperors to appoint
bishops and abbots within their own territories, a practice the German
king Henry IV regarded as essential to his own control over the German
church and, through it, over Germany's own great territorial lords.

## An excommunication answered with a council

When Gregory moved against lay investiture directly, Henry responded by
convening a council of German bishops that declared Gregory deposed.
Gregory's own answer went further still: he excommunicated Henry and
formally released Henry's own subjects from their oath of allegiance to
him - a sentence that mattered less as theology than as practical
politics, since German princes already resentful of Henry's authority
now had a ready pretext to abandon him. Facing rebellion at home, Henry
concluded that only Gregory's own forgiveness could save his throne.

## A king who came as a penitent

Henry crossed the Alps in the depths of winter and found Gregory taking
refuge at Canossa, a castle belonging to Countess Matilda of Tuscany.
For three days, Henry stood outside the fortress's inner gate wearing a
penitent's shirt over his other garments, begging admission, while
German bishops who had accompanied him arrived daily to pray for their
own absolution alongside his. Gregory hesitated throughout, distrustful
of a king's promises, until the intercession of Matilda and Henry's own
mother-in-law helped move him to relent. On 28 January 1077, Gregory
granted absolution - but only on terms that stripped Henry of his royal
authority until a formal council could judge him, and bound him by oath
to future obedience to the pope's own will.

## A victory measured in more than the moment

Gregorovius, writing of the scene centuries later, ranked it among the
handful of moments that most clearly displayed the papacy's own moral
authority over temporal rulers - a victory won without a single soldier,
achieved instead through the sheer weight of a spiritual sentence a
king could not simply ignore. It did not, in the end, resolve the
underlying conflict over investiture, which would continue for another
half-century after both men were dead. But no single episode did more to
establish, in the eyes of contemporaries and later historians alike, the
principle that a pope's judgment could unmake an emperor's own authority
- not merely bless it, as Leo III had done for Charlemagne, but strip it
away entirely until its holder had earned it back.
