By the spring of 1527 an army of some twenty thousand Spanish and
German troops under Charles, Duke of Bourbon, unpaid for months and
half-mutinous, had marched south through a wavering Italy with no
clear objective except plunder. Bourbon, a French prince serving the
Emperor Charles V after his own falling-out with the French crown, no
longer fully controlled the men he nominally commanded; many of the
Germans among them were Lutheran mercenaries who regarded a sack of
the papal city as something close to a holy errand. On 6 May they
reached the walls of the Leonine district around the Vatican.

## A fog the attackers called providence

The assault began without proper siege equipment - the imperial troops
scaled the walls on ladders hastily improvised from vineyard stakes,
under cover of a thick morning mist rolling off the Tiber that blinded
the defenders' gunners and, in the soldiers' own telling afterward,
felt like the hand of heaven clearing their path. Bourbon himself,
seeing the first assault waves thrown back, dismounted, seized a
ladder, planted it against the wall, and was climbing it when a shot
struck him in the stomach; he died within hours, his last words a cry
of "To Rome! To Rome!" His death did nothing to stop his army - if
anything it removed the last figure capable of restraining it, and
within three hours the Borgo had fallen, at a cost of some three
thousand Roman dead against only four hundred imperial.

## A pope smuggled across a bridge under a borrowed cloak

Clement VII was at prayer in the Vatican when the walls gave way; by
the time the enemy broke into St Peter's itself, cutting down the
Swiss Guard almost within his sight, he had no time left to do
anything but flee along the fortified passage connecting the Vatican
to the Castel Sant'Angelo. A companion threw an episcopal mantle over
him so he would not be recognized crossing the exposed wooden bridge,
where a single Lutheran soldier's shot could have ended the papacy on
the spot. More than three thousand people crowded onto that same
bridge behind him - cardinals, ambassadors, merchants, women and
children - trampling each other in the crush before the drawbridge
could be raised; one elderly cardinal was hauled through a window on
a rope, another pulled up in a basket. Thirteen cardinals reached the
fortress in time. Behind them, the city Bourbon's army now controlled
without any commander left to check it was given over to weeks of
looting, ransom, and killing that shocked even contemporaries hardened
to the routine violence of Italian warfare - the "Sacco di Roma" that
Clement, besieged in his own fortress for the better part of a year
afterward, would spend the rest of his pontificate trying to live
down.
