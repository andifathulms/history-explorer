The Hohenstaufen collapse the papacy had wanted since Frederick II's own
lifetime did not complete itself automatically. Frederick's illegitimate
son Manfred took the Sicilian throne regardless of the papacy's own
objections, and successive popes, unable to dislodge him by
excommunication or diplomacy alone, went looking for a prince willing to
do by the sword what the Church's own censures could not accomplish.
They found him in Charles of Anjou, brother of the King of France.

## A kingdom granted before it was conquered

Pope Clement IV invested Charles with the Kingdom of Sicily as a fief of
the Church in June 1265, before Charles had actually won a foot of the
territory - full possession was a matter still to be settled by his own
army. The terms were not generous. Clement first tried to bind Charles to
conditions that would have made him little more than a temporary papal
vassal, and only after difficult negotiation did Charles secure more
favorable ones: an indivisible, hereditary kingdom, in exchange for
substantial payment and a guarantee of full clerical immunity within it.
When Charles, on arriving in Rome, presumed to take up residence in the
Lateran palace itself without asking leave, Clement's own rebuke was
sharp and immediate - a reminder, delivered to the man the papacy had
just elevated, of exactly whose authority the arrangement actually
rested on.

## A battle that ended the argument by force

Charles met Manfred's army at Benevento on 26 February 1266. The battle
turned on infantry tactics as much as cavalry: French foot soldiers,
riding pillion behind their own knights, dismounted to finish off
unhorsed German men-at-arms with clubs, a brutal equalizer against
Manfred's heavier cavalry. When his own Italian feudal levies broke and
fled, Manfred called for his helmet, and when its ornamental eagle fell
to the ground as he put it on, he is said to have taken the omen for
what it plainly seemed to mean, remarking "Ecce, Signum Domini" before
riding to his own death rather than fleeing. His naked body was found
among the slain two days later, identified by men who had known him in
life, and buried without the rites of the Church whose armies had just
killed him.

## A dispatch written the same night

Charles wrote to the pope from his own camp at Benevento the night of
the battle, before he even knew for certain whether Manfred had died,
survived, or fled: a report of overwhelming victory, prisoners taken,
and a promise that once "the root of the evil" was fully cleared from
Sicily, he would restore the kingdom to its proper vassalage under the
Church. It was, in its own way, as clear a statement as any of what the
whole campaign had actually been for. The papacy had spent two
generations trying to remove one imperial dynasty from southern Italy by
every means short of outright war; Charles of Anjou, financed, invested
and legitimized entirely by Rome, had just finished the job those two
generations of popes could not complete on their own.
