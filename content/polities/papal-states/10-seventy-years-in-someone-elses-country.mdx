Pope Clement V, elected in 1305, never set foot in Rome at all during
his own pontificate. The reasons had less to do with Rome's own
condition, though it was genuinely poor and turbulent at the time, than
with the pressure Clement faced from Philip the Fair of France - pressure
that included a demand for a posthumous heresy trial of Clement's own
predecessor, Boniface VIII, and the mass arrest of the Knights Templar
in October 1307, an affair the papacy could not simply ignore or
outrun.

## A relocation chosen for what it was not

Facing the opening of a general council that would need to settle the
Templar affair, and unwilling to leave Philip in uncontested control of
the political situation while he traveled to Italy, Clement moved his
court to Avignon in 1308, entering the city itself in March 1309. The
location was chosen deliberately: close enough to France for easy
communication, but not itself French territory, since Avignon sat within
the Comtat-Venaissin, already a possession of the Holy See, and its
nominal overlords - the Angevin princes of Naples - were themselves
papal vassals with every reason to defer to Rome rather than press their
own claim. It offered, on paper, everything Rome currently could not: a
peaceful refuge with genuine guarantees of independence.

## A stay that kept not becoming permanent

Nothing about Clement's own arrangements suggested he expected to stay.
He lived simply in a Dominican convent, brought only the necessary
administrative registers from Italy, and left the greater part of the
papal treasure behind at the church of St. Francis in Assisi. But each
of his successors found their own reasons not to leave: further legal
entanglements, the ongoing war between France and England, and simply
the accumulated inertia of an administration that had, by degrees, built
itself a permanent home in a city that was never supposed to be one.

## Two attempts, one that failed and one that mostly succeeded

Urban V judged conditions favorable enough in the 1360s to attempt an
actual return to Rome, only to be driven back to Avignon by hostility
from his own Roman subjects - proof that the city's own instability, not
merely French pressure, had helped keep the papacy away for as long as it
had. It was Gregory XI who finally judged the risk worth taking again,
over the protests of Roman factions who plotted against the foreign
clergy filling his own court and, by some accounts, against Gregory's
own life. He returned to Rome and died there in 1378 - ending the
Avignon papacy after nearly seventy years, but not the crisis it had
helped store up. Within months of his death, a disputed election would
split Latin Christendom between rival popes for another four decades,
a schism whose roots reached directly back to the same relocation that
had, for two generations, put the bishop of Rome anywhere but Rome
itself.
