The century after Charlemagne's coronation left the papacy without an
emperor strong enough to protect it and without any Roman institution
capable of replacing that protection. Real power passed instead to
whichever noble family controlled the city's fortified households, and
for the better part of two generations that family was headed by two
women: Theodora, wife of the Roman aristocrat Theophylact, and her
daughter Marozia.

## A pope crowned by his patroness

Sergius III owed his election in 904 to Theodora's own faction, and
his seven-year pontificate was remembered less for its acts of
government than for the violence surrounding his rise - his
predecessors Leo V and the antipope Christopher were left to perish or
be murdered in confinement, and near-contemporary writers accused
Sergius of an intrigue with Marozia, Theodora's daughter, that later
chroniclers treated as scandal rather than fact. Whatever the truth of
it, the pattern was set: the papal chair now depended on the favor of
one Roman household rather than on any wider consensus, ecclesiastical
or imperial.

## A pope imprisoned by his own patroness's daughter

Marozia inherited her mother's influence and, in time, surpassed it.
Pope John X, who owed his own elevation to Theodora's patronage, spent
years maneuvering between Marozia's growing faction and an alliance
with King Hugh of Provence, whom he hoped could restore a strong
protector to Rome the way Charlemagne once had. Marozia answered by
marrying Hugh's own rival, the Margrave of Tuscany, and turning the
combined weight of both factions against the Pope. In 928, during a
Roman revolt she was widely believed to have engineered, John's
brother and chief military support was cut down in the Lateran itself,
and the Pope was seized and thrown into the Castel Sant'Angelo, where
he died the following year - by strangling or starvation, contemporary
accounts disagree, though none doubted Marozia's hand in it.

## A crown offered to a stepson with an axe to grind

Marozia's own power peaked with her third marriage, to Hugh of
Provence himself, now King of Italy - a match that briefly promised to
reunite Rome with a real royal protector, and perhaps even restore the
imperial crown to her new husband. It ended instead in farce and
revolution. Hugh, established in the fortress of Sant'Angelo, treated
Marozia's teenage son Alberic with open contempt, forcing him to serve
as his own page; when the boy spilled water while pouring a basin for
the King and received a blow across the face for it, he stormed out
and roused the Romans against the "Burgundian" outsider ruling them
through a woman. The city rose. Hugh escaped Sant'Angelo by rope in
the night and fled to Lombardy, abandoning his bride, his crown, and
the imperial ambitions the marriage had carried with it. The Romans
elected Alberic their own prince - and his first act was to send his
mother to prison for the rest of her life.
