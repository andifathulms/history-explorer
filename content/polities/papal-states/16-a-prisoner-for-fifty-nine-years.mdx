Pius IX never accepted the loss of Rome as final. He refused to
recognize the Kingdom of Italy's own authority over the city, refused to
leave the Vatican in protest, and styled himself, for the rest of his
reign, a prisoner within it - a posture every one of his successors
maintained for the following six decades, refusing to set foot outside
Vatican territory or to acknowledge the Italian state that had taken the
papacy's own former territory by force.

## A question Italy's own leaders had already tried to answer

Camillo Cavour, Italy's own architect of unification, had anticipated
the difficulty years before Rome actually fell, framing the relationship
he hoped to establish as "Libera Chiesa in libero Stato" - a free Church
in a free State, each independent of the other's own authority. Cavour
did not live to see whether the formula could actually work in practice;
he died in 1861, nine years before Italian troops entered Rome at all.
What his own successors inherited instead was six decades of open
hostility between the Italian state and a papacy that considered itself
robbed of sovereign territory rather than merely relieved of a political
burden.

## A treaty that finally settled what a battle could not

Resolution came only in 1929, under circumstances neither Cavour nor
Pius IX could have anticipated. Benito Mussolini's fascist government,
seeking the legitimacy that reconciliation with the Church could offer
his own regime, negotiated the Lateran Treaty, signed 11 February 1929
and ratified that June - ending fifty-nine years of open conflict between
Italian state and papacy by ceding the papacy sovereign authority over a
small, newly defined Vatican City, a fraction of a square mile in the
heart of Rome itself, in exchange for the papacy's own formal
recognition of the Kingdom of Italy and its capital.

## A day the country that won chose to forget

What is notable, looking back, is how little either side ultimately
wished to commemorate the conquest that had made the treaty necessary in
the first place. Rome's capture was declared a national holiday in Italy
in 1895, then quietly abolished in 1930 - the year after the Lateran
Treaty itself, once the political reconciliation the holiday's own
anniversary might have complicated was finally secured. A conquest that
had taken five hours and cost sixty-nine lives, and that had opened six
decades of open constitutional crisis between a modern Italian state and
the oldest continuously governed territory in Europe, ended not
commemorated by the victors but folded quietly into an arrangement that
suited both sides better left unremembered.
