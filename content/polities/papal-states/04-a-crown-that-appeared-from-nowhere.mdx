Pepin's donation had made the papacy a sovereign of territory. It took
another two generations, and a considerably more ambitious Frankish
king, to make it something more: the power that could bestow the title
of Roman emperor itself, extinct in the West for over three centuries.

## A ceremony arranged to look accidental

Charlemagne had, by the account of his own biographer Einhard, already
long consented to the idea of his own elevation to the imperial throne
before his visit to Rome at Christmas 800 - the timing, not the
substance, was what remained to be settled. Pope Leo III staged the
actual event to look like anything but a negotiated transaction. As
Charlemagne rose from prayer before the high altar of St. Peter's on
Christmas Day, Leo placed a golden crown on his head without warning,
and the assembled crowd, primed in advance to understand the signal,
answered with an ancient acclamation revived for the occasion: "Life
and victory to Charles the Most pious Augustus, crowned by God, great
and peace-giving Emperor of the Romans."

## A pope who made himself the one who mattered

The staging was deliberate, and its logic ran in the pope's own favor
rather than the new emperor's. By appearing to act on sudden, divine
inspiration rather than prior arrangement, Leo cast himself as the
active party in the transaction - the one who conferred the office -
rather than as a mere officiant crowning a candidate whose own claim
needed no papal confirmation at all. Nothing about the ceremony's actual
content was simple or unimposing; what made it appear so was precisely
the point.

## A title with no legitimate owner to answer to

Whether Charlemagne had any legal right to the title he received was, at
the time, a genuinely open question - the Eastern Roman Empire still
existed, ruled from Constantinople, with its own uninterrupted claim to
the same Roman inheritance. The papacy's own answer was that the
eastern throne, then held by a woman, Empress Irene, could be treated as
vacant for these purposes. It was a convenient argument rather than a
settled one, and it worked mainly because there was no power left in the
West capable of contesting it. A pope who had needed a foreign king's
army to become a sovereign at all had, within two generations, made
himself the arbiter of who could call himself Rome's own emperor.
