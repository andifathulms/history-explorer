---
title: A pope decides who is king

By 751, the Frankish monarchy had been a fiction for at least a
generation. Childeric III held the title of king, but every real power
of government belonged to Pepin, mayor of the palace and heir to a line
of mayors - his father Charles Martel and grandfather Pepin of Landen -
who had run the kingdom in fact while a series of increasingly powerless
Merovingians held it in name. Childeric himself is described in the
sources of the age as a man "of no sense," "useless and good for
nothing" - a king whose only qualification for the office was an
accident of birth into an exhausted royal line.

## A revolution that wanted someone else's blessing

Pepin and the Frankish nobility judged that the arrangement had become
untenable, but they were cautious enough to want more than their own
judgment behind deposing a reigning king - a precedent, they understood,
that could just as easily be turned against whoever replaced him.
Rather than act unilaterally, they sent an embassy to Rome, led by
Burchard, Bishop of Würzburg, and Pepin's own chaplain Fulrad, to put a
direct question to Pope Zachary: was it right that a man bearing the
name of king should hold no actual royal power, while another held the
power without the name?

## An answer that made a mayor into a king

Zachary's ruling was unambiguous. It was better, he judged, "that he
should be and should be called king who had the power of a king rather
than the one who had the name without the substance" - and he ordered,
by his own apostolic authority, that Pepin be made king and anointed by
Boniface. The Frankish nobility acted on the ruling without delay.
Pepin was raised on a shield and acclaimed king by his own soldiers, then
anointed in a solemn ceremony at Soissons in 752. Childeric was tonsured
- a mark of permanent exclusion from kingship - and confined to a
monastery for the rest of his life, along with his wife and son.

## A debt a king would not forget

Zachary's ruling gave Pepin's own throne a legitimacy no mere acclamation
by Frankish nobles could have supplied on its own - the sanction of the
one authority in Latin Christendom capable of pronouncing on whether a
king's deposition served God's own order rather than mere ambition.
Pepin did not treat the favor as a one-time transaction. When Zachary's
own successor found himself facing a Lombard army at the gates of Rome
a few years later, it was to Pepin, and to the debt Zachary's ruling had
created, that the papacy turned.
