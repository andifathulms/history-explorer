By 1388 the king had settled on a plan every reasonable adviser at his
own court considered ruinous: an invasion of Ming territory in
Liao-tung, launched while Japanese raiders were ravaging the south
and the kingdom's own finances lay in disarray. Gen. Yi T'ă-jo, named
lieutenant-general of the expedition, argued against it point by point —
a small country should not attack a great one, summer rains would
flood the campaign and spoil the army's bows, and stripping troops from
the south would invite disaster there. The king's only answer was a
threat: the next man who spoke against the war would lose his head.

## A river crossed, and a line drawn

Gen. Yi obeyed anyway, leading nearly forty thousand troops north and
across the Yalu to Wi-ha Island, where he made camp rather than press
further into Chinese territory. Desertions began almost immediately.
Rumors reached the court that Yi himself had fled, but when a second
general was sent to check, he found Yi still at his post, weeping openly
over what he called the certain destruction of Koryŏ if the campaign
continued.

## A question put to the soldiers, not the king

Rather than appeal again to a king who had already threatened his life
for objecting, Yi turned to the army itself. He and his fellow general
laid the choice before the assembled troops directly: stay and be swept
away by the rising floods that summer had brought, or turn back and
remove the commander-in-chief who had forced this war on them. The
soldiers answered with open acclaim. Mounted on a white horse, bow in
hand, Yi watched his own army turn south behind him — not toward the
Ming border he had been ordered to cross, but toward the capital whose
king had never expected his own instrument of war to become the
instrument of his undoing.

## A capital taken, a throne made and unmade

Yi's return to Song-do ended the campaign and effectively ended the
king's own authority in the same stroke. Within months the king was
banished and a new one installed under Yi's own supervision, the first
in a rapid sequence of enthronements and depositions that left no doubt
about where real power in Koryŏ now rested — with a general who still,
for the moment, stopped short of taking the throne himself.
