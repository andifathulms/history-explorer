title: A dam broken in a wooded valley

Kitan power tested Koryŏ repeatedly in the early eleventh century. In
1010 an invading army burned the capital's palaces and common houses
outright while the king fled south, at one point escaping an
assassination plot arranged by a governor who had decided to sell him
out. The kingdom survived only through a mix of diplomatic bluff — a
letter claiming the king had merely gone on a provincial tour rather
than fled — and a string of northern victories that cut down thousands
of the retreating Kitan army as it recrossed the Yalu.

## A general who read the ground

The peace that followed did not last. Kitan armies returned in 1016,
1017 and again in 1018, this time a hundred thousand strong under Gen.
So Son-ryŭng. Koryŏ met them with two hundred thousand men under Gen.
Kang Kam-ch'an, a general the source describes as small, plain and
carelessly dressed, but shrewd enough to turn terrain into a weapon: he
had a dam built across a wooded valley, let the water build up behind
it, drew the Kitan army into the valley below, and then had the dam
torn open. The flood swept away hundreds of the enemy outright and
threw the rest into a panic that Koryŏ's own numbers finished.

## A retreat that became a rout

Gen. So tried again the following year, feinting a withdrawal while
secretly pushing toward the capital, but found every point defended and
was forced back to Yŭng-byŭn and then to a final stand at Kwiju. There
a providential south wind turned the battle decisively: it drove dust
into Kitan faces and lent force to Koryŏ arrows, and the Kitan line
broke and fled with Koryŏ troops in full pursuit across the Sŏk-ch'ŭn
brook and the fields beyond, littering the ground with Kitan dead. Only
a few thousand exhausted survivors, Gen. So among them, made it back
across the Yalu — what the source calls the worst defeat Kitan ever
suffered at Koryŏ's hands.

## A hero's welcome, and a name changed to mark it

Kang Kam-ch'an returned to Song-do laden with captured banners and
plunder, and the king met him in person, presented him eight golden
flowers with his own hands, and renamed the meeting place "Place of
Lofty Righteousness" in his honor. Kang received six honorary titles
and the revenue of three hundred houses when he retired the following
year — remembered ever after, despite his unassuming appearance, as the
"Pillar of Koryŏ."
