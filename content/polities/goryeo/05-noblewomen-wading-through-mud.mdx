The first Mongol invasion, in 1231, ended the way most Mongol campaigns
of the era did: a siege the defenders could not break, humiliating
tribute demands, and a Mongol residency planted at Song-do to make sure
the terms held. One provincial fortress commander, Pak Sŏ at Kuju, held
out against every siege engine and forged surrender letter the Mongols
threw at him, drawing praise even from the enemy general who had failed
to take his walls — but his stand could not undo the settlement the
court itself had already accepted.

## A dictator's gamble

Neither the king nor his officials believed the trouble was over, and
the military dictator Ch'oe U moved first: over the objections of
courtiers he silenced by killing a few of them outright, he loaded his
own household onto government carts and relocated to Kang-wha Island,
threatening death for anyone who spoke against the removal. The king
followed within the year. The crossing, made in the depth of the rainy
season, left the royal procession mired axle-deep, noblewomen wading
barefoot through mud with bundles balanced on their heads, the whole
column's wailing audible for miles.

## An island the Mongols could never take

The retreat to Kang-wha bought Koryŏ's court nearly four decades of
physical safety at the price of everything beyond the strait itself.
Mongol armies returned again and again, ravaging the mainland,
demanding the king's own person at their court, and razing what
remained of Song-do's palaces outright — but a narrow channel of water
was enough to stop them cold, since, as the source notes flatly, the
Mongols were no seamen. Popular insurrections rose against Mongol
occupation across the mainland even as the court itself sat safely out
of reach, and successive Mongol khans spent years alternating between
diplomacy and threat in an effort to draw the king back onto solid
ground they could actually hold.

## A peace bought by returning to the mainland

The arrangement could not last forever, and the price of ending it was
the very thing Ch'oe U had refused to give up: the king's own return to
an exposed capital, under terms that left Koryŏ a Mongol son-in-law
state for the rest of the century, its princes married to Mongol
princesses and its own military strength bent, before long, toward
campaigns the Mongols wanted fought rather than ones Koryŏ chose for
itself.
