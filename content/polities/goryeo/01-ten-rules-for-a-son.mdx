Wang-gön did not seek the throne he ended up on. As Kung-ye's cruelty
toward his own family turned the army and the officials against him,
those same officials carried Wang-gön to the palace by force over his
own objections and proclaimed him king in Kung-ye's place — a general
who had spent years deliberately requesting distant postings to avoid
exactly the kind of prominence that made a ruler's favorite a target.
The following year he moved the permanent capital to Song-do, his own
home city, and began the work of turning a warlord's territory into an
administered kingdom.

## Two rivals absorbed, one throne secured

Wang-gön inherited a peninsula still split three ways. Silla's own last
king abdicated to him without a fight in 935; Later Baekje's founder,
Kyŏn Hwŏn, deposed by his own son, defected to Wang-gön's side and rode
in his army the following year against the kingdom he had built,
which fell within months. By 936 Wang-gön held, for the first time
since Silla's own brief unification centuries before, a peninsula
without a serious rival on it.

## Ten rules against the mistakes of one man's reign

Sensing his own end approaching in 942, Wang-gön set down ten rules for
his heir: honor Buddhism but build no more monasteries; let a worthy
younger son take the throne if the eldest proves unfit; never trust
Kitan; honor P'yŭng-yang as the old capital; keep the army's loyalty;
and, above all, stay always ready for emergencies. He died the same
year having named his own instincts, more than any law, as the thing he
most wanted his successors to keep.
