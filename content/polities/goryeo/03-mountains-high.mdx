The king who ruled Koryŏ through the 1160s favored Buddhist monasteries
over his own government and civil officials over the military men who
guarded him, and the resentment that built up among the generals broke
in a single afternoon in 1170.

## A slap that started a massacre

At a royal outing to a monastery, a civil official of comparatively
low rank struck a military officer in the king's own presence. The
insult was smoothed over in the moment, but once the royal party had
dispersed, the assembled generals turned their palace guards loose on
the two most senior civil officials present, killing them on the spot
- one of them dragged out from behind the king's own bed to die. What
followed was not a targeted purge but a general slaughter: officials
were identified by their civil dress and cut down in the streets,
proclamations called for killing anyone found in civil garments, and
by the end of it fifty more officials and twenty eunuchs had been
executed, their heads set on pikes. The chronicler's own phrase for
the result was that the dead lay in piles "mountains high."

## A king exiled, a brother enthroned

General Chŏng Chung-bu emerged as the coup's leading figure. He
demolished the houses of the dead and disgraced civil officials
outright - the origin, the source notes, of the later custom of
tearing down the home of anyone convicted of a serious crime against
the crown - and concluded that the king himself was beyond saving. He
had the king exiled to an island in Kyŏngsang province, exiled the
crown prince to a separate island, and put the king's own brother on
the throne in 1171. Every office of state was filled with military
men from that point on; a civil official who wished to keep any
standing at all married his own daughter into a general's family and
made a show of contentment with the new order.

## A pond, a kettle, and a body recovered by a swimming monk

The old order's last chance died with the exiled king himself. When
word reached the capital that he had traveled as far as Kyŏngju,
Chŏng sent General Yi Eui-mun to eliminate him for good. After killing
some two hundred of the ex-king's remaining guard, Yi took the former
monarch to the edge of a monastery pond and crushed his ribs in his
own arms, killing him instantly; the body was folded into two kettles
placed mouth to mouth and sunk in the pond. Yi returned to the capital
loaded with honors for it. A monk who was a skilled swimmer later
recovered the body and gave it proper burial - the only mercy the
whole episode records.
