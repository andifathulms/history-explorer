The generation after the 1170 coup passed through a rapid succession
of military strongmen, each one eventually overthrown by the next, until
General Ch'oe Ch'ung-hŏn seized power in 1196 and founded a family
dictatorship that would outlast four kings.

## A favorite too greedy to survive

By the 1190s power had settled on General Yi Eui-mun, who used his
position as court favorite to usurp every leading office and to serve
as the king's own procurer, forcibly bringing young women to the
palace for the monarch's use. Ch'oe Ch'ung-hŏn judged this
intolerable, and with his brother at his side surrounded the palace,
killed Yi Eui-mun and his circle outright, and drove out the king's
own illegitimate sons who had taken refuge as monks. Two years later
Ch'oe deposed the old king himself, exiling the crown prince and his
wife to Kanghwa Island with no warning at all - the couple bundled
onto horses in a cold rainstorm and hurried away the same day - and
put the deposed king's brother on the throne instead.

## A brother stopped at the palace gate

Ch'oe's own reputation for personal integrity survived an attempt by
his brother to abuse the family's new power. The brother forced the
reigning king to take his own daughter as queen, requiring the
banishment of a wife the king was genuinely attached to. When Ch'oe
learned of it, he confronted his brother privately and ordered him to
abandon the scheme, since their family was not high-born enough to
supply a queen. His brother agreed, then reversed himself the next
day and struck their own mother to the floor when she protested. Ch'oe
gathered an armed guard and rode to the palace gate himself; when his
niece arrived in her wedding chair to be installed as queen, he
physically barred her entry. A fight broke out between the two
brothers' retainers, Ch'oe's men won it, and he pursued his own
brother down and killed him. The rightful queen was restored to her
place the same day.

## A slave revolt drowned in the river

The reign that followed was six years of continuous unrest. Enslaved
people organized at a monastery outside the capital, arguing openly
that high station was not a decree of heaven and that a general as
low-born as Ch'oe himself proved anyone could rise - and planned to
seize and burn the ownership deeds that bound them. Ch'oe's agents
betrayed the plot before it could be carried out; a hundred of the
conspirators were captured, had stones tied around their necks, and
were drowned in the river. Provincial uprisings broke out across the
south regardless, in some places killing thousands who refused to
join them, and the unrest continued past the old king's death in 1205
into the reign of his successor - the family dictatorship Ch'oe had
founded now the one fixed point in a kingdom that could agree on
almost nothing else.
