Kongyang, the seventh descendant of a king seventeen generations back,
was raised to the throne in 1389 at Gen. Yi's own choosing — a
deliberate move to put a genuine member of the royal line back in
nominal power after a run of kings the Chinese emperor himself had
refused to formally recognize. It changed little in practice. The real
argument playing out at court was no longer about who sat on the
throne, but whether the Wang dynasty would be allowed to continue in
any form at all, and Koryŏ's last great loyalist, Chŏng Mong-ju, was
the one obstacle standing in the way of settling that question by
force.

## A bridge that still remembers

Yi's own son, later known by his title T'ă-jong, recognized that nothing
could move forward while Chŏng Mong-ju lived, and arranged to have him
struck down with stones on Sŏn-juk bridge as he returned home from a
banquet where he had gone specifically to judge, by his host's face,
whether the rumors of danger were true. Tradition holds that a
dark stain remains on the bridge's central stone to this day, turning a
deeper red whenever rain falls — a mute accusation, the source says,
against the men who killed him.

## A king with nothing left to hold

With Chŏng Mong-ju gone, resistance to Yi collapsed outright. His own
son had the murdered man's friends banished, confiscated his property,
and silenced what remained of the opposition; even the king's own sons,
who had sided against Yi, were sent into exile. Officials pressed
Kongyang into swearing a formal oath of eternal friendship with the man
who had just orphaned his own court of allies, an arrangement so
lopsided that the king performed it by proxy rather than in person
rather than face Yi directly.

## A throne given up rather than taken

Stripped of his sons, his ministers and his own freedom of action,
Kongyang watched the pressure to abdicate build past the point where
resistance served any purpose. He handed the royal seals to Gen. Yi
T'ă-jo without a final confrontation, retired first to Wŏn-ju and then
to more distant exile, and died three years later. A dynasty that had
begun with officials forcing an unwilling general onto the throne in
918 ended in 1392 the same way it started — with the court's own
choice, made for a ruler who did not have to fight for it.
