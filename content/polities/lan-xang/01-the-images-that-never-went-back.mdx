Jai Jett'a came to power by leaving a throne rather than seizing one.
Crowned Maharaja of Chiengmai shortly after the death of that kingdom's
own king in 1547, he chose within two years to abandon it and return to
Luang Prabang to fight his younger brother, who had claimed their father's
Lao throne on his sudden death. He took with him some of Chiengmai's most
sacred possessions: the Emerald Buddha, the crystal Buddha of Lamp'un, and
other images that had been kept in that city for generations. Only one of
them - a lesser image called the P'rasingh - was ever returned. Chiengmai's
own nobles, finding their throne effectively vacated, simply summoned a
rival prince to fill it.

## A new capital on the river

Some time in the years that followed, Jai Jett'a moved his own capital
from Luang Prabang downriver to Wiengchan, a site he named Sri
Satanakonahut - what later ages would know as Vientiane. The Emerald
Buddha moved with him, and would stay in his new capital for more than
two centuries.

## A princess sent, and a princess sent back

The new king's ambitions extended into marriage diplomacy with Siam
itself. He asked King Chakrap'at of Ayut'ia for the hand of Princess T'ep
Krasatri, daughter of the warrior queen Suriyot'ai; when the princess fell
ill before her journey, the Siamese king sent a different daughter in her
place. Jai Jett'a was, in the words of the chronicler who later recorded
it, a man whose "taste in collecting Princesses seems to have been equal
to his discrimination in amassing other people's images of Buddha" - he
sent the substitute back at once, with a blunt message that he would
accept T'ep Krasatri and no one else. In 1564 the princess finally set out
for Wiengchan, only to be intercepted en route by a Burmese force acting
on intelligence from a rival Siamese governor and carried off to Burma
instead - a diversion that left the marriage forever unconsummated, and
left Jai Jett'a and that governor enemies from that day forward.
