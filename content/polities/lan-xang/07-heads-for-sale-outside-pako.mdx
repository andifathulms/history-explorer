Two centuries after Jai Jett'a's reign, a chain of events set off by a
Siamese campaign against Champasak brought a reckoning to Wiengchan
itself. A Wiengchan noble named P'ra Woh, who had rebelled against Prince
Bun San, ruler of Wiengchan, fled south to Champasak territory and
submitted formally to the victorious Siamese army when it arrived there.
As soon as the Siamese withdrew, Bun San's forces caught P'ra Woh and cut
off his head. King Taksin of Siam took the killing as an act of war
against himself, and raised an army of twenty thousand men to invade
Wiengchan in response, joined by the rival Prince of Luang Prabang, who
saw in Siam's campaign a chance to settle old scores of his own.

## A siege months in the taking

Wiengchan held out for several months. During the siege of the nearby
town of P'ak'o, the besieging Siamese sent women out to sell boatloads of
severed heads outside the walls - a calculated act of terror the source
that records it compares, without much exaggeration, to Burmese methods
in their own invasions of the kingdom two centuries before. When
Wiengchan finally fell, the Siamese stripped it of everything of value
they could carry.

## The Buddha that finally left for good

Among the plunder taken from the captured capital was the Emerald Buddha
itself - the same image Jai Jett'a had carried off from Chiengmai more
than two hundred years earlier, and which had stood in Wiengchan ever
since. It went to Bangkok, where it remains. From 1779 until the French
protectorate arrived in 1893, both Luang Prabang and Wiengchan stood as
dependencies of Siam - a kingdom that had spent two centuries acquiring
sacred images and vassal thrones by turns finally reduced, in a single
campaign, to keeping neither.
