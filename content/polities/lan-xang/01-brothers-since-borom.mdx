Fa Ngum was born in 1316 the son of a Lao prince exiled for a fault
the chronicles remember only as a fit of temper - Phi Fa, "Spirit of
Heaven," a name his own father gave him in bitter irony after Phi
Fa's taste for women reached into the royal harem itself and got him
driven out before he ever ruled. Father and infant son found refuge
at the Khmer court of Angkor, where a Cambodian king raised the boy,
married him to his own daughter at sixteen, and, at some point between
1340 and 1350, gave him an army to go reclaim the kingdom of his
grandfathers.

## A conquest paid for in kinship

The Lao chronicle Nithan Khun Borom describes Fa Ngum's advance up the
Mekong through Bassac, Khammuan and Tran-ninh, negotiating a border
with Dai Viet along the way, before descending on Muang Chawa - the
future Luang Prabang - where he was proclaimed king. What made the
conquest possible, the same chronicle insists, was less force than a
shared claim of descent every Tai-speaking ruler in his path
recognized: the Tran-ninh envoys called him brother through their
common ancestor Khun Borom, a Tai chief of the Sip Song Phan Na made
the same appeal rather than fight him, and even the king of Ayutthaya,
moving to check Fa Ngum's advance onto the Khorat plateau, chose to
offer territory and a royal marriage instead of battle, reminding him
they were "brothers since Borom." Only at Wiangchan did Fa Ngum resort
to force outright - and even there by trick rather than storm, luring
the defenders out to gather a feigned retreat's abandoned gold and
silver arrows before turning on them in the open.

## A coronation, and a Buddha that gave the capital its name

Fa Ngum's own solemn coronation, which the sources place in 1353,
marks the founding of the kingdom that would come to be called Lan
Chang (Lan Xang). It joined Luang Prabang and Wiangchan under one
crown for the first time, at the expense of a Khmer garrison presence
on the upper Mekong that had held the territory since Jayavarman VII's
own reign a century and a half before. Fa Ngum did not forget the
court that had raised him: shortly after his accession he sent his old
religious tutor, Maha Pasaman, back to Angkor with a mission of monks
and artisans, who returned with sacred texts and a revered image, the
Phra Bang - the statue that would eventually give Luang Prabang its
own name, and the clearest sign that the kingdom Fa Ngum built on the
upper Mekong owed its script, its Buddhism and its court culture to
the same Khmer world his own family had once fled to as exiles.
