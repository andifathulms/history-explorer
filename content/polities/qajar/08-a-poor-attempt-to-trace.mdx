The dynasty was deposed in 1925 by the parliament it had granted in 1906 and
shelled in 1908, which is a tidy enough ending. What outlived it is the
parliament, the argument the parliament was about, and — in English at least —
one book.

## The professor

Edward Browne was the Cambridge professor of Persian who had spent his career on
mystical poetry and the biographical dictionaries. In 1910 he published *The
Persian Revolution of 1905-1909*, and he tells the reader in the first paragraph
of the preface exactly what kind of book it is.

He declines to call it a history, in view of the difficulty of impartially
criticising contemporary events, though he says it is intended for one. He then
explains why he wrote it: *Not willingly or without regret have I forsaken for a
while the pleasant paths of Persian literature to enter into the arid deserts of
international politics. But the call was imperious and the summons urgent to
neglect nothing of that little which lay in my power in order to arouse in the
hearts of my countrymen some sympathy for a people who have, in my opinion,
hitherto received less than they deserve.*

The dedication is in verse and is dedicated to everyone who had aided Persia in
her hour of need, whether by tongue or pen or sword, and asks that they approve
his poor attempt to trace this final effort of an ancient race to burst its
bondage and rise to life a nation once again.

That is not the preface of a disinterested account, and Browne does not pretend
it is. He was campaigning — writing to newspapers, raising money, lobbying the
Foreign Office against the Anglo-Russian Agreement of 1907 that had carved Iran
into spheres over the heads of the people who lived there. His chapter on that
agreement is titled *as seen through Persian eyes*, which was not the usual way
of putting it in London.

## Why that matters to the record

Because it is the book. For most of a century, what an English-speaking reader
knew about the Constitutional Revolution came from a partisan of one side, who
had the documents, read the language, corresponded with the participants, and
said on page one that he had a case to make.

There are worse arrangements. Browne is candid where a concealed partisan would
not be — he gives his own analogy for the limits of argument, that two travellers
can usefully discuss the best road to Penzance but not if one of them wants to
go to John o'Groats — and he prints the constitutional documents in full as
appendices so a reader can check him. The dishonest version of this book would
have been written in the same tone with the preface removed.

## What the revolution left

The constitution of 1906 was never repealed. Reza Shah ruled under it and
ignored it; his son ruled under it and ignored it; and it was still the formal
law of Iran until 1979, when it was replaced by an argument the clerical
establishment had been developing since the Safavids imported it — that in the
imam's absence authority belongs to the qualified scholars and not to a king.

Three things came out of the Qajar century and all three are still running. A
parliament that has been suspended, packed, shelled and reconstituted and has
never quite gone away. A clerical establishment with its own money that
discovered in 1891, over a tobacco monopoly, that it could overturn an act of
state in eight weeks. And a settled national conviction that the country's
resources are sold too cheaply to foreigners by governments that need cash this
year.

The dynasty is remembered for losing the Caucasus and granting the concessions.
What it actually bequeathed was the three parties to every Iranian political
argument since.
