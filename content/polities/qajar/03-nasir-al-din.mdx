title: Forty-eight years
drafted_from: amanat-2017

Nasir al-Din Shah ruled from 1848 to 1896, which is the longest reign in this
region's record and one of the longest anywhere in the corpus. He came to the
throne at sixteen with a chief minister, Amir Kabir, who in three years did more
to reform the Iranian state than anyone had since Abbas I: reorganised the
revenue, cut court expenditure, disciplined the provincial governors, founded
the Dar al-Funun — the polytechnic that trained the country's first
European-educated officer and administrative class — and suppressed the Babi
movement.

The shah had him dismissed at the urging of the court, whose incomes he had cut,
and then murdered in a bath-house at Kashan in 1852. Nasir al-Din is reported to
have regretted it for the rest of his life, which was another forty-four years.

The reign that followed is genuinely difficult to characterise and Amanat's
biography of him is largely an argument against the two easy versions — the
frivolous despot of the nationalist historiography and the helpless victim of
the imperial powers. The state acquired a telegraph network, a postal service, a
census, newspapers, photography (the shah was a serious amateur photographer and
the royal albums are a major visual record), a European-style ministry structure
on paper, and three royal tours of Europe that produced a travel diary published
in Persian and in translation.

What it did not acquire was revenue. The land tax was farmed to governors who
bid for their provinces and recouped the bid; the customs were farmed; there was
no central bank, no budget in any useful sense, and no way to pay for the army
the wars with Russia had shown was needed. So the government sold things
instead, and the fourth chapter is about that.

He was shot in 1896 at the shrine of Shah Abd al-Azim, a few days before the
celebration of his fiftieth year, by a follower of the reformist agitator
Jamal al-Din al-Afghani. The assassin said at his trial that he had killed him
because the country was being sold.
