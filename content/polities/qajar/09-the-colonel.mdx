The Persian Cossack Brigade was raised in 1879, on Russian advice, with Russian
officers, as a personal guard for the shah and a model unit. It was paid,
permanent and centrally commanded — the only force in Iran that reliably was —
and by the end of the First World War, with the country occupied and the
government without revenue, it was the only military organisation in the country
that functioned.

<Figure id="cossack-brigade" />

In February 1921 its Iranian commander, Reza Khan, marched it on Tehran together
with a journalist named Sayyid Zia and took the government. Reza Khan became
minister of war, then prime minister, put down the separatist republics in Gilan
and Khurasan, and in 1925 had the majles depose the absent Ahmad Shah and vote
him the throne. He was crowned the following year and took the dynastic name
Pahlavi.

<Figure id="reza-khan" />

It was an internal usurpation of exactly the kind that had ended the Ayyubids in
1250, and the recurrence is not a coincidence.

The same failure runs from Samarra in the ninth century to Tehran in the
twentieth. A ruler who
cannot raise a loyal army from his own society builds or hires a professional
one that owes him personally; the professional force becomes the only effective
power in the state; and eventually it makes a ruler it prefers. Samarra in the
860s. The Ghaznavids out of the Samanid guard in 962. The Bahriyya in 1250. The
Safavid ghulams, who did not quite. The Ottoman janissaries, repeatedly.

Tehran in 1921 is the last performance of it, and the details have moved with
the century: the force was raised by a foreign power on a foreign model,
its officers had been Russian until the revolution recalled them, and the man
who used it did not claim descent from anybody. He had the parliament vote for
him.

The dynasty that ended had reassembled Iran in 1796 out of fifty years of
anarchy and handed on a state with fixed borders, a constitution, a national
assembly and an oil industry, having lost the Caucasus, most of Central Asia and
a good deal of its own sovereignty in the process. Both halves of that are true
and the second is the one Iranian historiography has mostly recorded.
