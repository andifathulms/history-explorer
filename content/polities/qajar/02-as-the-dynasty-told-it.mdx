In 1811 Fath Ali Shah gave the departing British envoy a manuscript history of
his own family. It was translated and printed in London twenty years later, and
it is the Qajar account of the Qajar conquest — which makes it the best evidence
there is for what the dynasty thought was to its credit.

It begins by explaining the Zands away. Muhammad Hasan Khan, the shah's
grandfather, had raised the standard of universal sway in Mazandaran while
discord and insurrection overspread Iran, had beaten both Azad Khan the Afghan
and Karim Khan Zand several times, and had marched into Fars and besieged Karim
Khan in Shiraz — at which point several Qajar nobles conspired against him,
practised open treachery, and dissolved the camp, so that he had to retire to
Mazandaran, where in 1758 he drank the sherbet of martyrdom at the hands of two
or three men of his own tribe. The occurrence of this event, says the court
historian, furnished to Karim Khan Zand the means of attaining royalty and became
the cause of his elevation; and the verse quoted is *they removed the diadem from
the head of one nation, and bound the royal jewels around another nation's
brows.* Karim Khan then urged on the charger of usurpation into Mazandaran and
afflicted Astarabad, the capital of the illustrious Qajars.

Thirty-six years later the account reaches Kerman, where Lutf Ali Khan, the last
Zand, had been taken in by the governor and the townspeople. The siege ran five
months. Famine appeared inside; nine thousand people came out of the town and it
did not relieve the rest. Then the sentence the manuscript presents as an
achievement: *his majesty issued forth this order, powerful as the decree of
Destiny: let the amirs and soldiers assault the fort and take it by storm, and
let the inhabitants perish by the keen-edged scimitar.* They stormed it on a
Friday in 1794, and the victors gave full loose to plunder, devastation and the
making of prisoners. There was such exceeding slaughter made of the people of
Kerman, the historian writes, that the blood flowed in streams.

Lutf Ali Khan was not among the dead. He had got out towards Bam, where men of
Sistan hamstrung his horse, threw him down, tied him and brought him into the
royal presence; his eyes were put out and he was sent to Tehran and killed there.
And the paragraph closes on the item the court evidently thought was the point of
the whole campaign: two jewels that had belonged to the royal family, named
Taj-i Mah and Darya-i Nur, had been in Lutf Ali Khan's possession and were now
transferred back to his majesty.

Tiflis came the following year. Erekle, the ruler of Georgia, was defeated, the
fortress was entered, and what fell into the soldiers' hands is itemised in the
language of a poem — immense riches, maidens beautiful as the moon,
jessamine-bosomed youths, rosy-cheeked damsels — with the number of prisoners
given as nearly fifteen thousand. His majesty remained nine days at Tiflis. The
army then went on to Ganja and reduced Shakki and Shirvan, and returned to
Tehran.

In 1795 he placed the crown on his own head and set out to subdue Khurasan.

None of that is an accusation made by an enemy. It is a dynasty's official
account of its founder, written by his nephew's court, presented by a reigning
shah to a foreign government as a description of who his family were. The
massacre at Kerman and the recovery of the Darya-i Nur are set down in the same
paragraph and in the same tone, because to the men who wrote it they were the
same kind of fact: the restoration of what had been taken from the Qajars, and
the punishment of the people who had sheltered the man who took it.

Nine days at Tiflis produced a debt that Iran was still paying in 1828, and the
chapter that records it does not mention the Russians at all.
