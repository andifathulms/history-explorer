In 1890 the shah sold a British company the exclusive right to buy, sell and
export every leaf of tobacco grown in Iran. Tobacco was a smallholder crop, a
merchant commodity and a universal habit, and the concession put every grower,
every dealer and every smoker under a foreign monopoly at once.

<Figure id="qalyan" />

The bazaar organised. Handbills appeared; the ulama preached; and in late 1891 a
ruling was circulated in the name of Mirza Hasan Shirazi, the senior Shi'i
jurist of the age, resident in Samarra in Ottoman Iraq, declaring the use of
tobacco to be war against the hidden imam for as long as the concession stood.

<Figure id="tobacco-fatwa" />

The country stopped smoking. It stopped so completely that the shah's own
household is reported to have refused to prepare his water-pipe. Within weeks the
government cancelled the concession and borrowed half a million pounds from a
British bank to pay the compensation, which was the country's first foreign debt.

The line of this runs back four hundred years. A dynasty declared Twelver
Shi'ism the religion of Iran in 1501, discovered it had no clergy to enforce the
decision with, and imported jurists from Jabal Amil and Iraq to build one. Those
jurists and their successors developed, over the following two centuries, a
doctrine that in the hidden imam's absence his authority devolves on the
qualified scholars rather than on a king.

The Tobacco Protest is that doctrine cashed. A ruling issued from outside the
country, by a man holding no office in it, overturned an act of state in eight
weeks, and it did so because the clerical establishment had endowments the shah
did not control, a communication network he could not interrupt, and an alliance
with the merchant class whose interests the concession had destroyed.

Three hundred and ninety years after a fourteen-year-old imported an institution
to make his authority lawful, that institution demonstrated it could unmake his
successor's.

Rulers had borrowed legitimacy from clergy before and been embarrassed by it —
the Mamluks kept a shadow caliph in Cairo for exactly this purpose and had to
manage him. This is the completed version of the problem, and it performs twice
more, in 1906 and in 1979.
