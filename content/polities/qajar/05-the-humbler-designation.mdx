Nasir al-Din was sixteen when he was crowned at midnight on 20 October 1848, and
Watson — who was at the British legation and knew a good many of the people
involved — credits him with one piece of judgement: he had brought with him from
Tabriz a man who could run the country.

Mirza Taqi Khan's father had been a cook, promoted to steward in the household of
Muhammad Shah's first minister. The son entered the service of the army's
commander-in-chief, went to St Petersburg with the mission sent after the murder
of the Russian envoy Griboyedov — his only visit to Europe — and came back a
*mirza*, a writer. He was made a khan, became vizier of the Azerbaijan army, and
was sent to represent Iran at the Erzurum conferences on the Ottoman frontier,
where an English observer thought him beyond comparison the most interesting man
among the commissioners of four countries, and where he watched the Ottoman
Tanzimat reforms working at first hand.

When the new shah offered him the premiership on the road to Tehran, he did an
arithmetic that turns out to be the whole story. Each of the two preceding Qajar
shahs had put his first chief vizier to death. So he declined the title of
*sadr-azam*, which a prime minister normally took, and asked instead for the
humbler designation of *amir-i nizam*, commander of the army — hoping, as Watson
puts it, to reconcile ambition with prudence.

He found every department of the government in confusion and set about it: the
army paid, the peculation stopped, the tax farms audited, a polytechnic founded,
a newspaper started, the provincial governors made to account for what they
collected. Three years.

Then the measures proved distasteful to enough people that the shah listened to
the complaints, and the minister was dismissed and ordered to retire in disgrace
to Kashan. His guards called him out of his room every day to make sure he had
not escaped. His wife — a princess, the shah's own sister — went out with him at
first, and when she saw that the inspection was a formality she stopped, and
contented herself instead with tasting every dish set in front of him.

So a man was found who would do it without her knowing. Hajji Ali Khan, whom
Watson calls a clever and worthless adventurer, had been brought into the shah's
service by Mirza Taqi himself and made chief of the *farrashes*; to demonstrate
his zeal for his new master he volunteered to be his benefactor's executioner.
When he arrived at Kashan the household was delighted, assuming that a man who
owed his career to their lord had been sent with good news.

On 9 January 1852 the guards called the minister out as usual. He came alone. He
was seized, gagged and dragged into a nearby house, thrown on the floor, stripped
and tied, and the veins of both his arms and both his legs were opened, and he
was left to take several hours to die. Watson says he bore it with a resignation
in keeping with the consistent greatness of his life. When the princess grew
alarmed at her husband's absence, Hajji Ali Khan told her he had gone to the bath
to prepare himself for a robe of honour which the shah had sent.

What followed was undone at once, and Watson lists it without pleasure: the
soldiers unpaid again for years, peculation rampant in every department, the
clergy recovering their influence, and titles distributed to the great more
lavishly than before. Then the protests from foreign governments arrived, and
then the remorse. The shah is said to have resolved to keep every anniversary of
the death as a day of fasting, and the dead man's two infant daughters were
betrothed to two of his sons.

Watson draws a moral from it that says as much about 1866 as about 1852. He cites
Buckle for the proposition that a people makes its own government and that no
government can force progress on an unsound people, and concludes that the
upright minister fell because his measures displeased too many, and was replaced
by a vizier whose character was better matched to the men he had to govern. It is
a Victorian's answer and it does a certain amount of work for a British legation
official explaining why Iranian reform failed. The facts he assembled underneath
it support a narrower and harder reading: that the office was survivable and the
title was not, that Mirza Taqi had understood this precisely enough to refuse the
title, and that it made no difference at all.
