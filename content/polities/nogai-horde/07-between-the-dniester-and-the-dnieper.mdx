The hordes that would not live under the Kalmyks went west, and two centuries
after Saraychiq burned a French officer found their descendants pasturing horses
on the Black Sea steppe under a governor appointed from the Crimea.

## The migration

Under Ayuka, who took the Kalmyk throne in 1672, the Great Horde Nogai were his
subjects except a small group camped near Astrakhan, and most of them left the
Yaik and the Volga altogether for the Kuban and the Kuma. When Ayuka died in 1724
and his people fell into confusion, the Yedisan and Yembulad tribes took the
chance, crossed the Crimean steppes and joined the Tatars of Akkerman; the
Kitai-Kipchaks followed them. Fear of the Kalmyks was one reason; being on bad
terms with the Circassians and with the Little Nogai of Kasai was another.

By the middle of the eighteenth century the Yedisan were camped between the
Dniester and the Dnieper and the Yembulad between the Dnieper and Azov, the first
under a seraskier and the second under a kaymakam, both appointed by the khan of
the Crimea.

## What de Tott found

The Baron de Tott, sent to reside with Maqsud Giray in the 1760s, travelled among
them. They were governed by the khan's own son with the title of seraskier. They
were extremely discontented, because the khan had recently transferred to the
sultan the grain tax they paid. The pastures of the Yedisan were separated from
Bessarabia by the Dniester.

They lived, he says, in tribes settled in valleys fifty or sixty feet deep that
cut the plain from north to south.

That is the last position of the horde of Edigü: three or four clans in the
ravines of the Pontic steppe, taxed by a Crimean prince on behalf of an Ottoman
sultan, and cross about it.

## The reversal

It is worth setting the two ends of the story beside each other.

The Manghit began as the power that made khans — that put four successive
Chinggisids on the throne of Sarai, married its daughters into Kazan, decided who
ruled at Astrakhan, and in 1523 killed the khan of the Crimea in his tent because
he was becoming a nuisance.

They finished as the Crimean khan's tax base.

Nothing about that was a peculiar Nogai failure. It is what happened to mobile
pastoral power everywhere in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, when
gunpowder states with rivers and forts began to be able to reach places cavalry
had made unreachable. What is peculiar to the Nogai is how visible it is, because
they never had walls to lose — only a name, a genealogy, and the ability to be
somewhere a khan did not expect them.
