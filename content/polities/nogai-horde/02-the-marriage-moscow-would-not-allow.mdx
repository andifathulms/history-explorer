Musa, one of the two murzas who had killed Ahmed Khan of the Golden
Horde in 1481, spent the next three decades doing to Kazan's throne
what he had already done to the Great Horde's: deciding who sat on it
without ever wanting to sit there himself.

## A marriage granted, a marriage refused

Kazan's own throne was contested between two brothers, Muhammad Amin
and Ali Khan, and the Nogai chiefs backed the loser. When Ali was seized
by the Russians in 1487, Musa and Yamgurchi wrote to Moscow asking for
his release - and got nowhere. Ivan III then handled the rival house
with a single, precisely calibrated gesture: he let Musa marry his own
daughter to Muhammad Amin, the Russian-backed khan, but refused to let
Muhammad Amin's sister marry Yamgurchi's son. One alliance was useful to
Moscow; the other, tying two rival Nogai lines together through the
Kazan throne itself, was not going to be permitted at any price. Musa's
people kept plundering Russian fishermen on the Volga regardless.

## The uses of a kingmaker

Musa's own preference was Sheibani, not Moscow. When the young
Sheibanid adventurer was still fighting his way to power against
Burunduk Khan, Musa's envoys found him at Sighnak and promised Nogai
backing if he would come raid the Kipchak steppe. He came, the two
allied and beat Burunduk between them - and when Sheibani then asked
Musa to make good on whatever had actually been promised him, Musa
refused, on the plain grounds that his own amirs would not stand for it.
It was the same pattern Nogai power always ran on: enough cavalry to
decide a war, and no dynastic claim that bound the horde to honour the
peace afterward.

In 1505 that cavalry went to war on Moscow's own frontier instead - forty
thousand men from Kazan under Muhammad Amin, twenty thousand Nogais
riding with them, Musa's own son among the dead in the siege that
followed. Four years later a different Nogai prince, Timur, was
negotiating on Moscow's behalf to ransom a captured ally from Lithuania.
The horde fought both sides of every quarrel on the steppe because it
had cavalry for both sides and loyalty to neither.

## Five sons, then three

Musa is not mentioned again after 1509, and by the time Sigismund von
Herberstein reached Moscow in 1517 the horde he described had already
passed to the next generation: three brothers - Shidiak at Saraichik,
Hassan between the Kama and the Volga, and Sheikh Mamai holding the
rest - dividing the provinces Musa had spent three decades building
influence over without ever converting it into a throne of the horde's
own. Musa had left five sons in all; two of the others, Idiak and Yusuf,
would matter more to the next century than any of the three Herberstein
happened to meet.
