Sigismund von Herberstein was at Moscow in 1517 and again in 1526, and the Nogai
were the largest thing on the map his hosts could not draw. What he set down about
them is short and almost entirely about how they were governed.

## Dukes, not kings

They lived beyond the Volga, he writes, in the neighbourhood of the Caspian and
chiefly by the river Yaik. They had no kings. They were ruled by chiefs, whom he
renders as dukes and who are the murzas.

When he was in Russia three brothers held these duchies and had divided the
provinces equally between them. Shidiak held the city of Saraychiq and the
district along the Yaik. The second, whom Herberstein calls Cossum, had the
country between the Kama, the Yaik and the Volga. The third, Sheikh Mamai, held a
portion of Siberia and the land around it.

That is the whole constitution. A partible inheritance among brothers, no single
head that anyone outside could treat with, and a territory stretching from the
Kama to Siberia held by three men who were not required to agree.

## Why it worked anyway

Because the horde's weight was applied through other people's thrones, and there
were enough of those to go round.

Sheikh Mamai's daughter married Safa Giray of Kazan, and in 1530 he sent thirty
thousand men to help his son-in-law resist a Russian attack. His brother Yusuf's
daughter Söyembikä was married first to Jan Ali of Kazan and then, when Jan Ali
was killed in a rising, to Safa Giray — and became the central figure of Kazan's
last twenty years. Their cousin Aguish was fighting the Crimea alongside the khan
of Astrakhan in 1509.

One family, in one generation, supplying queens to one khanate and armies against
another.

## Saraychiq

The senior brother's town was the one fixed point. Saraychiq stood on the lower
Yaik on the caravan road from the Volga to Khwarazm, it held the graves of
princes, and it was where envoys went — Russian agents, Bukharan matchmakers,
Kazakh messengers — when they needed to speak to the Nogai rather than chase them.

It was not a capital in the sense Kazan had a capital. The horde lived in the
open; the town was where its business was done and its dead were kept. Both facts
mattered in 1580.
