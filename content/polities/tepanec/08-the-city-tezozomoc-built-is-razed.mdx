Maxtla inherited his father's throne without his father's patience.
Where Tezozomoc had spent decades disguising his own ambitions behind
professions of loyalty and mercy, Maxtla ruled Azcapotzalco openly as a
tyrant, and his open hostility toward Tenochtitlan - compounding
Tepanec demands the Mexica had tolerated for years under his father -
pushed the Mexica toward exactly the alliance Tezozomoc's own dying
nightmare had warned him to prevent. Nezahualcoyotl, restored to enough
standing in Texcoco to raise an army of his own, joined forces with
Itzcoatl, the newly elected king of Tenochtitlan, against the throne
that had killed both of their own fathers.

## A war the Tepanecs did not expect to lose

The combined Mexica and Acolhua forces defeated Maxtla's own army in the
field, driving him back on his own capital. Maxtla fled to the baths of
Azcapotzalco itself as the city fell around him, and was dragged out and
put to death by the same rites Tepanec kings had once inflicted on their
own defeated enemies. Azcapotzalco, the capital Acolhua had founded on
land granted by a Chichimec emperor three centuries earlier and
Tezozomoc had built into the dominant power of the whole valley, was
razed to the ground; its wasted territory became, from that point on,
the valley's own slave market.

## A dominance that outlived its own destroyer

What Tenochtitlan, Texcoco and Tlacopan built from Azcapotzalco's own
ruin was not a restoration of the old Chichimec imperial order but
something new: a three-city league in which none of Tezozomoc's own
former vassals answered to a single Tepanec throne again. Tezozomoc's
own patient accumulation of power across a reign longer than most of
his rivals' lives had built the very system his son's arrogance would
lose within a single year of his own death - proof, if any more had
been needed, that the empire he built was a personal achievement rather
than an institution able to outlast the man who made it.
