title: A long minority and a longer reign

Tezozomoc came into his own inheritance only gradually. Too young to
rule at his father's death, he grew up under a regency his own mother
held only in name, since her marriage to the king of Culhuacan left
effective power over Azcapotzalco in Culhua hands for years. By the time
he had come of age and secured the Tepanec throne outright, the valley's
political landscape had shifted again: the Mexica, once hunted as
outlaws for the killing that had cost his own father his life, had since
been granted a wretched island refuge by the Culhuas and were slowly
turning it into a functioning city of their own.

## An old rivalry recast as an alliance

Rather than continue his father's war against the Mexica indefinitely,
Tezozomoc drew them into his own service instead, accepting their
warriors as auxiliaries in Tepanec campaigns against other valley
powers. Under the reign of the Chichimec emperor Techotl, who held the
imperial title from 1305 to 1357 in a reign otherwise remembered for its
unusual peace, Tezozomoc's own Tepanec forces took a leading part in
suppressing a revolt at Xaltocan, with Mexica troops fighting alongside
them and receiving a share of the conquered territory for their trouble.
Nominal deference to the Chichimec emperor remained in place, but real
power in the valley was steadily accumulating in Tezozomoc's own hands
rather than the increasingly ceremonial imperial office at Texcoco.

## A king who outlasted his own century

What distinguished Tezozomoc from every rival was not any single
conquest but the sheer length of his own reign, stretching from the
early fourteenth century into the 1420s - long enough to outlive most of
the rivals, allies and vassal kings who had once stood alongside him,
and long enough to turn a vassal lordship inherited as a child into the
dominant power of the entire Valley of Mexico by the time of his own old
age.
