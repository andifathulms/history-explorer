Around the end of the thirteenth century, the still-migrant Mexica
people, then settled precariously in Tepanec and Culhua territory,
killed a prince named Copil who had spent years inciting the valley's
established peoples against them, tearing out his heart and casting it
into the lake. The killing did not go unanswered. Copil's own allies and
kin rallied nearly every ruler in the valley, the Tepanecs among them,
into a war of extermination against the Mexica, who had by then made
themselves feared and hated as much as courted for their fighting
prowess.

## A defence that turned into a massacre of its own

The Mexica leader Huitzilihuitl put up a fierce defence, defeating a
Tepanec force in open battle - but in the same campaign his own men
seized and cut to pieces Acolnahuacatl, the king of Azcapotzalco and a
former Chichimec emperor in his own right. The killing of a king did
not save the Mexica. While their army was drawn off fighting the
Culhuas, another allied force took and burned the Mexica's own refuge at
Chapultepec, and the Mexica army itself was shortly after defeated and
nearly wiped out, its scattered survivors driven into the lake marshes.
Huitzilihuitl himself was captured and put to death, along with his
daughter and sister, in direct revenge for Copil's killing and
Acolnahuacatl's own murder. The events are dated to about 1297.

## A throne inherited before it could be claimed

Acolnahuacatl's own killing left the Tepanec throne to an infant son,
Tezozomoc, too young to rule in his own right. His mother governed as
regent during his minority - and, since she was also married to
Coxcoxtli, the king of Culhuacan, effective control of Azcapotzalco
passed for some years into Culhua hands rather than staying with the
Tepanecs themselves, a subordination the young heir would spend the rest
of his own long life reversing.
