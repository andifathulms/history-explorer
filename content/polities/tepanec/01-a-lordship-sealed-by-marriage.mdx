The Tepanecs entered the recorded history of the Valley of Mexico as one
of several Nahua-speaking peoples who tendered their allegiance to
Xolotl II, the Chichimec ruler whose own dynasty had claimed authority
over the valley since an earlier migration into it. Like the Acolhua and
other groups arriving around the same time, the Tepanecs traced their
own claim to nobility back to an ancient and illustrious lineage, the
Citin - a claim the Chichimec court, whether from genuine conviction or
simple political convenience, chose to accept.

## Three chiefs, three daughters, three capitals

Xolotl II rewarded the arriving chiefs generously. To the Tepanec lord
Acolhua he granted several districts south of Tenayocan, with the town
of Azcapotzalco as its own capital, along with the hand of his eldest
daughter Cuetlaxochitl in marriage. Two other chiefs received similar
grants elsewhere in the valley on comparable terms - a district around
Xaltocan for the Otomí lord, and Coatlichan for a young Acolhua noble
named Tzontecoma - each marriage binding a newly settled people
more tightly to the ruling Chichimec house than tribute or homage alone
could have done.

## A capital founded on the emperor's own terms

Azcapotzalco's founding as a Tepanec capital was, in this sense, a
grant rather than a conquest: a lordship created by an emperor
distributing territory and daughters among favoured chiefs, not a
kingdom the Tepanecs themselves had to fight their way into. What began
as one vassal lordship among several settled around Lake Texcoco would,
within two centuries, outgrow the Chichimec authority that had created
it and come to dominate the whole valley in its place.
