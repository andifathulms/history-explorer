---
title: An eagle and a tiger in a dying king's dreams
drafted_from: bancroft-1875
phase: peak
---

Tezozomoc lived on for years after destroying Texcoco, into an old age
so advanced that he had to be kept alive, by the end, through artificial
warmth and constant attendance. A temperate life and a strong
constitution had carried him well past the usual span of his own time,
and he kept full use of his mental faculties to the last - faculties
that, in his final days, turned against the one mercy he had shown in an
otherwise ruthless career. He came to regret sparing Nezahualcoyotl's
life, dreaming that an eagle tore his own head apart and a tiger
devoured his feet. His astrologers told him plainly what the dream
meant: the eagle and the tiger were Nezahualcoyotl himself, who would
overthrow Tepanec power unless he could be killed first.

## A last order his own sons chose not to carry out

Tezozomoc's final instruction to his sons and nobles was to have
Nezahualcoyotl killed during his own funeral, when the exiled prince
would very likely be present to pay his respects. He died in 1427,
naming a son, Tayauh, his successor on both the Tepanec and Chichimec
thrones, and urging him to govern gently and cultivate the loyalty of
vassal lords rather than rule by fear. A large gathering of princes,
Nezahualcoyotl among them, attended the funeral rites despite his
friends' own warnings - he trusted a sorcerer's assurance that he could
not be killed at that particular time. Tayauh, disposed to carry out his
father's dying wish, was talked out of it by his own brother Maxtla, who
argued that murder would disgrace so solemn an occasion - a scruple that
had less to do with respect for the dead than with Maxtla's own
unfinished ambitions.

## A reputation no one, not even his admirers, could soften

Every source agrees on one point about the king they had just buried:
Tezozomoc was the most unscrupulous and tyrannical ruler ever to hold
power in the valley, however much his own strict personal morality and
evenhanded enforcement of law within his own territory might complicate
that verdict. His skill as a diplomat and a schemer, built patiently
across a reign longer than most of his rivals' whole lifetimes, was
beyond serious dispute - and it was a skill his own sons, in the months
that followed his death, would prove far less capable of matching.
