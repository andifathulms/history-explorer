title: The guards disbanded

Husain Shah began his reign by restoring basic order in a capital his own
followers had spent four months besieging, punishing plunderers severely
enough that the booty recovered from them refilled the royal treasury, and
then set about reforms aimed squarely at the two mechanisms that had let
the previous seventeen years happen at all.

## Disarming the guard, expelling the kingmakers

The first target was the paik, a corps of Hindu infantry that had served
as palace and royal guards for generations and had gradually acquired a
position Haig compares directly to the Praetorian Guard at Rome — able to
make and break emperors by controlling access to the one person whose
safety determined everyone else's. Husain disbanded most of the corps and
posted the remainder far from the capital, replacing them with Muslim
troops answerable, in practice as well as in theory, to the crown.

The second reform was blunter: the wholesale expulsion of every African
from the kingdom. Their numbers had grown large enough, and their recent
taste of real power sharp enough, that Husain judged the risk of a
permanent African ruling caste too dangerous to leave unaddressed. The
exiles sought refuge in Delhi and Jaunpur, where their reputation preceded
them too thoroughly to be welcome, and most drifted instead to the Deccan
and Gujarat, where men of African descent had already been employed in
large numbers for years and one more wave of arrivals raised no particular
alarm.

## An old rival's last refuge

In 1495 Husain Shah of Jaunpur, driven from his own kingdom by Sikandar
Lodi, arrived in Bengal as a refugee and was given honourable
accommodation at Kahalgaon, where he lived out his remaining years until
his death in 1500 — one deposed dynasty's last king finding shelter, by
simple coincidence of name, at the court of a very different Husain Shah
whose own kingdom was, at that moment, near the height of its power.

## What thirty years of order actually built

With the capital secured and the routes to power narrowed back down to
ordinary service rather than palace intrigue, Husain Shah turned outward:
recovering provinces that six disordered preceding reigns had let slip
away, pushing the frontier as far as Orissa, and in 1498 invading Assam
against Nilambar, last king of the Khen dynasty. It was, on the evidence of
what both the Africans before him and the dynasty after him managed, the
one stretch of the whole sultanate's history when court intrigue and
military strength were pointed in the same direction rather than working
against each other — and Haig's own judgement ranks him, with the single
exception of the dynasty's founder a century and a half earlier, the
greatest of all the Muslim kings who ever ruled Bengal.
