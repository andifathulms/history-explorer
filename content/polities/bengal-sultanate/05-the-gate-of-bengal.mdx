Ghiyas-ud-din Mahmud Shah took the throne in 1533 by usurping his own
nephew, whom Sher Shah — then still an obscure Afghan officer — had been
permitted to wield near-royal power over. The usurpation immediately drew
him into a war he had not chosen and could not really afford: his own
brother-in-law rebelled in alliance with Sher Khan Sur, an Afghan who had
been steadily building an independent power base in neighbouring Bihar
since the death of its previous ruler.

## A dangerous ally's first taste of Bengal's wealth

The two rebels defeated and killed the governor Mahmud sent against them,
and Sher Khan came away from the victory with elephants, war material and
treasure substantial enough to fund a serious expansion of his own power.
When his former partner rose again without him and was crushed, Sher Khan
marched on Bengal himself to avenge the death — and found, at the natural
chokepoint between the Rajmahal hills and the Ganges known as the Gate of
Bengal, a defence stiffened for a full month by Mahmud's own Portuguese
captives, who preferred a fighting chance at freedom to indefinite
captivity.

## The siege that starvation decided

The gate eventually gave way, Mahmud's main army was beaten in the field,
and Sher Khan besieged him in Gaur itself. The siege pressed on even
through a fresh rising in Bihar, which Sher Khan suppressed in person
while leaving his son and a trusted officer to keep the pressure on the
capital. Famine inside the walls did what direct assault had not: on 6
April 1538 Mahmud led his starving garrison out in one last attack,
was defeated and put to flight, and watched his sons captured as Gaur was
sacked and occupied.

## What Sher Khan did with the conquest

Sher Khan pursued the fleeing Mahmud, defeated and wounded him again, and
entered Gaur in triumph to assume the royal title for himself. Mahmud fled
to Humayun for protection — the Mughal emperor had, in fact, already used
Sher Khan's preoccupation with Bengal to seize Chunar from his officers —
and died at Kahalgaon while Humayun's own relief force was still on its
way, having just learned that Sher Khan had executed both his sons. The
Sayyid dynasty Husain Shah had built into Bengal's golden age ended there,
in a fortress its own last king had fled to rather than defended, and Sher
Khan went on, within two years, to defeat Humayun himself at Chausa and
drive the Mughal emperor out of Hindustan entirely — resources first won,
in the end, at the Gate of Bengal.
