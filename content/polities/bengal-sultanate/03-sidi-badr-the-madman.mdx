For seventeen years before Husain Shah's accession in 1493, real power in
Bengal belonged to African courtiers who had risen through the palace
guard and the royal household — a run of dominance that produced three
kings of African descent and ended only when the third of them was
overthrown by his own minister.

## A monopoly, then a murder

When the boy-king Nasir-ud-din Mahmud II came to the throne, administration
fell to an African official titled Habash Khan, whose monopoly on power
provoked the resentment of every other courtier competing for royal favour.
One of them, another African known as Sidi Badr the Madman, killed him and
took his place. Sidi Badr's ambitions were entirely self-interested. In
1490 he had the young king put to death and mounted the throne himself
under the grandiloquent title Shams-ud-din Abu Nasr Muzaffar Shah.

## A reign of purges, and one good decision

Muzaffar spent his three-year reign executing most of the kingdom's leading
men. His single defensible act of judgement was the choice of a minister:
Ala-ud-din Husain, a Sayyid of a family originally from Tirmiz on the Oxus,
respected for his lineage, his ability and his character in roughly equal
measure. Husain likely restrained the worst of Muzaffar's violence for as
long as restraint was possible, but a ruler who both raised the burdens on
his own people and cut the pay of his own army had made an enemy of the one
institution his survival actually depended on. Worn down by the tyranny
his position implicitly endorsed, Husain withdrew his support — and found
himself, without seeking it, at the head of an open revolt.

## What the nobles chose instead

The rebel troops besieged Muzaffar in Gaur for four months. He died leading
a desperate sortie from the fortress rather than surrendering it. The
nobles who had followed Husain into rebellion then elected him king in
1493, on terms Haig compares, only half in jest, to a European
constitutional settlement — guarantees extracted from the new ruler before
the throne was handed to him, rather than assumed to be his by simple
right of conquest. It was the closest thing to a negotiated succession
Bengal had produced in generations, and it ended, at last, the run of
kings whose claim to the throne had rested on nothing sturdier than
control of the palace guard.
