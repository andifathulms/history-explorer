By the early fifteenth century real power in Bengal had passed, in
substance if not in name, from the throne to a Hindu chieftain named Raja
Ganesh — styled Raja Kans by most Muslim chroniclers — whose influence
became dominant after he defeated the reigning sultan in 1404, even though
he left no coins or inscriptions of his own and never openly claimed the
crown.

## A saint's ultimatum

Accounts of Ganesh's character conflict sharply. Some describe him as
secretly sympathetic to Islam, others as tolerant, and the most detailed
surviving account as an outright bigot whose persecution of Bengal's
Muslims drove the saint Qutb-ul-Alam to invoke the aid of Ibrahim Shah of
Jaunpur. Ibrahim invaded. Ganesh, in his terror, sought the saint's
intercession — refused, on the grounds that a misbeliever's plea for mercy
was not his to grant — and, casting about for an escape, offered his own
son, Jadu, to Qutb-ul-Alam for conversion, so that the boy might be
proclaimed king in his place and the whole country spared a religious war.

## The dupe and the double-cross

The saint accepted the arrangement in good faith, prevailed on Ibrahim
Shah to withdraw his army, and then discovered he had been played for a
fool: Ganesh treated his own son's proclamation as a formality without
substance, resumed and intensified his persecution of Muslims, and
attempted to reclaim the boy back to his old faith. The convert's
ceremonial purification — passed bodily through golden images of cows,
afterward broken up and distributed to Brahmans as charity — had been
costly and elaborate. It did not take. The young convert, whatever else
had been done to and for him, refused to return to his father's religion,
and was imprisoned for the refusal.

## The persecuted becomes the persecutor

Qutb-ul-Alam lived to see his own miscalculation punish the very community
he had tried to protect, forced to witness Muslims persecuted under his
own failed guarantee. Death came to Ganesh in 1414, and the imprisoned
convert was raised to the throne as Jalal-ud-din Muhammad — who turned on
his father's faith with the particular ferocity of a man who had been
locked away for refusing to abandon his own. The Brahmans who had staged
his purification ceremony were forced to swallow the flesh of the animal
they had used to cleanse him, a permanent defilement in payback for a rite
that had failed to hold, and a reign of seventeen years followed in which,
Haig judges, the numerical dominance of Islam across eastern Bengal — in a
population with no plausible descent from conquering invaders to explain
it otherwise — most likely took the shape it still has today.
