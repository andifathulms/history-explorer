Bengal was a Delhi province in name for over a century before it became one
in fact, and the distance between the two conditions was mostly a matter of
geography rather than politics.

<Figure id="bg-adina" />

## A byword for disobedience

The country was too far from Delhi, and the climate too unforgiving for
months at a stretch, for any sultan to reliably enforce obedience on
whoever he sent to govern it. The loyalty of Bengal's governors became, in
Haig's phrase, a byword at the capital — sometimes nominal, sometimes
openly defiant, always contingent on how much military attention Delhi
could actually spare for a province that took weeks to reach and longer to
subdue. Royal titles were assumed more than once by governors who judged,
correctly, that Delhi's response would be slow or absent. The first serious
rebellion against a genuinely strong sultan came under Balban; the first
unquestioned, lasting use of the royal title in Bengal came a generation
later.

## Two capitals, two claimants

The disintegration that produced a real independent kingdom took decades
rather than a single declaration. By 1338 the province had effectively
broken from Delhi's control. What followed was not one clean secession but
years of rival claimants ruling separate halves of the delta from separate
capitals — one at Sonargaon in the east, one at Lakhnawati and then Pandua
in the west — assassinating, deposing and replacing each other while Delhi,
consumed by Muhammad bin Tughluq's own catastrophic reign, had neither the
will nor the resources to reassert control over any of them.

<Figure id="bg-map" />

## One throne, at last

It was Shams-ud-din Ilyas Shah — nicknamed Bhangara for his fondness for
hemp preparations — who finally closed the gap between fragmentation and a
real kingdom. Having assassinated his own foster-brother to take the
western throne in 1345, he spent the following years defeating his eastern
rival and, in 1352, took Sonargaon itself, uniting the whole of Muslim
Bengal under a single crown for the first time since the province had
first slipped from Delhi's grip. Firuz Tughluq marched against him in
retaliation for raids into Tirhut and the frontier of Delhi's own
territory, besieged him at his fortress retreat of Ikdala, and was forced
to withdraw without extracting so much as formal recognition of his
sovereignty. In December 1356 Firuz gave up the pretence and formally
recognised Bengal's independence — a recognition whose gifts, delivered by
envoy, arrived too late even to be presented, since Ilyas Shah had already
died and been succeeded by his son before the mission reached Bihar.
