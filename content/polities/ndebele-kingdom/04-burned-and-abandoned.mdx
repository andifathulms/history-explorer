The British South Africa Company had been settling Mashonaland, land
Lobengula's own regiments still claimed the right to raid, for three
years when the incident that ended his kingdom began with a cut
telegraph wire. In July 1893 Lobengula sent an impi under the induna
Umgandine to punish the Mashona he blamed for the damage - but the
soldiers, whether exceeding their orders or simply carried away, fell
on Mashona now under Company protection and chased the survivors into
Fort Victoria itself. Dr. Jameson, the Company administrator, gave the
impi an hour to withdraw across the boundary or be fired on; when a
patrol sent after the slow-departing Indunas was itself fired on, the
skirmish that followed killed several of Lobengula's men and gave
Jameson the pretext for war he had, by most accounts, already been
quietly preparing for.

Lobengula, for all his annoyance, still did not want to fight. He
warned the Europeans living in Matabeleland to leave while they could
and sent messages to Jameson seeking to avoid war even as Company
columns from Salisbury, Victoria and Tuli converged on Bulawayo
through October. He could not hold what firearms and organised
columns were coming to take. On the morning of November 3, as the
lead column neared Bulawayo, its scouts saw smoke and heard an
explosion rising from the town: Lobengula had burned his own royal
kraal and fled north rather than let it fall into Company hands intact.
Company troops occupied the ruins the following day.

## The Shangani and the end

Retreat did not save him. A small patrol of some thirty-four men
under Major Alan Wilson, sent ahead to press the pursuit, was cut off
by the Shangani river in December and wiped out to the last man - a
disaster remembered ever after as the Shangani Patrol, and one that
briefly threatened to unravel the whole campaign, but bought
Lobengula only weeks, not survival. Ill for some time with fever or a
mild case of smallpox, and reportedly heartbroken as the men still
loyal to him drifted away to their own kraals one by one, he collapsed
while walking near his camp in late January 1894 and died early the
next morning without regaining consciousness - on or about the 22nd
or 23rd of the month, by the account of the traders travelling with
his fugitive court. Within weeks his surviving chiefs, queens and
children came in to surrender, and the kingdom Umsilikazi had built
twice, first on the highveld and then beyond the Limpopo, did not
build itself a third time.
