Beyond the Matopo hills, Moselekatse built a second kingdom that
outlasted the first by three decades. His regiments raided
Mashonaland much as they had once raided the Betshuana country,
reducing much of its population to poverty and forcing survivors to
live among rocky hills where cattle could not easily be seen or
stolen. European hunters and traders came and went under his
protection, so long as they observed his notion of proper courtesy and
brought suitable gifts; missionaries of the London Missionary Society
were permitted to settle at Inyati from 1859, though none of them made
a single convert, and the old military system Moselekatse had brought
from Zululand went on unchanged beneath the visitors' presence.

He died in October 1868, and the kingdom he had built twice discovered
it had no settled way to choose a third ruler. His acknowledged heir,
a son named Kuruman, had disappeared years earlier under
circumstances no one could agree on - killed by his father's own
order, by some accounts, or fled to another country and still living,
by others. The aged induna Nombati, appointed regent while the
question was resolved, sent messengers in every direction to search
for him, and a man was eventually found in Natal claiming to be
Kuruman himself. Examined closely, his own account of his life
convinced most of the indunas, Nombati included, that he was not
Kuruman at all, but a son of Moselekatse's of far lower rank hoping to
claim a throne that was not his.

## One battle, and a pardon

The indunas turned instead to Lobengula, a son by an inferior wife
whom Moselekatse had nonetheless had adopted by his great wife -
making him, in Ndebele reckoning, his father's chosen successor after
all. Lobengula accepted the position reluctantly, and was installed
with the full ceremony of a war-dance nine or ten thousand strong on
January 24, 1870. Not every regiment accepted the outcome: the
veteran Sokindaba, the most famous fighting band in the kingdom,
backed the Natal claimant instead, and the dispute was settled the
only way Ndebele succession disputes were ever settled - in a single
battle, which Lobengula's forces won outright.

<Figure id="nd-baines" />

What he did with the victory said as much about him as the victory
itself. Where Moselekatse would have exterminated a defeated rival
faction, Lobengula drafted the surviving Sokindaba soldiers into
regiments that had fought against them, and let his beaten rival live
out his years in exile rather than hunting him down. It was the same
mildness, foreign visitors would later note, that made Lobengula reluctant
to fight at all when the next serious threat to his kingdom finally
arrived - one that no single battle would be enough to settle.
