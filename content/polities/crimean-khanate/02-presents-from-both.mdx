A Habsburg ambassador spent 1517 in Moscow trying to make peace between the
prince of Muscovy and the king of Poland, and failed, and explained why in a
sentence about a third party.

Sigismund von Herberstein is the fullest outside witness to the Jochid successor
states while they still existed. He never went to the Crimea. What he had was
what the Muscovite court knew, and the Muscovite court had been dealing with the
khan for thirty years.

## The position

> The prince of Muscovy also used from time to time to conciliate him by
> sending presents, which he did because, as they were constantly embroiled in
> mutual wars, each strove to overwhelm the other by engaging the cooperation of
> the Tartar forces. He being aware of this, deluded both with vain hopes while
> he accepted presents from each.

Herberstein watched the mechanism operate against his own embassy. Unable to get
Moscow to accept terms, the king of Poland bribed the khan to attack Moscow from
the south while Poland attacked from the west. Moscow, finding this out, sent its
own envoys to the khan to point out that Lithuania was unguarded and off its
watch. *The Tartar, consulting only his own advantage, followed his advice.*

The Crimean khanate's foreign policy for two centuries is in that sentence. Two
Christian powers at war with each other, each paying the same third power to
attack the other, and the third power taking both payments and choosing the
softer target.

## What it bought him

Herberstein is clear that this was not parasitism but accumulation: *as his
power thus increased by the quarrels of these princes … his ambition enlarged
itself in proportion.* He also names what the increase paid for. The khan
compelled the king of Poland to a fixed annual stipend, on the understanding
that Polish forces could call on Tatar help in an emergency — which made a
tribute look like a retainer, and was easier for a Christian king to explain at
home.

## The limit

And then Herberstein records what happened when the khan tried to convert the
position into an empire, which is the subject of the next thing in his account
and the reason the Crimean khanate stayed a peninsula.

Mehmed Giray, allied with one of the Nogai princes, marched in January 1523,
took Astrakhan, and stayed in the city. His ally's brother pointed out that a
neighbour that strong was a danger to them both. The Nogai then sent the khan
some professional advice: that he should not corrupt his soldiers and neglect
their discipline by keeping them housed, but should come out and live in the open
field *according to the custom of the Tartars*.

He took the advice, brought his army out of Astrakhan and camped in the country.
The Nogai joined forces, attacked him at dinner with no warning, killed him,
destroyed most of his army and pursued the rest across the Don to the gates of
Perekop, where they gave up.

The khan who had been playing two kingdoms against each other was removed by an
argument about military discipline from people whose interest he had not
considered.
