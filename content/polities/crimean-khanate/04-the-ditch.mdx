The khanate's constitution was a bargain between a dynasty that could not be
replaced and four clans that could not be removed, and it worked for three
hundred years because neither side could win.

## The Girays

Only a descendant of Chinggis could be khan, and in the Crimea that meant only a
Giray. The rule was never broken. It removed at a stroke the commonest cause of
death for steppe polities — a successful general deciding he would do the job
himself — because no general was eligible.

What it did not do was say which Giray. There were always many, they were
distributed among the Ottoman provinces and the Circassian mountains, and the
sultan kept a supply of them at Rhodes and in Rumelia. A khan who displeased
Istanbul was replaced with a cousin; a khan who displeased the clans found the
cousin already being discussed. Over three centuries the throne changed hands
more than forty times, and a substantial majority of those changes were
depositions rather than deaths.

## The clans

The other half was the *karaçı* begs — the heads of the four great clans, of
whom the Şirin were first and could put more horsemen in the field than the khan.
Their consent was required for a new khan's recognition, their leaders held the
frontier commands, and the Şirin beg's own relations with Moscow were conducted
partly in his own name.

Alongside them sat the *kalgay*, the designated heir, and later a second
deputy, the *nureddin* — both Girays, both with their own establishments. A
khanate with a khan, two official heirs and four clan chiefs who could not be
sacked is a state with a great many people entitled to disagree.

## The Nogai

Beyond the ditch, on the mainland steppe, were the Nogai hordes, who were not
Crimean subjects and are not always usefully described as allies either.

They pastured between the Dnieper and the Kuban, provided a large part of the
manpower for the raids, took the khan's orders when it suited them and raided on
their own account when it did not. The khanate's ability to put eighty thousand
horsemen into Poland depended on them, and so did its frequent inability to stop
a raid that the Porte had forbidden.

## Why the arrangement held

Because each component was indispensable to the others and none could absorb the
rest.

The clans needed a Chinggisid to legitimise a levy and could not produce one.
The khan needed the clans to raise the levy and could not conscript them. The
Ottomans needed the khanate as the shield of their northern frontier and the
supplier of their slave markets, and had no interest in governing a peninsula
they could not feed from Anatolia. Moscow and Poland needed the khan on their
side more than they needed him destroyed, until the day they did not.

That day came in the eighteenth century, when Russia acquired an army that could
cross the steppe in strength and supply itself at the far end. The ditch had
never been forced. In 1771 a Russian army went through it.
