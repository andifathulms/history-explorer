In May 1571 a Crimean khan burned the capital of Russia to the ground
in a single afternoon, and Ivan the Terrible answered the man who did it
with a threat rather than an army.

## The approach nobody stopped

Devlet Geray came north with, by Jerome Horsey's count, two hundred
thousand horsemen - a figure no contemporary could verify but that every
account agrees was larger than anything the frontier garrisons could
face. Ivan's own field army, a hundred thousand strong, held the river
crossings on the Oka under strict orders not to move beyond its assigned
ground. The Crimean host simply swam the river where the Russians were
not, and found nothing between itself and Moscow, ninety miles off. Ivan
fled north with his sons, his treasury and his personal guard to the
fortified Trinity monastery, sixty miles away, and left the city to
whatever was coming.

## Six hours

Devlet Geray's men set fire to the tall steeple of St. John's church on
Ascension Day. A violent wind came up at the same moment, and a capital
built almost entirely of fir and oak timber - churches, palaces, houses,
for thirty miles of city and suburb - burned to the ground within six
hours. Thousands who could not outrun the fire suffocated in stone
cellars and church vaults that should have been shelters. Others drowned
trying to reach the river and the moats around the walls, weighed down
by the gold, jewels and plate they had grabbed on the way out; the
waterways stayed choked with corpses for months, and people were still
dredging them for valuables a year later. Horsey, who arrived in Russia
within two years of the fire and talked to people who had lived through
it, calls it a massacre no history had matched, and does not exaggerate
by much: an English merchant company operating in Moscow lost its own
factory in the blaze along with twenty-five of its people.

Devlet Geray watched the burning from a monastery four miles off,
gathered what plunder and captives his men could carry, and withdrew
south the way he had come rather than risk the army still massed at
Serpukhov.

## The knife

What followed was not a peace but an insult delivered as diplomacy.
Devlet Geray sent an envoy to Moscow dressed, deliberately, in a
sheepskin coat rather than court robes, who told Ivan to his face that
his master had sent him "the scourge of fire and famine" and now offered
him, pulling out a plain knife, the means to cut his own throat with it.
Ivan had the ambassador thrown out before he could be answered in kind,
then sent his own envoy south with a message calling the destruction
God's judgment on Russia's own sins rather than any credit to the khan
- and left that envoy to spend seven years as a prisoner in Crimea for
his trouble.

## What burned and what did not

Ivan rebuilt. Seven thousand masons raised a stone wall around Moscow
inside four years, and the city that had burned in an afternoon of wind
and timber was, by the time it was finished, built to survive the next
one. What the raid actually proved was narrower and more durable than
the fire itself: that the Oka line Ivan had spent a generation fortifying
against exactly this khan could still be swum around by an army willing
to look for the gap, and that the Tsar who had broken Kazan and
Astrakhan within Devlet Geray's own lifetime could still be humiliated,
in his own capital, by the one Chinggisid throne he had not yet found a
way to take.
