In January 1769 a French officer rode out of the Crimea with a Tatar army into
Russian territory, and wrote up the last great raid the khanate ever made.

Baron de Tott had been sent to reside with the khan by the duc de Choiseul in
1767. He was there to keep the Tatars in the war on the French side and to
report; he had not solicited the posting and accepted it, he says, as a favour.
Two years later the khan Kırım Giray took him along.

## What the army was

De Tott's value is that he was a professional soldier looking at a system he did
not understand and describing its mechanics.

There was no commissariat, no baggage train in any European sense, and no pay.
Every man brought remounts and rode in the depth of winter because that was when
the rivers and the marshes were hard and a horse could cross ground that was
impassable in summer. The column moved at a speed European armies could not match
and arrived where it was not expected.

It was also the last time it worked. The raid burned a stretch of the Russian
frontier and came home, and Kırım Giray died within weeks — poisoned, de Tott
believed, by a Greek physician in Ottoman pay.

## The two years after

What followed is the reason the campaign is worth a chapter rather than a line.

The Russian army that came south in 1771 did not raid. It came with siege
artillery and a supply system, went through the lines at Perekop that had held
for three centuries, and occupied the peninsula. The khan fled to Istanbul. The
Russians installed one of his relatives, and then another.

The treaty that ended the war in 1774 declared the khanate independent of the
Ottoman empire. That was the Russian demand, and it was granted, and it destroyed
the state: the only power with an interest in defending the Crimea was
contractually removed from it, and the Girays were left as the sovereigns of a
peninsula with a Russian garrison on it.

Nine years later Catherine II annexed it by manifesto. The last khan abdicated
and died in Ottoman exile.

## The word independence

It is worth stopping on, because it is the least obvious mechanism in the whole
of this history.

The Crimean khanate had spent three centuries as a client. Every khan since 1475
had been appointed and removable by a sultan in Istanbul, which is the standard
description of a subordinate state and the thing an eighteenth-century European
cabinet would call a loss of sovereignty.

It was also the thing that kept it alive. The protectorate meant that an attack
on the Crimea was an attack on the Ottoman empire, and for three hundred years
nobody attempted it. When Russia could not conquer the khanate cheaply, it
arranged for the khanate to be freed — and then took it in a decade, from a
position no one was obliged to defend.

De Tott was gone by then, reorganising Ottoman artillery at the Dardanelles. His
book came out in 1785, two years after the state he had been posted to had
ceased to exist, and he does not say so.
