The Crimean khanate is remembered, where it is remembered, as the last of the
Tatars. What it actually left behind is a map.

## The empty quarter

For two and a half centuries the grassland between the forest edge and the Black
Sea was uninhabitable by settled people, and the reason was the raid.

Russian and Polish sources call it the wild field. It was not empty because it
was poor — it is some of the best soil in Europe — but because anybody farming it
would be taken. The Muscovite state built and rebuilt fortified lines across the
approaches for three hundred years, moving them south a few dozen miles at a
time, each line an admission that the previous one had not worked.

When the raids stopped, the field filled up within two generations. The
agricultural south of Russia and Ukraine, the grain export that made Odessa, and
the whole nineteenth-century Russian position on the Black Sea are consequences
of the khanate ceasing to exist.

## What Moscow learned from the Tatars

The older argument — that Muscovite autocracy was a Mongol inheritance — has been
argued over for two centuries and Halperin's position is that it is mostly wrong
and partly indispensable.

What is not in dispute is the specific and the administrative. The Russian
postal relay system, the *yam*, is Mongol in name and organisation. The military
census, the household tax, the customs vocabulary and a good deal of the
chancery's technical language came the same way. So did the practice of
regarding the ruler's servitors as his to dispose of, which the Muscovite state
took much further than the Horde ever had.

What Halperin insists on is that the Russians never said so. The Mongol yoke was
something to be expunged from the record, not a model to be acknowledged; the
borrowing happened silently and was attributed to Byzantium.

## The Girays afterwards

The dynasty did not die out. Girays served in the Ottoman army, a few in the
Russian; one commanded a Crimean Tatar cavalry regiment for the tsar in the
Napoleonic wars. The family is still extant.

What ended was the political fact that a Chinggisid on a throne meant something
to anybody. For four hundred years, from the Volga to the Amu Darya, descent
from the eldest son of Chinggis Khan had been a qualification that could not be
acquired by any other means and that no amount of power could substitute for.
Amir Timur, who ruled from Delhi to Damascus, never called himself khan; he
married a Chinggisid princess and ruled through puppets who could.

The Crimean khanate was the last place where the rule still had force. When it
went, the qualification became a genealogical curiosity, which is what it had been
in every other part of the world for a century and a half.
