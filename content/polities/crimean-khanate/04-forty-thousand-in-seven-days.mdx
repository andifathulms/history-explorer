The French consul in the Crimea in 1758 watched a Tatar army come back from
Moldavia, and what he wrote down is the clearest description anybody has left of
what the raid economy actually looked like when it was unloaded.

Charles de Peyssonnel's account was printed in English as a hostile appendix to
the memoirs of his successor, whose facts he spent a hundred pages correcting.
On this he was not correcting anybody. He was there.

## The numbers

Krim Giray had seized the throne during a disturbance and had brought his army
back through Moldavia on the way. Peyssonnel and the consul sent to replace him
went out to meet the new khan at his camp.

> It is scarcely possible to form any idea of a pillage so sudden and rapid; and
> it is difficult to conceive how an army of eighty thousand men could in seven
> days overrun a great Province, and carry off forty thousand slaves and all the
> flocks, herds, and tents in which they were kept, besides an enormous quantity
> of other plunder.

Then the sight:

> We saw the plain of Kichela covered, as far as the eye could reach, with male
> and female Slaves of every age, oxen, camels, horses, sheep, and utensils of
> every kind piled up at different distances.

Forty thousand people, sorted into lots on a plain with the livestock and the
household goods, waiting to be moved to the coast.

## What a raid was

It was not war and it was not banditry. It was a harvest, and it was organised
like one.

The army that did this was raised the way the khanate always raised armies: the
clans brought their followings, every man found his own remounts, and nobody was
paid a wage. What they were paid in was what they carried off, of which the khan
took a fixed share. Eighty thousand men who provision themselves and are
compensated in captives can be put into the field at very short notice and cost
the treasury nothing, which is the whole reason the institution lasted three
centuries.

The destination was the Black Sea ports, and from there the markets of the
Ottoman world. Caffa was the largest slave market in Europe, and the Crimean
khanate was its principal supplier for two hundred and fifty years.

## The gesture

Peyssonnel then records what the new khan did next, and reports it as a piece of
statecraft rather than as mercy.

> Krim Guerai, after having given the Ottoman Government a proof of his power,
> was willing also to give as striking an example of his justice. He sent back
> all the Slaves who did not consent to remain.

Moldavia was Ottoman territory. The khan had just demonstrated that he could
strip a province of the sultan's own empire in a week, which was the argument for
recognising him; and then returned the people, which was the argument for not
punishing him for it. Peyssonnel notes, without softening it, that many had died
in the meantime.

## What the account is worth

Peyssonnel was a professional consul in the trade the khanate lived on, writing
to correct another Frenchman's book, and his purpose throughout is to show that
he understood the country better than his successor did. That gives him no reason
to inflate the figures and every reason to have them right.

It also means the passage is not an indictment. He describes eighty thousand men
carrying off forty thousand people in seven days in the tone of a man explaining
a commercial system to readers in Paris, because that is what it was to
everybody involved except the forty thousand.
