Tradition placed the Piast family on the Polish throne more than a
century before any of it could be checked against an outside chronicle -
a folk story about an artisan elevated to rule that a nineteenth-century
narrative history of Poland explicitly declined to build its own account
on. That history treats the actual beginning of an accountable Polish
state as starting instead with Mieszko I, who came to power in 964 and
whose reign is the first the sources can independently confirm.

## A courtship that ended paganism

Mieszko's conversion, by this account, had nothing to do with theology.
He wanted to marry Dombrowka, daughter of the Duke of Bohemia, and she
would not have him unless he accepted baptism first. He agreed for her
sake in 965 and, having done so, became one of the faith's most
committed champions in the region - tearing down pagan idols with his
own hands, raising churches on the sites of the temples he destroyed,
and founding archbishoprics at Gniezno and Kraków that would anchor the
Polish church for centuries.

## A crown from an emperor's pilgrimage

Mieszko's son Bolesław inherited both his father's piety and his
willingness to use it politically. When the Holy Roman Emperor Otto III
made a pilgrimage to the tomb of the martyred Bishop Adalbert and
received Bolesław's hospitality along the way, the emperor repaid him
with a grant of royal rank, later confirmed by the pope. From 1025
onward the Piast rulers of Poland held the title of king rather than
duke - a rank the family would go on to lose amid civil war and win
back more than once over the following three centuries.

## A warrior's kingdom, not yet a settled one

Bolesław used his new rank chiefly for war: against Bohemia, whose duke
he blinded after a hard-fought conquest; against the princes of Rus',
whose civil war he intervened in as far as Kyiv itself; and against the
Saxons, whose lands he raided to the Elbe before withdrawing within
Poland's own natural boundaries. None of these campaigns left Poland
larger for long. What they left instead was a kingdom whose royal title
rested on real military standing rather than ceremony alone - and a
precedent for how thoroughly that standing could still collapse once a
weaker heir inherited it.
