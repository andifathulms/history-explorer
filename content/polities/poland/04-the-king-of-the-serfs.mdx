By 1306, when Władysław I - nicknamed Łokietek, "elbow-high," for his
short stature - took the throne, the Piast kingdom Bolesław had won had
long since fractured into competing duchies. The royal title itself had
lapsed for decades, revived only in 1296 by one of Władysław's
predecessors, and the Poles withheld it from Władysław personally until
he had proven himself worthy of it through hard fighting against the
Teutonic Order, which had carved out a permanent settlement in Prussia
and continually raided Poland's northern frontier.

## A deathbed instruction about an old enemy

Władysław spent his reign reclaiming what fragmentation had scattered,
and when he died in 1333 he left his son Casimir explicit instructions
about the one enemy he had never fully subdued: never yield to the
Teutonic knights or the Marquis of Brandenburg, he told him, and bury
yourself under the ruins of your own throne before abandoning any part
of your inheritance to them.

## A king who built with paper rather than only with soldiers

Casimir settled his dispute with the Teutonic Order and turned instead
toward Rus', bringing most of what would become the Polish province of
Red Ruthenia under his own rule through a vigorous campaign. But his
lasting claim on his subjects' gratitude, this account insists, rested
on something sturdier than conquest. Poland before his reign had no
unified code of law: disputes were settled by a local noble's own
judgment, oaths whose outcome depended more on a defendant's composure
than on any fixed standard, and trial by ordeal. Casimir convened a diet
at Wiślica, building on preparatory work begun in Great and Little
Poland separately, and produced from it the first consistent Polish law
code.

## Favour that earned him a title of his own

The Wiślica code did not treat every subject equally - compensation for
crimes still varied by rank, and serfs remained beneath nobles in law as
in practice - but it genuinely improved the condition of the lower
orders, allowing an oppressed serf to sell his goods and change masters
rather than remain bound to a cruel one. Regular courts with fixed
judicial fees replaced arbitrary lordly justice in every province. For
this, Casimir earned a title no earlier Polish king had claimed: king of
the serfs, alongside the more familiar honorific history remembers him
by - the Great.
