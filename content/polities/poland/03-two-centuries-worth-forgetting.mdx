Władysław, ruling without the royal title his brother's murder of
Bishop Stanisław had cost the family, made the same mistake that had
already cost Poland dearly once: he divided his own territory among
his sons rather than leaving it whole. The division produced exactly
the friction it usually did, and it fell to his son Bolesław III,
who took power in 1103, to reassemble his brothers' inheritances into
a single patrimony again before he could do anything else with it.

## A siege that held past its own deadline

Reunified, Bolesław found a use for his restored strength almost
immediately: aiding the king of Hungary against the German emperor
Henry V, drawing German forces onto the Polish frontier at Silesia in
the process. The garrison at Głogów, besieged and told by Bolesław he
could not relieve them within the six days they had promised to hold
out, held anyway - refusing to honor a surrender deadline they judged
less important than their own liberty - until Bolesław's approach
forced Henry to break off the siege and turn toward Wrocław instead.
It was the kind of stubborn, decentralized resistance that had already
come to characterize Polish warfare against German pressure, and would
keep doing so for centuries after a later defeat, brought on by a
subordinate's treachery rather than any German army, broke Bolesław's
own spirit badly enough that he died soon after, in 1139.

## A testament that split the inheritance for good

Bolesław III made the same choice his own father had, and made it
worse: his will divided the dukedom among four sons rather than one,
under a settlement meant to let the eldest hold a kind of seniority
over the rest. It satisfied nobody. "All, or none," Fletcher records
as the brothers' own verdict on the arrangement, and years of
contention followed before the second son, also named Bolesław,
fought his way to sole mastery of the reunited duchy in 1146 - the
last such reunification this source records for a very long time.

## A silence the source itself admits to

What followed is, by Fletcher's own account, simply not there to
tell: the royal title itself lapsed for more than two centuries,
briefly reclaimed by Przemysł II in 1296 before his own assassination
cut the revival short, and the history running from Bolesław III's
own testament to Władysław Łokietek's reign in 1306 is dismissed in
this source as a string of "succeeding reigns, in which nothing
occurred worthy to be remembered." Whatever individual dukes ruled
fragments of a Piast inheritance divided and subdivided across those
two centuries, and whatever brought Przemysł II briefly back to a
crown his own murder ended almost as soon as he claimed it, is a
history this source does not preserve. What is certain is the
condition Łokietek inherited when the Poles finally judged him worthy
of the same crown: a kingdom that had spent longer without a king than
with one since Bolesław the Bold's own disgrace, and that Łokietek
himself would have to win back province by province rather than simply
assume.
