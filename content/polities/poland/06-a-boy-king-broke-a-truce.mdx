The union Władysław Jagiełło's marriage had sealed in 1386 was tested
within a generation by the same Teutonic Knights who had bled Poland and
Lithuania for three centuries, and then nearly undone by a nine-year-old
king's own regency and a broken truce that cost his teenage successor
his life.

## Grunwald, and a knighthood broken

Jagiełło spent the early part of his reign fighting the recurring war
his marriage had not ended. In 1410 his combined Polish-Lithuanian army
met the Teutonic Order in a great battle at Grunwald and broke it
outright — a defeat severe enough that the Order sued for peace rather
than risk a second engagement. Jagiełło used the years of peace that
followed to put the kingdom's law on a firmer footing, confirming at the
diets of 1422 and 1423 the principle, already old by then, that no
Pole could be imprisoned without conviction. He died in 1433 having
outlived the threat that had shadowed his whole reign, leaving the crown
to a son not yet nine years old.

## A truce broken, and a king lost at Varna

That son, Władysław III, came of age just in time to be drawn into a war
against the Ottoman sultan Murad II — first winning enough in the field
that a truce was agreed, then persuaded to break it on the promise of a
decisive Christian victory. The gamble failed. Władysław was killed at
the Battle of Varna in 1444, not yet twenty, and the crown passed to his
younger brother Casimir.

## Casimir IV, and Prussia won by exhaustion

Casimir IV's reign was defined by the same enemy his grandfather had
broken at Grunwald and never quite finished. The Prussian towns and
lesser nobility, worn down by the Order's own taxes and arbitrary rule,
rose against it and placed themselves under Casimir's protection in
1454. The Knights did not yield their remaining territory without a
fight: the war that followed lasted twelve years and laid the province
to ruin, by one contemporary count reducing twenty-one thousand villages
to scarcely thirteen thousand and destroying close to two thousand
churches. The Order was finally forced to submit, ceding Polish Prussia
outright and holding what remained as a fief of the Polish crown —
Danzig, Toruń, Elbląg, and Chełmno passing to Poland as valuable Baltic
trading towns in their own right. Casimir organized the new territory
into four palatinates under the kingdom's ordinary government, and in
1467 the first general diet of the Polish parliament met, called to
debate renewing the very war that had just ended. Learning flourished
alongside the territorial gain: Poland's first printing press was set up
at Kraków in the 1470s, the Latin language spread among the gentry by
royal edict, and scholars of humble birth — the historian Kromer, the
poet Janicki, the philosopher Gregory of Sanok — rose through the
kingdom's schools to European reputations regardless of what family had
raised them.

Casimir governed for nearly forty-eight years, longer than any Polish
king before or after him, extending the kingdom's territory, breaking
its oldest enemy, and building the representative diet that would
outlast every dynasty that sat above it. He died in 1492, leaving the
throne to the third of his sons, John Albert.
