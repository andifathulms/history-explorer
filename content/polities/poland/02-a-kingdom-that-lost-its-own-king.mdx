---
title: A kingdom that lost its own king
drafted_from: fletcher-1836
phase: expansion
---

Bolesław's son inherited the throne in 1025 and, choosing nine years
of what Fletcher calls "peaceful luxury" over his father's constant
campaigning, left a kingdom no stronger than he found it. His own son
Casimir succeeded him too young to rule outright, and the regency fell
to his mother Rixa - who taxed heavily, favored German appointees over
Polish ones, and was eventually driven from the kingdom by her own
outraged subjects, taking the royal treasury with her. Casimir fled as
well, and Poland was left with no king at all.

## A revolt that burned its own churches

What followed was not simple disorder but a genuine reversal: the
enserfed peasantry, freed of any authority capable of stopping them,
rose against their own masters and against the church that had
underwritten their servitude for two generations, burning church
buildings and killing priests and lords alike in a rejection of
Christianity as thoroughly as Mieszko I's own conversion had once
imposed it. The rebellion collapsed only when Bohemian and Rus'
invaders took advantage of the chaos to ravage Poland from both sides
at once - a foreign threat severe enough that the same nobility who
had just been the revolt's targets rebuilt the churches, sought papal
absolution, and sent out a search for the exiled Casimir to bring him
back.

## A monk recalled to the throne

They found him, after years of searching, living as a monk in a
French abbey, his royal claim seemingly foreclosed by vows the church
did not lightly dispense with. The pope granted an exception - on
condition, by Fletcher's account, that Poland pay Peter's pence to
Rome and that Polish men adopt a distinctive shaved-head style as a
mark of the settlement - and Casimir returned to restore order,
married a Rus' princess to secure his eastern frontier, and reigned
sixteen years before handing his son a kingdom considerably steadier
than the one his own mother's regency had nearly destroyed.

## A second collapse, engineered this time by the king himself

The recovery did not last through the next reign. Bolesław II spent
years campaigning as far as Kyiv, and when his army settled into years
of comfortable occupation rather than returning home, Fletcher records
a scandal that shook the kingdom on their eventual return: wives left
behind for years had taken up with their own household slaves in their
husbands' absence, and the returning soldiers answered with a
punitive violence that fell on wives and slaves alike. It was in the
aftermath of that bloodletting that the bishop of Kraków, Stanislaus,
rebuked Bolesław publicly and barred him from Mass - and Bolesław, in a
fit of rage, killed the bishop at the altar himself. Papal
excommunication and deposition followed; Bolesław died in exile,
reportedly working as a kitchen servant in a Carinthian monastery, and
the pope stripped his own sons of any claim to the throne, allowing
only Bolesław's brother Władysław to rule - and even then only as a
palatine, without the royal title his own brother had disgraced.
