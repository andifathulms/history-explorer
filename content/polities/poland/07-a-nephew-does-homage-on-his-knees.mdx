The two generations between Casimir the Great's namesake grandson and
Sigismund I's death saw the nobility entrench its own privilege at the
crown's expense even as Poland's kings dismantled, this time for good,
the military order that had troubled the kingdom since before its
Lithuanian union.

## John Albert's plan, and a nobility that would not be managed

John Albert, advised by his Italian tutor Buonacorsi, tried to curb the
nobility's growing power before it hardened into permanent privilege.
The plan leaked before it could be carried out, and when a Polish army,
raised almost entirely from the nobility's own ranks, was ambushed and
cut down during a campaign against the Wallachians, suspicion of royal
treachery hardened the nobles' resolve rather than weakening it. What
Albert failed to prevent in politics he could not undo in law: the
diet barred merchants from owning land or church preferment in 1496, and
from this reign the nobility's authority over its own serfs went
effectively unchecked, with no commercial class positioned to counter
it. Albert did what he could to balance the loss, tightening and
regularizing the kingdom's law courts, but died in 1501 having narrowed,
rather than broadened, who held power beneath the crown.

## Alexander, and a treasury the diet controlled

His successor Alexander inherited a crown further diminished: the diet
passed the Statutum Alexandrinum barring the king from raising money or
spending revenue without its own consent, prompted, by one telling, by
Alexander's own extravagance toward the musicians he favored. The same
reign saw Poland's laws collected and revised into a single code under
the chancellor Jan Łaski, still known by his name.

## Sigismund I, and a fief instead of an enemy

Sigismund I inherited a kingdom no easier to hold than his brothers had.
He put down a Lithuanian revolt and its Russian auxiliaries early in his
reign, helped by artillery newly adopted from his Bohemian allies. The
Teutonic Order's last chapter played out under his own eyes: his own
nephew Albert of Brandenburg, elected Grand Master in the hope that
family ties would keep the Order loyal, instead renounced his allegiance
to Poland outright on taking office. Sigismund brought him back to
obedience, and the settlement that followed ended the Order's standing
in Prussia for good — Albert did homage to his uncle in 1525 and received
the secularized territory back as an ordinary hereditary duchy, a
Lutheran vassal state answering to the same crown the Order had once
defied by force of arms. Western Prussia remained directly Polish
territory, administered no differently from the rest of the kingdom.

Sigismund's own domestic peace was less settled than his frontier one:
his wife Bona Sforza's influence over the court provoked the nobility
into a formal assembly of grievance at Lwów, a protest the sources
record as dispersing, in the end, under nothing more than a rainstorm —
but one that set a precedent for organized noble opposition to the
crown that would recur through the rest of the kingdom's independent
existence. Sigismund died in 1548, and the throne passed to his son,
already crowned as his co-ruler, Sigismund II Augustus.
