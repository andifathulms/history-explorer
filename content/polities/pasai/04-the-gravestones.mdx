The hardest evidence for any of this is a slab of imported marble with a date
on it.

Malik al-Salih's gravestone at Pasai is dated 696 AH, which is 1297, and it is
the earliest dated Muslim royal gravestone in Southeast Asia. The stone is
Gujarati work, of a type produced at Cambay and shipped, and there are others
like it along this coast and later at Gresik on Java.

What a stone like that establishes is narrow and unusually firm: on this date,
at this place, a ruler was buried by people who wanted an Arabic inscription in
the international idiom of Muslim monarchy and were willing to import the
marble to get one.

## Why the hard evidence is a burial

Almost everything else known about Pasai is softer than that.

The founding date of 1267 is traditional. The conversion narrative is a
hikayat. The marriage of 1414 that converted Melaka comes from a chronicle
written a century later at a court in exile. And nobody can put a name and a
reign to the sultan who was in place when the Portuguese arrived.

The gravestones are the exception, and this is a general condition rather than
a peculiarity of Pasai. A polity whose records were on palm leaf in a tropical
climate leaves its firmest traces in stone and in other people's archives, and
what people put in stone is who died and when.

## And what they say about the trade

There is a second thing the marble establishes, and it has nothing to do with
religion.

Cambay is in Gujarat. A carved and inscribed slab is heavy, fragile, and worth
shipping only if there is regular traffic in both directions and someone at
this end with the standing and the money to order one and wait.

The stone is therefore evidence for the trade network as well as for the
throne, and it is dated.
Nothing anybody wrote describes Pasai's finances
directly. What establishes that this was a port living off the traffic through
it is the plain fact that a thirteenth-century ruler on this coast could
commission monumental stonework from western India and have it delivered.
