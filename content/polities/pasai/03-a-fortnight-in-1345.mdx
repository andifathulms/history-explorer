Ibn Battuta stopped at Pasai on the way to China and spent about a fortnight
there, and what he wrote down is the only view of this court from the inside
that anyone has.

What he describes is a sultan, al-Malik al-Zahir, who held disputations with
jurists after Friday prayers, kept scholars from Persia and India at his court,
followed the Shafi'i school, campaigned against unbelievers in the interior,
and observed the forms of Muslim rulership in a manner the traveller found
unremarkable.

<Figure id="ibn-battuta" />

The unremarkableness is the point. Ibn Battuta was a Moroccan jurist who had by
then seen Cairo, Damascus, Mecca, the Delhi of Muhammad ibn Tughluq and the
Kilwa of the Swahili coast, and he was not easy to impress and complained about
most places. Seventy-eight years after its founding, a sultanate at the far
edge of the Muslim world was a normal part of it: connected enough that a
jurist could arrive from the Atlantic end and find people to argue law with in
a language of scholarship they shared.

## What a source like this is

He is an eyewitness who wrote it down years afterwards, from memory and with
the help of a court secretary and other people's books, and parts of the
*Rihla* are demonstrably borrowed or embellished.

He is worth trusting here for one thing: what the court of Pasai looked like
from the inside to a visiting jurist in the 1340s. That is exactly the kind of
claim an eyewitness is good for and no inscription can supply. He is not worth
trusting for dates, distances, or anything he was told rather than saw.

Nothing happened in 1345. No battle, no succession, no reform — a traveller
arrived, stayed a fortnight, and left. It is on the list of the polity's
turning points anyway, because a kingdom known otherwise from gravestones and
other people's tribute registers becomes, for two weeks, a place with named
scholars in it and arguments going on after Friday prayers.
