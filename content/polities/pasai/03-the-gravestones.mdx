The hardest evidence this record has is a slab of imported marble with a date on
it.

Malik al-Salih's gravestone at Pasai is dated 696 AH, which is 1297, and it is
the earliest dated Muslim royal gravestone in Southeast Asia. The stone is
Gujarati work, of a type produced at Cambay and shipped, and there are others
like it along this coast and later at Gresik on Java.

What a stone like that establishes is narrow and unusually firm: on this date, at
this place, a ruler was buried by people who wanted an Arabic inscription in the
international idiom of Muslim monarchy and were willing to import the marble to
get one.

## Why the hard evidence is a burial

Almost everything else on this record is softer.

The founding date of 1267 is traditional. The conversion narrative is a hikayat.
The turning point at 1414 — the marriage that converted Melaka — comes from a
chronicle written a century later at a court in exile. This record's `last` ruler
field is null because this site has not opened a source that fixes a name and a
reign to the sultan in place when the Portuguese arrived.

The gravestones are the exception, and this is a general condition rather than a
peculiarity of Pasai. A polity whose records were on palm leaf in a tropical
climate leaves its firmest traces in stone and in other people's archives, and
what people put in stone is who died and when.

## And what they say about the trade

There is a second thing the marble establishes, and it has nothing to do with
religion.

Cambay is in Gujarat. A carved and inscribed slab is heavy, fragile, and worth
shipping only if there is regular traffic in both directions and someone at this
end with the standing and the money to order one and wait.

The stone is therefore evidence for the trade network as well as for the throne,
and it is dated. `revenue_basis` on this record reads `trade-toll` alone, which
makes it one of eleven on this site coded that way — and the case for the coding
rests less on any description of Pasai's finances than on the fact that a
thirteenth-century ruler on this coast could commission monumental stonework from
western India and have it delivered.
