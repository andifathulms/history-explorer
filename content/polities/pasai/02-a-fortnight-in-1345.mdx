Ibn Battuta stopped at Pasai on the way to China and spent about a fortnight
there. It is the first citation of him anywhere on this site.

What he describes is a sultan, al-Malik al-Zahir, who held disputations with
jurists after Friday prayers, kept scholars from Persia and India at his court,
followed the Shafi'i school, campaigned against unbelievers in the interior, and
observed the forms of Muslim rulership in a manner the traveller found
unremarkable.

The unremarkableness is the point. Ibn Battuta was a Moroccan jurist who had by
then seen Cairo, Damascus, Mecca, the Delhi of Muhammad ibn Tughluq and the
Kilwa of the Swahili coast — three of which are records on this site — and he was
not easy to impress and complained about most places. Seventy-eight years after
its founding, a sultanate at the far edge of the Muslim world was a normal part
of it: connected enough that a jurist could arrive from the Atlantic end and find
people to argue law with in a language of scholarship they shared.

## What a source like this is

He is an eyewitness who wrote it down years afterwards, from memory and with the
help of a court secretary and other people's books, and parts of the *Rihla* are
demonstrably borrowed or embellished.

This record relies on him for one thing: what the court of Pasai looked like from
the inside to a visiting jurist in the 1340s, which is exactly the kind of claim
an eyewitness is good for and no inscription can supply. It does not rely on him
for dates, distances or anything he was told rather than saw.

The turning point is typed `reform`, which is the nearest value in this site's
closed vocabulary and is not a good fit — nothing was reformed in 1345. The list
has nine types and none of them is "a description of what was already there".
The date is on the record because the description is the most valuable single
piece of evidence about this polity, and a turning-point list that could only
hold battles would leave it off.
