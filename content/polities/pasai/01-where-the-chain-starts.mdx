Islam had been in these waters for five hundred years before Pasai existed.
Arab and Persian shipping used this route from the eighth century, and there
were Muslim merchant communities in the northern Sumatran ports long before any
of them had a Muslim ruler.

What changed around 1267 is that a state adopted it.

Merah Silu, who took the name Malik al-Salih, is placed at Samudera at about
that date. His gravestone is dated 1297 and is the earliest dated Muslim royal
gravestone in Southeast Asia — a slab of imported Gujarati marble on the north
Sumatran coast with a sultan's name on it in Arabic. When Marco Polo passed
along this coast in 1292 he noted that the towns had converted and the interior
had not.

## Why a throne matters more than a quarter

The distinction between merchants and a court is the whole of why this small
port is where the story starts.

What a merchant community does is trade. It can be large, rich and permanent
without changing the religion of anybody outside it, and on this coast it had
been exactly that for centuries. What a court can do is different: it can endow
scholars, build mosques, adjudicate in the name of a law, receive envoys as a
Muslim ruler receiving other Muslim rulers — and it can marry its daughters
into other courts.

That last is not a metaphor. In 1414 the ruler of Melaka married a princess of
Pasai, converted, and took the name Iskandar Shah. Melaka went on to become the
dominant port of the archipelago and carried Islam to the eastern islands
through its own merchants and its own marriages. Everything downstream of that
— Java, Sulawesi, Maluku, Borneo, the southern Philippines, over the next three
centuries — runs back through a wedding on this coast.

## What Pasai was not

It was not the first Muslim place in the region, and it is worth being exact
about that, because "the first Muslim state in Southeast Asia" is a phrase that
invites the wrong picture.

Perlak, a little way down the same coast, had converted earlier — Marco Polo
says in 1292 that of the six north Sumatran kingdoms he listed, Perlak was the
one that had gone over to Islam, and only in the town at that. There were
Muslim rulers and Muslim ports before Pasai.

What Pasai had was reach. It was the port at the mouth of the strait, on the
route everything from India and the Arab world had to take, at the moment when
Srivijaya's old command of that passage had broken up and no single power
replaced it. A small sultanate in the right place, with a court that behaved
like a court, at a moment when the sea lanes were open.
