Ten records in this region carry `religions_carried: 1` for Sunni Islam, and this
one is upstream of the other nine.

Melaka's is for the ports of the eastern archipelago, spread by merchants,
marriages and the prestige of the region's dominant court — and Melaka's own
conversion, around 1414, came from a marriage to a princess of Pasai. Demak's is
for taking Islam inland across Java by force after 1527 and Banten's for the
Sundanese highlands and the Lampung pepper districts. Aceh's is for the Sumatran
interior and for being the archipelago's point of embarkation for the
pilgrimage. Makassar's is for the campaigns through South Sulawesi from 1608.
Ternate's is for Halmahera and the Sula islands and Tidore's for the Raja Ampat
and the coasts of western New Guinea beyond them. Brunei's is for the coasts of
Borneo and Manila bay, and Sulu's for the archipelago between Borneo and Mindanao
and the Zamboanga coast past it.

Pasai is where Islam first held a throne in Southeast Asia.

Merah Silu, who took the name Malik al-Salih, is placed here around 1267. His
gravestone is dated 1297 and is the earliest dated Muslim royal gravestone in the
region — a piece of imported Gujarati marble on the north Sumatran coast, with a
sultan's name on it in Arabic. Marco Polo, passing in 1292, noted that the towns
of this coast had converted and the interior had not.

## What "first" means and what it does not

Islam had been in these waters for centuries before 1267. Arab and Persian
shipping used this route from the eighth century; there were Muslim merchant
communities in the ports long before any of them had a Muslim ruler.

What changed at Pasai is that a state adopted it. Coding rule 2 does not count
adoption — this record's count of one is for propagation past a boundary, and the
boundary it crossed was into Melaka in 1414. But the adoption is what made the
propagation possible, because what a port's merchants do is trade and what a
court can do is marry its daughters into other courts.

The ten records and their ten counts are one movement seen in ten places, running
from a throne on this coast in 1267 outward to Java, Sumatra, Sulawesi, Maluku,
Borneo and the southern Philippines over the next three centuries.

Each crossed a different boundary, which is why each counts separately and none
double-counts another — a claim the Tidore record has to defend explicitly,
because its boundary and Ternate's are two kilometres apart, and one this corpus's
Gelgel record throws into relief by being the single polity in the region that
pushed a religion the other way across a line this movement had already crossed.

Ten counts of one religion is the densest run of anything in the influence
columns anywhere on this site, and it was assembled backwards: this page, the
head of it, was among the last written.
