Samudera is the older of the two names in Samudera Pasai, and it did something
no other port on this coast managed. It got attached to the whole island.

Sumatra is Samudera, worn down by four centuries of foreign pronunciation. The
kingdom grew prominent enough during the fourteenth century that outsiders
began using its name for the landmass it sat on, displacing the older
designations altogether — and by the sixteenth century, when the Portuguese
arrived, the town itself had faded so far that Barros no longer heard the name
at all. Only Aceh.

An island is still called after a port that was already forgotten when the name
stuck.

## How a small port became visible

The Chinese records are the clearest measure of it, because they show a place
going from unmentioned to routinely dealt with inside a decade.

In 1282 a Chinese mission returning from the Coromandel coast put in at
Sa-mu-tu-la. The ruler received it and sent two of his own ministers back with
it to China; their names were Husain and Sulaiman, so they were Muslims, and
this is fifteen years before the gravestone that is usually treated as the
first hard evidence of a Muslim court here. In 1285 Samudera sent an envoy of
its own. In 1286 it was one of ten reigning families represented in a major
mission to the Chinese court — the other eight were Indian.

That is a very small state on a very long list. The point is that it was on the
list at all: a port at the mouth of the strait had become one of the places a
Chinese court expected to hear from.

## Reach across the water

How far the claim eventually ran is recorded on a grave.

At Minye Tujoh, between the Pasai and the Jambu Ayer rivers, two inscriptions
mark the death of a woman in 1389 — one in Arabic, naming her as a queen and
the daughter of the late Sultan Malik al-Zahir, and one in an Indic script that
defied reading for twenty years until Stutterheim worked through it in 1936.
The second calls her a *raja iman*, a queen of the Faith, and describes her
house as one that holds Kedah and Pasai.

Kedah is on the other side of the strait, on the Malay peninsula. Whatever the
exact force of the claim, a royal family at this port was asserting rights on
both shores a century after it had converted.

The two stones are worth a moment on their own account. A Muslim queen of a
Muslim sultanate was commemorated in Arabic on one side and, on the other, in
an Old Sumatran alphabet related to the script of Adityavarman of Malayu, in
the metre of a *sha'ir*. The court had been Muslim for a century and was still
burying its dead in two traditions at once.
