Pasai wrote its own history, and the book very nearly did not survive.

The *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai* is the chronicle of this kingdom, composed in
Malay, with parts of it demonstrably written after 1360. It tells the story
from Merah Silu's wanderings to the flight of a sultan named Ahmad, who
abandoned Pasai and set up his court at a place called Menduga, about fifteen
days' journey away. Then it stops. Nothing is said about what happened next,
and the reason, as Hill puts it, is obvious: the author knew nothing.

## How it got here

One manuscript exists.

It was copied for Stamford Raffles at Batavia by a scribe who recorded, in the
last paragraph after the fashion of Malay copyists, that he finished the work
on 2 January 1814. He did not give his name, and the handwriting is not that of
Ibrahim, the senior scribe who did most of Raffles's copying.

Below the final line of the text there is a single sentence in old Javanese
script. It says the book came from Kiai Suradimanggala, senior regent of the
province of Demak, in the year 1742 of the Saka era — which is 1814. So the
original had been sitting in a Javanese official's library on the north coast
of Java, a thousand miles from Pasai, and was lent to Batavia to be copied and
taken home again.

Of that original, nothing further is known. It has never been seen since.

The copy went to England with Raffles in 1816 and passed to his widow when he
died. A panel on page three of it reads "Presented by Lady Raffles to the Royal
Asiatic Society January 16th 1830", and a pencil note inside the cover, in van
der Tuuk's hand, gives it its catalogue name: *Hikayat Raja Pasei — Raffles No.
67*.

So the entire textual survival of the first Muslim kingdom in Southeast Asia
runs through one borrowed volume in a Javanese regent's house, one anonymous
scribe working in Batavia over the turn of a year, and the widow of a British
lieutenant-governor making a donation in London.

## What kind of book it is

Not annals. Hill is careful about this and the distinction matters.

The Malay word usually rendered as chronicle is *sejarah*, from the Arabic for
genealogy, and the *Sejarah Melayu* of Melaka has some claim to be called
annals. The Pasai text is a *hikayat* — a semi-historical romance, in which a
hard core of fact appears here and there inside stories drawn from the old
myth-complexes of the region. Snouck Hurgronje dismissed the whole thing in
1907 as "a children's fairy tale."

Hill's response, across two hundred pages, is that the aspersion is undeserved,
while conceding the real point: the events in it were, to the people who
compiled it, subjectively rather than objectively true.

That is roughly the position a reader should hold about the founding of Pasai
generally. The date of 1267, the conversion story, the name Merah Silu — all of
it comes through this text or others like it, and none of it has the standing
of the gravestone.

## What outlived the kingdom

Aceh took the coast in 1524, and what it could not take by conquest it
inherited anyway.

Pasai had been a centre of Muslim learning on this shore for two and a half
centuries, and its scholarship did not stop when its throne did. The greatest
of the scholars at the Acehnese court in its golden age is remembered by an
epithet that gives the whole thing away: Shams al-Din of Pasai.

The kingdom became a district. The learning moved forty miles up the coast and
was still being written about three hundred years later.
