Yeshe-O, remembered in Tibetan sources by the title lha bla ma -
"royal lama" - inherited a kingdom his ancestors had built as a
refuge, and turned it into the staging ground for reviving a religion
that had gone into decline everywhere else on the plateau. In a Fire
Male Monkey year, by the traditional reckoning, he laid the
foundations of a great temple complex at Tholing. The building took
eight years, employing artisans on a scale the small kingdom of Guge
had never before attempted.

<Figure id="gg-tholing" />

## An ordination on a regional scale

What Yeshe-O organized alongside the building work was even more
ambitious than the construction itself. While his own sons were
ordained at a mountain hermitage, the king gathered two hundred young
men from across the whole western Tibetan region to be trained as the
next generation of Buddhist teachers and translators - a hundred from
Guge itself, forty from neighboring Purang, thirty from the Spiti
valley, and thirty more from the territory later associated with
Ladakh. It was an investment in human capital on a scale no single
temple foundation could represent on its own: an entire cohort of
religious specialists, drawn deliberately from every part of the
kingdom's own sphere of influence.

## A network, not a single monument

When the great temple at Tholing was finally consecrated, the
celebration lasted twenty-one days. But Tholing was never meant to
stand alone. Over the same eight years, more than a hundred temples
were built or endowed across Guge, Purang, Spiti and the surrounding
valleys, each filled with statues and murals depicting the same
Buddhist cosmology the young men trained at Tholing would go on to
teach. Yeshe-O had not simply built a monument to his own reign; he
had built the institutional infrastructure an entire religious
revival would run on for generations afterward.
