Rinchen Zangpo, who lived from 958 to 1055, spent decades of that long
life translating Buddhist scripture into Tibetan and, in the process,
became as central to Guge's religious program as Yeshe-O himself. The
texts he translated and revised over the course of his career - work
that continued for decades rather than being completed in any single
burst - eventually filled substantial portions of the Tibetan Buddhist
canon, giving the kingdom's revival a body of scripture in its own
language rather than dependence on texts only a handful of scholars
could read in the original Sanskrit.

## A temple at Nyarma, and a network beyond it

Among the temples associated with this program was one at Nyarma, in
the territory of Mar yul - later the core of the kingdom of Ladakh -
built around a central statue of the Buddha Dipamkara. It stood
alongside a wider constellation of religious foundations across the
western Tibetan region: a temple at Khorchag in Purang, one at Tabo in
Spiti, others scattered through the smaller valleys of the frontier
country. Later Tibetan chronicles link the building of this network to
the period immediately following the assassination of one of Yeshe-O's
own religious officials - violence at the royal court apparently
coinciding with, rather than interrupting, a surge in temple
construction.

## A career longer than most kings' reigns

What makes Rinchen Zangpo's own role distinctive is simply how long he
was active. A near-century of life, most of it spent translating and
overseeing temple foundations across multiple reigns, meant that his
own work outlasted the specific kings who had first sponsored it. The
temples he helped found and the scriptures he translated remained
Guge's most durable legacy long after the kingdom's own later
political history stopped being the kind of thing anyone troubled to
write down.
