The Tibetan Empire ended, by tradition, with a single arrow. Langdarma,
the last king of the old line, was killed by a lama who had smeared his
own pony black, ridden into the royal presence disguised as a dancer,
and shot the king from close range during a performance staged for
that purpose - then fled, washing the charcoal from his mount at a
river crossing and turning his robe inside out to escape recognition.
Whatever the literal truth of the story, its aftermath was real enough:
with Langdarma's death the long line of Tibetan kings came to an end,
and the unified state they had ruled broke apart into competing local
chiefdoms.

## A kingdom rebuilt on the empire's own edge

The old royal line did not vanish along with the throne it had lost.
Members of the family moved west, into the high country of Ngari far
from the center of the collapsed empire, and rebuilt a kingdom there
on a much smaller scale. Kyide Nyima-gon, of that same royal
bloodline, is remembered as the founder of the line that would
eventually split into the separate western Tibetan kingdoms of Guge,
Purang and Ladakh - his own descendants dividing what had once been a
single inheritance among themselves within a few generations.

## Forts on the hills, fields on the plains

The country these successor states governed looked nothing like the
unified empire that had preceded them. A Tibetan saying from the
period after Langdarma's death captures the new arrangement plainly:
forts on the high ground, fields on the plains below, each protecting
and feeding the other in a landscape of small, locally rooted powers
rather than one central authority. Guge, when it emerged from this
fragmentation, was one part of that same pattern - a fortified refuge
kingdom built by a displaced royal family rather than a restoration of
what had been lost.
