Andrade was recalled from Tibet before 1630 to govern the wider Jesuit
mission-province of Goa, and the king's enemies waited only a short
time after his departure to act. Powerful military commanders - very
likely lamas themselves, since the profession did not bar such posts
- rose in open revolt, invited the king of Ladakh to intervene, and
offered him the crown outright. A Ladakhi army laid siege to
Tsaparang that same year.

## An impregnable town surrendered anyway

Tsaparang was built to withstand exactly this kind of siege: fortified
walls along the lower slopes of its hill, a second line of
fortifications on a plateau above, reachable only by a stairway cut
into the mountain's own interior, and a second rock-cut stairway
running more than four hundred meters down to the springs that
supplied it. The castle held a thousand firearms and ample powder and
shot. Loyal retainers urged the king to shelter behind those walls and
wait the Ladakhis out. His own brother, the Chief-Lama he had
stripped of revenue four years earlier, advised surrender instead -
on the promise that the king of Ladakh would take tribute and withdraw
once it was paid. After a month of siege the king followed his
brother's counsel. The king of Ladakh took no such mercy: he had the
king and his family seized and carried off to Leh, and the whole
kingdom passed under Ladakhi rule. Tsaparang's roughly four hundred
Christian converts were singled out afterward, many reduced to slavery
and forced to share their king's exile.

## What the garrison would not surrender

Not every part of Tsaparang went as quietly. A portion of the
garrison retreated into the castle's own inner defenses and held out
with enough determination that the victors eventually let them
withdraw under arms, with military honors, to the eastern Tibetan
province of Ü-Tsang - the one part of this defeat the record keeps as
a mark of pride rather than betrayal. The Jesuit mission itself was
sacked, though without loss of life, and its two resident priests were
taken prisoner to Leh before being released and sent back. Andrade's
own account of the whole catastrophe, sent from Goa on 4 February
1633, remains the fullest surviving record of a kingdom's actual
collapse - not the death of a religious figure decades earlier, but a
war over a rejected second marriage, an alienated brother's advice,
and a month-long siege that ended with the last king of Tsaparang
carried out of his own capital in chains.
