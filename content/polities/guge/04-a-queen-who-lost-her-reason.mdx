When the Portuguese Jesuit António de Andrade reached Tsaparang in
1624 - the first European to cross the Himalaya and enter Tibet - he
found a kingdom already worn down by a war with its own neighbor
Ladakh that had, by his own later account, run on and off for
eighteen years, ruining fields and mines the crown could no longer
work in peace. The war's origin was a succession crisis at the
Tsaparang court itself: the king's only son and heir, born around
1612, had come at the cost of his mother's sanity, and when two years
of failed cures convinced the king she would not recover, he broke
with Tibetan custom - which permitted no bigamy - and arranged a
second marriage to a sister of the king of Ladakh. The new queen was
turned back two days from Tsaparang on the king's own sudden order,
for reasons Andrade never learned; Ladakh answered the insult with war.

## A king who favored a foreign priest over his own brother

The king who received Andrade so warmly - remembered in later
missionary correspondence by titles rendered Chodapo or Chodagpo, and
identified by later scholarship with an inscription naming a King
Khri-bkra-shis-grangs-pa-lde reigning at Tsaparang between roughly
1600 and 1630 - used his own authority aggressively to promote the new
mission, stripping revenues from his own brother, the kingdom's
Chief-Lama, after the Chief-Lama admitted a hundred and thirty new
monks into the religious fraternity without royal leave. The king's
stated fear was that his brother was building a private army of monks
under religious cover; the practical effect was to turn the kingdom's
own Buddhist establishment, headed by his own kin, into a standing
grievance against him. Andrade's fellow missionaries wrote home in
1627 expecting Tsaparang to become one of the Society of Jesus's most
flourishing missions anywhere. They did not see what was accumulating
underneath that favor: a war Ladakh had not forgiven, and a religious
establishment, led by the king's own brother, that had not either.
