---
title: Holding the Hijaz
drafted_from: petry-1998
phase: formation
---

Muhammad ibn Tughj was granted Egypt in 935 and took the title *al-Ikhshid*, an
old Central Asian princely style from Farghana, where his family came from. The
arrangement was the Tulunid one repeated: an autonomous province, an army of
Turkish and Sudanese slave soldiers, the Abbasid name on the coin, and the
revenue kept at home.

What distinguishes the Ikhshidids is what they were holding the line against. The
Fatimids had been established in Ifriqiya since 909 and attempted Egypt three
times; the Ikhshidids beat them off each time. They also held the Hijaz, which
meant the Friday sermon at Mecca and Medina was preached in the Abbasid caliph's
name and not the Fatimid one — the most valuable symbolic ground in the Islamic
world, contested by two claimants to the same office.

The last effective ruler was not a member of the family at all but Kafur, an
Abyssinian eunuch bought as a slave who had been regent for two of Ibn Tughj's
sons and then ruled outright until 968. The Fatimids took Egypt the year after he
died, against a divided court and a failed harvest, and the symbolic ground went
with it.
