Abu al-Misk Kafur was bought as a slave, described in the sources as an
Abyssinian eunuch, and given charge of Ibn Tughj's sons. He governed Egypt as
regent for both of them and then, from 966, as ruler in his own name, with the
khutba pronounced for him.

His court is where al-Mutanabbi went after leaving Sayf al-Dawla at Aleppo, and
the poems he wrote about Kafur after failing to obtain the governorship he wanted
are among the most savage invective in Arabic. They are also the reason Kafur's
reputation reached later centuries in the shape it did, which is a caution worth
carrying: the most quotable source on a ruler is frequently a disappointed
client.

What Kafur actually did was hold Egypt, Syria and the Hijaz together for two
decades against Fatimid pressure and internal factions, at a time when the
Abbasid caliphate had been a Buyid possession for twenty years and could offer
nothing.

He died in 968. The Fatimids took Egypt the following year against a divided
court, a disputed succession and a failed Nile. This site's regions place the
Ikhshidids in the central lands and the Fatimids in the caliphates, and the edge
between them crosses that boundary — which is correct, because what changed in
969 was not only who governed Egypt but which caliph the Friday prayer named.
