The man who took Egypt in 935 had been a prisoner in Damascus with his father,
who died there. His route to power ran the length of the caliphate's collapse,
and the name he ruled under came from a valley in Central Asia.

## Three generations

Muhammad ibn Tughj came of a princely family in Ferghana, on the Jaxartes, whose
rulers bore the title *Ikhshid* the way the sovereigns of Persia and Tabaristan
were styled Kisra and Ispahbadh.

His grandfather Juff was among the Turkish officers al-Mu'tasim brought into
Iraq — the same recruitment that built Samarra and that eventually swallowed the
caliphate that made it. His father Tughj served with distinction in the armies of
Khumarawayh, the Tulunid ruler of Egypt, fought the Byzantines as commandant of
Tarsus, and was rewarded with the government of Syria. Then, Lane-Poole says,
the pride of success brought its punishment, and he ended his life in the prison
of Damascus.

Muhammad shared that captivity, and got himself released.

What followed was a career assembled out of the pieces of a disintegrating
state: service under Takin, command of the disorderly Hawf district in Lower
Egypt, a run of appointments in Syria where he earned the caliph's approval, and
in 930 the governorship of Damascus.

## Taking Egypt twice

He was nominated to Egypt in 933 and could not go, because Syria would not let
him leave. He was recognised as governor in the Friday prayers at Fustat that
year and sent a deputy to stand in for him while somebody else actually held the
post.

He came in person in 935, on a second nomination, and had to fight his way in.
The treasurer al-Madharani, who was the real power in Egypt, pushed the sitting
governor into resisting the appointment. They were beaten at Farama; the Syrian
fleet came up the Nile from Tinnis to Giza and commanded the capital until the
army arrived and took possession.

The title came afterwards. Four years into his rule the caliph gave him special
permission to use his ancestors' Ferghana style, and he became *the Ikhshid* —
a Central Asian royal title, granted by an Abbasid caliph, to the ruler of
Egypt. His coins were struck at Fustat, at Ramla in Palestine and at Damascus,
which is a fair map of what he held.

## What holding it looked like

The measure of the government is a negative one and Lane-Poole makes the point
sharply: in the eleven years of the Ikhshid's rule there is not a single
insurrection or disturbance on record, in a country that had spent the previous
two decades in continuous anarchy.

The army recognised its master. His Syrian troops overawed whatever disaffection
remained among Egyptians. He was cautious rather than brilliant — he would sign
a treaty, give up territory, even pay tribute, rather than prolong a doubtful
war. He is said to have gone in fear of assassination and taken extraordinary
precautions. He was also said to be so strong that no other man could draw his
bow, which is the kind of thing recorded about rulers people were relieved to
have.

The size given for his army — four hundred thousand, with eight thousand in the
bodyguard — is a chronicler's figure of the sort the period produces and is not a
count. What is not in doubt is that it was large enough to stop the Fatimids,
who had been driven back from Alexandria in his first year and did not seriously
try again while he lived.

## The world he was operating in

By this time the caliph's temporal power had simply gone. The Buyids held Persia,
the Samanids the lands beyond the Oxus, the Hamdanids upper Mesopotamia, and a
succession of Turkish emirs fought over Baghdad and the office of gaoler to the
commander of the faithful.

In that setting the Ikhshid's achievement is exact and limited. He did not build
anything new. He took the province his father had governed and the province his
father's employer had ruled, held them both with an army raised on the same
principle his grandfather had been recruited under, and kept them quiet for
eleven years — which in the 940s was more than anyone else in the Islamic world
was managing.
