title: A negotiated surrender

Kafur died in 968 and the Ikhshidid position collapsed inside eighteen months,
not because anyone beat it but because three things arrived at once.

The Nile came in low, twice. Egypt is entirely a function of the flood, as this
site's Fatimid page records the shidda demonstrating a century later, and a
famine in 968 emptied the treasury and the granaries at the same time. The
court divided between factions supporting an eleven-year-old Ikhshidid and
various commanders. And the Fatimids, who had spent fifty-nine years and three
failed invasions on this problem, had a general named Jawhar, an army of Kutama
Berbers, and enough grain to feed Egypt through the crisis.

What happened in 969 is one of the few conquests in this corpus that was
substantially negotiated. Jawhar came east; the Egyptian notables and the
jurists of Fustat came out to meet him; and terms were agreed in writing before
he entered — safety of persons and property, the maintenance of the Sunni legal
schools and their judges, no forced conversion, the restoration of the water
supply, and the endowments respected. There was a brief resistance and then
there was not.

The Fatimids kept the terms in substance for two centuries, which this site's
Fatimid page records: Egypt stayed largely Sunni and substantially Coptic
throughout Isma'ili rule, and the regime made little sustained attempt to
change that.

Two things this leaves.

The Hijaz went with Egypt, immediately. The sermon at Mecca and Medina was
being pronounced for the Fatimid caliph within a year, and the argument the
previous chapter describes the Ikhshidids winning for thirty years was lost in
one season by a bad harvest.

And Egypt's autonomy, which the Tulunids had demonstrated and the Ikhshidids
had confirmed, became permanent in a form neither had intended. Jawhar founded
Cairo just north of Fustat that same year — a palace city, on the same instinct
and roughly the same ground as the quarter this site's Tulunid page has the
Abbasids burning in 905. What the Tulunids proved was that Egypt could pay for
itself. What 969 established was that whoever held it did not have to pretend
otherwise.
