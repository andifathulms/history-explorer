A little over a year after Kafur died, the Fatimid army entered Fustat. With
the fall of the last Ikhshid, Egypt stopped being counted among the provinces
of the eastern caliphate, and stayed out of them for two hundred years.

What the dynasty left behind is very little. The physical record Lane-Poole
could assemble comes to one inscription — Kafur's, on the east wall of the
Haram at Jerusalem — and the coins, struck at Fustat, Ramla, Damascus, Homs and
Tiberias, which are the map of what the dynasty held. Everything else about the
Ikhshidids is in books written afterwards by people who worked for somebody
else.

## The verdict of 1901

Lane-Poole closes his account of the whole period from the conquest to 969 with
a dismissal, and it is worth quoting because it is wrong in an instructive way.

Three hundred and thirty years after the Arabs entered the Nile valley, he
writes, Egypt had produced no commanding men of its own. The few who counted —
Ibn Tulun, the Ikhshid, Kafur — were foreigners, and even they were only a step
above the ordinary official. They attempted no great expansion, they did not
extinguish the Fatimids next door, and though they had fleets they never used
them against Europe. Their public works were almost entirely in the capital and
for their own pleasure. Egypt was still well behind Baghdad, Damascus and
Cordoba; al-Azhar did not yet exist; no Egyptian Muslim had yet written poetry,
history or criticism of the first rank.

Nearly every clause is factually defensible and the judgement built out of them
is the wrong shape.

## What was actually handed over

The complaint is that these rulers did not conquer anything and did not build
outside their capital. It is worth asking what they did instead.

They kept a rich province quiet through a run of earthquakes, a fire that
destroyed seventeen hundred houses, several bad Niles, a Qarmatian raid, the
loss of a pilgrim caravan of twenty thousand camels, and a Nubian invasion of
the Sa'id — without a revolt. They held Syria against the strongest warlord of
the age. They fed the holy cities. They paid an army out of Egyptian revenue
for a century, which was the thing Ibn Tulun had discovered was possible and
which nobody in Egypt afterwards forgot.

The state the Fatimids walked into in 969 — an intact revenue system, an army
habit, a governing class used to being governed from Fustat rather than from
Iraq — is the state these men assembled. Within a generation of taking it over,
the new dynasty founded al-Azhar and made Cairo the thing Lane-Poole complains
Egypt had not yet become.

It is possible to be the necessary condition for somebody else's golden age and
get no credit for it, and the surviving evidence makes that outcome more
likely, because what a careful administration leaves is quiet years, and quiet
years do not leave monuments. A dynasty that governed Egypt and Syria for
thirty-four years is legible today in five mints and a line of writing on a
wall in another country.
