Abu'l-Misk Kafur — "Musky Camphor" — was an Abyssinian slave bought from an
oilman for a sum Lane-Poole converts as less than ten pounds. The Ikhshid,
having noticed what he was, made him tutor and governor to his two sons, and
the relation of tutor and ward lasted as long as they lived.

The princes were given an allowance of four hundred thousand dinars and told to
enjoy themselves and stay out of government. They did, with scarcely a
struggle, according to taste — the harem or the Qur'an — and died in luxurious
obscurity. In 965 Kafur took the position openly, with the caliph's approval,
as *ustadh*, master, of Egypt and its dependencies.

## The detail on the coins

He struck no coins in his own name. His currency ran in the caliph's name
alone.

That is the whole of his political method in one fact. The man was governing
Egypt, Syria as far as Aleppo and Tarsus, and the holy cities; he had fought
Sayf al-Dawla to a standstill and had the tribute of 945 cancelled; and he put
nothing of himself on the money. An African slave running the richest province
in Islam did not need the caliph's permission, and took care never to be found
without it.

Egypt in his years had terrific earthquakes, a fire that destroyed seventeen
hundred houses in Fustat, bad Niles and the scarcity that follows them, a
Qarmatian raid into Syria, the capture of an Egyptian pilgrim caravan of twenty
thousand camels, and a Nubian irruption that carried fire and famine through
the Sa'id. It did not revolt once. The Ikhshid had got the country into order
and Kafur knew how to keep it there.

## The court

He collected poets and critics and had them argue in front of him in the
evenings, or read him the history of the caliphs of old, and he paid enormously
for it.

A poet who explained in choice verse that the frequent earthquakes were caused
by Egypt dancing for joy at Kafur's virtues was thrown a thousand dinars. A
sharif of the Prophet's family who picked up his riding whip found himself the
owner of a baggage train. The daily provision for the kitchen ran to a hundred
sheep, a hundred lambs, two hundred and fifty geese, five hundred fowls, a
thousand pigeons and other birds, and a hundred jars of sweets; fifty skins of
liquor were allowed to the servants alone. He drank quince cider, and the judge
of Asyut sent fifty thousand quinces every season to make it.

Al-Mutanabbi, the greatest Arabic poet of the century, was among his intimates
for two years, praised him extravagantly, left, and spent the rest of his life
satirising him.

## Reading the descriptions

Both of the surviving portraits are worthless as evidence and valuable as
something else.

Al-Mutanabbi's praise was bought and his invective was revenge, and neither
tells you what Kafur was like; what they tell you is what a great poet thought
would wound a Black ruler in tenth-century Egypt, which turns out to be exactly
what you would expect.

The nineteenth-century European account has the same problem from the other
direction. Lane-Poole gives the facts carefully — the coins, the campaigns, the
kitchen accounts — and then explains that when in power Kafur showed the love
of luxury and ease that marks the Black in office, and that like all Blacks he
delighted in music. The kitchen figures are checkable. The sentences around
them are a British curator in 1901 telling you about 1901.

What is left when both filters are removed is a man bought for less than ten
pounds who governed Egypt for twenty-two years, kept it quiet through
earthquakes, fire and famine, held Syria against the most formidable warlord of
the age, and was careful enough never to put his own name on a coin.
