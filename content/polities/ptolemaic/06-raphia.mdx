In 217 Antiochus III invaded southern Syria and Ptolemy IV met him at Raphia,
near Gaza, with an army that included about twenty thousand native Egyptians
trained and equipped as phalangites. The Ptolemaic army had been Greek and
Macedonian by design for a century; arming Egyptians in that number was a
departure made because the kingdom did not have enough Greeks.

<Figure id="ptolemy-iv" />

The battle was won. Antiochus withdrew and Coele-Syria stayed Ptolemaic for
another seventeen years. And within a decade Upper Egypt was in open revolt, the
Thebaid had seceded, and two Egyptian pharaohs — Horwennefer and his successor
Ankhwennefer — were ruling from Thebes with priestly backing and their own regnal
years. It took the crown twenty years to recover the south.

<Figure id="raphia-decree" />

Ancient and modern writers have connected the two events, cautiously and in
different ways: the men who fought at Raphia went home having discovered
something about the relation between the Greek army and Egyptian manpower. The
connection is not provable and the revolt had its own causes — taxation, the
grain price, temple grievances that Manning's account puts at the centre. What
is not in dispute is the sequence, and that the dynasty drew a conclusion from
it. Native troops were used again, but never again in that proportion, and never
again decisively.

<Figure id="medinet-habu" />

Raphia is the kingdom's last victory over a Seleucid army, and it belongs in the
record as a hinge rather than as a triumph, because of what followed it. What
Raphia altered was the crown's confidence in its own country.
