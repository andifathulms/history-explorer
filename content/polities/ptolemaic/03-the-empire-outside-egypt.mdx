---
title: The empire outside Egypt
drafted_from: holbl-2001
phase: peak
---

For the first century the Ptolemies were a naval and imperial power, not a
kingdom on a river. They held Cyprus, Cyrenaica, southern Syria and Phoenicia,
much of the Anatolian coast, and at times the Aegean islands and the ports of
Thrace. The fleet was the largest in the Mediterranean. Under Ptolemy III, who
reached the Euphrates in the Third Syrian War, the reach was as far as it ever
went.

The empire existed for reasons that were fiscal and material rather than
prestigious. Egypt has no timber. A naval power that grows no trees needs
Lebanon, and holding Coele-Syria — the coast and hinterland from Gaza to the
Orontes — is what made a Ptolemaic navy possible at all. The overseas
possessions also produced silver, which Egypt does not have, and the harbours
that carried the grain trade.

Six Syrian Wars were fought with the Seleucids over that territory between 274
and 168, and the two dynasties are the closest thing in this corpus to a
permanent structural rivalry between successors of the same empire. This site
records no succession edge between them, because there is none to record: they
were partitioned from the same parent and fought about it for two hundred years,
which is a relationship the vocabulary has one type for — `partitioned from`,
pointing back at Alexander — and no type for pointing at each other.

The outward-facing kingdom is also where the ruler cult was for. Ptolemy II
deified his parents, then himself and his sister-wife Arsinoe as the Theoi
Adelphoi, with a dynastic priesthood at Alexandria whose holder's name dated
every document in the kingdom. Greeks in Cyprus and the Aegean were being asked
to be loyal to a god, because they could not be asked to be loyal to an Egyptian
king and would not have been loyal to a Macedonian one at that distance.
