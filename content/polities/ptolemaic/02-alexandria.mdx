---
title: The city by Egypt
drafted_from: holbl-2001
phase: expansion

Alexandria was Greek, deliberately and formally. Its official style was
Alexandria *ad Aegyptum* — Alexandria by Egypt, not in it — and the phrase was
not a geographical nicety. It had a Greek constitution, Greek magistrates and
Greek citizenship that most people living in Egypt could not obtain, and it sat
at the edge of the country on a strip between the sea and a lake, connected to
the Nile by canal.

It was also the largest and richest city in the Mediterranean world for most of
three centuries, and the Ptolemies spent on it in a way few dynasties have ever
spent on a city. The Museum, a state-funded research institution with salaried
resident scholars and no teaching obligations. The Library, whose project was to
acquire a copy of every book in Greek and which pursued it by confiscating the
scrolls off arriving ships. The Pharos, one of the tallest structures in the
world. A royal quarter that covered something like a quarter of the city.

What was produced there is wildly disproportionate to the kingdom's political
weight: Euclid's
*Elements*; Eratosthenes measuring the circumference of the earth to within a
few per cent; Aristarchus proposing that the earth goes round the sun eighteen
centuries before it was believed; Herophilus and Erasistratus dissecting human
bodies, which almost nobody else in antiquity did; the Septuagint, the Hebrew
scriptures translated into Greek for a Jewish community that had stopped reading
Hebrew. Callimachus catalogued the library in a hundred and twenty scrolls.

The Ptolemies paid for it, and it is worth being clear about why. A Macedonian
dynasty ruling Egypt needed to be the centre of the Greek world, because the
alternative was to be a provincial outpost of it. The Library was a claim to
legitimacy as much as a collection, and by the measure of what survived it is
about the most successful claim of that kind anybody ever made.
