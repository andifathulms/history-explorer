Egypt did not become an ordinary Roman province. It became the personal
possession of the emperor, governed by a prefect of equestrian rank, closed to
senators without permission, and run as the grain supply of the city of Rome —
which is what the Ptolemies had built the apparatus to do, working for somebody
else. The temples kept operating and the priests kept carving the pharaoh's
titulary on the walls, with a Roman emperor's name inside the cartouche. The
last hieroglyphic inscription anyone cut was at Philae in the fourth century AD,
more than four hundred years after the last Ptolemy died.

## What that means

Two things end at different times, and the gap between them is the whole point.

<Figure id="kom-ombo" />

The dynasty ended in 30 BC. The state it had built did not end at all. The
Ptolemies had spent three centuries constructing an apparatus for extracting a
grain surplus from the Nile valley and moving it out through Alexandria —
surveyors, granaries, tax registers, a monetised economy sealed off behind a
closed currency, a priesthood paid to certify the ruler. Rome took the apparatus
intact and pointed it at Rome.

That is why Egypt was not made an ordinary province. An ordinary province had a
senatorial governor and could be used as a power base; Egypt was too valuable
and too easy to hold, so the emperor kept it personally and let no senator enter
it without permission. The machine that had fed the Ptolemaic court fed the city
of Rome for the next six hundred years, and the men running it were the same
class of Greek-speaking officials working from the same kind of documents.

## And the other end

The temples are the stranger survival, because nobody made them continue.

Egyptian priests had been carving a pharaoh's five-part titulary on temple walls
for three thousand years, and they had done it for Persian kings and Macedonian
ones without much apparent difficulty. They went on doing it for Roman emperors
who never came, whose names went inside the cartouche in hieroglyphs, in a
script the emperors could not read, in a ceremony asserting that the man in Rome
was the son of Ra and had defeated chaos on Egypt's behalf.

The last hieroglyphic inscription anyone cut was at Philae, in the fourth
century AD. That is more than four hundred years after the last Ptolemy died,
and something over three thousand years after the convention began.

<Figure id="philae-graffito" />

The Ptolemies were the last dynasty to take that system seriously enough to be
crowned inside it. What outlasted them was not their kingdom, which Rome
absorbed in an afternoon, but the two machines they had inherited and kept
running: the one that counted the grain, and the one that told the walls who the
king was.
