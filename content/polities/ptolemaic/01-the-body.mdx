Alexander died at Babylon in 323 and his generals divided the administration of
an empire they all agreed still existed and had a king. Ptolemy son of Lagos, a
Macedonian officer of no royal blood, asked for Egypt, which was the least
glamorous of the great satrapies and the most defensible: one river, two
approaches, and the richest agriculture in the world.

<Figure id="ptolemy-i" />

Two years later the funeral cortege carrying Alexander's embalmed body west
towards Macedon was intercepted in Syria — by arrangement or by force, the
sources differ — and diverted to Egypt. Ptolemy buried Alexander first at
Memphis and then in Alexandria, in a tomb that became the centre of a cult and a
tourist attraction for six hundred years. It was among the most effective
political acts of the period. A satrap holding a province for a dead man's
family had the dead man.

<Figure id="serapeum" />

<Figure id="alexander-sarcophagus" />

In 305, after the last serious attempt to reassemble the empire failed, Ptolemy
took the diadem and the title of king, and the other successors did the same in
the same year. The kingdom can be dated from either 323 or 305, both are in use,
and the difference between them is exactly the
distinction the first twenty years turn on: whether he was holding Egypt for
somebody, and when he stopped saying so.

The dynasty he founded lasted two hundred and seventy-five years. Every male
ruler of it was called Ptolemy.
