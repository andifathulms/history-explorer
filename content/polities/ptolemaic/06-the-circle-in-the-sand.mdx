title: The circle in the sand

Ptolemy IV died in 204 and left a six-year-old. Antiochus III and Philip V of
Macedon are said to have agreed to divide the overseas possessions between them,
and whether or not the pact was real the partition happened. At Panion in 200
Antiochus destroyed the Ptolemaic field army and took Coele-Syria permanently.
The timber, the silver, the ports and the land frontier went with it, and what
remained was Egypt, Cyprus and Cyrenaica.

Thirty-two years later Antiochus IV invaded Egypt itself, defeated the Ptolemaic
army, took Memphis and the king, and was preparing to besiege Alexandria. He was
met outside the city by Gaius Popillius Laenas, a Roman envoy carrying a decree
of the senate requiring him to leave. Antiochus asked for time to consult his
advisers. Popillius drew a circle in the sand around him with a stick and told
him to answer before he stepped out of it. He agreed, and withdrew.

That is the Day of Eleusis, 168 BC, and it is the hinge on this page. The
Ptolemaic kingdom lasted another hundred and thirty-eight years — longer than
most polities in this corpus manage in total — and it lasted because Rome had
decided a weak Egypt was preferable to a Seleucid one. The dynasty's
independence became a Roman policy rather than a Ptolemaic achievement, and
everyone involved understood it that way immediately.

What follows is a century of civil war inside the family, conducted with Rome as
the referee and eventually as the creditor. Brothers and sisters ruled jointly
and deposed each other; Alexandrian mobs killed kings; Cyrene was left to Rome
in a will and Cyprus annexed on a pretext; Ptolemy XII was restored to his own
throne by a Roman army he had borrowed the money to hire, at interest, from a
Roman financier. By the time Cleopatra VII inherited, the kingdom's independence
was a question of which Roman.
