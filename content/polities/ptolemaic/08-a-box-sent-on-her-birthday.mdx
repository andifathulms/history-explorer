Ptolemy VI Philometor spent much of his reign as a captive of
circumstance rather than a ruler of anything. Seleucid Antiochus IV
overran Egypt in 170, captured Philometor at Memphis, and had himself
crowned Pharaoh over him - only for Alexandria to raise Philometor's
own teenage brother to the throne in his place as a rival the invader
could not simply co-opt. That younger brother took the name Euergetes,
and history remembers him as Euergetes II, or by the nickname his own
cruelty earned him later: Physcon, "the Potbelly."

## Two brothers and a sister sharing one throne

Rome's intervention - Popillius Laenas's circle drawn in the sand at
Eleusis in 168 - saved Philometor's throne from Antiochus, but it did
not resolve the rivalry between the brothers. For years Philometor,
Physcon and their sister Cleopatra II ruled jointly, an arrangement
that satisfied nobody and produced open warfare between the brothers
more than once. When Philometor died in 145, Physcon inherited alone -
and moved immediately to secure his position by marrying his own
sister and former co-ruler Cleopatra II, and then, not content with
that, marrying her daughter Cleopatra III as well while remaining
married to the mother.

## A birthday present sent in a box

Physcon's fifteen-year reign grew so hated for its cruelty that in 130
he fled Alexandria for Cyprus, taking with him his own teenage son by
Cleopatra II, a boy named Memphites. The Alexandrians promptly restored
Cleopatra II to the throne in his absence. Physcon's revenge was
recorded by multiple ancient sources with something close to
disbelief: he had his own son murdered, the body cut into pieces, and
the box delivered to Cleopatra II as a gift for her birthday. Cleopatra
raised an army against him regardless, lost the resulting battle, and
fled to Syria - while Physcon, remarkably, was eventually reconciled
with the same sister he had mutilated her son to spite, ruling
alongside her again in his final years as though the crime had never
happened.

## A kingdom bequeathed away in pieces

Physcon's death in 116 opened decades of fragmentation rather than
stability. Cyrenaica, ruled separately since Physcon willed it to an
illegitimate son, Ptolemy Apion, passed to Rome outright when Apion
died childless in 96 - the first piece of the old Ptolemaic empire
formally annexed rather than merely dominated. In Alexandria itself
the throne passed between rival siblings and cousins - Ptolemy IX,
expelled and restored twice; Ptolemy X, drowned at sea fleeing a
mutiny his own mercenaries raised over unpaid wages; and finally
Ptolemy XI, sent back from Rome by Sulla to marry his own stepmother
and co-ruler, Berenice III, and murder her nineteen days into the
marriage - a crime the Alexandrian mob answered within hours by
dragging him from the palace to the gymnasium and killing him in turn,
ending the dynasty's legitimate line in a single year, 80 BC.

## What the century actually settled

By the time Ptolemy XII "Auletes" - an illegitimate claimant whose own
right to rule rested more on the absence of any better candidate than
on any clean succession - took the throne the mob had left vacant,
the question that mattered was no longer which Ptolemy would rule but
which Roman faction would back him. Auletes borrowed the money to buy
Julius Caesar's recognition at ruinous interest from a Roman financier,
and spent much of his reign servicing a debt his own kingdom's revenues
could barely cover. His daughter, Cleopatra VII, inherited a throne
whose independence had already become, in every way that mattered, a
question of Roman convenience rather than Ptolemaic strength.
