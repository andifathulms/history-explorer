Joseon had gone over to the Manchus once already, in 1619, when a
Korean general sent to help the Ming abandoned his own emperor's ally
mid-battle on royal instructions rather than risk his troops against a
rising power. It was not enough. In the winter of 1636 the Manchu
emperor invaded outright, determined to end Korea's continued loyalty
to the Ming for good.

## A king who could not reach the island he meant to reach

Manchu cavalry crossed the border and reached the outskirts of Seoul
within days, moving so fast that the court barely had time to react.
The king tried to reach the island fortress of Kanghwa, historically
impregnable even to the Mongols, but Manchu riders had already cut the
road; his own advisor Ch'oe Myŏng-gil rode out with ten cattle and ten
casks of wine to stall the enemy while the king doubled back toward the
mountain fortress of Namhan instead. A storm of sleet on the road
nearly killed him before he even reached it - the king, on foot because
the roads were too slippery for his horse, had to be carried the last
distance in a hastily built litter. Only six of his retinue were still
with him when the gates of Namhan finally closed behind him that night.

## A siege endured on half rations and a superstition

The Manchu army encircled Namhan - a fortress wall some ten miles
around - within a single night, and what followed was weeks of siege
under a cold severe enough that horses and cattle inside the walls
died of it. The king gave his own blankets to his soldiers and slept
in his ordinary clothes; his food narrowed to rice with a single
chicken leg or wing. On the last day of the old year, magpies were
seen building a nest in a tree near the king's own quarters, and the
starving garrison seized on it as a hopeful omen - a measure, the
source notes dryly, of how far reduced to superstition a trapped court
could become. Provincial relief armies tried repeatedly to break the
siege and failed each time, one of them destroyed when its own
gunpowder store exploded by accident in the middle of the battle.

## Two men who drank wine with the king before walking to their deaths

When Kanghwa itself fell and the royal family there was captured, the
king's own son wrote to him confirming it, and resistance inside Namhan
collapsed at last. The Manchus demanded that the officials who had most
strenuously opposed surrender be handed over for punishment; two of
them, O Tal-ch'e and Yun Chip, volunteered rather than let the argument
tear the court apart further. The king had them brought before him,
wept openly, and said he was ashamed to look them in the face; they
answered that there was no cause for mourning, since the fault was
their own. He had a eunuch pour them wine with his own hands - the
highest honor he had left to give - and promised to see their families
cared for before they set out for the Manchu camp. The Manchu emperor,
satisfied by the gesture, ultimately spared both men's lives and kept
them under guard rather than executing them.
