King Yŏngjo, whose long reign is remembered as one of Joseon's most
stable and productive, spent his final years destroying his own son -
a catastrophe the source traces to court factionalism and personal
malice working together on a suspicious old man rather than to any
real crime.

<Figure id="js-yeongjo" />

## A plot built from mourning clothes and false tears

The Crown Prince had grown distant from the dominant Noron faction that
controlled his father's court, and its leaders feared his eventual
succession would restore their rivals to power. His own sister, wanting
her husband made king instead, and a palace woman with a private
grudge joined the scheme against him. Conspirators instructed the
prince's own guards to steal from the palace and blame him for it when
questioned. When the king happened upon a hidden pit filled with
mourning clothes, he was told his son had prepared them in impatient
anticipation of his death; when he found the same palace woman in
tears, she claimed the prince had insulted her. Piece by piece, the
fabricated evidence convinced the king his son was plotting to seize
the throne by force.

## A father who would not let his son die out of his sight

On a stifling summer day the king summoned the Crown Prince to answer
for his supposed treason. When the prince denied any wrongdoing, the
king, already convinced, flew into a rage and ordered him to die on the
spot, drawing his own sword and forcing the assembled courtiers to draw
theirs as well. The prince asked only to be allowed to die in his own
apartments rather than before his father's eyes; the king refused. The
prince tried to strangle himself with his own sash - the horrified
court cut him down against the king's own shouted orders - then tried
to dash his brains out against a stone, an official throwing himself
between the prince and the rock to take the blows instead. When the
prince's own small son was brought in to plead for his father's life,
the king kicked the child back into the arms of those who had carried
him in.

## Seven days in a rice chest

With no other option left, the king ordered a heavy wooden chest
brought and commanded his son to climb inside. In the dark, no one
noticed a knot-hole in the box's side until an official spoke to the
prince through it; when the prince, overcome by heat, asked for water
and a fan, they were passed in to him - until someone reported this
kindness to the king, who had the hole boarded over and banished the
man responsible. The prince was left sealed in the box to starve, a
palace servant striking it with a stone each day. On the fourth day the
prince asked only to be left in peace, dizzy; by the seventh there was
no answer at all, and the servant bored through to find the body cold.
He was given the posthumous name Sado. The source records, without
further comment, that his father never once expressed sorrow for what
he had done in the fifteen years he lived afterward - and that Sado's
own grandson would one day inherit the throne his father died accused
of trying to seize too soon.
