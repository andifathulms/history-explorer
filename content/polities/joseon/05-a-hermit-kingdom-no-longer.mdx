For most of three centuries after the Japanese invasion, Joseon's own
policy toward the outside world settled into a defensive isolation that
foreign observers came to call the hermit kingdom. That posture broke in
1875, when a Korean shore battery fired on the Japanese gunboat Unyo
off Kang-wha, mistaking its foreign dress and unfamiliar flag for a
European threat rather than a Japanese one. Japan's own government was
split over how to respond — one faction, led by Saigo of Satsuma,
wanted war outright; the other favored a treaty, backed by the threat
of one.

## An embassy backed by gunboats

The peace faction prevailed, and in 1876 a Japanese naval and military
expedition of some eight hundred men arrived off Kang-wha to press the
point diplomatically rather than by force outright. China, sounded out
by Japan beforehand, disclaimed any responsibility for Korea's actions
and all but conceded the peninsula's independence in doing so — a
signal Korea's own dominant court faction took as license to negotiate
directly rather than wait for a Chinese lead that was not coming.

## A treaty that ended isolation without asking whether Korea wanted it to

Korean and Japanese commissioners argued over questions of protocol and
imperial titulature before turning to the practical dispute that had
started it all - the shore battery's mistaken attack - and by February
27th, 1876, a treaty of peace and friendship was ratified over the
objections of Korea's own retired regent, who had pressed to the last
for no agreement at all. Korea was, in the source's own words, "a
hermit no longer." The treaty opened trade and diplomatic relations
with a foreign power for the first time in centuries, on terms Japan
had written and Korea had mostly just accepted.

## An opening that widened rather than closed

What followed was not a single relationship but a scramble: within two
decades Russian, American and other foreign advisers, concessions and
interests would compete for influence at a court no longer able to
regulate the pace of its own opening to the outside world. The treaty
of 1876 did not decide who would end up dominant in Korea - it only
guaranteed that the question would now be settled by outsiders as much
as by the Koreans themselves.
