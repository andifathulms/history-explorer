Two centuries without a serious land war had left Joseon almost
entirely unready for what Hideyoshi's Japan launched against it in
1592. Japanese armies swept north with little effective resistance,
took the capital, and drove the court to the Chinese frontier while a
Ming counter-invasion was organized to answer the appeal for help. On
land the war ground on for years through sieges and negotiations that
repeatedly collapsed; at sea, it turned almost entirely on the
performance of a single Korean admiral.

## An admiral who answered a call that was not his to answer

When a Japanese fleet threatened Kyŭng-sang Province, its own admiral
prepared to scuttle his ships and flee rather than fight. Yi Sun-sin,
commanding the neighboring province's navy, needed no persuading to
help: "Is not Kyŭng-sang Province as much the country of our king as
Chŭl-la?" He sailed to join forces, and at Ok-po used fire arrows and
the advantage of the wind to burn twenty-six enemy ships in his first
engagement of the war — a victory the source calls the decisive turn
of the entire conflict, since it severed the Japanese armies ashore
from their own supply and reinforcement.

## A shell of iron and a hull built for speed

Much of Yi's success rested on a vessel of his own design, the
Kwi-sŭn or "Tortoise Boat": a hull roofed in curved iron plate bristling
with spikes against boarders, armed with firing ports on every side,
and fast enough to run down fleeing ships one at a time rather than let
any escape. Impervious to the fire arrows that had worked against
ordinary wooden hulls, it anticipated the ironclad warship by close to
three centuries, and its reputation alone was enough to make fleeing
Japanese sailors declare the vessel could not be of human workmanship.

## A war fought to attrition rather than decision

Six years of naval defeats, sieges, forged surrender letters and failed
truces wore both sides down without producing a clean resolution on
land — the Chinese army that had come to Joseon's aid proved, in its
own way, nearly as difficult to manage as the invaders it had come to
expel. What finally ended the war was not a Korean or Chinese victory
on the peninsula, but events on the other side of the strait entirely.
