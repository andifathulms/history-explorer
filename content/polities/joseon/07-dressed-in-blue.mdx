The terms the Manchu emperor finally offered Joseon in early 1637 read
less like a peace treaty than an inventory: gold, silver, tens of
thousands of bags of rice, thousands of bolts of silk and cotton, bows,
swords, tiger and otter and squirrel skins by the hundred, named down
to the piece. Along with the tribute came political terms that ended
Joseon's world at a stroke - the Chinese calendar abandoned for the
Manchu one, embassies to the Ming stopped outright, the crown prince
and the prime minister's own son sent north as hostages, no more
fortresses to be built without permission. The Koreans, the source
notes, never seriously attempted to deliver more than a fraction of
the tribute demanded, but the political terms held.

## A gate chosen for its dishonor

The surrender ceremony itself was specified down to its symbolism. When
Joseon's envoys asked whether the king might wear his royal robes, the
Manchu general refused: he was to come out dressed in blue, the color
associated with the east and therefore with Korea's own position as
the "Eastern Country." When they asked whether he might leave by the
South Gate, the answer was blunter still - no one who had done wrong
was permitted to leave by the honorable gate. He would leave by the
West Gate instead.

## A fog, a blue robe, and a bow struck against the back of the head

The surrender took place on the last day of the first month of 1637,
under a heavy morning fog. The West Gate of Namhan swung open and the
king came out with the Crown Prince, both dressed in blue exactly as
ordered, followed by soldiers gaunt from weeks of near-starvation who
would have defended the walls to the end had the king given the word
instead. Manchu cavalry lined the road on both sides the whole way
down to the valley - drawn up, the king was told when he asked in
alarm, purely in his honor. At the base of the hill, under a
yellow-silk awning, the Manchu emperor sat enthroned on a raised
platform with trumpeters arrayed in front of him. The king dismounted
and was led to the dais by the general who had directed the siege.
Reaching the entrance, he bowed three times and struck his own hand
against the back of his head in the prescribed gesture of submission,
then entered and bowed again on a mat laid before the emperor himself -
the final motion of a kingdom's formal transfer from Ming vassalage to
Manchu, performed in the mud below the same fortress where its king
had just spent a winter starving rather than perform it.
