Sejong, the fourth son of King T'ă-jong, was proclaimed heir only after
his own elder brother proved too dissolute to trust with the succession
and was banished. T'ă-jong retired in his favor in 1419, having spent
his own reign forcing through reforms his son would inherit the benefit
of rather than the risk. Sejong reigned for over three decades and left
behind, by his own government's own account, the single most
consequential act of any Korean king before or since.

## A script built for a language it was never given

For two thousand years Korean had been written only in borrowed Chinese
characters, a system so demanding that literacy remained the property
of a narrow class. Earlier attempts to ease the burden — a scholar's
diacritical marks in Silla, another effort late in Goryeo's own reign —
had never taken hold widely. Sejong set out to solve the problem
outright: not by modifying Chinese characters but by inventing an
entirely new phonetic alphabet, one built on the insight that a vowel
and its consonant belonged together as "mother and child" in every
syllable. Two scholars, Sin Suk-ju and Sŭng Sam-mun, carried the
technical work forward, consulting a banished Chinese scholar in
Liao-tung no fewer than thirteen times in the process.

## A dictionary printed in the new script

The finished system, called ŏn-mun, was compiled into a dictionary
titled the Hun-min Chŏng-ŭm and printed under Sejong's own
supervision from a building he had erected inside the palace grounds
specifically for the project. Whatever debate later scholars would have
over how much the alphabet owed to Sanskrit encountered through
Buddhist texts, the practical effect was immediate and permanent: for
the first time, a Korean of any station could learn to read and write
in a system built for the sound of their own language rather than
borrowed wholesale from someone else's.

## A reign that closed on its own accomplishment

Sejong died in 1450 having reoriented, in one stroke, what literacy in
Korea would mean for every generation that followed. His son and
successor, by contrast, reigned only two years and spent much of it
prostrate with grief — a reminder, even at the dynasty's own high
point, of how quickly one king's achievement could give way to another's
limitations.
