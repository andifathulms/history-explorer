By 1895 Japan's own minister at Seoul, Miura Goro, had concluded that
Queen Min's influence at court stood squarely against Japanese
interests and against the reform program Japan had been sponsoring
there. He arranged with Korea's own retired regent, the Tă-wŭn-gun, to
force a change at court, and before dawn on the eighth of October
Japanese-organized forces entered the palace, searched out the Queen,
and killed her.

## A trial that convicted no one

Japan's own government, forced by international outcry to investigate,
tried Miura and dozens of others before a court in Hiroshima. The
tribunal's own published findings laid out the plot in granular detail
- the conferences at the legation, the pledges extracted from the
Tă-wŭn-gun, the cover story invented to explain a suspicious visit to
his residence - and still concluded there was insufficient evidence to
convict anyone of the killing itself. Miura was recalled from his post,
and the affair ended there so far as any formal accounting went.

## A king who no longer felt safe in his own palace

The assassination left the Korean court in a state the source describes
as one of surrounded fear, uncertain which of its own attendants might
be complicit in what had happened and unable to trust its own security
inside the palace walls. The king eventually found refuge for a time at
the Russian legation, and in the years that followed Russian influence
at court grew rapidly - military instructors, financial advisers, and
a newly assertive Russian minister who pushed for concessions with a
bluntness that alarmed the other foreign powers watching Seoul.

## An empire declared while the declaring was still possible

Amid this jostling for influence, Ko-jong made his own move. Judging
that Korea now answered to no foreign suzerain in any formal sense, he
went to the Imperial Altar on October 17th, 1897 and was crowned Emperor
of Tai-han. The treaty powers recognized the new status within months, placing
Korea, on paper, as an equal of China and Japan rather than a tributary
of either. Russian and Japanese rivalry over influence in Korea
continued to sharpen in the years that followed, unresolved by the new
imperial title alone.
