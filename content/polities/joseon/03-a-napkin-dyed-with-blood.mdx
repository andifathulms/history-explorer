Prince Yŏnsan took the throne in 1494 at twenty, the son of a queen who
had been driven from the palace and put to death after clawing the
king's face in a fit of jealous rage. On her deathbed she had given her
son a napkin stained with her own blood and charged him never to forget
his vow to avenge her. When he finally sat on the throne, he kept his
promise with a thoroughness that consumed the kingdom around him.

## A deer speared to prove a point

The signs were visible before his reign properly began. As a boy,
Yŏnsan had once kicked a tame deer that nuzzled his father's arm and
been sharply reprimanded for the cruelty; the day he became king he
sent for the same animal and speared it through with his own hand in
front of the court. A senior official who watched him do it read the
gesture correctly and retired from court immediately. The king's own
old tutor, who had once tried to curb his wildness, was beheaded soon
after for the offense of having done so.

## A purge that emptied the schools

In 1498 Yŏnsan moved against everyone connected to his mother's
disgrace and death - officials who had recorded the facts, messengers
who had carried the orders, several hundred people in all, executed and
dismembered; the bodies of those who had already died were dug up,
their bones broken and scattered to the wind or ground to powder. Kim
Chong-jik, a former prime minister who had already fled to the
countryside, was accused of having questioned the dynasty's own
legitimacy and was executed along with many of his associates and
students - a purge severe enough that the capital's schools closed
outright and a "deadly silence," in the source's own words, settled
over the city, with no one certain who might be named next.

## A reign of theatrical cruelty

What followed for the next several years reads as a catalogue of
individual atrocities rather than a program of government. When
placards in Korea's own alphabet appeared in 1504 denouncing the king,
he had the exiled officials he suspected recalled and put to death by
beheading, poison or beating, then asked the provinces to consider
abolishing the native script that had embarrassed him. He murdered two
of his late father's concubines for their supposed role in his
mother's downfall, then headbutted the dowager queen to the ground when
she rebuked him for it. He conscripted three hundred women into the
palace and stationed dancing girls in every prefecture in the kingdom;
he seized a courtier's wife for himself and had the husband killed when
she would not smile at him; he shot an elderly eunuch through with an
arrow for daring to protest the seizure of the public ferry boats for
his own sport. He emptied three entire districts of their population to
build a private hunting park and forbade anyone from resettling there
on pain of death.

## A conspiracy that met no resistance at all

By 1506 three officials - Song Hŭi-an, Pak Wŏn-jong and Yu Sun-jŏng -
concluded the kingdom could not survive the reign and organized its
end. They gathered an armed band by night, forced their way into the
palace, beheaded six of the king's favorites, and threw open the
prisons to arm themselves further with the freed prisoners' help.
Resistance never materialized - even the king's own allies were
horrified by what he had done. The conspirators went to the dowager
queen he had assaulted and asked her to let her own son take the
throne; she demurred once, was overruled by acclamation, and consented.
The new king was crowned the same day. Yŏnsan was exiled to an island
with his own son and denied, permanently, the posthumous royal title
every other king of the dynasty received - remembered instead simply
as the Lord of Yŏnsan, a reign struck from the honor roll of the
kings who came before and after him.
