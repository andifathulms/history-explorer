Yi Seong-gye did not seize the throne of Goryeo so much as accept it
after every other option had closed around him. With Goryeo's last king
reduced to a puppet, its most loyal minister murdered, and the court
itself clamoring for a change, the general took the royal seals on the
sixteenth day of the seventh moon of 1392. He was already an old man,
past the age, by his own reckoning, when he could personally oversee
the "house-cleaning" the moment demanded — so his first acts were
political rather than martial: loyal officials promoted, opponents of
the old regime pardoned and recalled, and a tablet raised in the
capital honoring the men who had helped him to power.

## A name chosen in Peking

Word soon reached Yi that the Chinese Emperor questioned his own
legitimacy outright — "a man can become king only by the decree of
Heaven," the message read. "How is it then that the people of Sam-han
have made Yi king?" Rather than contest the point, Yi sent an envoy
asking the Emperor to choose between two proposed names for his new
kingdom: Cho-sŭn, "Morning Freshness," or Wha-ryŭng, "Peaceful
Harmony." The Emperor chose Cho-sŭn, and Goryeo's own royal seals were
surrendered to China in exchange for new ones bearing the new dynasty's
name — a founding legitimized, in the end, by the same suzerain whose
approval Yi had needed to secure it.

## A capital moved, then moved back, then moved again

Custom held that a new dynasty required a new capital, and Yi first
broke ground at Kye-ryong Mountain before abandoning the site on the
strength of an old prophecy pointing him instead toward Han-yang, the
future Seoul. The move did not stick immediately — an unpopular new
capital sent the court briefly back to Song-do — but Han-yang would
become, and remain, the seat of Korean government for the rest of the
dynasty's existence and long after it.
