Hayam Wuruk died in 1389 and Gajah Mada had died thirteen years before him.
What followed, from about 1404, was a war between the western court and the
eastern one over the succession — the *Paregreg*, which means something like
the slow-burning or grinding one.

It ran a couple of years and was settled by negotiation rather than by victory.
That is the whole problem in one sentence: the throne was hereditary in the
dynasty, and there was no rule at all for deciding between two claimants who
each had a court behind them.

## What a succession war costs a thalassocracy

More than it costs a territorial state, and the difference is the point.

An empire with garrisons and provinces loses a few years of tax collection and
picks up where it left off. The Ottomans went through an eleven-year
interregnum in exactly these years, from 1402 to 1413, and came out of it
stronger.

Majapahit's authority over the archipelago was overlordship: tribute sent by
coastal chiefs who calculated annually whether sending it was still the right
move. Two years of a Javanese court unable to sail anywhere is two years of
that calculation coming out differently, and the arrangement does not resume on
request.

The Chinese records show it. Before the Paregreg they deal with the Javanese
court; afterwards they deal with the north-coast ports directly, and with
Palembang, and with a new place on the strait.

## Melaka

Melaka was founded on the Melaka strait at about this moment — the traditional
date is around 1400 — by a prince out of Palembang, in flight from Javanese
pressure, who took a position on the strait and made it pay.

Which is Srivijaya's arrangement, on Srivijaya's water, five hundred years and
one collapse later. The strait was still the only sea route between the Indian
Ocean and the South China Sea, and it did not stop being that when the polity
holding it stopped existing. Somebody was always going to be there.

Melaka took Chinese recognition, became Muslim within a generation or two, and
by the mid-fifteenth century was the trade centre of the archipelago.

So the polity that displaced Majapahit commercially, the polity that reoccupied
Srivijaya's old position, and the city the Portuguese took in 1511 to open
their route to the spices are all the same place — founded, on the traditional
dating, in the same years Java was fighting itself over a throne.

## And the court kept going

The thing worth holding onto is that none of this looks like a fall from
inside.

The court at Trowulan went on. Hayam Wuruk's descendants went on reigning, a
Javanese rival took the capital in 1478 and the dynasty continued east of it,
and inscriptions and land grants continue into the sixteenth century. Ricklefs
is careful about this, and it is why the kingdom is dated to 1527 rather than
to one of the tidier years.

What ended was not the state. It was the state's reach, and it ended in a
succession war nobody won.
