Hayam Wuruk died in 1389 and Gajah Mada had died thirteen years before him. What
followed, from about 1404, was a war between the western court and the eastern
one over the succession — the *Paregreg*, which means something like the
slow-burning or grinding one.

It ran a couple of years and was settled by negotiation rather than by victory.
This record's `succession_rule` is null, and the Paregreg is the reason it cannot
be anything else: the throne was hereditary in the dynasty and there was no rule
that decided between two claimants who each had a court behind them.

## What a succession war costs a thalassocracy

More than it costs a territorial state, and the difference is the point.

An empire with garrisons and provinces loses a few years of tax collection and
picks up where it left off. This corpus's Ottoman record survives an interregnum
of eleven years — 1402 to 1413, in exactly this period — and is stronger
afterwards.

Majapahit's authority over the archipelago was overlordship: tribute sent by
coastal chiefs who calculated annually whether sending it was still the right
move. Two years of a Javanese court unable to sail anywhere is two years of that
calculation coming out differently, and the arrangement does not resume on
request.

The Chinese records show it. Before the Paregreg they deal with the Javanese
court; afterwards they deal with the north-coast ports directly, and with
Palembang, and with a new place on the strait.

## Melaka

Melaka was founded on the Melaka strait at about this moment — the traditional
date is around 1400 — by a prince out of Palembang, in flight from Javanese
pressure, who took a position on the strait and made it pay.

Which is Srivijaya's arrangement, on Srivijaya's water, five hundred years and
one collapse later. This corpus's Srivijaya record's first chapter calls the
strait the only sea route between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea, and
the strait did not stop being that when the polity holding it stopped existing.
Somebody was always going to be there.

Melaka took Chinese recognition — Zheng He's fleets called, and this corpus's
Ming record carries those voyages — became Muslim within a generation or two, and
by the mid-fifteenth century was the trade centre of the archipelago.

It is not a record on this site, which means the polity that displaced this one
commercially, and the polity that reoccupied Srivijaya's position, and the state
the Portuguese took in 1511 to open their route to the spices, are all the same
absent page.

## And the court kept going

The thing worth holding onto is that none of this looks like a fall from inside.

The court at Trowulan went on. Hayam Wuruk's descendants went on reigning, a
Javanese rival took the capital in 1478 and the dynasty continued east of it, and
inscriptions and land grants continue into the sixteenth century. Ricklefs is
careful about this and it is why this record's span runs to 1527 rather than to
one of the tidier dates.

What ended was not the state. It was the state's reach, and it ended in a
succession war nobody won.
