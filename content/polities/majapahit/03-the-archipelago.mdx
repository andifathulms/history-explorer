The first chapter is the founding. This one is what the state did with the sixty
years afterwards.

Under Hayam Wuruk and his minister Gajah Mada in the fourteenth century it
claimed authority over most of the archipelago — Sumatra, Borneo, the eastern
islands, the Malay peninsula — through a mixture of naval force, marriage,
tribute and the same oath-bound arrangements with coastal chiefs that Srivijaya
had used four centuries earlier. The court poem *Nagarakertagama* lists the
dependencies, and how much of that list was administration and how much was
aspiration is a live scholarly question.

Its `reach_km2` is empty for the same reason Srivijaya's is. Power over sea lanes
does not convert into square kilometres of land under state control, and this
site will not compute an area from a map polygon and present it as a figure. Not one of the
twenty-two records this site carries across the archipelago has a reach figure at
all, which is itself information: the measures were built from a literature about
territorial empires, and nothing here is one.

What is *not* empty any more is the institutions block, which did not exist on
this record until recently and now carries three of four values. The second
chapter is about the first of them, `land-tax`, and why a Javanese state could
have it and a Sumatran one could not.

The decline is the fifth chapter and the ending is the sixth.
