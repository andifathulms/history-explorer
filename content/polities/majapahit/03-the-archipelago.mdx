Under Hayam Wuruk and his minister Gajah Mada, in the middle of the fourteenth
century, Majapahit claimed authority over most of the archipelago — Sumatra,
Borneo, the eastern islands, the Malay peninsula — through a mixture of naval
force, marriage, tribute and the same oath-bound arrangements with coastal
chiefs that Srivijaya had used four centuries earlier.

How much of that was administration and how much was aspiration is the central
question of Majapahit historiography, and the next chapter is about the
document the question turns on.

## Why it cannot be measured

There is no figure for how large Majapahit was, and there is not going to be
one.

Power over sea lanes does not convert into square kilometres of land under
state control. Neither Majapahit nor Srivijaya nor any other maritime power in
these islands appears in the tabulations that supply extent figures for the
empires of the period, because those tabulations were built from a literature
about territorial states and none of these was one.

That absence is itself informative. A polity that can put a fleet into the
Moluccas, take tribute from Sumatra, and be obeyed on the Malay peninsula is by
any ordinary standard a great power — and there is no number that expresses it,
because the thing it controlled was traffic rather than ground.

## What it actually held

The honest description is a gradient rather than a border.

Java itself was governed: taxed, administered through princes and regional
lords, supplying the rice and the men.
Beyond that came a ring of places where
Javanese authority was real but negotiated — the north coast, the nearer
islands, the ports that needed Javanese rice. Beyond that again came places
that sent tribute when it suited them, took Javanese titles because titles were
useful, and calculated annually whether any of it was still worth doing.

None of those three is the same kind of thing, and a map drawn in one colour
cannot show the difference. The next hundred and fifty years are largely the
story of the outer ring doing the arithmetic differently.
