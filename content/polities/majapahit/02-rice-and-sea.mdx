This region has two records and they were built on opposite things, which the
institution fields now say and which is most of what there is to understand about
either.

Srivijaya's `revenue_basis` reads `trade-toll` and nothing else. It sat on a
strait with a swamp behind it. It had no agriculture worth taxing, no
hinterland, no population to levy, and its whole apparatus — the oaths, the sea
peoples, the Buddhist endowments — was a way of making a chokepoint pay in the
absence of anything else.

Majapahit's reads `land-tax, trade-toll, tribute`.

## The volcano

Java's soil is volcanic and its rainfall is monsoonal and reliable, and the
combination supports wet-rice terracing at densities almost nowhere else in the
pre-modern world matched. The island was probably carrying several million people
in the fourteenth century on an area smaller than England.

That gave the Javanese court something no Sumatran power ever had: a surplus it
could tax without anybody's ships arriving. The rice fed the capital, fed the
armies, and — the part that matters for this region — fed the ports.

Because the archipelago's spice islands to the east could not feed themselves.
Cloves came from a handful of small islands in the Moluccas and nutmeg from the
Bandas, and those places imported their rice. Whoever supplied the rice had a
hold on the spice trade that no amount of naval force on its own could produce.

Majapahit was a thalassocracy with a granary, and it is the granary that made the
thalassocracy work.

## What that bought

The eastern archipelago, in a way Srivijaya never had it.

Srivijaya's power ran along the strait between India and China — a line, with the
polity sitting on it. Majapahit's ran east into the islands, where the goods
originated, and the fourth chapter's list of dependencies is heaviest exactly
where the spices are.

The mechanism was the same in both cases: overlordship over coastal chiefs,
maintained by marriage, tribute, prestige and occasional force. This record's
`military_basis` carries `client-levy` for the identical reason Srivijaya's
does — a maritime power fights with other people's ships. What is different is
that Majapahit could also raise Javanese levies through the princes and the
regional lords, which is the first value in the field, and Srivijaya could not.

## And what it cost

A Javanese state with an agrarian base and a maritime empire has two centres of
gravity, and they pull apart.

The court was inland, at Trowulan, among the rice. The trade was on the north
coast, in ports that dealt with Chinese, Indian and later Muslim merchants. For a
century the court held both. The fifth chapter is what happened when a succession
war weakened the court, and the sixth is what happened when the coast found a
religion the court did not share.

Srivijaya, having no interior, never had this problem and never had the
resources that produced it.
