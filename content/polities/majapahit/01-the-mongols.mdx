In 1289 Kublai Khan sent an envoy to Kertanegara of Singhasari on Java demanding
submission. Kertanegara had the man's face branded or his ear cut off — the
accounts differ on which — and sent him back.

The expedition that came in response was substantial: something like a thousand
ships out of Quanzhou, with twenty or thirty thousand men. This corpus's Yuan
record carries it among the overseas failures of that reign, alongside the two
attempts on Japan.

By the time it arrived Kertanegara was dead, killed by a vassal named Jayakatwang
who had taken the throne.

## What Raden Wijaya did

Kertanegara's son-in-law, Raden Wijaya, had survived and was in the forest with a
few followers. He presented himself to the Mongol commanders as the rightful heir
of the ruler they had come to punish, offered submission, and asked for help
against the usurper.

They gave it. The combined force destroyed Jayakatwang and took his capital.
Wijaya then asked leave to go home and fetch the tribute and the princesses he
had promised, was allowed to go with a small escort, killed the escort, turned on
the Mongol army while it was dispersed and celebrating, and drove it back to the
ships.

It sailed in the same year it had arrived. Its commanders were punished on
return.

Majapahit is dated from that: a state founded in the space cleared by an invasion
it then expelled, by a man who had used the largest army in the world as a
temporary ally and not paid for it.

## What it was founded on

Not a new kingdom, formally. Wijaya's claim was descent — by marriage — from
Kertanegara, and this record's `legitimation` field carries `descent` for that.

The Singhasari state he was continuing is not a record on this site, and neither
are the Javanese kingdoms before it. What is here is the record on the other side
of the Java Sea: this corpus's Srivijaya page ends in 1275 with Kertanegara's
Pamalayu expedition to Sumatra, eighteen years before this one begins.

So the two records in this region are joined by a man who appears on neither of
them. Kertanegara ended the first and fathered-in-law the second, and Singhasari
has no page.

## And the founding is the peak of the coded fields

One thing worth noting before the chapters that follow.

Raden Wijaya's manoeuvre in 1293 is the most striking single episode on this
record and it is not what the record is about. The reign this page names as its
peak is Hayam Wuruk's, sixty years later, and the interesting material is the
arrangement he and Gajah Mada built and the poem that describes it.

A founding that reads like an adventure story followed by a century of
administration is a shape this corpus has seen — the Ghana record's al-Bakri
paragraph and the Ottoman record's early frontier both work that way — and the
fields on this page describe the second half.
