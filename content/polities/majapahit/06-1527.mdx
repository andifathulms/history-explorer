Majapahit has no fall either. What 1527 dates is the point at which Demak — a
Muslim sultanate on the north Java coast, founded by a family of the port
aristocracy — absorbed what was left of the inland Javanese court. There is no
siege anybody records, no treaty, and no last king whose death ends a line.

## The Islamisation nobody planned

The north Java ports became Muslim over the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries,
merchant community by merchant community, in the ordinary way of the Indian Ocean
— through traders from Gujarat, Bengal and the Arab world who settled, married
and stayed, and through Sufi teachers whom Javanese tradition remembers as the
*wali songo*, the nine saints.

There is no conquest in it. The earliest Muslim gravestones on Java are in the
port towns, and some of them are in Majapahit's own royal cemetery at Trowulan —
Muslims of the court, buried with Javanese dating and Islamic formulae, while the
king was still performing the old ceremonies a few hundred metres away.

What that produced was not a religious war. It was a coast with a different
religion from its interior, in a state whose whole economic structure depended on
the interior's rice reaching the coast's ships.

## Why the traditional date is 1478

Javanese tradition puts the fall of Majapahit at 1478 and attributes it to Demak
and to Islam, in a chronogram — *sirna ilang kertaning bumi*, vanished and gone
the prosperity of the earth — that encodes the year.

Neither part is right. In 1478 the capital was taken by a rival Javanese prince
who was Hindu. The dynasty went on for another fifty years. Demak's absorption of
the remnant is later, and quieter.

The date stuck because a court tradition written under Muslim sultans needed a
year at which the old order ended and the new one began, and because a chronogram
is easy to remember. It is a common enough manoeuvre: Ghana is supposed to have
been destroyed by an Almoravid conquest in 1076 that probably never happened, and
Meroë is still widely credited to Aksum on evidence that will not carry it. Three
kingdoms on three continents, and the same instinct to date the end of each to
whoever turned up next.

## What did not end

The literature, the music, the theatre and the religion.

Javanese court literature in the Old Javanese metres continued under the Muslim
sultanates and was still being copied in the nineteenth century. Gamelan and
wayang continued. And the Hindu-Buddhist tradition the court had practised moved
east to Bali, where a Javanese-derived aristocracy and a Javanese-derived
Hinduism are still there, with the Majapahit connection claimed in genealogies.

## Who claimed it

One polity claimed to be Majapahit's heir, and it was the one that ended it.

The Javanese chronicles trace Raden Patah's line — Demak's founding house — back
to Majapahit's own royal family. That genealogy is the entire basis on which a
north-coast trading port claimed a right to rule Java rather than merely to trade
from it, and it was still being invoked a century later, when Pajang's founder
claimed descent from Majapahit's last king and from the Demak sultan he had just
overthrown, at the same time.

Indonesian nationalism made a much later claim of a different kind, reading the
*Nagarakertagama*'s list of dependencies as a first unification of the
archipelago and a precedent for the modern republic's boundaries. That is a
twentieth-century argument about a fourteenth-century poem, and it belongs to the
history of the poem rather than to the history of the kingdom.
