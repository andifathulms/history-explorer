This record's `ended.type` is `gradual absorption` with an empty `by`, and the
date is the least confident on the page.

What is being dated is the point at which Demak — a Muslim sultanate on the north
Java coast, founded by a family of the port aristocracy — absorbed what was left
of the inland Javanese court. There is no siege anybody records, no treaty, and
no last king whose death ends a line.

## The Islamisation nobody planned

The north Java ports became Muslim over the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries,
merchant community by merchant community, in the ordinary way of the Indian Ocean
— through traders from Gujarat, Bengal and the Arab world who settled, married
and stayed, and through Sufi teachers whose Javanese tradition remembers as the
*wali songo*, the nine saints.

There is no conquest in it. The earliest Muslim gravestones on Java are in the
port towns and some of them are in Majapahit's own royal cemetery at Trowulan —
Muslims of the court, buried with Javanese dating and Islamic formulae, while the
king was still performing the ceremonies this record's fourth chapter describes.

What that produced was not a religious war. It was a coast that had a different
religion from its interior, in a state whose whole economic structure — the
second chapter — depended on the interior's rice reaching the coast's ships.

## And why the traditional date is 1478

Javanese tradition puts the fall of Majapahit at 1478 and attributes it to Demak
and to Islam, in a chronogram — *sirna ilang kertaning bumi*, vanished and gone
the prosperity of the earth — that encodes the year.

Neither part is right. In 1478 the capital was taken by a rival Javanese prince
who was Hindu; the dynasty went on for another fifty years; and Demak's absorption
of the remnant is later and quieter.

The date stuck because a court tradition written under Muslim sultans needed a
year at which the old order ended and the new one began, and because a chronogram
is memorable. This record carries 1478 as a turning point marked contested, for
what actually happened, and runs its span to 1527.

This corpus has been here before. The Ghana record's fourth chapter dismantles an
Almoravid conquest of 1076 that probably did not happen and explains that the
story lasted a century because it was tidy. The Kush record declines to credit
Aksum with the fall of Meroë for the same reason. Three records, three
continents, the same instinct to date the end of a kingdom to whoever arrived
next.

## What did not end

The literature, the music, the theatre and the religion, which is why this
record's `religions_carried` count is 1 and its `descendant_scripts` is 0.

Javanese court literature in the Old Javanese metres continued under the Muslim
sultanates and was still being copied in the nineteenth century. Gamelan and
wayang continued. And the Hindu-Buddhist tradition that this record's court
practised moved east: to Bali, where a Javanese-derived aristocracy and a
Javanese-derived Hinduism are still there, with the Majapahit connection claimed
in genealogies.

The fourth chapter notes that Indonesian nationalism later read the
*Nagarakertagama*'s list of dependencies as the first unification of the
archipelago and made it a precedent for the modern republic's boundaries, and
that this site's coding rules exclude modern invocations from the successor-claim
count.

Both things are true at once. The count is null because rule 1 is about political
succession within the period, and the poem is on the wall of a ministry in
Jakarta.
