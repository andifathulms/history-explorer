Coedès puts it in one sentence: Bali played the same role of preserver for Java
as Tibet did for Buddhist India.

Indian culture took refuge in certain districts of eastern Java and, above all,
on Bali — and the island became, and has remained, the place holding the
essentials of an Indo-Javanese literature and religion that Islam caused to
disappear rapidly on Java itself.

## What crossed the strait

Not a court in flight, or not only that. What moved was a tradition.

Javanese court literature in the Old Javanese metres continued under the Muslim
sultanates and was still being copied in the nineteenth century, but it
continued on Bali as a living religious practice rather than as inherited
literature. The Hindu-Buddhist observance the Majapahit court had kept is still
there. So is the Javanese-derived aristocracy, which traced its own line back
to the governing house Majapahit installed on the island in 1343 and went on
tracing it for another three hundred years at Gelgel.

Gamelan and wayang stayed on both sides of the water. The *Bharatayuddha*,
written at Kediri two centuries before Majapahit existed, is still performed in
both places.

## Two heirs, two kinds of claim

Majapahit was claimed by two successors and they wanted completely different
things from it.

Demak — the Muslim sultanate on the north coast that absorbed the remnant —
traced Raden Patah's line back to Majapahit's own royal family. That genealogy
is the entire basis on which a trading port claimed a right to *rule* Java
rather than merely to trade from it, and it was still being invoked a century
later, when Pajang's founder claimed descent from Majapahit's last king and
from the Demak sultan he had just overthrown, at the same time.

Gelgel wanted the opposite. Its claim was not a right to rule Java but a right
to be what Java had stopped being: the place where the old observance
continued, under a dynasty that had been installed from Majapahit and had never
converted.

One heir claimed the throne and changed the religion. The other claimed the
religion and never had the throne.

## And a third claim, much later

Indonesian nationalism read the *Nagarakertagama*'s list of dependencies as a
first unification of the archipelago and made it a precedent for the modern
republic's boundaries.

That is a twentieth-century argument about a fourteenth-century poem, made by
people who needed a precedent, about a document written by a court poet who
needed a patron flattered. It belongs to the history of the poem rather than to
the history of the kingdom — but it is the reason Majapahit is the one polity
on these pages whose name is still doing work in a national argument, six
hundred years after the last king stopped issuing charters.
