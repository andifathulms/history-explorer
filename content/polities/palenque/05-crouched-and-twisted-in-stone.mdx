Kan Bahlam's younger brother, K'an Joy Chitam II, succeeded him and
inherited a rivalry with Tonina that had never been settled. The
highland kingdom's own nobles had spent decades absorbing repeated
defeats at Palenque's hands, and on 26 August 711 they finally
delivered a reversal severe enough to be remembered on their own
monuments for generations after: a "star war" attack that penetrated
the Palenque capital itself and seized its king.

## A captive carved in triumph

A sandstone panel recovered at Tonina records the moment in vivid
detail, showing K'an Joy Chitam crouched and twisted, his arms bound
and strips of paper drawn through his earlobes in a gesture of
ritual humiliation, his own name and title carved beside him so that
no one who saw the stone could mistake who had been brought so low.
It was commissioned not by a king in his own right but by the
lieutenants of Tonina's own child-ruler - a boy who had been crowned
at Tonina three years earlier, in November 708, still too young to
govern, while the nobles who administered his kingdom on his behalf
proved vigorous enough agents to bring down the ruler of Palenque
itself.

## A capital left to lesser hands

No inscription says what became of K'an Joy Chitam II afterward,
whether he died in captivity or was eventually released; his own
name disappears from Palenque's record at this point. The kingdom he
had ruled did not vanish with him - its later architecture, including
substantial work on the royal Palace complex, is credited to a king
called K'uk' Bahlam II, attested in the 760s and 780s - but the
capture of 711 marked the last time a Palenque king's own defeat was
important enough for a rival capital to carve it in stone.
