K'inich Janaab Pakal ruled for sixty-eight years, dying in 683 as an
old man who had spent much of his reign, in his final years, building
his own memorial: the Temple of the Inscriptions, a stepped pyramid
raised over a vaulted burial chamber at its base. He did not live to
see it finished; the final texts and embellishments were completed
under his son and successor, K'inich Kan Bahlam II.

## A discovery in 1952

The temple kept its secret for over twelve centuries. In 1949 the
Mexican archaeologist Alberto Ruz Lhuillier, restoring the pyramid's
inner sanctuary, noticed a floor slab pierced with a ring of
stone-plugged holes - designed, it turned out, to lift the slab
itself. Beneath lay a rubble-packed stairway descending eighty feet
into the heart of the pyramid. Four excavation seasons later, on 13
June 1952, an intrusive flashlight beam finally reached a sealed
triangular doorway at the bottom and revealed a sight with no equal
anywhere in the Maya world.

## The sarcophagus

Behind the door was a vaulted chamber dominated by a huge stone
sarcophagus, its lid carved with what is today the single most famous
image in Maya art: the king shown reborn from the jaws of the
underworld, rising along the trunk of a world tree toward the heavens,
framed by a sky band studded with the symbols of sun, moon and stars.
The chamber's walls name the nobles - the sajal and aj k'uhuun of
Pakal's own administration - who had overseen the temple's design,
its labour, or perhaps its payment. It stood, and stands still, as the
clearest surviving expression of everything Pakal's reign had rebuilt
after the ruin his mother had crowned him amid as a boy of twelve.
