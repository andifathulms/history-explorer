K'inich Kan Bahlam II was already forty-eight years old when he
inherited the throne from his father in 684 - a long wait for a man
whose portraits, whether carved in relief or modeled in stucco, are
among the most distinctive of any Maya king, marked by a strikingly
realistic imperial nose and full lower lip. He spent the eighteen
years of his reign building at a pace that, in places, outdid even
his illustrious father's own achievements.

## Three temples for three gods

His masterwork was the Group of the Cross: the Temples of the Cross,
the Sun and the Foliated Cross, dedicated together in 692 as a single
coordinated program blending myth and history across an immense
range of carved panels and inscriptions. Each temple gave a spiritual
home to one of the three gods of the Palenque Triad, and each housed
a wall tablet showing Kan Bahlam twice over - once as the mature king
dedicating the building, once as a child venerating the same god - a
visual argument binding his own reign to the mythic founding of the
kingdom itself. A rare conjunction of Jupiter, Mars, Saturn and the
moon in July 690 was marked by the ceremonial burning of one temple
and, the following day, the dedication of another; the same date was
carved onto a jade portrait of the king that eventually made its way,
by means no inscription explains, into the great sacred well at
distant Chichen Itza.

## Wars to the south

The Cross Group's esoteric themes have long dominated how Kan Bahlam
is remembered, but his reign rested on real military success as much
as ceremony. To the south he fought the highland kingdom of Tonina,
Palenque's major rival, and both the Temple of the Sun and Temple of
the Cross describe a successful campaign against it in September 692.
Three years into his reign he also moved against Pipa', a former
Calakmul vassal, installing its king under Palenque's own patronage
and extending his influence along the southern bank of the
Usumacinta as far as the lords of Yaxchilan and Anaite. That
influence did not go unanswered - by as early as 692 Kan Bahlam had
already suffered a defeat that cost him the same region to his
highland rivals. He died on 16 February 702, at the age of sixty-six,
and was buried the same day; excavators clearing the pyramid beneath
the Temple of the Cross in 1993 found a dedicatory cache of more than
a hundred ceramic incense-burners arranged around its base.
