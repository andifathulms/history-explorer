Palembang's wealth through the eighteenth century rested on two exports, and
the more important of the two was not grown at all.

## Bangka

The island of Bangka, off the mouth of the Musi, held tin deposits that
became commercially significant from the early eighteenth century, and the
sultanate held authority over the island and its output.
Tin, unlike
pepper, was not subject to the same boom-and-bust cycles of taste and supply
that periodically undercut Southeast Asian pepper producers, and a
sultanate with reliable access to it had a revenue base considerably more
stable than most of its pepper-exporting neighbours.

<Figure id="tin-mine" />

The Dutch East India Company pursued a tin monopoly contract with Palembang
much as it pursued pepper monopolies elsewhere: a fixed price, a guaranteed
buyer, and a relationship that bound the sultanate to the Company's own
commercial interests while nominally leaving its sovereignty intact.
Andaya's
account treats the tin trade as the central fact of Palembang's eighteenth-
century relationship with the Dutch, more consequential to that relationship
than the pepper trade the sultanate also carried on.

## Pepper, and the upstream country

Pepper continued to be grown in the upriver country behind Palembang itself,
supplying the second half of the sultanate's export economy and connecting
Palembang's downstream authority to the same kind of upstream dependency
described in the chapter on Jambi: a capital that needed the interior's
produce and a series of upstream lineages and local rulers whose cooperation
in actually getting that produce downriver could not simply be commanded.

## What the combination bought

A sultanate producing both a globally significant supply of tin and a
steady quantity of pepper had leverage a purely agricultural or purely
maritime state on this coast typically lacked: it could negotiate with a
European trading company from a position where its cooperation was worth
protecting rather than worth simply extracting by force, for as long as the
Company judged a working sultanate more valuable than a conquered one. That
calculation, on the Dutch side, would eventually change, and the chapters
that follow describe what happened when it did.
