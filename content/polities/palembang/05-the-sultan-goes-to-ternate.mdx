Restored Dutch authority and a sultan who had already demonstrated, in
1811, what he would do to a garrison he judged vulnerable were never going
to coexist peacefully for long. The war that ended the sultanate began in
1819.

## Two years of fighting

Sultan Mahmud Badaruddin II's resistance against renewed Dutch attempts to
assert direct control over Palembang and its tin and pepper revenue proved
considerably more effective than the Dutch initially expected: Badaruddin
II's forces won engagements against Dutch expeditions in 1819, delaying
what colonial planners had presumably expected to be a brief punitive
campaign into a genuine war lasting into 1821.

It ended the way most such contests on this coast eventually did. Dutch
forces took Palembang on 25 June 1821, after a campaign that combined naval
bombardment with a land assault the sultanate's own defences could not
indefinitely withstand. Badaruddin II was captured, brought to Batavia, and
within a matter of weeks exiled to Ternate in the far eastern archipelago —
a location chosen, as with other deposed rulers this coast produced, for
being as far as practically possible from any territory the exile might
still command loyalty in.

## The puppet, and the end of the office itself

The Dutch did not immediately abolish the sultanate outright. They installed
a more compliant relative of Badaruddin II on the throne in his place,
repeating the pattern the British had already established a decade earlier.
That arrangement lasted only until 7 October 1823, when the Dutch colonial
government formally abolished the Palembang sultanate and annexed its
territory directly into the structure of the Dutch East Indies.

Two attempts, by two different European powers a decade apart, to solve the
problem of an inconvenient sultan by replacing him with a more manageable
one had each ended the same way: not with a durable puppet government, but
with the office itself judged not worth maintaining at all.
