Palembang's final crisis began with the wrong European power. Before the
Dutch ever came to take the city by force, the British had already done so
once.

## A massacre during a change of colonial hands

When Britain took Java from Dutch and French control during the Napoleonic
Wars, the reigning sultan at Palembang, Mahmud Badaruddin II, used the
disorder of the transition to move against the Dutch garrison that had
remained in the city — accounts describe a massacre of the garrison's
personnel in 1811, carried out at a moment when neither European power was
in a strong position to respond immediately.

The response, when it came, was British rather than Dutch. A British
expedition under Robert Rollo Gillespie was sent to punish Palembang for
the killing of what had, until recently, been an enemy garrison but was now
an inconvenient precedent for a colonial power trying to establish its own
authority over Java and its dependencies. The expedition took the city,
deposed Badaruddin II, and installed a more compliant relative in his
place.

## What the episode established

Badaruddin II did not stay removed. He returned to the throne once the
British restored Dutch rule over the East Indies at the peace settlement
following Napoleon's defeat, and resumed governing Palembang much as
before. But the episode had demonstrated, to Badaruddin II and to whichever
European power held Java next, that an outside naval force could take the
city, depose its ruler, and install a replacement without needing
anything resembling the sultanate's own consent.

That was a lesson the Dutch, resuming control of the East Indies after
1816, had every reason to have absorbed themselves. When the same sultan
who had massacred their garrison in 1811 proved equally difficult to manage
under renewed Dutch authority, they already had a precedent for how to
handle him.
