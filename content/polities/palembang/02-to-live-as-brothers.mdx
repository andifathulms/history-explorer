Palembang's most consequential relationship for most of its history was not
with a European trading company. It was with Jambi, the neighbouring
sultanate on the next river system north, and Andaya's study — which treats
the two states as a single subject rather than as separate case studies — is
organised entirely around understanding why that relationship kept the shape
it did.

## Kinship as a political idiom

Malay political thought in this region did not describe the relationship
between two neighbouring courts primarily in the vocabulary of alliance and
rivalry a European observer would reach for first. It described it through
kinship: two rulers, two courts, two river systems draining the same stretch
of eastern Sumatra were "brothers", and the language of brotherhood carried
real content — an expectation of mutual support, a hierarchy of relative
seniority that mattered and was argued about, and an obligation to
reconcile after a quarrel rather than to treat a quarrel as final.

That is not a metaphor imposed after the fact. It is, in Andaya's reading,
the actual framework Palembang's and Jambi's own rulers used to describe
their dealings with each other, in correspondence and in the terms they
negotiated with the Dutch East India Company, which had to learn to operate
inside a political idiom it had not invented and did not fully control.

## Upstream and downstream

The second organising idea Andaya draws from the material is the relation
between an upstream and a downstream: the interior country that produced tin
and pepper and the river-mouth capital that controlled the port those goods
had to pass through to reach the outside world. Palembang and Jambi were
each, internally, an upstream-downstream pair in exactly this sense, and
the two sultanates' dealings with each other were frequently disputes over
which capital's downstream authority a given stretch of upstream country
actually owed obedience to.

## Cyclical hostility, and why it never became final

Palembang and Jambi fought each other, competed for the same upstream
tributaries, and periodically broke off relations entirely across the
seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. None of those quarrels produced a
permanent conquest of one sultanate by the other. The kinship idiom
described above supplies part of the explanation: two courts that described
each other as brothers had a vocabulary already available for reconciliation
that two courts describing each other only as rivals would not have had, and
they used it, repeatedly, across generations.
