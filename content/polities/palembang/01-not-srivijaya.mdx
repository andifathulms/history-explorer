Palembang sits on the same river as the capital of the ancient thalassocracy
of Srivijaya, and the fact is worth addressing before anything else, because
it explains nothing about the sultanate that actually governed the city from
1659.

Srivijaya's own attested history ends in the thirteenth century, and nearly
four hundred years separate its disappearance from Palembang's own
sultanate. In between, the region passed through Javanese overlordship,
Islamic conversion, and the political reshuffling that followed the fall of
Melaka to the Portuguese in 1511 — Malay traders and rulers displaced from
the strait scattered to found or reinforce a scatter of successor ports, of
which Palembang, on its own river with its own hinterland, was one among
several rather than an obvious heir to anything.

## What actually founded the sultanate

Andaya's study treats 1659, and the reign of Susuhunan Abdurrahman, as the
point at which Palembang's ruling house is properly called a sultanate: a
Muslim polity with its own claim to legitimacy, its own relationship to the
Dutch East India Company then extending its reach across the Java Sea and
the strait, and its own defined territory running up the Musi river into the
tin and pepper country of the interior.

Nothing about Abdurrahman's own claim to rule invoked Srivijaya. The
ancient thalassocracy was not a memory the sultanate's founders were working
to restore, in the way the Sasanians invoked the Achaemenids or the way
later Indonesian nationalism would eventually invoke Srivijaya itself as a
symbol of pre-colonial greatness. It was simply the ground the new state
happened to occupy, four centuries after the older one had stopped
occupying it for reasons the sultanate's own founders had no particular
reason to investigate.

## What the sultanate actually was

A downstream capital controlling the mouth of a major river, taxing the
traffic that came down it, and dealing directly with the Dutch company that
wanted the tin and pepper the upriver country produced — that is what the
sultanate actually was, and it is a shape that owes far more to the general
pattern of river-mouth sultanates common across the Sumatran and Bornean
coasts in this period than to anything specific to the ground's own older
history.
