Pomare IV endured what the record calls thirty-five years of French
tutelage with the same independent spirit that had once made her
flee rather than submit to it outright. She died on the 17th of
September, 1877, sixty-five years old, and her eldest son Ariiaue was
proclaimed king under the hereditary name, becoming Pomare V. The
succession itself was arranged less by Tahitian custom than by the
French admiral then governing the islands, who reconciled the new
king with his estranged wife, had the pair jointly proclaimed King
and Queen, and settled the line of succession for two further
generations - a Tahitian royal house whose own future was now a
matter for a French commandant to decide.

## The cloak thrown aside

The Protectorate had always been, in substance, a formal cover for
French possession rather than a genuine partnership; what had been
merely implicit in 1843 was made explicit in June 1880, when Pomare V
was persuaded by the commandant to cede the nominal sovereignty he
still nominally held to the power that had long since held its
reality, in exchange for a life pension of twelve thousand dollars a
year - an income that let him live out his remaining years free of
the constant tutoring that had made his own kingship a wearisome
burden without any of its honour. The annexation of Tahiti was
formally proclaimed at Papeete in 1881, with a festival of salutes,
parades and feasting that gave the newly annexed island every
outward appearance of celebrating its own absorption. Of the kingdom
Pomare I had built on borrowed muskets and mutineers' aid, nothing
remained but a pension, a name, and a flag that was no longer his
own.
