When the mission ship Duff anchored in Matavai Bay on the 6th of
March, 1797, the missionaries who came ashore found a district chief
named Pomare already the effective master of Tahiti. He had been born
to nothing more than the chieftaincy of Pare, but a combination of his
own ambition, the favour of visiting British captains, gifts of
firearms and powder, and the standing help of European sailors - not
least the mutineers of the Bounty, who had drifted through the island
for a decade - had let him assume and hold supreme authority over an
island no single chief had ever securely ruled before. He granted the
newcomers the district of Matavai to settle in: the earliest dated act
of him exercising the authority of an established king rather than
merely a powerful chief.

That authority was never as settled as it looked. Pomare's power
rested on the same religious order it also, at moments, threatened.
In 1802 he moved to seize Oro, the great war god whose ark and
attendant rites legitimised his own rule, from the district of
Atehuru, whose chiefs had custody of the idol and refused to give it
up. When his men tore the god away by force, the Atehuruans read it
as nothing less than a declaration of war.

## Fire, flight, and a doubtful peace

What followed was remembered in Tahiti as Te tamai o Rua, the war of
Rua, named for the rebellion's principal leader - the most powerful
and alarming uprising the island had yet seen. Atehuru's warriors
burned their way through the districts of Faa and Pare, driving out
the inhabitants and threatening Matavai itself, where the
missionaries and a handful of shipwrecked Englishmen were the only
force standing between the rebels and the coast. Reinforcements from
Eimeo reached Pomare's side, but were driven back in confusion before
the rebels could be checked; only the accident of an armed British
crew, cast ashore days earlier when their brig wrecked in the bay,
held the pass above Matavai long enough for the king's beaten
warriors to retreat in order.

The rebellion did not overthrow Pomare, but it proved something more
lasting than any single battle could have: that his rule, however
wide, still had to be fought for district by district, and that the
god whose favour he claimed could just as easily arm his enemies as
his own men.
