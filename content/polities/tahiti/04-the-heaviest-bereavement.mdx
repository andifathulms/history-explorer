Pomare's conduct after his 1819 baptism gave his oldest friends among
the missionaries little comfort. The author, who had known him since
the ceremony and recorded it as a moment of real and cautious hope,
also records plainly that the king's subsequent behaviour was a matter
of the deepest regret to his best friends - a disappointment left
largely unspecified in the source but referred to often enough to
make clear that the convert baptised before thousands in 1819 did not
live out his remaining years as they had hoped.

His health had been declining for some time when the author spent a
Sabbath afternoon with him at Eimeo in the early October of 1821. The
king was by then unable to leave his house, though not thought to be
in immediate danger. The end, when it came, came suddenly rather than
as the slow decline his friends had been watching: Pomare II died on
Eimeo on the 7th of December, 1821, the day before a party of visiting
missionary deputies reached the islands expecting to find him still
living.

## The heaviest bereavement since Christianity's introduction

The author, present in the islands at the time, records the loss in
terms he gives to no other event in either volume: the heaviest
bereavement that the mission and the nation had suffered since the
introduction of Christianity. It was a description earned by twenty-
two years of history rather than sentiment alone. The man who died on
Eimeo had been, by the missionaries' own account, present at nearly
every turn of that history - the district chief's son who inherited a
throne his father had built on foreign muskets, the king driven into
exile by his own people's rebellion, the convert whose doubts about
Oro were first entertained in that same exile, and the leader whose
stand at Bunaauia had broken the old religion's hold on Tahiti for
good. His son's coronation as Pomare III in 1824, and the dynasty's
own continuation under later rulers of the same name, both lie beyond
the years Ellis narrates here.
