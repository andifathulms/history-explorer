Pomare II's daughter Aimata took her father's own regnal name on
becoming queen in January 1827, succeeding her brother Pomare III as
Pomare IV, and reigned over a Tahiti at peace for a decade and a half.
That peace ended in 1843, when a French admiral, Dupetit-Thouars,
having already seized the Marquesas by force, anchored his flagship
at Papeete and sent the queen an ultimatum: pay some thirty thousand
dollars in indemnity for insults to the French flag, or watch her
undefended capital bombarded.

The insult, such as it was, amounted to nothing more than Pomare and
her Christian subjects declining to let French Catholic priests
settle and found a rival mission on an island already served by the
London Missionary Society. Terrified that the admiral meant what he
said, and unable to meet his demands, the queen fled by night in a
canoe to the neighbouring island of Moorea, hoping that no decisive
action could be taken in her absence. It bought her nothing. The
British consul, George Pritchard, her closest adviser through the
crisis, was arrested, held in solitary confinement for ten days, and
put aboard a ship out at sea without trial - removed from the islands
for the crime, as the French government itself later admitted in
private, of having too much influence with the queen for the
admiral's liking.

## A salute with borrowed gunpowder

With Pritchard gone and the queen in exile on Moorea, the French
Protectorate was declared over Tahiti outright. The warship Reine
Blanche ordered the queen and her chiefs to salute the new
Protectorate flag - and when they proved unable to comply, for want of
gunpowder of their own, the admiral politely supplied the powder
himself, so that the ceremony could proceed. Tahiti's own independence
ended not with a battle but with a borrowed charge of powder, fired in
salute to the flag of the power that had just taken it.
