By the beginning of 1815 the question of Christianity or idolatry on
Tahiti and Eimeo had become impossible to leave unsettled. The pagan
chiefs who still held Tahiti invited Pomare and the Christian refugees
sheltering with him on Eimeo to return and reoccupy their old lands;
Pomare crossed over to reinstate them in person, and after a tense
landing - the idolatrous party fired on his canoes before agreeing to
a truce - the two sides negotiated what appeared to be a settlement.
It was not. While the pagan chiefs exchanged assurances of friendship,
they were, the source records, already arming in secret for the
destruction of the king's party.

## Ambushed at worship

The break came on the 12th of November, 1815, a Sunday. Some eight
hundred of Pomare's people had gathered at Narii, near the village of
Bunaauia in the district of Atehuru, for their regular Sabbath
worship, when musket fire announced a pagan army advancing on the
camp under the flags and idols of the old religion. Pomare rose and
told his people to hold their places: they had gathered under God's
protection to worship him, and worship was not to be abandoned merely
because an enemy approached. The hymn was read, the service finished,
and only then did the Christians arm themselves and form their lines.

The battle that followed went against them at first. The pagan
vanguard broke the Christian front ranks, and the fighting became a
running, disordered melee through the district's trees and brush,
with men on both sides falling before the advance seemed to be
carrying the day for the idolatrous army. It was checked only when
the attackers reached the position held by Mahine, the Christian king
of Huahine, and Pomare-vahine, a chief's daughter from Raiatea who
fought armed with a musket and spear beside him. Their stand held the
line long enough for the battle to turn, and by its end the
idolatrous chieftain leading the attack was dead and his army broken.

Pomare's party showed the victory little of the old religion's own
cruelty. Rather than pursue a massacre, the king and his chiefs
extended clemency to the defeated, and turned instead to the war
god's own house: the image temple and altars of Oro, the idol whose
seizure thirteen years before had cost Tahiti the war of Rua, were
destroyed. Word of the victory carried quickly to Eimeo and the
islands beyond, and with the old religion's central shrine gone, the
source records a general and lasting reception of Christianity across
Tahiti that no sermon in the eighteen years since the Duff's arrival
had achieved. Pomare himself did not take the final public step of
baptism until the 16th of July, 1819, kneeling before a congregation
of four or five thousand in the Royal Mission Chapel at Papaoa - the
first person in the islands to receive the rite, and, in the eyes of
the missionaries who had known him since Matavai, their most uncertain
convert made, at last, their most public one.
