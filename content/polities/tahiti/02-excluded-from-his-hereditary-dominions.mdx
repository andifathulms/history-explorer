Pomare died suddenly on the 3rd of September, 1803, collapsing in a
canoe as he set out toward an anchored ship, not long after a fresh
round of trouble over his seizure of Oro at Atehuru. He was buried at
Pare, where his household wished him to remain rather than be carried
to his son at Atehuru, and within the year the strange, almost
accidental origin of his own name - taken, tradition held, from a
cough caught sleeping exposed in the mountains - passed to that son.
In 1804 Otu assumed the name Pomare, fixing it as the regal name of
Tahiti from that day forward.

<Figure id="th-otoo" />

The inheritance was less secure than the name suggested. A resemblance
was drawn at the time between Pomare and his contemporary in the
Sandwich Islands: both men had risen from comparatively humble
standing to supreme authority through their own energy and their
dealings with foreigners, and both left their conquests to sons very
different from themselves. Pomare II lacked his father's patient,
laborious temperament, and the government he inherited did not long
survive the transfer intact.

## Defeat, and two years on Eimeo

Renewed civil war brought Pomare II's rule on Tahiti down entirely.
The missionaries, who had preached in the islands for twelve years
without a single convert to show for it, fled the fighting for Port
Jackson in New South Wales, abandoning the mission they had held
since the Duff first anchored. Pomare himself fared no better: driven
from Tahiti, he spent the years that followed excluded from his
hereditary dominions and in exile on the neighbouring island of
Eimeo, his government dissolved and his family scattered by the same
disorder.

<Figure id="th-eimeo" />

It was in that exile, stripped of the authority his father had built,
that something in Pomare began to shift. Whether it was the collapse
of the system of idol-worship he had always relied on for success in
war and politics, or simply the leisure enforced idleness gave him to
think, the king who had once relied on Oro as completely as his
father had now began to entertain doubts about it. When the
missionaries, invited back by letters from the king himself, returned
to the islands in the autumn of 1811, they found not the ruler they
had left but a man newly and seriously willing to hear what they had
to say.
