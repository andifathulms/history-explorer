There is no account of how this kingdom ended. There is a Babylonian king's
campaign, a recovered statue, and then almost nothing Elamite for something like
three hundred years — no royal inscriptions that can be securely dated into the
gap, no sequence of kings, no dated levels at Susa that fill it convincingly.

That is a different kind of ending from the ones this region's other records
carry, and it is worth being precise about the difference. Assyria's Middle
Assyrian record also ends in a silence, and this site codes that `fragmentation`
and says on the page that the state shrank onto its core and stopped writing. The
Hittite record ends in a burned capital that nobody claims to have burned, coded
`fragmentation` too. Here there is a named external agent, a dated campaign, and
a defeat: `conquest` is the right value, and the only thing missing from the
field is a polity id to attach to it.

## The three-century problem

What was happening in Elam between about 1100 and about 800 is one of the larger
blanks in Near Eastern history, and it is a blank of the awkward kind — not a
region nobody has excavated, but a well-dug region whose material in this period
is hard to date and impossible to attach to a political narrative.

The Neo-Elamite record in this corpus starts at 770 with a securable king list
and a war against Assyria. What connects the two records is the language, the
gods, the royal titulature and the ground. What does not connect them is anything
this site can draw.

## Ur, and the Elam this corpus still does not carry

One more absence belongs on this page rather than in a note somewhere.

This site's Ur III record ends in 2004 BC with an Elamite coalition taking the
city and carrying Ibbi-Sin off to Anshan, and its `ended.by` field is empty. That
Elam is neither of the two records now in this corpus. It is the Elam of Awan and
Shimashki and then the *sukkalmah* — a period of six hundred years with a
distinctive federal structure and a succession system in which a brother followed
a brother and then a sister's son followed him, which is one of the more
genuinely unusual constitutional arrangements in the ancient world and which this
site's `succession_rule` vocabulary has no value for.

So Elam is now here twice and the corpus still points at a third one. That is not
a failure of these two pages. It is what it looks like when a site organised
around polities meets a tradition that produced states, dissolved, and produced
them again over two and a half thousand years, and it is the same shape as the
two Babylons, the two Assyrias and, on this page's own reckoning, the reason all
three pairs exist.
