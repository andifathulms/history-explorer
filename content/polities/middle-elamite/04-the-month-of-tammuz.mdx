The Babylonian counter-attack that ended Elam's century of dominance is recorded,
in the fullest surviving account, on a shaped block of limestone about two feet
high — because a chariot officer wanted his villages exempted from tax, and the
charter granting it had to explain what he had done to deserve that.

## What the document is

Kudurrus are property deeds. This one, found near Sippar in 1882, is a charter
issued by Nebuchadnezzar I to Ritti-Marduk, head of the House of Bit-Karziabku,
and its business end is entirely administrative: his towns are freed from the
jurisdiction of the neighbouring city of Namar, from all taxation and dues and
confiscation by royal officers, from the corvée for public works, from arrest by
imperial soldiers quartered there, and from having to billet those soldiers, whose
maintenance is charged to Namar instead.

Thirteen high officials are then named as witnesses, and the gods whose emblems
are carved above the text are invoked against anyone who alters it.

The historical narrative exists only because a grant has to be justified. To
explain the exemptions, the charter first has to say what Ritti-Marduk did.

## The march

Nebuchadnezzar is introduced as *the despoiler of the Kassites, the appointer of
kings, the prince beloved of Marduk*, and the campaign is given a motive in four
words: he raised his weapons *to avenge Akkad* — retaliation for Elamite raids
into northern Babylonia, of the kind that had been carrying Mesopotamia's
monuments to Susa for a century.

The army went from Der and marched thirty double-hours.

> In the month of Tammuz he undertook the campaign.

Tammuz is high summer in Iraq, and the text does not pretend otherwise. What
follows is one of the very few passages in ancient Near Eastern royal writing that
describes a march as an ordeal rather than a procession:

> The axe burned like fire, and the roads scorched like flame. There was no water
> in the wells, and the drinking supply was cut off. The splendour of the great
> horses failed, and the legs of the strong man turned aside.

*The legs of the strong man turned aside.* Men were falling out. The horses were
finished. And this is in a document composed to praise the king.

The king is then praised anyway, in the same breath — he advances with the gods
supporting him, he has no rival, he does not fear the difficult country — which is
how these texts work: the hardship is admitted because it makes the perseverance
larger.

Ritti-Marduk's first service was on that march. His station was at the king's
right hand, and what the charter credits him with is keeping the chariots moving.

## The Eulaeus

The battle was fought on the bank of the Eulaeus, the river of Susa.

> The kings took their stand round about, and offered battle. In their midst fire
> was kindled, by their dust was the face of the sun darkened; the hurricane
> sweeps along, the storm rages. In the storm of their battle the warrior in the
> chariot perceives not the companion at his side.

Ritti-Marduk led the attack on the Elamite confederation, and for that — and for
the march — he got his charter.

## What it cost Elam

The Elamite kingdom that had taken Babylon, ended the Kassite dynasty and carried
off Marduk himself was beaten in its own country, at its own river, by an army
that had nearly destroyed itself getting there.

Nothing in the Elamite record answers this. After it there is a silence of about
three centuries in Elam's own inscriptions, and the kingdom that emerges at the
far end is a different sort of state.

There is a symmetry worth naming. Elam's greatest achievement is known from the
objects it stole, read at Susa where French excavators dug them out. Elam's
defeat is known from a Babylonian tax exemption. In both cases the kingdom is
visible only in somebody else's paperwork — and in this one, the paperwork
survives because a man wanted his villages left alone.
