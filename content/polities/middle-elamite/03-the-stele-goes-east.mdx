In about 1158 Shutruk-Nahhunte invaded Babylonia. His son Kutir-Nahhunte
finished the job three years later, ending the Kassite dynasty that had ruled
Babylon for something over four centuries. What came home to Susa is why this
kingdom matters to everybody else's history.

The statue of Marduk, taken from Esagila. The victory stele of Naram-Sin of
Akkad — the one showing the king climbing a mountain over his enemies, wearing
the horned crown of a god — carried off from wherever it had stood for a
thousand years, and re-inscribed by Shutruk-Nahhunte, in Elamite, down the
side, recording that he had taken it. And the diorite stele of Hammurabi's
laws.

## Two civilisations, in one city

Akkad is a state most of whose physical remains are gone and whose capital has
never been located. What survives is the Naram-Sin stele and a handful of other
objects — and the stele survives because an Elamite king took it to Susa, where
a French expedition dug it out in the 1890s.

The First Dynasty of Babylon is known above all for a law code on a block of
diorite, and that block was found at Susa too, for the same reason.

Both of Mesopotamia's most reproduced monuments are in the Louvre because an
Elamite king put them on a boat.

## The strange shape of that

Measured by influence, Middle Elam did nothing. It adapted its writing from
Mesopotamia rather than exporting a script; it carried no religion outward;
nobody afterwards claimed to continue it.

What it did instead was the opposite of transmission. It removed. It gathered
the surviving monumental evidence of two other civilisations into one city,
where it sat undisturbed until archaeologists arrived three thousand years
later.

That is not influence in any ordinary sense and should not be dressed up as
one. It is still true that a kingdom which spread nothing is a substantial part
of the reason two much more famous kingdoms have anything left to look at.

## Why it stopped

Nothing about the Shutrukid conquest was durable. Elam held parts of Babylonia
for a few decades, and around 1110 Nebuchadnezzar I of Babylon — of the Second
Dynasty of Isin, and not the far more famous Nebuchadnezzar of five centuries
later — came the other way, beat the Elamites and took Marduk back.
The recovery was a founding event for Babylonian religious literature; it is
plausibly the occasion for which Marduk's promotion to head of the pantheon was
composed.

For Elam it was the ending. The kingdom stops appearing in anybody's records,
and the country goes quiet for three centuries.
