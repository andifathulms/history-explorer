There is no account of how this kingdom ended. There is a Babylonian king's
campaign, a recovered statue, and then almost nothing Elamite for something
like three hundred years — no royal inscriptions that can be securely dated
into the gap, no sequence of kings, no dated levels at Susa that fill it
convincingly.

It is a different kind of ending from the others in this region, and the
difference is worth stating. The Middle Assyrian kingdom shrank onto its core
and stopped writing. Hatti ends in a burned capital that nobody claims to have
burned. Here there is a named enemy, a dated campaign and a defeat — and then
the same silence anyway.

## The three-century problem

What was happening in Elam between about 1100 and about 800 is one of the
larger blanks in Near Eastern history, and it is a blank of the awkward kind —
not a region nobody has excavated, but a well-dug region whose material in this
period is hard to date and impossible to attach to a political narrative.

The next Elamite kingdom is visible from about 770, with a king list that can
be secured and a war against Assyria. What connects it to this one is the
language, the gods, the royal titulature and the ground. What connects them
politically is nothing anybody can trace.

## The third Elam

There is an older one still, and it is worth naming because it is the one that
took Ur.

When the Third Dynasty of Ur fell in 2004 BC, the coalition that took the city
and carried its last king off to Anshan was Elamite — but it belongs to neither
of the kingdoms described here. It is the Elam of Awan and Shimashki and then
of the *sukkalmah*, six hundred years with a federal structure of its own and a
succession in which a brother followed a brother and then a sister's son
followed him, which is one of the genuinely unusual constitutional arrangements
of the ancient world.

So there are at least three Elams, separated by long silences, sharing a
language, a pantheon and a pair of cities. That is what a tradition looks like
when it produces states, dissolves, and produces them again over two and a half
thousand years — and it is the same shape as the two Babylons and the two
Assyrias next door.
