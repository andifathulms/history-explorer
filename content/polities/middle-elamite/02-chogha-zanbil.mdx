Around 1250 BC Untash-Napirisha built a city from nothing on empty ground forty
kilometres south-east of Susa, and called it Al-Untash-Napirisha, the town of
Untash-Napirisha. Three concentric enclosure walls, the outermost about four
kilometres round. Temples inside them to gods gathered from across the kingdom,
highland and lowland both. Palaces, with a vaulted underground tomb complex.
Water brought in by a canal from the Karkheh, fifty kilometres away.

At the centre, a ziggurat. It survives to about twenty-five metres, which makes
it the best-preserved ziggurat in the world and the largest one still standing
— better preserved than anything left at Babylon, Ur or Ashur, because after
the city was abandoned nobody came back to quarry it.

The city was never finished. Thousands of unused bricks were found stacked
where they had been left. After Untash-Napirisha's death the court seems to
have returned to Susa, and the site was occupied thinly and then not at all.

## Was it a capital?

Calling it one is a claim, and the claim is disputed.

For: the king built a royal city, moved his cult establishment into it, and
brought the gods of the highland and the lowland together inside one wall.
Every part of that is a statement about what the kingdom was: a state that had
been an arrangement between two regions asserting itself as one thing, in
brick, at enormous cost.

Against: no source calls it a capital, the administrative centre appears to
have stayed at Susa, and there is a reading on which this was a sacred complex
and a royal burial place rather than a seat of government at all. That reading
may be right. What is not in dispute is that it was built, and what it cost.

## What is not known

Almost nothing is known about how this state worked, and it is worth putting
that directly beside the ziggurat. A state that could conceive, resource and
very nearly complete a walled city with a twenty-five-metre monument at its
centre must have had a way of raising labour, a way of feeding it, a fiscal
apparatus and a rule about who inherited the throne. Not one of those can be
described.

Mitanni is in the same condition and the reason there is that nobody has found
its capital. Here the capital has been excavated since 1897 and the second city
is standing. What is missing is not the buildings. It is the paperwork. Elamite
royal texts are dedications, building inscriptions and curses on anyone who
defaces them; they say what the king built and which god told him to and what
will happen to you if you interfere. There is no Elamite equivalent of the Ur
III delivery receipts, the Middle Assyrian provincial archives or the
Babylonian temple accounts, and without one the institution fields are guesses.

A guess would be worse than the silence, because a guess repeated becomes a
fact. So the honest description of Middle Elamite government is that there
isn't one.
