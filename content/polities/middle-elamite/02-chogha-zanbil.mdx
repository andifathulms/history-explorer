Around 1250 BC Untash-Napirisha built a city from nothing on empty ground forty
kilometres south-east of Susa, and called it Al-Untash-Napirisha, the town of
Untash-Napirisha. Three concentric enclosure walls, the outermost about four
kilometres round. Temples inside them to gods gathered from across the kingdom,
highland and lowland both. Palaces, with a vaulted underground tomb complex.
Water brought in by a canal from the Karkheh, fifty kilometres away.

At the centre, a ziggurat. It survives to about twenty-five metres, which makes
it the best-preserved ziggurat in the world and the largest one still standing —
better preserved than anything left at Babylon, Ur or Ashur, because after the
city was abandoned nobody came back to quarry it.

The city was never finished. Thousands of unused bricks were found stacked where
they had been left. After Untash-Napirisha's death the court seems to have
returned to Susa, and the site was occupied thinly and then not at all.

## What it is doing on this page

This record's `turning_points` codes it as a `capital-move` and marks it
`contested: true`, and both halves need defending.

The move: the king built a royal city, moved his cult establishment into it, and
brought the gods of the highland and the lowland together inside one wall. Every
part of that is a statement about what the kingdom was, and the *changed* test in
coding rule 4.1 is satisfied — a state that had been an arrangement between two
regions asserted itself as one thing, in brick, at enormous cost.

The contest: no source calls it a capital, the administrative centre appears to
have stayed at Susa, and there is a reading on which this was a sacred complex
and a royal burial place rather than a seat of government at all. That reading
may be right. `contested: true` is this site's instrument for saying that the
event's status as a hinge is disputed, which is exactly the dispute here.

## The record this page cannot fill

Three of this record's four institution fields are null.

That is worth putting directly beside the ziggurat. A state that could conceive,
resource and very nearly complete a walled city with a twenty-five-metre
monument at its centre must have had a way of raising labour, a way of feeding
it, a fiscal apparatus and a rule about who inherited the throne, and this site
cannot name any of them.

The Mitanni record in this region has the same one-of-four score and the reason
there is that its capital has never been found. Here the capital has been
excavated since 1897 and the second city is standing. What is missing is not the
buildings. It is the paperwork. Elamite royal texts are dedications, building
inscriptions and curses on anyone who defaces them; they say what the king built
and which god told him to and what will happen to you if you interfere. There is
no Elamite equivalent of the Ur III delivery receipts, the Middle Assyrian
provincial archives or the Babylonian temple accounts, and without one the
institution fields are guesses.

This corpus's rule is that a guess in a coded field is worse than a gap, because
a gap renders as a gap and a guess renders as a fact. So the fields are empty and
this chapter is the explanation.
