Elamite is a language isolate. It has no established relatives — not Semitic,
not Indo-European, not Hurro-Urartian, not Sumerian — and it was written for
something over two thousand years, from the late fourth millennium down to the
Achaemenid chancery, by people living on the plain of Khuzestan and in the
mountains of Fars.

That is the first thing to say, because everything else about Elam has been
read through Mesopotamia. Susa is ninety kilometres from the Tigris floodplain
and has been in and out of Mesopotamian political orbit since Sargon of Akkad;
the Elamites wrote in Mesopotamian cuneiform, borrowed Mesopotamian gods,
fought Mesopotamian wars, and are described almost entirely in Mesopotamian
sources. And the language underneath all of that belongs to nothing else in the
ancient world.

## Two capitals and one title

The Elamite royal style is *king of Anshan and Susa*, and the pairing is the
state's structure in four words. Susa sits on the lowland plain, agriculturally
rich, wide open to Mesopotamia, and has been dug since 1897. Anshan — modern
Tal-e Malyan, in Fars, five hundred kilometres away and fifteen hundred metres
up — is highland, pastoral, defensible, and was not securely identified until
1971.

A state that names both is claiming a lowland and a highland at once, and
Potts's argument is that this is not a detail. Elam is not a Mesopotamian
kingdom that happens to be in Iran. It is a persistent arrangement between a
plain and a mountain range that kept reconstituting itself over two millennia
in configurations that a Mesopotamian scribe could name and not understand.

## Two Elams

The Middle Elamite period is conventionally dated from about 1500 BC, and its
first century is names on bricks. The state proper begins around 1400 with the
Igehalkid dynasty, where inscriptions, a sequence of kings and something
describable as a state all start together, and it ends around 1110 when a
Babylonian king wrecked it.

Then there is a gap of roughly three centuries with almost nothing in it, and
on the far side of the gap there is another Elamite kingdom, fighting Assyria.

That pattern is everywhere in this region. There are two Babylons, a thousand
years apart; two Assyrias; two Elams. In each case what returns after the
interruption is the same ground, the same gods and the same tradition, under a
different family, and in no case did anybody claim to be succeeding the earlier
one. They were resuming a place, which is a different thing.

Three pairs was enough to do something about. The later record of each pair now
carries a `resumes` field naming the earlier one, which renders as a sentence
and is drawn by no thread. The Neo-Elamite record carries it pointing here.

Three pairs is no longer an oddity. It is the organising fact about this
region: a small number of places kept producing states, and the states are
separated by dark ages rather than by successions.
