Elamite is a language isolate. It has no established relatives — not Semitic,
not Indo-European, not Hurro-Urartian, not Sumerian — and it was written for
something over two thousand years, from the late fourth millennium down to the
Achaemenid chancery, by people living on the plain of Khuzestan and in the
mountains of Fars.

That is the first thing to say about this record, because everything else about
Elam has been read through Mesopotamia. Susa is ninety kilometres from the
Tigris floodplain and has been in and out of Mesopotamian political orbit since
Sargon of Akkad; the Elamites wrote in Mesopotamian cuneiform, borrowed
Mesopotamian gods, fought Mesopotamian wars, and are described almost entirely
in Mesopotamian sources. And the language underneath all of that belongs to
nothing else in the ancient world.

## Two capitals and one title

The Elamite royal style is *king of Anshan and Susa*, and the pairing is the
state's structure in four words. Susa sits on the lowland plain, agriculturally
rich, wide open to Mesopotamia, and has been dug since 1897. Anshan — modern
Tal-e Malyan, in Fars, five hundred kilometres away and fifteen hundred metres
up — is highland, pastoral, defensible, and was not securely identified until
1971.

A state that names both is claiming a lowland and a highland at once, and Potts's
argument, which is why this site's Elamite records are drafted from him, is that
this is not a detail. Elam is not a Mesopotamian kingdom that happens to be in
Iran. It is a persistent arrangement between a plain and a mountain range that
kept reconstituting itself over two millennia in configurations that a
Mesopotamian scribe could name and not understand.

## Where this record starts, and why there are two

The Middle Elamite period is conventionally dated from about 1500 BC, and its
first century is names on bricks. This record begins around 1400 with the
Igehalkid dynasty, where inscriptions, a sequence of kings and something
describable as a state all start together, and it ends around 1110 when a
Babylonian king wrecked it.

Then there is a gap of roughly three centuries with almost nothing in it, and on
the far side of the gap there is another Elamite kingdom, fighting Assyria, which
this corpus carries as a second record.

That is the third time in this region the same arrangement has come up. Babylon
is here twice, a thousand years apart. Assyria is here twice, a hundred and forty
years apart. Elam is here twice, three hundred years apart. In each case the
later record is the same ground and the same tradition resuming after an
interruption, in each case this site treats them as two polities because it
measures polities and not places, and in each case there is no edge between the
two, because none of it is succession.

Three pairs was enough to do something about. The later record of each pair now
carries a `resumes` field naming the earlier one, which renders as a sentence and
is drawn by no thread. The Neo-Elamite record carries it pointing here.

Three pairs is no longer an oddity. It is the organising fact about this region:
a small number of places kept producing states, and the states are separated by
dark ages rather than by successions.
