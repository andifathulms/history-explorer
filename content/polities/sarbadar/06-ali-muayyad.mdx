Ali Mu'ayyad took Sabzavar in 1362 and held it, with interruptions, for nineteen
years — much the longest tenure in the state's history, achieved by breaking the
dervish faction and killing or expelling its leadership. That solved the internal
problem by removing half the polity, and left him ruling a small state in a
crowded neighbourhood: the Kartids at Herat, the Jalayirids and the Muzaffarids
to the west, Turkmen amirs in Mazandaran, and a rival Sarbadar claimant backed
by some of the dervishes he had driven out.

In 1381 Timur crossed into Khurasan. Ali Mu'ayyad submitted, and did more than
submit: he had already written asking him to come, wanting the Timurid army
against his own neighbours and his own exiles. He got what he asked for, joined
Timur's service, and died a few years later on campaign in Timur's army.

So there was no siege and no massacre. The Muzaffarids, a few hundred miles
south-west, were exterminated to a man; the difference was entirely in whether
the ruler had made his calculation early enough. Sabzavar became a Timurid town.
The Sarbadar name turns up once or twice afterwards attached to risings, and
then does not.

Forty-four years is not long. It is longer than the Palmyrene empire, longer
than most reigns anywhere, and it was managed without a dynasty, without a
succession rule, without a tribal army and without a legitimating genealogy, in
the middle of a country where every neighbour had all four.

The last of them ended it by writing to Timur and asking him to come.
