---
title: Two parties in one town
drafted_from: smith-1970
phase: expansion

The Sarbadar state had two constituencies and they wanted incompatible things.

The dervishes — the *shaykhiyya*, Hasan Juri's organised following, with cells
across Khurasan and a discipline the chronicles describe with alarm — wanted a
religious order in power: Shi'i, expectant, hostile to the landed and the
comfortable, and understanding the movement as a preparation for something. The
other party were the landowners and townsmen who had risen in 1337 and who
wanted what people in that position usually want, which was to hold the district,
collect the tax themselves, and be left alone. They were largely Sunni and were
entirely uninterested in a theocracy.

For a few years the combination worked and it worked well. Mas'ud took Nishapur,
Tus and Mashhad, pushed west, and by the early 1340s the Sarbadars held most of
western Khurasan and were a power the Kartids of Herat and the amirs of
Mazandaran had to deal with. The dervishes supplied the discipline and the
recruitment; the landowners supplied the administration and the money.

It broke at Zawa in 1342. The Sarbadars fought the Kartids of Herat, and Hasan
Juri was killed during the battle — struck down by his own side, according to
the version the chronicles preferred, and this site marks the entry
`contested: true` because whether that is what happened is exactly what cannot
be established. What is not in doubt is the effect. The one man both factions
accepted was gone, and for the following thirty-nine years the leadership swung
between a dervish and a landowner and back, each new man undoing his
predecessor's policy and a good many of them killing him first.

Mas'ud himself was killed in 1344, campaigning north into Mazandaran, and the
expansion ended with him. What remains after that is a small, well-defended,
internally violent state in Khurasan that outlived several larger neighbours.
