---
title: Not a dynasty
drafted_from: smith-1970
phase: aside
---

Almost every state of this period is a dynasty or is trying to become one. This
one never was, and two things about it are consequently hard to describe in the
ordinary vocabulary.

The first is how the leadership passed, which is that it did not pass at all. In
forty-four years there were about a dozen leaders, most of them killed by their
own side, with the position alternating between two parties that could neither
agree nor separate. Lane-Poole counts twelve successive chiefs of whom nine
suffered violent deaths, and notes that not one of them held the command for
more than six or seven years. That is not a succession rule with a name. It is a
standing fight, interrupted by funerals.

The second is what the army was. It was the movement in arms: a religious
brotherhood's membership under discipline, alongside the armed retainers of the
landowners who had risen with it. It was not a tribal levy — these were
villagers and townsmen, explicitly not a tribe. It was not a conscription,
because there was no administration to do the levying when it started. It was
not a slave guard, a mercenary corps, or men holding land against service.
Nobody has a word for a state whose army is a Sufi order and a landowners'
association in an uneasy alliance, and it is better to say so than to reach for
the nearest familiar label.

There is a third thing, which is what this polity has been made to mean. Soviet and Iranian Marxist historiography read the Sarbadars as a
peasant republic — an early rising of the common people against feudal
oppression, with the dervishes as its ideological cadre. Hostile medieval
chroniclers read them as heretics and bandits. Smith's book is largely an
attempt to get behind both readings to what the coins and the inscriptions can
actually establish, and his answer is more modest than either: a factional
regional state, unusual in its origins, held together by the fact that everyone
in it had more to fear from outside than from each other.
