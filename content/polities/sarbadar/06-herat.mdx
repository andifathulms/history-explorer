---
title: The neighbour who lasted forty years
drafted_from: browne-1920
phase: contraction
---

The Sarbadar rising had a near neighbour of almost exactly its own age and the
opposite character, and for most of both their existences they were fighting
each other.

Mu'izz al-Din of the Kart dynasty came to the throne at Herat in 1331. Browne
notes what his accession nearly coincided with: the death of Abu Sa'id, which
in practice ended Mongol rule over Iran; the birth of Timur; and the rising at
Sabzavar. Those are the three things that decide the next sixty years of
Khurasan, and two of them arrived within a few years of each other in the same
province, a fortnight's ride apart.

Everything about the two states was opposed. Herat was a dynasty with a
genealogy, a settled capital, a forty-year reign and the Sunni law; Sabzavar
had a shifting command, Shi'i and expectant, and a leadership that killed itself
about once every five years. Browne, following Lane-Poole, gives the figure that
defines the period between Mas'ud's death and Ali Mu'ayyad's long tenure: twelve
chiefs, nine of them violently killed, not one of them holding the command more
than six or seven years. Herat had one ruler for the whole of it.

That did not make Herat the civilised party. After taking Badghis, Mu'izz al-Din
built two towers of the heads of his enemies — Browne's phrase is that it was
done in the style later made familiar by Timur, and the point is that the style
was familiar in Khurasan a generation before Timur reached it. He reigned, in
Browne's careful formula, not ingloriously, though not without occasional acts
of barbarity which were characteristic of the time. He died in 1369 and was
buried at Herat beside the Ghurid sultan Ghiyath al-Din Muhammad Sam.

The frontier between them ran through Nishapur and it moved. Ali Mu'ayyad, the
one Sarbadar who held power long enough to have a policy, took Bistam and
Farhadjird and brought Nishapur over; the Karts took Nishapur back in 1375 or
1376. That is the whole of it. In forty years neither state could finish the
other, and neither could stop trying, and what they succeeded in doing was
exhausting the province they were both living on at the moment a third party
was assembling an army in Transoxiana.

Both were finished by that third party within a few years. Timur occupied Herat
in the spring of 1381, took Ghiyath al-Din Pir Ali and his eldest son to
Samarkand and imprisoned them, imprisoned two more of the family elsewhere, and
had all of them put to death after a rebellion at Herat in 1389 gave him the
occasion. Browne's chronology has the Kart dynasty ending in that year and puts
the extinction of the Sarbadars a year earlier, in 1388 — which does not agree
with the modern reckoning that closes the Sarbadar account in 1381, when Ali
Mu'ayyad submitted and went into Timur's service. Both dates are defensible and
they are measuring different things: one the last Sarbadar regime, the other the
last Sarbadar. The disagreement is worth leaving standing rather than split,
because there was never a moment at which this state was formally abolished, and
a polity without a dynasty does not have the kind of ending that can be dated by
a death.
