Ali Mu'ayyad took Sabzavar in 1362 and held it, with interruptions, for nineteen
years — much the longest tenure in the state's history, achieved by breaking the
dervish faction and killing or expelling its leadership. That solved the internal
problem by removing half the polity, and left him ruling a small state in a
crowded neighbourhood: the Kartids at Herat, the Jalayirids and the Muzaffarids
to the west, Turkmen amirs in Mazandaran, and a rival Sarbadar claimant backed
by some of the dervishes he had driven out.

In 1381 Timur crossed into Khurasan. Ali Mu'ayyad submitted, and did more than
submit: he had already written asking him to come, wanting the Timurid army
against his own neighbours and his own exiles. He got what he asked for, joined
Timur's service, and died a few years later on campaign in Timur's army.

This site types the ending `gradual absorption` rather than conquest. There was
no siege and no massacre — the contrast with the Muzaffarid record two pages
back could not be sharper, and the difference was entirely in whether the ruler
had made a calculation early enough. Sabzavar became a Timurid town. The
Sarbadar name reappears once or twice afterwards attached to risings, and then
does not.

What it leaves is a question the corpus is otherwise not equipped to ask.
Forty-four years is not a long time, but it is longer than the Palmyrene empire
and longer than most of the reigns on this site, and it was managed without a
dynasty, without a succession rule, without a tribal army and without a
legitimating genealogy, in the middle of a country where every neighbour had all
four. The site cannot rank that: the influence counts are null, the extent is
null, and of the four institutions the one that would say how such a state
raised an army is the one the vocabulary cannot reach. The page is here because a corpus of sixty
dynasties should carry the one thing in its own period that was not one.
