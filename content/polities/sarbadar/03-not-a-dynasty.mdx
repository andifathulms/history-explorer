---
title: Not a dynasty
drafted_from: smith-1970
phase: aside
---

Every other polity on this site is a dynasty or is trying to be one. This one is
not, and the fields show the strain in two different ways that are worth telling
apart.

`succession_rule` is `factional`, and that is a real coding rather than a
disguised gap. The rulebook created the value for the Mamluk sultanate, where
the throne went to whichever military faction could impose its man and recording
that as "unaddressed" would lose the most distinctive thing about the polity.
The Sarbadars are the same answer reached from the opposite direction: about a
dozen leaders in forty-four years, most of them killed by their own side, with
the leadership alternating between two parties that could not agree and could
not separate. Two of the corpus's clearest instances of a state with no
succession rule are a slave-soldier sultanate in Cairo and a coalition of
dervishes and landowners in Khurasan.

`military_basis` is null, and it is null for a different reason from most of the
nulls in this corpus. Usually the field is empty because nobody has opened a
source that addresses the question. Here a source addresses it and the closed
vocabulary cannot hold the answer. The Sarbadar force was the movement in arms —
a religious brotherhood's membership under discipline, alongside the armed
retainers of the landowners who had risen with it. `tribal-levy` is wrong; these
were villagers and townsmen, explicitly not a tribe. `conscript` would assert an
administrative obligation, and there was no administration doing the levying
when it started. `land-grant`, `slave-soldier` and `mercenary` are all simply
false.

The coding rules anticipate this. Standing requirement 4 in part three says that
when a polity does not fit, the answer is null or a vocabulary change argued on
the merits, and never the nearest familiar label. This page takes the first
option, and states the case here so the second remains available.

There is a third thing the fields cannot say, which is what this polity has been
made to mean. Soviet and Iranian Marxist historiography read the Sarbadars as a
peasant republic — an early rising of the common people against feudal
oppression, with the dervishes as its ideological cadre. Hostile medieval
chroniclers read them as heretics and bandits. Smith's book is largely an
attempt to get behind both readings to what the coins and the inscriptions can
actually establish, and his answer is more modest than either: a factional
regional state, unusual in its origins, held together by the fact that everyone
in it had more to fear from outside than from each other.
