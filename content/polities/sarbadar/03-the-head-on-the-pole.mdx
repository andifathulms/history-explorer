The furthest the Sarbadars ever reached was Mazandaran, and the best account of
what happened there is in the local chronicle of the country they invaded —
which is to say, written by the other side.

## What the Tabaristan chronicle saw

The continuation of Ibn Isfandiyar's history of Tabaristan picks up the
Sarbadars from underneath. Amir Mas'ud had taken Khurasan from Herat to Gurgan
and Qumis, placed governors in the important towns, and come to Astarabad
intending to subjugate Mazandaran. He tried first to win the local rulers over,
and one of them — Kiya Jamal al-Din Ahmad Jal, whom the chronicler describes as
a powerful, sagacious and experienced noble — went to negotiate with his
nephews, was well received, and was then made to accompany the army as it
marched on.

The kings of Mazandaran refused, in blunt terms. Mas'ud marched on Amul, and the
two armies met on the plain outside it on 13 April 1343, the Mazandaranis
defending a stockade they had thrown up beside their king's palace.

Then the chronicler gives the speech that decided it. Jamal al-Din, seeing the
battle was unavoidable and that Mas'ud would not listen to him, sent repeated
messages to his kinsmen telling them to defend the honour of Mazandaran and not
to trouble themselves about what became of him: *I am an old man, who have lived
my life; if I perish and Mazandaran continue in honour it is better than that ye
should submit to subjection and abasement.*

The province responded. All of them, from Tammisha to the frontier of Gilan,
forgot their private quarrels and presented a united face.

## The bargain

Mas'ud, alarmed by their firmness, asked the men he had been holding to get him
out of Amul. One of his own amirs deserted to the Mazandaranis, which frightened
him further, and he gave Kiya Ahmad Jal five *kharwars* of silver to guide him
out of the country.

The Kiya sent the money to his kinsmen and told them to build earthworks from
the river Hurmuz to Sari and close the roads.

After ten days in Amul the Sarbadar army left, and a parasang outside the city
it was met in front by the ustandar and attacked in the flank by the soldiers of
Mazandaran. Mas'ud's head was cut off, stuffed with straw, and displayed to
everyone on the exact spot where he had camped a few days before with so great
and well-equipped an army.

## What it cost

That is the end of Sarbadar expansion. The state held western Khurasan for
another thirty-eight years and never again threatened anybody outside it.

Two things are worth taking from the way this is recorded. The first is that the
Khurasani sources date Mas'ud's death to 1344 and the Tabaristan chronicle puts
the battle at Amul in April 1343, and the discrepancy has never been resolved;
it is the ordinary condition of a period in which every province kept its own
chronicle and nobody reconciled them.

The second is the picture from below. Seen from Sabzavar the Sarbadars are a
movement of villagers and dervishes against oppression. Seen from Amul they are
a Khurasani army arriving to subjugate the province, bought off with silver by
an old man who then used the money to dig earthworks against them, and beaten
by a country that briefly stopped quarrelling with itself in order to do it.
