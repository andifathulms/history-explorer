The name is a declaration. *Sar be dar* — head on the gallows — is what they are
reported to have said: better our heads on the gallows than to go on living like
this. Whether anyone said it in those words at the time is the kind of thing the
sources cannot settle, but the name stuck and it is the one the coins and the
chronicles use.

The thing itself began in 1337 in a village called Bashtin, near Sabzavar in
western Khurasan, and it began over tax. The Ilkhanate had collapsed two years
earlier and the collectors were still coming, now on behalf of whichever amir
currently claimed the province. A local landowner named Abd al-Razzaq killed one
of them — the accounts vary on the provocation and several of them involve his
household — and instead of fleeing he raised the district, took Sabzavar, and
kept it.

States generally begin with a conquest, a secession, a general, or a family
already in possession of something. This one begins with a tax collector being
killed in a village, and it lasted forty-four years.

It also began the way it would continue. Within a year Abd al-Razzaq had been
stabbed to death by his own brother, Wajih al-Din Mas'ud, during an argument.
Mas'ud took the leadership, and turned out to be the best commander the movement
produced.

Behind the revolt was something older than the tax. Khurasan in the 1330s was
full of dervish brotherhoods, and one of them — the followers of Shaykh Khalifa,
who had been murdered in Sabzavar a couple of years earlier, and after him of
his disciple Shaykh Hasan Juri — was preaching something more than piety.
Mas'ud released Hasan Juri from prison and made him a partner. That decision is
what turned a tax revolt into a state, and it is what the state could never
afterwards resolve.
