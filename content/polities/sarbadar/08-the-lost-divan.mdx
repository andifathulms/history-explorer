The one substantial poet attached to the Sarbadars lost his entire life's work
in the battle that broke them, and the consequence is that nobody can now
establish whose side he was on.

## What happened at Zawa

Ibn-i Yamin — Amir Mahmud, son of Yamin al-Din, a government accountant of
Faryumad — was at the battle between Zawa and Khwaf in July 1342, where the
Sarbadars fought the Kartids of Herat and Shaykh Hasan Juri was killed.

Fasihi's chronicle records the two events in the same breath, which is how they
have come down: the death of the shaykh, and the loss of the complete poetical
works of Amir Fakhr al-Din Mahmud ibn-i Yamin, looted in the battle. The poet
wrote a fragment about it himself. It fell into the hands of the spoilers, and
thereafter no trace of it was found.

He was taken prisoner by Malik Mu'izz al-Din Kart, the enemy commander, who
received him well and treated him with honour — and afterwards Ibn-i Yamin sent
him verses from Sabzavar.

## Why the loss matters

Browne is precise about the consequence, and it is not merely sentimental.

Because the divan is gone, it is impossible to determine with certainty who Ibn-i
Yamin's patrons were or to whom his panegyrics were chiefly addressed. One
biographical tradition says he was the panegyrist of the Sarbadars, which Browne
doubts. Another says he was the panegyrist of Toghay Temür, one of the Mongol
claimants the Sarbadars fought. A third gives a date for his death that is
almost certainly wrong.

So the single literary figure the movement produced cannot be assigned to it.
Everything that would have settled the question — the dedications, the
occasional poems, the addresses — was in the book that was carried off the field
at Zawa by the men who had just killed the shaykh.

## What is left

The *Muqatta'at*, the Fragments: several hundred short pieces, mostly
philosophical, ethical or mystical, which Browne thought among the best things
of their kind. They survived because they had already been copied out
separately, being the sort of verse people write down for themselves.

That is a fair description of what happened to the Sarbadars as a whole. The
thing that was organised — the dervish order, the alliance, the state — was
destroyed and left almost no record it wrote itself. What came through was the
loose material that had already been dispersed: the coins with the Shi'i
formulae, the name, a legal manual written in Damascus for a state that no
longer existed by the time it arrived, and a few hundred fragments by a man
whose politics cannot be recovered.

Nine of twelve chiefs died violently, and the poet lost his book. It was not a
regime that was good at keeping things.
