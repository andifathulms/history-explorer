A band of Italian mercenaries called the Mamertines had seized the Sicilian
city of Messana by treachery, murdering the men and taking the women for
themselves - the same kind of freebooting seizure Rome's own consuls had
just finished punishing at Rhegium, on the Italian side of the strait, by
executing every member of a similar garrison to the last man. When Hiero of
Syracuse moved to crush the Mamertines in turn, they appealed to Carthage
first and got a garrison; then, distrusting their new protectors, they
appealed to Rome as well. The Roman Senate, Bosworth Smith notes, faced an
open contradiction between the gratitude it owed its ally Hiero and the
strategic folly of leaving a fortress commanding the Sicilian narrows in
Carthaginian hands. Ambition won. Rome intervened, and the two great powers
of the western Mediterranean went to war in 264 BC over a garrison of
pirates neither side could really claim to be defending on principle.

## Two campaigns nobody expected

The war that followed ran twenty-three years, and Bosworth Smith argues
against the standard verdict of his own century that it was the dull,
forgettable half of the Punic Wars next to Hannibal's exploits: unlike the
Second War, the First still shows Carthage as a genuine naval power,
contesting the sea Rome had barely begun to build a fleet for. The Romans,
starting from almost nothing, built and lost entire fleets to storms twice
over and kept building more. Regulus, having beaten the Carthaginian navy
and landed an army in Africa itself, came within sight of dictating peace
terms to a Carthage in real danger - until a Spartan mercenary captain named
Xanthippus, hired almost as an afterthought, retrained the Carthaginian army
around its elephants and cavalry and destroyed Regulus's own force outright,
capturing the consul himself. In Sicily the war ground down into a siege of
attrition at Lilybaeum that Bosworth Smith calls as long, in its way, as the
siege of Troy - until a young commander named Hamilcar Barca seized the
heights above Panormus and held them against the whole weight of a Roman
army for three years, raiding the Italian coast almost at will and making
his own name, by the war's own last years, synonymous with everything
Carthage still had left to fight with.

## A peace Carthage could not really afford to keep

None of it was enough. A final Roman fleet, funded by patriotic citizens
after the treasury ran dry, cut off Hamilcar's own supply line at sea, and
Carthage sued for peace rather than let his army starve. The Lutatius treaty
of 241 BC cost Carthage Sicily outright - the first Carthaginian territory
ever surrendered to a foreign power - along with a war indemnity. Hamilcar,
Bosworth Smith is careful to note, submitted to it as a professional soldier
accepting a political decision he had no part in, not as a beaten man.

## A war worse than the one just finished

The peace exposed what the war had cost at home. Carthage's own mercenary
army, unpaid and unfed on the government's usual excuses, mutinied outside
the city walls, and native Libyan subjects - taxed to breaking point to fund
the war just lost - rose with them. What followed, remembered as the
Truceless War, matched the Punic War itself for length and outdid it for
sheer cruelty on both sides; Hamilcar, recalled to sole command only after
the government's own mishandling had let the revolt spread to Sardinia and
Hippo and Utica as well, put down the mercenaries by force that included
having prisoners trampled by elephants in reprisal for what had been done to
his own men. Rome, watching a client it had just defeated nearly bleed out
entirely, chose that moment to seize Sardinia and Corsica outright under the
transparent pretext that Carthage's own war preparations against the rebels
were secretly aimed at Rome - an act of naked opportunism that cost Carthage
another indemnity on top of the first and left Hamilcar, in Bosworth Smith's
own judgment, nursing a grievance he would spend the rest of his life
preparing to settle.
