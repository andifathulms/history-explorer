Before he crossed to Spain to begin rebuilding what the First Punic War and
the Truceless War between them had cost Carthage, Hamilcar Barca took his
nine-year-old son to a sacrifice, sent the attendants away, and asked the
boy if he wanted to come to the wars with him. Hannibal said yes. "Lay your
hand then," Hamilcar told him, "on the sacrifice and swear eternal enmity to
the Romans." Hannibal swore it, and - by the later account he himself gave
to King Antiochus, which Bosworth Smith takes as substantially genuine -
kept the oath to his last breath.

## An empire built to pay for the next war

What Hamilcar built in Spain over the following nine years was not a raid
but a state: silver mines, native alliances secured by marriage and
diplomacy as often as by the sword, and an army recruited and trained on
Iberian soil rather than shipped from Africa. He had watched Carthage lose a
war for want of resources and a mercenary army it could not pay; Spain was
his answer to both problems at once, a base rich and secure enough to fund a
second war without depending on a government in Carthage that had failed
him during the first. Roman writers, hostile to Carthage on principle, still
could not help admitting what Hamilcar had accomplished: a treaty he
concluded fixed the Ebro as the boundary of Rome's own interest in Spain, an
acknowledgment from Rome itself that a private soldier operating half in
revolt against his own city's caution had built something too substantial
to ignore. Hamilcar did not live to see the war he was preparing for. He
died in battle against a native tribe, and command passed to Hasdrubal, his
son-in-law.

## New Carthage

Hasdrubal did not merely hold what Hamilcar had won. He organized it,
founding New Carthage on a fine natural harbor on the southeast coast, close
to further silver deposits, and gave the Barcid enterprise in Spain a
capital worthy of the name it carried - the nearest thing to a second
metropolis Carthage's empire outside Africa would ever have. Hannibal, now
grown, served under Hasdrubal's own command through these years,
"watching in silence," as Bosworth Smith puts it, the patient construction
of the power he had already sworn as a boy to turn against Rome. When
Hasdrubal too was killed, the army in Spain did what a Carthaginian army had
never formally been asked to do before: it acclaimed Hannibal commander on
the spot, and the government at Carthage, whatever its own private
reservations, confirmed a choice it had had no real part in making.

## A war waiting only for its occasion

By the time Hannibal held sole command, the empire his father and brother-
in-law had spent sixteen years assembling was ready for exactly the purpose
it had been built for. Rome, distracted by wars elsewhere and reassured by
the Ebro treaty it had itself accepted, had left a Greek trading city called
Saguntum - south of that river, inside what Hannibal considered his own
sphere - without any clear protection worth the name. Hannibal needed only a
pretext. Saguntum would shortly give him one.
