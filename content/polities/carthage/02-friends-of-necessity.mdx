Carthage did not stay a peaceable trading factory forever. Bosworth
Smith reads the sixth century BC as the hinge: a rising tide of Greek
colonisation had already pushed Phoenician settlement out of Italy,
Greece and the islands of the Levant, and Carthage's own leaders
recognized that Africa, Sicily and Spain would go the same way unless
the city's own policy changed from accommodation to open resistance.
The clearest sign of that shift came in 537 BC, when Carthage allied
with the Etruscans - a people its own fleets would spend much of the
following two centuries fighting - for one shared purpose: driving
Phocaean Greek exiles out of Corsica before they could establish a
permanent foothold there. It was an alliance of necessity rather than
friendship, and it marked Carthage's own transformation from a
merchant colony content to pay rent for its own soil into a naval and
military power willing to contest an entire sea.

## A rival too large to ignore

The same instinct for self-preservation drove Carthage's own actions
elsewhere in this period: pressuring Cyrene to look toward Egypt
rather than westward for its own expansion, and working to secure
Carthaginian interests in Sicily against the same Greek colonial
pressure pressing on Corsica. None of these individual moves made
Carthage a Mediterranean superpower overnight, but together they mark
the moment a trading city started acting like a state with borders to
defend rather than a factory with only goods to protect - the
beginning of the reach that would eventually make Carthage, in
Bosworth Smith's own later judgment, the one power Rome could never
simply out-wait or outgrow, and had to destroy outright instead.
