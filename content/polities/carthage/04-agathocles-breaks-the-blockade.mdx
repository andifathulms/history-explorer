For a century and a half Carthage had fought its wars with Sicily's
Greek cities on Sicilian soil, secure in the knowledge that the
fighting, however costly, happened somewhere else. In 310 BC a
Syracusan tyrant broke that assumption entirely, carrying the war to
Carthage's own African heartland and showing, for the first time,
exactly how the city could be attacked at home.

## A tyrant escapes his own siege

Agathocles seized power at Syracuse after the death of Timoleon, the
liberator whose victory at the Crimesus decades earlier had briefly
secured the city two decades of peace. War with Carthage resumed under
his rule, and by 310 BC a Carthaginian fleet had Agathocles blockaded
inside his own capital, with a Carthaginian army waiting on land to
finish what the fleet had started. Rather than accept a siege he had
little hope of winning, Agathocles broke through the blockading
squadron with a small flotilla of ships and sailed directly for Africa -
a move so unexpected that the Carthaginian force that had been
besieging Syracuse found itself, within weeks, having to defend
Carthage's own countryside instead.

## Three years in the Carthaginian heartland

What Agathocles found on landing was a country that had never been
fought over: gardens and plantations threaded with irrigation canals,
estate after estate crowded with orchards, vineyards and livestock,
built up over generations of uninterrupted peace by the wealthiest
families in Carthage. He spent three years overrunning it, occupying,
by report, an extraordinary number of Carthaginian towns and stirring
up open revolt among the African subject peoples who had never had
reason to rise against Carthage while its armies were busy overseas.
Carthage responded the only way a city under direct threat could: pouring
out its own citizen infantry and cavalry, its war-chariots, and the same
elite Sacred Band that had died to the last man at the Crimesus a
generation before, all committed now to defending the capital itself
rather than a distant Sicilian battlefield. Agathocles held the
initiative for the whole of his African campaign before finally
withdrawing back to Sicily to fight the wars closer to home that his
absence had let fester.

## A lesson Carthage could not undo

The physical damage Agathocles had inflicted was real but repairable,
and Carthaginian wealth and energy soon restored what his campaign had
burned or plundered. What could not be undone was the demonstration
itself. Agathocles had shown that Carthage's own African heartland -
never seriously threatened in a hundred and fifty years of Sicilian
warfare - could be reached and ravaged by an enemy willing to gamble
everything on a single crossing. He had probed the empire's actual
weakness rather than its Sicilian periphery, and although the Greeks of
Sicily lacked the strength to follow up what Agathocles had started, a
far more formidable power, within a lifetime of his death, would study
the same lesson and apply it with far greater resources than a single
tyrant's escaping fleet.
