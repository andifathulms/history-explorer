Carthage recovered from Zama faster than either side expected. Stripped of
its overseas empire, reduced to Africa alone, and bound to a war indemnity
paid out over fifty years, the city nonetheless rebuilt its own trade and
its own treasury so quickly that Rome's own suspicion of it began to look,
within a generation, like the more urgent problem again. Hannibal himself,
still trusted by his own people despite the defeat that had ended the war,
spent several postwar years as a civilian magistrate working to reform the
oligarchy that had mismanaged Carthage's finances throughout the fighting -
until the same men he had reformed against denounced him to Rome, which
demanded his surrender. Rather than let his own presence become the pretext
for a war Carthage could not survive twice, Hannibal fled into exile,
eventually reaching the court of Antiochus of Syria and, later, Bithynia,
where - hunted still, decades after Zama, by a Rome that never stopped
fearing him - he died by his own hand rather than be taken.

## The neighbor Rome built on purpose

What made the peace that followed Zama a lasting danger rather than a
lasting settlement was Massinissa, the Numidian king Rome had installed and
then deliberately used against the city it claimed to be at peace with.
Bosworth Smith is direct about the mechanism: Massinissa was encouraged to
raid Carthaginian territory, and Carthage was then left to complain to
Rome if it dared defend itself - "the lamb in the fable," whose own guilt or
innocence never mattered because the verdict had already been decided by
the wolf sitting in judgment. Carthage endured this for decades rather than
risk a war its own treaty with Rome technically forbade it from fighting
without Roman permission - permission Rome had no intention of granting.

## Figs from three days away

What finally tipped Roman anxiety into open resolve was not a Carthaginian
provocation but a Carthaginian recovery too visible to ignore. Cato the
Elder, sent to arbitrate yet another Numidian border dispute, came home and
displayed a basket of fresh figs to the Senate - fruit so large and so
recently picked, he told them, that it must have grown in Carthaginian
territory only three days' sail from Rome itself. The point needed no
further argument: a city Rome had spent a war and a peace treaty trying to
cripple was, within living memory of Zama, prosperous enough to alarm a
senator with a piece of fruit. From that point, whatever the actual
provocation offered in any given year, Cato is remembered for ending every
speech he gave in the Senate, on any subject at all, with the same three
words - Carthage must be destroyed. Rome had already decided the outcome
long before it found the occasion to act on it.
