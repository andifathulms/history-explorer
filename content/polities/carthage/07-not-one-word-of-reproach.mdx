Hannibal took Saguntum by siege in 219 BC, and when Rome demanded he answer
for it, Carthage's own government backed him rather than surrender him -
Bosworth Smith reads the decision, whatever the private misgivings of the
peace faction at home, as proof of how completely the Barcid family's own
standing had come to carry the state along with it. Rome declared war.
Hannibal did not wait to be invaded.

## Alps

A Carthaginian fleet could probably have carried an army safely along the
coast to Italy, avoiding both the dangerous Sicilian waters that had cost so
much in the First War and the mountains altogether. Hannibal chose to march
instead - through the Pyrenees, across the Rhone, and over the Alps
themselves, in a campaign that would take months rather than days and could
not be reversed once begun. Bosworth Smith weighs the decision without fully
resolving it: perhaps Carthage's own government could not or would not
supply the ships, perhaps Hannibal simply underestimated a route no friendly
account of the mountains could have prepared him for. What decided him,
almost certainly, was the alliance already struck with the Gallic tribes of
the Po valley and beyond, whose own restless hostility to Rome promised
reinforcement no sea route could offer. The march cost him men, animals and
time no history has ever fully accounted for. It also put a Carthaginian
army, against every Roman expectation, on Italian soil within reach of Rome
itself.

## Trebia, Trasimene, and a dictator who would not fight

The Romans, caught planning an invasion of Africa rather than a defense of
Italy, scrambled to respond. Hannibal broke a Roman army at the Trebia river
before the year 218 was out, then destroyed a second in 217 in an ambush
along Lake Trasimene that killed the consul Flaminius along with most of his
men. Rome's answer was Fabius, given dictatorial power and a strategy his
own troops despised: shadow Hannibal everywhere, risk battle nowhere, and
let an invading army that could not be resupplied from home wear itself down
by simply existing in hostile country. It earned him the nickname
Cunctator - the Lingerer - meant as an insult before it became, in
Bosworth Smith's own judgment, the only policy that had actually kept Rome
from a third consecutive disaster.

## Cannae

Fabius's own caution did not survive his term of office. In 216 the new
consuls, Aemilius Paullus and Terentius Varro, took the field under explicit
Senate orders to force a battle, and got one at Cannae on a plain that
suited Hannibal's own cavalry far better than it suited Rome's infantry
mass. The result was, by the ancient world's own reckoning, the worst single
defeat Rome ever absorbed and survived: tens of thousands of Roman dead in
a single day, a consul among them. Hannibal, in the full flush of the
greatest victory of his life, offered peace - he had no wish, he told the
Roman prisoners he gathered to hear it, to fight a war of extermination, and
he sent some of them home on parole to open negotiations. Rome refused to
speak the word peace at all. It refused even to ransom the captured
soldiers who had survived by surrendering. And when Varro himself, the
consul whose own decisions bore much of the blame for the catastrophe,
returned to the city, the citizens went out to meet him and thanked him,
Bosworth Smith records, for not having despaired of the republic - not one
word of reproach for a defeat that would have ended most governments outright.

## Zama

That refusal to negotiate was the war's real turning point, more than any
single battle after it. Hannibal held much of southern Italy for over a
decade, winning battles he could no longer convert into a peace Rome would
accept, while a young Roman commander named Scipio built a new army in
Spain, in Hannibal's own former base, and carried the war to Africa itself.
Recalled at last to defend Carthage directly, Hannibal met Scipio at Zama in
202 BC and lost the only major pitched battle of his career. Twenty thousand
of his own men fell; twenty thousand more were taken prisoner; Hannibal
himself escaped with a handful of survivors to Adrumetum, and told his own
government plainly that he had been beaten not merely in the battle but in
the war. Carthage's own peace envoys tried to lay the blame on Hannibal
alone, claiming the war had been his private enterprise rather than the
state's - a claim Bosworth Smith calls too gross even for the Romans to
credit, and one the whole course of the war it had funded, armed and backed
for seventeen years plainly contradicted.
