Carthage's confidence after Alalia was tested a generation later on
Sicilian soil, in a defeat so total that a Carthaginian commander is
said to have thrown himself onto a burning sacrificial pyre rather than
return home to report it - and the wars that followed, on and off, for
a century and a half, would decide who actually ruled the island Rome
would one day fight Carthage over in earnest.

## Himera, and a general who did not come home

By 480 BC, Greek colonies had spread along Sicily's coast far enough to
press directly on the older Phoenician settlements clustered in the
island's western corner. Hamilcar, son of Mago, led a large army drawn
from across Carthage's own sphere of influence to Sicily that year to
push the Greeks back. The armies met at Himera, and the battle lasted
from morning to evening before ending in the complete rout of the
Carthaginian force. Hamilcar himself, who had spent the battle
sacrificing to Carthage's own gods in his combined role as chief
magistrate and commander, disappeared once the defeat became clear and
was never seen again - the Carthaginian tradition itself held that he
had flung himself onto his own sacrificial fire rather than survive the
disaster, and that his memory was honored with something close to
divine reverence afterward. Carthage drew back to her three oldest
Sicilian strongholds and did not attempt another major Sicilian war for
seventy years, watching instead as Greek cities fought each other over
the island she had just failed to win.

## Revenge, and a century of ruin

In 410 BC the city of Egesta, invoking an old grievance against the
Greek city of Selinus, appealed to Carthage for help - and after long
hesitation, Carthage agreed. Command went to Hannibal, grandson of the
Hamilcar who had died at Himera and, in the judgment of the Greek
historian Diodorus, a man with a family reason to hate the enemy he was
about to fight. Selinus fell almost immediately, its people slaughtered
and its temples leveled. Himera fell the same way the following year:
Hannibal refused a Syracusan appeal to spare its temples, executed
three thousand prisoners on the exact spot where his grandfather had
died, and sailed home only after the city's destruction was complete.
He returned three years later to take Agrigentum after a seven-month
siege, and then Gela and Camarina in turn - erasing Greek civic life
from most of Sicily's southern coast within a few years of campaigning.

The devastation did not go unanswered. A plague that killed half of
Hannibal's own army cut the second campaign short, and Syracuse used
the reprieve to hand power to a new strongman, the tyrant Dionysius.
Once secure in office, Dionysius reversed the tide of invasion entirely,
driving Carthaginian settlers from the towns Hannibal had conquered and
pressing as far as the island fortress of Motya itself, whose garrison
fought street by street before the city finally fell. Carthage answered
with an even larger expedition under Himilco in 397 BC, a hundred
thousand men who retook Motya and Eryx within a year and pushed
Dionysius back to Syracuse itself - only for a fresh plague to break
the Carthaginian army a second time, forcing Himilco to abandon the
campaign and, by one account, to buy his own army's safe passage home
by betraying the mercenaries who had fought alongside it.

## Crimesus, and a storm that decided a battle

The wars between Carthage and Syracuse's tyrants continued in cycles
for generations after Himilco's retreat, but one battle from this long
contest stood out even to ancient writers for how the outcome turned:
in 341 BC, the Corinthian liberator Timoleon met a Carthaginian army at
the river Crimesus, and a sudden storm of rain, hail and thunder broke
directly in the Carthaginians' faces at the critical moment, swelling
the river into a torrent that swept away chariots, horses and heavily
armored infantry alike. Carthage's elite Sacred Band, twenty-five
hundred nobles who fought in gold rings and rich armor, held their
ground and died to the last man rather than break; the rest of the
army did not, and Timoleon returned to Syracuse laden with captured
wealth. Sicily would remain contested ground between Carthage and the
Greek cities for decades more, but no single defeat in that long contest
would come closer to costing Carthage its Sicilian foothold entirely
until a Syracusan tyrant, a generation later, decided that the safest
way to fight that war was to stop fighting it on Sicilian soil at all.
