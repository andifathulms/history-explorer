Carthage's final war with Rome, fought between 149 and 146 BC, ended
in a siege the city could not survive and its own captors were not
inclined to soften. When the last resistance collapsed, Bosworth
Smith records the death of Hasdrubal's own wife in vivid, almost
certainly embellished detail - dressed in her finest, she reportedly
appeared on the roof of a temple with her two sons, denounced her
husband for surrendering himself to Scipio while she and their
children fought on, killed the boys herself, and threw herself with
them into the flames rather than accept Roman captivity. Hasdrubal
himself survived as a prisoner; Bosworth Smith notes the detail with
some irony, since the ancient sources make him out to be the least
worthy of the city's own defenders to keep both life and liberty,
which the Romans allowed him regardless.

## What Scipio wept for

Scipio himself, standing over the smoking ruin his own army had made,
did not treat the victory as simple triumph. Bosworth Smith records
his friend Polybius overhearing the general quote Homer's own lines
foretelling the eventual fall of every great city, sacred Troy
included - a moment of reflection Bosworth Smith reads as genuine
unease at the scale of what Rome had just done, rather than mere
performance. That unease did not stop the Senate from ordering total
destruction regardless. Every standing building was levelled, the
ground itself was ploughed over, and a formal curse was pronounced
against anyone who tried to rebuild the city or even live on its
site - a severity Bosworth Smith notes Rome had not imposed even on
Capua, a betrayer closer to home.

## The rival Rome could not simply outlast

What Carthage's destruction actually removed, in Bosworth Smith's own
final assessment, was not merely a defeated enemy but the one
Mediterranean power that had ever met Rome on genuinely equal terms -
an entire civilization's arts, laws, literature and religion "swept
away at a single stroke, leaving hardly a wrack behind." Utica, which
had deserted Carthage's cause in time, was rewarded with extra
territory; every town that had stayed loyal to the end shared
Carthage's own fate. A city that had begun, by its own legend, with a
fugitive queen buying only as much African soil as an ox-hide could
encircle ended three and a half centuries later as the clearest
proof in the ancient Mediterranean that Rome, once it decided an
enemy could never safely be left standing, was capable of making that
enemy disappear entirely.
