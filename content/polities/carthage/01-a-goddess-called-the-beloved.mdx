Carthage's own origins, stripped of Virgil's later embellishment,
look modest. Bosworth Smith reads the earliest Phoenician settlement
as a mere trading factory whose inhabitants treated themselves as
tenants rather than owners of the land, paying rent to the Berber
population for the ground their houses stood on. The famous story of
Queen Dido fleeing Tyre after her brother Pygmalion murdered her
husband, buying only as much land from the Berbers as an ox-hide cut
into strips could encircle, and founding the city on it - the legend
Virgil later built the Aeneid's own love story around - rests, in
Bosworth Smith's reading, on a real memory of peaceful settlement and
friendly relations with the native population, wrapped around what
was probably a folk etymology: the Phoenician word for fortress,
"Bozra," resembling the Greek "Byrsa," ox-hide.

## A name that was once a goddess

Bosworth Smith goes further with the queen's own name. "Dido" shares
a root with the Hebrew names David and Jedidiah, meaning "the
beloved," while the Virgilian "Elissa" is simply the feminine form of
"El," the Hebrew word for God - and Carthage worshipped Dido as a
goddess for as long as the city stood. The likeliest explanation,
Bosworth Smith argues, is that the city's own legendary foundress
began as the patron deity of the wider Phoenician people rather than
a historical exile at all. What is not legend is the pattern the
story preserves accurately: a colony sent out from Tyre itself, real
civil unrest as its occasion, and a peaceable beginning among African
neighbours who had no reason yet to see the newcomers as a threat.
