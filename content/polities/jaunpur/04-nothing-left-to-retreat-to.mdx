Husain Shah's military resources, on the figures the chroniclers give, far
exceeded Bahlul Lodi's. After the four-year truce that followed his
brother's death, he assembled — if the numbers can be trusted at all — an
army of three hundred thousand men and fourteen hundred elephants for a
predatory campaign into Orissa, marching through Tirhut and along the
frontier of Bengal itself to get there, and returned with an immense
ransom in elephants, horses and treasure that Muslim chroniclers preferred
to describe as tribute rather than plunder.

## Twice within reach of Delhi, twice turned back

None of that scale of resources translated into a decisive victory over
Bahlul. Twice Husain advanced on Delhi with what looked like overwhelming
force, and twice the campaign ended without the conquest it seemed to
promise. The first time, in 1479, he agreed to withdraw after an appeal
to his own filial memory of his mother's kindness toward a captured Lodi
hostage — a moment of misplaced sentiment Bahlul immediately exploited,
attacking the retreating army in violation of the very terms he had just
granted and capturing a large part of its baggage, its elephants, and
forty of Husain's principal nobles.

## A retreat that never found solid ground again

That betrayal marked the turn of the whole war. Bahlul pursued the
demoralised army of Jaunpur, annexed one district after another, and beat
Husain again and again — at Chhibramau, at Suhnuh, at Rapri — each defeat
pushing Husain's frontier further back and his following further into
disarray. He lost wives and children fording the Jumna in flight, was
plundered by a predatory tribe on the Chambal, and was kept in the field
only by the loyalty of a Gwalior raja who supplied him money, troops and an
escort. Bahlul, for his part, simply kept coming: he took Etawah after a
three-day siege, forced the Ganges when a local raja's support gave him a
ford, and finally marched directly on Jaunpur itself, taking the city and
installing his own governor.

## The end of the line

Husain rallied one more time while Bahlul was occupied elsewhere,
retaking Jaunpur briefly before Bahlul's return forced him out again —
and, at last "despairing of recovering his kingdom," fled into Bihar with
Bahlul in pursuit as far as the Ganges near Ballia. With his flight, the
Sharqi line of Jaunpur ended. Bahlul installed his own son Barbak as
governor with the right to coin money and use the royal title, and Husain
lived out the rest of his life in Bengal under the protection of its own
sultan, making no further attempt to reclaim his kingdom beyond quietly
encouraging a succession dispute between Barbak and his brother Sikandar —
a hope Sikandar's own victory and formal absorption of Jaunpur into Delhi
finally extinguished. Husain died in exile in 1500, eighty years and more
after Malik Sarvar had first stopped taking orders from the city that, in
the end, absorbed everything he had built.
