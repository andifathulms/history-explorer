Malik Sarvar's rise began as loyal service, not rebellion. In March 1393
the eunuch minister, known by the title Khvaja Jahan, placed his own choice
of candidate — Nasir-ud-din Mahmud, grandson of Firuz Tughluq — on the
throne of Delhi. He then put down Hindu rebellions across the Gangetic
Doab and Oudh on his new sultan's behalf, extending his own authority as
he went west to Koil and east into Tirhut and Bihar. The king of Bengal,
alarmed at how close this advance had come to his own frontier, sent
tribute — elephants owed by treaty to Delhi — directly to Khvaja Jahan
instead, since Delhi was no longer strong enough to insist the payment go
where it was nominally owed.

## A province that stopped asking permission

At some point in this process — the sources do not mark the exact moment —
Khvaja Jahan simply stopped deferring to the throne he had just installed.
When Timur attacked Delhi in 1398, he sent no help and the record does not
show him taking any notice of the invasion at all. He died the following
year, leaving his territory intact to an adopted son, Malik Qaranful, who
took the royal style Mubarak Shah, struck his own coin, and had the khutba
read in his own name — the formal declarations of sovereignty a eunuch
founder, with no heir of his own body, could only pass to a son he had
chosen rather than fathered.

## Two brothers, one throne

Mubarak died suddenly in 1402 while preparing to repel a threatened
invasion, and was succeeded by his younger brother, who took the throne as
Shams-ud-din Ibrahim Shah. The pattern was already set: a dynasty founded
by a man who could produce no natural heirs, continuing itself through
adoption and fraternal succession rather than any fixed rule, in a kingdom
whose entire claim to independence rested on nothing more than having
once, quietly, stopped taking orders from the capital it had been sent to
pacify.
