Ibrahim Shah reigned for thirty-four years, longer than any other Sharqi
king, and spent a good part of that time doing what most of his neighbours
could not afford to: patronising scholarship rather than only making war.

## A court built for learning

Ibrahim was, by every account, a genuinely cultured prince, and his court
became a refuge for theology and law at a moment when both needed one. Works
on both subjects issued from Jaunpur in numbers that later gave the city its
lasting epithet: the Shiraz of India, a comparison to the great Persian
centre of poetry and scholarship that no other Indian court of the period
earned. It is a striking legacy for a dynasty whose kings, on the evidence
Haig weighs and does not dismiss, were themselves of African descent — Malik
Sarvar a eunuch, his adopted sons probably slaves, Mubarak's own name before
his accession, Qaranful, "the Clove," a term of address applied to African
slaves. No portrait of the period survives to settle the question further,
but nothing in the record argues against it either.

## Wars fought without losing the capital

None of this scholarly patronage kept Ibrahim out of the field. Delhi's own
weakness twice tempted its rulers to try to reclaim Jaunpur outright, and
Ibrahim met both attempts — expelling one Delhi governor from Kanauj after
a four-month siege, and forcing a diplomatic retreat from a planned march
on Delhi itself when he heard that Gujarat's own sultan had come to his
rival's aid. He annexed the district of Sambhal east of the Ganges, and in
1433 raced Hoshang Shah of Malwa to claim the contested town of Kalpi — and
lost the race when a report of an attack on Jaunpur forced him to turn back
before he could complete the annexation himself.

## The campaign that stopped short of conquest

His most consequential foreign venture was a war he never intended to
fight to a finish. Between 1409 and 1414, urged on by the Muslim saint
Qutb-ul-Alam, Ibrahim invaded Bengal to punish Raja Ganesh, a Hindu
chieftain who had accumulated more real power there than the kingdom's
nominal ruler and was using it to persecute Bengal's Muslims. Ganesh,
cornered, negotiated his way out through the same saint who had brought
the invasion down on him — and Ibrahim, satisfied with the appearance of a
settlement, withdrew his army rather than press the advantage to outright
annexation. It would prove, in hindsight, the furthest Jaunpur's arms ever
reached under a ruler more interested in what his court could produce than
in what his army could hold.
