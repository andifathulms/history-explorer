Ibrahim's son Mahmud Shah reigned twenty-one years, fought an inconclusive
war with Malwa over the misconduct of a minor vassal, and made the mistake,
late in his career, of attacking Bahlul Lodi of Delhi — a rash venture whose
consequences would outlast him. He died in 1457 just as he was about to
face Bahlul in the field, and the throne passed to his son Bhikan, who
took the title Muhammad Shah.

## A brother murdered, a mother taunted

Muhammad Shah proved, almost immediately, both violent and reckless. After
making a truce with Bahlul Lodi, he ordered the chief magistrate of Jaunpur
to execute his own younger brother Hasan Khan and a captive Lodi kinsman.
The magistrate refused — the king's own mother was sheltering the condemned
men — so Muhammad lured her out of the city with a false promise of
consultation over his brother's future, and had Hasan Khan murdered the
moment she was gone. When she remained at Kanauj to mourn, Muhammad sent
word that she might as well grieve for her other sons at the same time,
since they would soon join Hasan in the grave.

## The threat that turned an army against him

The taunt reached the princes it was meant to threaten, and they moved
first. Convincing their brother that Bahlul was planning a night attack,
they persuaded him to place thirty thousand horse and thirty elephants
under their command to meet it — and used the force to defect outright.
Bahlul, watching the movement, correctly read it as a threat to his own
communications and pursued. Muhammad, discovering his brothers gone,
retreated toward the Ganges and found, on nearing Kanauj, that his power
had already evaporated: his surviving elder brother Husain had been
acclaimed king there in his place. Deserted by what few nobles remained
loyal, Muhammad Shah died trying to defend himself against an attack from
the army that had, only days before, still answered to him.

## What the fratricide actually cost

Husain Shah, the new king, made peace with Bahlul on favourable terms —
recovering his captured brother in exchange for the Lodi kinsman Muhammad
had originally wanted dead — and the two rulers observed a four-year truce
neither had any real reason to break yet. But the episode had shown Bahlul
something Jaunpur's own kings had not fully reckoned with: a throne whose
last transfer of power had been decided by an army changing sides rather
than by any settled rule of succession was not a stable enough neighbour to
leave alone indefinitely.
