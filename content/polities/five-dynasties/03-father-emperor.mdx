Mingzong's death in 933 unwound in barely two years everything his own
reign had held together. His son reigned one troubled year before a rival
general, Li Congke, drove him from the throne with the backing of the
palace itself. Li Congke did not enjoy his own victory long: within a year a
rival ambition caught up with him too, and rather than face capture he
retired with his family to a tower in his own palace and set it on fire,
burning with them inside rather than surrender. "So expired," in Boulger's
own words, "the brief dynasty of the Later Tangs."

## A throne built on a foreign army

Shi Jingtang, the man who had first urged Li Congke toward the throne and
then turned against him once that throne became inconvenient, took power in
the vacuum - but not on his own strength. He owed his position to Khitan
military backing, and he paid for it on Khitan terms: addressing the Khitan
ruler as "Father Emperor," ceding border territory in the north, and
enduring the protests of ministers who found the arrangement a disgrace to
Chinese dignity. Shi Jingtang's own answer to the objection was practical
rather than proud - the Khitan, one of his own ministers pointed out, were
no longer the unlettered barbarians of an earlier age; they had absorbed
enough Chinese territory and Chinese civilisation to retain the hardy
military edge of their steppe origins while matching the sophistication of
the court they now dominated. A war between that combination and Shi
Jingtang's own exhausted state could end only one way.

## The vassalage a successor could not stomach

Shi Jingtang's tact held the arrangement together for the seven years of
his own reign, at the cost of border cities surrendered and an annual
subsidy paid without complaint. His successor and nephew had none of the
same patience. Encouraged by ministers who found the whole relationship
shameful regardless of the strategic argument for it, the new ruler
defaulted on the deference his uncle had maintained - and discovered, when
the Khitan ruler retaliated in force, that the shame of the arrangement had
been the price of a peace his own army could not actually enforce without
it. Besieged, cut off from supplies and finally starved into surrender, he
abdicated and retired into private life. The dynasty that had bought its
throne with a foreign patron's army lost it the moment it tried to stop
paying for the patronage.
