Li Cunxu inherited his father Li Keyong's rivalry with Zhu Wen along with
his father's army, and spent more than a decade grinding down what Zhu
Wen's own dynasty had built. In 917, with the Yellow River frozen hard
enough to bear the weight of his infantry and cavalry, he crossed it without
loss and stormed the small forts the Later Liang had built to hold the line.
By 923 he had collected the largest army of his career, proclaimed his
intention of taking the throne outright, and given his own dynasty a name
before he had finished conquering it: Tang, in deliberate tribute to the
house Zhu Wen had destroyed, which Li Cunxu declared his ambition to renew
rather than merely replace.

## A splendid barbarian

The final siege went as badly for the Liang as the preceding sixteen years
had gone well for their enemies. Zhu Wen's own successor, cornered in his
capital with his ruin plainly inevitable, ordered his own execution rather
than wait for the city to fall - "thus terminating also the brief reign of
the Later Liangs," which had lasted sixteen years from Zhu Wen's original
abdication ceremony. Li Cunxu, having desecrated the Liang ancestral tombs
to seal the victory, then did to his own reign roughly what Zhu Wen had done
to his - governed well enough at first, then let the palace's luxury undo
the discipline that had won him the throne. His own soldiers, disaffected by
a leader more interested in spectacle than in the camp discipline that had
made his name, eventually forced the gates of his palace at Luoyang; a
poisoned cup of sour milk, reportedly sent by his own empress, finished what
the assault had started. He was thirty-five.

## The reign that actually worked

His adopted brother and best general, Li Siyuan, took the throne after
suppressing the disorder Li Cunxu's death set off - not by seizing it
outright, but by first governing simply as "Governor of the realm" until
order was restored, and only then accepting the title he had already been
offered and had twice refused. As Emperor Mingzong, from 926 to 933, he
governed the most stable of the five dynasties' many short reigns:
successful campaigns against the Khitan, the encouragement of scholarship,
and - during his reign specifically - the first practical use of woodblock
printing for the mass reproduction of books, more than five centuries
before Caxton brought the same technology to England. He even released
captured Khitan officers as a gesture of goodwill, against the advice of
ministers who warned that everything they had learned in China would be
turned against him one day. Mingzong judged the risk worth taking. It was,
by the standard the other four dynasties set, as close to enlightened
government as the whole interregnum ever managed.
