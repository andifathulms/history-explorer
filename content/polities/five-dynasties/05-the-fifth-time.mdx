Guo Wei ruled only three years before his own death, leaving the throne to
an adopted son, Chai Rong (Emperor Shizong), whose brief six-year reign
Boulger treats with more sympathy than almost any other figure in the whole
interregnum. Chai Rong drew up an ambitious plan to finally expel the
Khitan from the territory Shi Jingtang had ceded them two decades earlier,
extended Later Zhou's own authority along the Yangtze in a series of real
successes, and died before any of it could be carried through to
completion. His son, a child, inherited a throne with nothing behind it but
a dead man's unfinished ambitions.

## A general acclaimed on the march

Zhao Kuangyin had risen through Later Zhou's own service and commanded
its army when a threatened invasion - real or manufactured, the sources
never entirely settle which - required troops to be sent north from the
capital. On campaign, his soldiers surrounded him and made the same
declaration that had elevated at least one general before him in this
interregnum: the empire needed a master, and no one present was more
worthy of the position than their own commander. Zhao Kuangyin returned to
the capital and took the throne from Chai Rong's child heir without a
battle to settle the question - the fifth time in fifty-three years that an
army's acclamation, rather than any rule of succession, had decided who
would rule next.

## What made the fifth time the last one

Nothing about the mechanism was new. What changed was what the new emperor
did with the throne once he held it. Zhao Kuangyin named his own dynasty
Song, and spent his reign building precisely the kind of standing,
examined, centrally answerable military establishment none of his four
predecessors had ever managed to hold in place long enough to matter -
converting the same institutional weakness that had elevated five separate
houses in five decades into the one problem his own new dynasty was
explicitly designed to solve. The interregnum that had begun with Zhu Wen
drowning Tang princes in a lake ended with a general who understood, better
than any of the men who had taken the same throne before him, exactly why
his own throne would not be safe until the trick that had put him on it
could never be repeated by anyone else.
