Zhu Wen served first under Huang Chao, the rebel leader whose uprising had
already broken the Tang court's authority beyond real repair, before
defecting to the imperial side once he judged Huang Chao's cause was not
going to outlast him. From there his rise followed the pattern the whole
next half-century would repeat: military command, growing personal
following, and the steady conviction that the throne he was defending might
as well become his own.

## The eunuchs, the rival, and the emperor

Zhu Wen filled every post he could reach with his own men and marked
himself as indispensable by exterminating the court eunuchs who had run the
palace for a generation - "only thirty old men and children" spared, by his
own reckoning. His one serious rival, the general Li Keyong, held the field
against him for years without either man managing to finish the other. In
904 Zhu Wen forced Emperor Zhaozong to move from Chang'an to Luoyang, where
he could be more easily watched, and kept him there under close guard. When
the emperor tried to poison Zhu Wen rather than continue as his prisoner,
Zhu Wen survived the attempt and, within months, had him murdered outright.

## A banquet by the water

The official story blamed mutinous soldiers for the murder, a cover Zhu Wen
undermined by executing his own son as the supposed culprit - a punishment
that convinced nobody who was paying attention. He was not finished. Every
surviving prince of the Tang imperial family still at Luoyang was invited to
a grand banquet on the shore of a lake; once they had been fed, soldiers
appeared and threw every one of the guests into the water. The senior
officials who might still have opposed him were rounded up next, marched in
chains to the Yellow River, and drowned there in turn.

## The throne, taken by ceremony rather than battle

Zhu Wen installed one of the dead emperor's youngest sons as a placeholder,
who abdicated after two powerless years rather than wait to become the next
victim. In 907 Zhu Wen accepted the abdication, took the name Taizu, and
founded his own dynasty under the name Liang. The ceremony that made him
emperor changed nothing about the substance of what he already controlled -
only its name, and the fact that no Tang prince remained who could
plausibly claim it back from him.
