Early in the sixth century, Huari grew from a valley settlement into a
genuine urban centre, eventually covering several square kilometres and
laid out to follow the local terrain rather than any single rigid
plan. A secondary site, Conchopata, developed some twelve kilometres to
the south, and from this two-centre heartland Wari's own economic and
political reach began extending across the central Andes.

## Rooms that let people in but not through

What made Wari architecture instantly recognizable, at Huari and at
every site it later built, was its own distinctive orthogonal form:
great rectangular enclosures laid out on a rigid grid and subdivided
into square or rectangular patio groups, each with a central open patio
ringed by long narrow rooms called galleries, sometimes stacked two or
three storeys and rooms deep. Doorways connected each patio to its own
surrounding galleries freely enough - but doorways between one patio
group and its neighbour were kept deliberately scarce. Movement within
a single enclosure was easy; movement across the settlement as a whole
was not.

## An architecture built to be recognized from a distance

That same rigid, compartmentalized layout became archaeology's own most
reliable fingerprint for Wari presence anywhere in the Andes - a
building style distinctive enough that its appearance at a site,
whether a small provincial outpost or Huari itself, is taken as
reasonably solid evidence of Wari's own hand in it, even where no
other evidence of political control survives. A state that left no
written record of its own borders had, without meaning to, left behind
walls that still mark them.
