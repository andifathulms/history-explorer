Wari's own provincial centers were never simply smaller copies of the
capital. Where Huari sprawled across several square kilometres and bent
its own layout to the local terrain, each provincial center was
typically a single, self-contained rectangular enclosure - a
scaled-down but still unmistakably Wari design, dropped into a
landscape far from Ayacucho and clearly meant to project the same
authority there that the capital projected at home.

## A wall eight hundred metres on a side

The largest of these provincial centers, Pikillaqta, stood just
southeast of what is now Cuzco - a single great rectangular enclosure
some eight hundred metres on a side, subdivided internally into
dozens of patio groups. Its central portion saw a century or more of
real occupation, but other parts of the site were still under active
construction when the wider Wari state collapsed, work simply halted
and never resumed.

## A valley reorganized for maize

Further south, in the province of Jincamocco, Wari's own presence took
a different, more agricultural form. Investigations in and around the
site show the surrounding valley extensively terraced and, by most
readings of the evidence, reorganized under Wari direction specifically
to increase maize production - not merely a garrison imposed on
existing farmland but an active reshaping of how the land itself was
worked, expanded further as Wari's own occupation there continued. Between
a fortress-scaled administrative center at Pikillaqta and an
agriculturally re-engineered valley at Jincamocco, the provincial network
shows two rather different faces of the same expanding state - one built
to hold territory, the other built to make it produce more than it
otherwise would have.
