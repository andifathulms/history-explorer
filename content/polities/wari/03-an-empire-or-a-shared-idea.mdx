Whether Wari was a genuine centralized empire, exercising direct
political and military control over the territory its architecture and
artifacts turn up in, or something looser - a shared religious and
economic system that spread through trade, ritual authority and
imitation rather than conquest - is a question archaeology has not
settled, and the evidence pulls in both directions depending on where
it is examined.

<Figure id="wa-tunic" />

## Reasons to see an empire

The case for centralized control rests on real coercive infrastructure:
military raiding that produced trophy heads from captured men and
children, walled provincial centers built to a standardized plan and
clearly designed to restrict movement and control access, valleys like
Jincamocco's whose own terracing and maize production were reorganized
under what the evidence suggests was direct Wari direction, and a road
network substantial enough that the Inca simply inherited large parts of
it centuries later rather than building their own from nothing.

## Reasons to doubt it

Against this, several of Wari's most significant sites resist a simple
imperial reading. Construction at Viracochapampa in the north began and
was never finished - evidence, perhaps, that the surrounding polity
negotiated cooperation rather than being conquered outright, or perhaps
that Wari's own reach never actually extended that far in practice.
Pachacamac, a major religious center clearly related to Wari in style,
cannot be confidently classified as either an independent shrine complex
with its own wide influence or as a Wari province proper. And burial
practice at the Wari heartland itself, compared directly against
Tiwanaku's, shows no shared funerary customs tied to the Staff God
imagery the two states otherwise held in common - a sign that even where
Wari and its neighbours shared religious symbols, they did not
necessarily share the same underlying institutions.

## A debate the evidence keeps reopening

Coastal control is the clearest example of how thin the evidence for
outright conquest can be: the collapse of the Moche polity on the north
coast happened around the same time Wari influence first appears there,
but nothing establishes that Wari caused it rather than simply
outlasting or replacing it after an unrelated failure. Wari artifacts
found along the coast could as easily reflect trade between independent
neighbours as they could tribute extracted from conquered subjects,
and nothing yet excavated settles which explanation is correct.
