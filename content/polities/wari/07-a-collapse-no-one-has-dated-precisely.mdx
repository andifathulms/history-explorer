Around 1000 CE, Huari and most of the provincial network built to
project its own authority across the central Andes were abandoned.
Construction that had been underway at Pikillaqta simply stopped,
leaving parts of the site permanently unfinished; the provincial centers
that had linked Cajamarca to Cuzco fell out of use along with the
capital that had directed them. A state that had grown rapidly from a
single valley town into a network spanning much of highland Peru came
apart within roughly the same span of time it had taken to build.

## A cause still missing from the record

What ended it is, at present, genuinely unknown. Unlike neighbouring
Tiwanaku, whose own collapse has recently been dated with real
precision through large radiocarbon sampling programs and tied to
specific, closely studied social changes in the decades beforehand, no
comparably rigorous study of Wari's own decline has yet settled the
question of when, exactly, the collapse began or what combination of
internal strain, environmental change, or external pressure actually
caused it.

## A road network too useful to abandon

What did not disappear, even after Huari itself emptied out, was the
physical infrastructure Wari had built to hold its own territory
together. The road network linking the capital to its provinces
survived the state that built it by centuries, absorbed wholesale into
the Inca highway system that would eventually connect an empire built,
in more than one sense, on foundations Wari had already laid.
