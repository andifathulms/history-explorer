Long before Huari existed as a city, the Ayacucho basin in the central
Peruvian highlands was home to a distinct pottery tradition archaeologists
call Huarpa, spanning roughly the first six centuries of the common era.
Toward the end of that period, the valley's own people came into
sustained contact with the Nasca culture of the south coast - a contact
visible in shared iconography and in a new practice that would become
one of the most distinctive markers of the state that followed: the
taking of trophy heads.

## A practice borrowed and then redesigned

Nasca trophy heads, prepared with a hole through the forehead for a
carrying cord, had circulated in the region for generations before
Huarpa and early Wari sites began producing their own version. The Wari
form was different enough to be told apart from its Nasca model on sight:
a perforation through the crown of the skull instead of the forehead,
designed so the head could be displayed upright and facing forward
rather than hung. Only men and children were treated this way, a
selectivity that suggests the practice carried specific meaning about
who counted as an appropriate trophy rather than being applied
indiscriminately to any enemy taken in war.

## A tradition inherited rather than invented

What Huarpa and early Wari communities took from their Nasca neighbours,
they did not copy wholesale. The redesign of the trophy head - a small
technical change with real symbolic weight - is one of the clearest signs
archaeology has of a highland society actively reworking outside
influence into something distinctly its own, in the same period that
the settlement at Huari itself was beginning the rapid growth that would
turn it from a valley town into a capital.
