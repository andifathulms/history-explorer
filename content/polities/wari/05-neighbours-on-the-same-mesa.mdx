In the Moquegua Valley of far southern Peru, Wari built an
administrative and ceremonial center at Cerro Baúl, expanding
agricultural output in the upper valley with new canal systems and
terracing. The location was no accident: Moquegua was the one place
Wari's own territory came into direct contact with the other great
power of the Middle Horizon Andes, the contemporary state centered at
Tiwanaku on the shore of Lake Titicaca, which held its own settlements
in the same valley.

## A question archaeologists long assumed had an easy answer

For decades, scholars who noticed how much iconography Wari and
Tiwanaku shared - both venerated a version of the same Staff God figure
- assumed the simplest explanation: that Tiwanaku, as the older and
more established power, had shaped Wari religion and preceded it in
time. Excavation at Cerro Baúl itself has complicated that assumption
considerably. Radiocarbon dates recovered there give no support to
Tiwanaku predating Wari's own presence in the valley; instead, the
evidence indicates both states occupied Moquegua simultaneously for
roughly the last three centuries of the Middle Horizon.

## Neighbours who never came to blows

What excavation at Cerro Baúl has not found is any sign that this
shared frontier produced open conflict. Wari built in its own
distinctive orthogonal style and Tiwanaku in its own, each recognizably
itself even while occupying the same valley and drawing on the same
religious imagery, and nothing in the archaeological record points to
violence between them. Two of the largest states the pre-Columbian
Andes had yet produced shared a border for centuries and appear, on the
evidence available, to have simply lived beside one another.
