Derbend is a small place with a very large asset, and its politics for two
centuries are the politics of who controlled the asset.

The emir did, formally. The Hashimid family held the emirate by inheritance,
named the Abbasid caliph in the khutba, and took the tolls. But the chronicle
Minorsky edited is largely a record of that family being deposed, exiled,
recalled, imprisoned and killed — not by outside conquerors, and not usually by
rivals within the house, but by the city.

Derbend had a *rais*, a head of the community drawn from its notables, and an
armed population organised by quarter. The ghazis endowed to hold the frontier
had descendants who were still there and still armed. The merchant families who
handled the traffic had their own interests, and the highland principalities
beyond the wall — the Sarir in Daghestan, the pagan and Christian mountain
lordships — could be appealed to by any faction. An emir who taxed too hard, or
made the wrong alliance with the Shirvanshahs next door, discovered that his city
could remove him and frequently did.

<Figure id="juma-mosque" />

Nobody can say how the emirate of Derbend raised troops, and the reason is that
the question is wrongly put. Its fighting strength was not something the emir
raised. It was a city that armed itself, by quarter, out of the descendants of a
garrison, and it was as often pointed at him as anywhere else. Aleppo's *ahdath*
under the Mirdasids were the same kind of thing, and are the same kind of
problem: a militia that belongs to a town rather than to a ruler is not a levy,
not a conscription and not a tribe, and does not behave like any of them.

<Figure id="kyrkhlyar" />

The relationship with the Shirvanshahs is the other constant. The two are the two
subjects of Minorsky's book and they behaved like it — intermarrying,
campaigning against each other, each intervening in the other's succession
disputes, and neither ever finishing the other. Shirvan had the land, the
naphtha and the silk; Derbend had the pass. Neither could take what the other
lived on.
