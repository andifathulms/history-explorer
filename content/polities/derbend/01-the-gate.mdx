---
title: The gate of gates
drafted_from: minorsky-1958
phase: formation
---

The Caucasus range runs from the Black Sea to the Caspian and is crossable, by
an army, in very few places. At the eastern end the mountains come down almost
to the sea and leave a gap about three kilometres wide between the last ridge
and the water. Everything moving by land between the steppe north of the
Caucasus and the countries south of it goes through that gap.

Khusrau I Anushirvan fortified it in the sixth century, and the work is
extraordinary and still standing: a citadel on the hill, two parallel walls
running down the slope to the sea about four hundred metres apart with the town
between them, and a long wall, the Dagh-Bary, running west into the mountains
for tens of kilometres to close the flanking routes. The Arabic name is Bab
al-Abwab, the gate of gates. The Persian is Darband, the barred gate.

What it was built against was the steppe: Huns, and then for three centuries the
Khazars, whose khaganate this site carries in its Inner Asia region and who
fought the Umayyads through this pass in a series of wars that reached deep into
both directions. After the Arab conquest, Derbend became the caliphate's
northern frontier post and one of the great *ribats* — a garrison town settled
with volunteers endowed to hold a frontier against unbelievers, on the same
model as the Ifriqiyan and Central Asian frontier towns this corpus records
elsewhere.

So the polity on this page began as somebody's fortress, and in 869 stopped
being one. Hashim ibn Suraqa, an Arab of the Sulaym, established a hereditary
emirate at Derbend as Abbasid authority in the provinces failed. It lasted two
hundred and eight years.

Almost everything known about it comes from one text: a local chronicle
preserved only inside a much later Ottoman compilation, which Minorsky
reconstructed, edited and translated in the same volume as the Shirvanshah
material on the neighbouring record. The two polities are in one book because
they are in one chronicle, and the chronicle exists because a seventeenth-century
Turkish historian copied it.
