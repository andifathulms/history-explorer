The emirate ended in 1077. The gate did not, and the descriptions the Arabic
geographers left of it are worth having in full, because they are a portrait of
what a polity built on a piece of ground actually looks like from the outside.

## The harbour

Ibn Hawkal, writing in the tenth century, says Bab al-Abwab was larger than
Ardabil, which was then the capital of Adharbayjan — a considerable claim for a
frontier fort.

The harbour was closed. Two moles ran out into the Caspian, built of stone
blocks fastened together with lead, and at their far ends stood a water-gate shut
with chains, so that no ship could enter or leave except by permission. A stone
wall with towers enclosed the town, and it had two land gates besides. They made
linen there and exported it, and saffron came from the country round about.

Read that back slowly. The pass through the mountains was walled; the flanking
routes were walled; and the sea approach was closed with a chain across a
purpose-built harbour mouth. Everything that could pass was made to pass through
something that could be taxed or refused.

## The frontier of Islam

The geographers are also clear about what kind of place it was. There was a fine
mosque in the market square, and Bab al-Abwab was the frontier town of Islam,
because in early days it was surrounded on the landward side by people who were
not Muslims.

Yaqut, describing the mountains west of it, says that among the tribes of the
Caucasus seventy different languages were spoken and no man could understand
his neighbour. He also describes the long wall running west along the hill-crests
from Derbend to keep the barbarians out, and attributes it — correctly — to
Khusrau Anushirvan in the sixth century.

That is the whole thing in one paragraph of a thirteenth-century dictionary: a
Sasanian wall, a Muslim frontier town behind it, and seventy languages on the
other side.

## The position outlives everybody

The Hashimid emirs held it for two hundred and eight years and are remembered by
one chronicle that survived by being copied into a later book. The wall they
inherited was six centuries old when they got it and is fifteen centuries old
now, and it is still standing, and Derbend is still a town on the same three
kilometres of ground.

An emirate is an arrangement about a piece of geography. When the arrangement
lapses, the geography does not, and the next arrangement is made about the same
thing. Nothing about this position has ever needed a dynasty. It needed a wall,
and it got a very good one.
