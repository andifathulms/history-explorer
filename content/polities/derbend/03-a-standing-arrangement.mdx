---
title: Two centuries of the same thing
drafted_from: minorsky-1958
phase: aside
---

Nothing happened at Derbend, and that is the most accurate thing that can be said
about it.

Two hundred and eight years of the same three things, over and over: the pass
taxed, the city deposing its emir, and the Shirvanshahs and the highland
lordships manoeuvring around both. The chronicle records dozens of events. Not
one of them altered what the emirate was subsequently able to do, because what
the emirate was able to do was determined by a gap in a mountain range, and the
gap did not move.

That is a rarer condition than it sounds. Most polities have hinges — a battle, a
conversion, a capital moved, a dynasty replaced, after which the same state is
doing something different. Derbend had none. Its whole strategic position was a
standing arrangement rather than a sequence of decisive days, and a standing
arrangement produces revenue, not history.

Palmyra is the instructive comparison, because it began the same way and did not
end that way. An oasis with water where there was none, taxing a road across a
desert, its ground producing nothing and its position producing everything — and
then thirteen years in which it used that position to try to become an empire,
which is what Palmyra is remembered for and very nearly all it is remembered for.

Derbend never tried. It held the gate for two centuries, taxed what came through,
argued about who was collecting, and produced no campaign, no conquest and no
claim on anybody. The emirate outlived states many times its size for exactly
that reason: it was worth more to everyone functioning than conquered, and it
never gave anybody a reason to reconsider.
