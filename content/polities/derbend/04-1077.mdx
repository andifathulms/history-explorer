title: The Seljuks come through

The Seljuk sultanate reached the eastern Caucasus in the 1060s and 1070s, in the
same movement that took Ani in 1064 and reached Manzikert in 1071. Derbend was
taken in 1077 and the Hashimid emirate ended.

There is a particular irony in the direction of it, and the chronicle's compilers
were aware of it. The pass and its walls had been built by a Sasanian emperor and
maintained by four centuries of Muslim rulers for one purpose: to stop
Turkic-speaking peoples from the northern steppe from entering the countries
south of the Caucasus. In the eleventh century Turkic-speaking peoples entered
those countries anyway — not through the gate but round it, out of Central Asia
by way of Iran, and they took the gate from the south.

The fortifications were not superseded. Derbend went on being a fortress and a
frontier post under the Seljuks, the Shirvanshahs, the Mongols, the Safavids —
whose Derbendid branch of the Shirvanshah house on this site's neighbouring
record is named for the place — the Ottomans, the Afsharids, and finally Russia,
which took it in 1806 and whose Caucasian frontier it remained. The walls are
standing and are a UNESCO site.

What ended in 1077 was the emirate, not the position. That is the ordinary fate
of a polity whose whole basis is a piece of ground: the ground goes on being
valuable and somebody else collects.
