title: The side the wall did not cover

Al-Mas'udi described the Gate in the 940s, and what he found worth writing down
is the country it stood in rather than the fortification, which is the more
useful half.

The wall he gives as Anushirvan's work: a town built in one of the passes of the
Caucasus, a wall running from it a full mile out into the Caspian and away west
about forty *farsakhs* to the summit of the range, crossing mountains and
valleys, ending at the fortress of Tabarsaran. Every three miles, more or less,
depending on how important the route it closed was, an iron gate; and behind
every gate a population settled there on purpose to guard that gate and the
stretch of wall beside it.

What it was built to check he lists by name: the Khazars, the Alans, the various
Turkish hordes, the Sarir. Every one of them comes from the north, by land,
through a pass.

Around the Gate, Mas'udi found the kind of country that makes a wall necessary
and a state impossible. Seventy-two nations on the mountain, each with its own
king and its own language. The nearest people to Derbend are the Haydan, whose
king is a Muslim descended, he says, from Kahlan — and whose children and
household are the only Muslims in his entire dominions. Between Haydan and the
Gate lives a population of Arab descent who speak nothing but Arabic, settled
there since the conquest, living in villages among forests, jungles, valleys and
big rivers; they are on the frontier of Haydan and nobody's subjects, because
their country cannot be got into.

And then, in a different chapter and without connecting the two, Mas'udi records
what happened shortly after the year 300 of the Hijra. The Rus brought their
ships out of the Black Sea, down the Khazar river, and into the Caspian. Everyone
on that sea rose at once — the Byzantine borderlands, Adharbayjan, Baylaqan, the
country round Bardha'a, Daylam, Gilan, Jurjan, Tabaristan — and Mas'udi gives the
reason in a single sentence: they had never
before seen an enemy coming against them from that quarter, nor was such an
invasion recorded since the most remote time. He adds that the event is too
widely known in those countries to be denied.

Derbend was the most expensive fortification in the Caucasus and it closed the
only land route between the steppe and Iran, and the wall ran a mile into the
water precisely so that nobody could wade round the end of it. It worked. What
it could not do was hold a sea, and in about 913 a fleet came down a sea that no
hostile fleet had ever used, passed the Gate, and raided the coasts on both
sides of it for as long as it chose.

The emirate went on taxing the pass for another century and a half after that,
because the pass was still there and still had to be used by everyone travelling
by land. A standing arrangement does not stop being profitable because somebody
has proved it can be bypassed. It stops being the answer to the question it was
built to answer, which is a different thing, and harder to notice from inside.
