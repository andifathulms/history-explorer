Emperor Wu died in 290 and left the throne to his son Sima Zhong, a weak
ruler already thirty years old, under the regency of a minister named Yang
Jun whom Boulger describes as ambitious but weak-willed - a man more
interested in advancing his own position than in serving the dynasty that
employed him. It was not Yang Jun, in the end, who did the most damage to
the new reign. It was the new emperor's own wife.

## An empress with old scores to settle

Empress Jia had grievances from Emperor Wu's own reign that she had never
forgiven, and she pursued them with a determination her husband entirely
lacked. Her partisans stormed Yang Jun's palace and put him and his
followers to the sword; Emperor Wu's widow, the dowager empress, was
stripped of her rank and reduced to the status of a commoner. Empress Jia
then installed ministers of her own choosing, who learned nothing from Yang
Jun's fall except how to build their own factions in turn. Through it all,
Boulger writes, the emperor himself remained "a miserable and harmless
prince" - a puppet first of a scheming minister, then of his own wife,
capable of neither ruling nor objecting to the fact that he did not.

## What the paralysis at the centre cost the frontier

A court this consumed by its own intrigue had no capacity left to answer
insults from its neighbours with any real vigour. Local risings, left
unrepressed while the generals sent to put them down quarrelled over
precedence instead, grew into larger ones. Tartar kings on the western
frontier extended their authority over territories nominally still Chinese
vassals, and the court's only response was that such places were too remote
to be worth the trouble. The one bright exception was a eunuch named
Mengguan, given command of an army as a last resort after every established
general had already failed, who marched in the depth of winter and broke a
major rebellion in a single decisive campaign - proof, Boulger notes, that
the capacity for effective action had not vanished from the empire
entirely, only from the people who were supposed to be exercising it at the
top.

## A poisoned heir

Mengguan's single victory changed nothing about the trajectory the dynasty
was actually on. Empress Jia had the crown prince - not her own son, and
therefore an obstacle to her own ambitions for the succession - poisoned on
her orders, removing the one clear alternative to the chaos she had done so
much to create. The dynasty Emperor Wu had spent ten years completing by
force of arms was, within four years of his death, being dismantled from
the inside by exactly the kind of palace intrigue his own family had once
used to win it.
