Emperor Hui died in 306, a release Boulger calls happy from a reign that had
meant nothing to anyone, including its own occupant. His brother succeeded
him as Emperor Huai with real intentions of governing in person, but
intentions were not what the crisis required. Liu Yuan and his son kept
advancing south through the years that followed, and in 311 an army under
the King of Han's command finally appeared before the gates of Luoyang
itself.

## The capital taken

The city fell. Its captors pillaged it and put it to the torch, executed the
crown prince, and carried Emperor Huai off in captivity to Pingyang, the Han
kingdom's own capital. There he was kept under close watch for two years,
until Liu Song, taking advantage of a separate military defeat to salve his
own temper, chose to humiliate his prisoner before killing him: dressed in
black, the Jin emperor was made to wait at table on his Tartar conqueror,
and was then executed on a charge Boulger says no evidence whatever
supported. It was, so far as the sources allow it to be judged, the first
time a Chinese emperor had been made to perform menial service for a foreign
conqueror's entertainment before being put to death for the spectacle of it.

## A second capital, a second captivity

Word of Huai's death reached the Jin loyalists still holding out at
Chang'an, who proclaimed his younger brother emperor there under the name
Emperor Min. The new reign changed nothing about the direction the war was
running. Three years into it, Liu Song's generals appeared before Chang'an
in turn and took it after only a token resistance. Emperor Min was carried
off to Pingyang exactly as his brother had been, held for a year under
repeated humiliations, and then executed in his turn - the same catastrophe
visited on the dynasty twice within half a decade, each time ending in the
same captive city and the same result.
