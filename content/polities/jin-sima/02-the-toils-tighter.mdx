Sun Hao's protestations of friendship convinced nobody at the Jin court for
long. Emperor Wu's ministers drew the toils steadily tighter around Wu for
years before striking: a fleet of war junks built in secret from the timber
of Sichuan, far up the Yangtze where Wu's admirals could not see it take
shape, while a separate army massed to sweep down the river from the north.
Wu's own minister Lu Kai had urged moving the capital from Wuchang to the
better-defended Jiankang, in the coastal province of Zhejiang, and the
change was made — but Lu Kai died in 269, and his death left Wu, in
Boulger's words, "a divided and headless state at the mercy of its powerful
neighbour."

## A war fought on the water

When Emperor Wu finally launched the campaign, the river fleet descended
from Sichuan on admirals who had had years to prepare and had not used them
well, while the land armies advanced along multiple fronts at once. The
details of the individual river battles have not come down whole, but the
outcome did: Wu's resistance collapsed faster than either side had expected,
and Sun Hao's kingdom, the last of the three that had divided the Han
inheritance sixty years before, surrendered.

## What ten years bought

For the first time since the Han's own collapse, one government held the
whole of the old imperial territory again. Emperor Wu had reigned over Wei's
former lands for fifteen years before the conquest and would reign over the
reunified empire for ten more after it — a decade in which, by his own
court's later admission, the qualities that had won the war gave way to the
comforts that came with having won it. The native historians who recorded
his reign did not spare him the comparison: the ruler who had spent years
building a secret fleet to finish a war his father and grandfather had
started spent his last decade, by their account, indulging exactly the
vices victory was supposed to have made unnecessary.
