In 304 the Xiongnu settled inside the empire's own northern frontier were
organised, on the Jin court's own authority, into five clans under a single
appointed chief: Liu Yuan. His son Liu Song had been given the full benefit
of a Chinese education on top of the martial qualities expected of a Xiongnu
noble, and between them father and son commanded a following that had never
actually forgotten the tradition of independent rule its people held before
the empire absorbed them.

## Turning the opportunity to their own account

Liu Yuan and his people watched the disunion consuming the Jin royal family
with the same eye that Empress Jia's rivals had watched each other, and drew
the obvious conclusion. They concealed their intentions behind professions
of loyalty, sided with the imperial party while the crisis was still in
doubt, then withdrew to Liaodong once the moment was right and established
an independent authority there. Liu Yuan had himself proclaimed king - and
not merely a regional king, but a claimant to the empire itself, taking the
title King of Han on the strength of a claimed descent from the Han imperial
house through the female line. A Tartar seizure of power dressed itself, from
the first, in the most legitimately Chinese costume its founder could find
for it.

## Twelve years at the gates

Liu Yuan's armies pressed south for the better part of a decade. Luoyang was
threatened repeatedly and held, on one occasion, only through the efforts of
a general who had built the river fleet for the conquest of Wu thirty years
earlier and was still, in old age, the one commander capable of buying the
dynasty more time. Liu Yuan's death interrupted the campaign only briefly:
his son Liu Song secured the succession against his own brothers by killing
two of them and their chief partisans, proclaimed himself King of Han in his
father's place within a year, and resumed the advance on Luoyang immediately
after.
