Emperor Yuan, a surviving Jin prince, took the throne over what was left
once word of Emperor Min's death reached the south, and moved the capital
from the twice-captured cities of the north to Jiankang, on the Yangtze -
gaining, in Boulger's phrase, a temporary immunity from insult that the
dynasty's two previous capitals had conspicuously failed to provide. China
had, in effect, split again into the pattern the Jin's own founder had once
spent a decade erasing: a northern half ruled by the Xiongnu kings who had
broken Luoyang and Chang'an in turn, and a southern rump still carrying the
Jin name.

## A hundred and fifty-five years, fifteen emperors

Four more emperors followed Yuan over the next twenty-eight years, none of
whose reigns Boulger judges worth individual description. What held the
southern court together in this period was less any particular ruler's
capacity than the simple fact of the Yangtze as a barrier the northern
kingdoms could not easily cross - a defence of geography standing in for the
military strength the dynasty itself no longer reliably possessed.

## Liu Yu

The man who eventually ended the Jin was, like Emperor Wu's own ancestors,
a soldier who rose on ability rather than birth - in Liu Yu's case, from
genuine poverty: abandoned by his own father, raised on others' charity, and
apprenticed for a time to shoemaking before he found his way into the army
and discovered a talent for it that promotion could not keep pace with. He
broke a pirate chief's reputation before anyone else managed to finish him
off, defeated a rebel general in a battle fought with a smaller force on
both land and water, and was rewarded with command of the whole Jin military
establishment. From there his rise was rapid and, in the end, entirely
predictable: campaigns that restored order province by province, victories
over the northern state of Wei and the southwestern principality of Qin, and
a growing recognition among his own soldiers that the man leading them had
long since outgrown the modest rank the dynasty had first given him.

## The scaffold in the field

Liu Yu forced one Jin emperor's murder by strangling and installed his
brother, Emperor Gong, as a placeholder he had no intention of leaving on
the throne for long. Gong reigned less than two years before Liu Yu deposed
him in turn - though "deposed" undersells the theatre of it: a scaffold was
built in an open field outside Jiankang, a throne placed on it, and before
the assembled thousands of the capital and its great officials, Gong
descended from it to make way for Liu Yu in person. With that staged
ceremony, in the year 420, the house Sima Yan had founded on the strength of
his own family's accumulated service a hundred and fifty-five years earlier
reached what Boulger calls its consummation - fifteen emperors, over a
century and a half, leaving behind, in his own final verdict, an impression
"as vague and indistinct as the part they played in the history of their
age."
