Sima Zhao died in 265 with the throne of Wei already his in everything but
name, and his son Sima Yan finished the transaction his father and
grandfather had spent three decades assembling the pieces for. He deposed
Wei's last emperor without needing so much as the pretence of a battle,
proclaimed a new dynasty under the name Jin, and took the title Emperor Wu.
Boulger's judgement on the transfer is unsentimental: "the Jin won the
Empire by the sword" — not their own sword, in the end, but the one Cao Cao
had first drawn against the Han, which the Sima family had spent two
generations learning to hold.

## An emperor with one unfinished task

What Sima Yan inherited was not yet the whole of China. Wei's old rival in
the south, the kingdom of Wu, still held the lower Yangtze under its ruler
Sun Hao, and reuniting the fractured Han inheritance under a single throne
became, in Boulger's phrase, the one object of Emperor Wu's life. Sun Hao
sent an embassy to Luoyang to offer his congratulations and a nominal
submission when Sima Yan first took power, and Emperor Wu received the
ambassador with every courtesy — while neither man mistook the gesture for
anything more than what it was.
