The khanate survived Galdan comfortably. Tsewang Rabtan, the nephew who had fled from
him with seven followers and come back to beat him, ruled for thirty years and sent
an army into Tibet; Galdan Tseren after him held the Qing off for two decades more.
What finished it was a succession quarrel and a man who went to Beijing for help.

## Amursana

After Galdan Tseren's death the khanship was disputed. Dawachi took it with the help
of Amursana, a Khoit prince; the two then fell out, Amursana was beaten, and he took
refuge in China.

He expected to be executed. Instead the emperor used him: Qing armies went into
Dzungaria in 1755, took the Ili without much fighting, captured Dawachi, and
nominated Amursana chief of Dzungaria.

It did not hold, and Howorth explains why with more sympathy for the Dzungars than
for either party. Amursana was a very secondary chief with no claim to the
over-khanship of a confederation whose legitimate line he did not belong to; and the
Qing had no intention of letting him have it anyway. They had decided to abolish the
supreme authority altogether and put the four Oirat divisions back under four
separate khans, breaking up the formidable power the Kalmuks wielded when united.

Amursana took up arms. He was driven out, sheltered for a while among the Kazakhs —
whose chief Ablai was supposed to hand him over and instead furnished him with the
means to get away — and finally crossed into Russia.

He died there of smallpox, in Siberia. The Qianlong emperor's memoir turns it into
theology:

> Insulted heaven had fixed the hour of vengeance. A dreadful disease was the
> instrument it used... Abandoned by his people, who feared to catch his disease,
> scarce could he in this strange land find any one to render the funeral honours to
> his body.

The emperor wrote repeatedly to the Russians demanding the corpse, so that he might
make of the rebel's ashes an example of terror. They showed it to his commissioners
and refused to give it up. *Each nation has customs which it holds sacred*, they
answered. *A custom deemed sacred with us is not to expose the cold fragments of an
unfortunate who has sought refuge among us to ignominy. Your enemy is dead, we have
shown you the body, that ought to suffice.*

## The four khans

Meanwhile the settlement was being carried out, and it destroyed the people it was
imposed on.

Dawachi had been named khan of the Choros and died too soon. His successor was
assassinated by a nephew, who was killed in turn by Galdan Torgui, whom the emperor
then appointed himself and gave the title of Han and its prerogatives. *Becoming a
monster, I was constrained to purge the earth of him.* He was captured by a Qing
officer, beheaded, and his head sent to Beijing to be exposed as a warning.

> I uprooted his race. I changed into a desert the unhappy country where the
> perfidious Choros formerly dwelt.

Bayar, made head of the Khoshuts, was the first to break away, and ended, the emperor
writes, *a life of which he had so often made himself unworthy, in the midst of
tortures*; his subjects were killed or enslaved and left nothing behind but the name.
Chakturman of the Khoits was merely suspected of conspiracy; the general Yarhashan
was so satisfied of his intentions that he did not wait for orders from Beijing, took
him and executed him outside the gates, and gave up to his soldiers every Khoit who
did not run. Howorth's comment is that this last massacre was made on very small
excuse.

Of the four, only the Dörbet leader came through. Of the twenty-one chiefs of the
lesser hordes nearly all perished, by the sword or the executioner; a few got north
into Russia and the rest were enslaved.

## The number

Valikhanov, whom Howorth quotes, puts it at half a million Oirats.

Howorth does not soften it. The Manchu soldiers destroyed the Kalmuks mercilessly and
their provocation was very slight; the campaign was a chapter in the annals of human
butchery of the same kind as those against the Miao of Sichuan and the Muslims of
Yunnan; and a tolerably populous and thriving empire was converted into almost a
desert. He is writing in 1876, about an empire he was generally inclined to admire,
and this is what he says about it.

That is the end of the Dzungars and the end of something larger. No independent nomad
state was ever again assembled on the Eurasian steppe.
