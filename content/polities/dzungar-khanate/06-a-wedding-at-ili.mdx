Tsewang Rabtan spent the first years of his own rule fighting the uncle
who had murdered his way to the throne, then spent the next thirty
governing a confederation at peace with China for longer than it had
ever managed before or would again - before ending his reign by
capturing the holiest city in the Buddhist world through a wedding
invitation.

## The nephew who did not forgive

Galdan had killed Tsewang Rabtan's brother and stolen his betrothed
rather than let a rival branch of the family outlive him. Tsewang
Rabtan answered in kind: he defeated Galdan in the field around 1689,
recovered his bride and avenged his brother, then spent the rest of
Galdan's reign as an open rival rather than a subordinate, refusing him
support in the wars against the Qing that finally broke him. When
Galdan died in 1697, the Kangxi Emperor demanded his ashes and his
surviving daughter be sent to Beijing as proof the rebellion was
finished. Tsewang Rabtan refused both - a small defiance that set the
tone for a reign the Qing court never fully trusted, even through three
decades in which the two empires mostly left each other alone.

## A prince invited to his own wedding

What ended that peace was Tibet. The Khoshut Mongols had governed there
as the Dalai Lama's protectors since Güshi Khan's own generation, and by
the 1710s their chief, Lha-bzang Khan, had grown close enough to the
Qing court that Tsewang Rabtan read it as a rival empire's client
sitting on ground the Oirats had once controlled themselves. He offered
his own daughter in marriage to Lha-bzang's son, invited the young man
to the wedding festival at Ili - and seized and imprisoned him the
moment he arrived.

Two armies followed the same year. One marched toward Xining; the
other, under the general Tsering Dondub, crossed the mountains south of
Khotan and appeared before Lhasa in November 1717. The city fell to
treachery from within its own walls, and many Tibetans welcomed the
Dzungars as liberators from a Khoshut protectorate they no longer
wanted. Lha-bzang Khan fled to the Potala, was captured, and put to
death; his son, the bridegroom who had never made it to his own
wedding, was taken prisoner along with him. It was the last time a
Khoshut prince held the protectorship the family had claimed since
Güshi Khan first won it in the 1640s, and it drew a Qing army into
Tibet within three years to drive the Dzungars back out - the opening
move of the wars that would eventually finish the khanate itself.

## What Galdan Tseren inherited

Tsewang Rabtan died in 1727 and was succeeded by his son Galdan Tseren,
who held the Qing to a standoff for roughly two decades without either
the Tibetan ambition or the personal notoriety of his father or his
great-uncle - a reign secure enough that no source consulted here
gives it the kind of episode-by-episode narrative Galdan's own
generation left behind. What is not in doubt is what it bought the
khanate: another twenty years in which the confederation Erdeni Baatur
had built a century earlier still answered to one khan, still held the
Ili valley, and still faced the Qing empire as something closer to an
equal than a subject - the last stretch of time that would be true.
