Erdeni Baatur ruled the confederation his father had fought to keep for
eighteen years and his son held it for barely half that long before he
was murdered by his own brothers. Neither reign produced the kind of
chronicle Galdan's did - the sources that cover them are fragments of
other people's wars rather than a sustained narrative of their own -
but the fragments are enough to show what kind of state Erdeni Baatur
actually built.

## A war fought through an ally

In 1643 Erdeni Baatur went to war with Jahangir Sultan, the over-chief
of the Kirghiz Kazakhs, and asked the Khoshut chief Kundelung - a
neighbouring Oirat prince nominally under his authority - to join the
campaign. Kundelung refused: he was at peace with Jahangir, whose own
submission he already held. Erdeni Baatur, furious at being refused by
a subordinate, tried to have his own father-in-law march against
Kundelung in punishment - a plan that collapsed only because the
messenger carrying the order was ambushed on the road. It is a small
episode, but it shows the confederation's real shape: an over-khan who
could summon armies from four separate tribal households, and
households that could still simply say no when the summons cut against
their own interests.

Five years later it was Kundelung's own turn to make trouble on
someone else's frontier. In 1648 he raided the Khivan province of Kat,
carrying off prisoners, and Abu'l-Ghazi Khan of Khiva - who kept the
fullest chronicle of any ruler bordering the Oirats in this period -
rode out in person to punish him. He caught Kundelung's retreating
column, broke it twice in running fights, and came home with the
Khoshut standard as a trophy. Whatever authority Erdeni Baatur held
over his own confederation's member tribes, it did not extend to
answering for what they did on somebody else's border.

## What held the four tribes together

By his death in 1653, Erdeni Baatur had turned a father's shaky
inheritance - contested salt lakes, disputed tribute rights, a rival
line of cousins scattered by the Khalkhas a generation before - into
the four-tribe confederation of Choros, Dörbet, Khoit and Khoshut that
every later Dzungar ruler inherited whole. He did it through exactly
the mechanism the Jahangir and Kundelung episodes show: marriage into
the other tribal houses, war fought as often through an allied prince
as by his own army, and an over-khanship that had to be maintained by
continuous negotiation rather than assumed by right.

## Senge

His son Senge inherited the title and held it for roughly seventeen
years, long enough to marry into the Khoshut line the way his father
had - his own wife Ana Dara was the daughter of Ochirtu Khan, the most
powerful Khoshut chief of the day - but not long enough to leave a
record of his own reign beyond that alliance and the manner of his
death. His half-brothers murdered him. What is known of Senge's rule
is, in the end, mostly what came after it: the killers seated on his
own throne only until a third brother, a Buddhist monk trained at
Lhasa who had already resigned every claim on the succession, decided
the resignation no longer applied.
