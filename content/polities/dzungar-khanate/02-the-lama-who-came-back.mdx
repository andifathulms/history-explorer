Galdan was born in 1645 and was put young into the service of the Grand Lama at
Lhasa. He became a monk himself, and resigned all his rights in the succession to
his elder brother Senge.

## The dispensation

Senge was murdered. Galdan, who was always of a warlike and turbulent disposition —
*unlike what a Lama should be*, the Chinese account observes — got a dispensation
from the Grand Lama to resume his rights, attacked his remaining brothers, killed
them, and had himself declared taiji.

It is worth pausing on the mechanism. A monk of the Gelugpa order applies to his
own church for permission to leave it and take a throne by killing his family, and
receives it. The church in question had been put in power in Tibet a generation
earlier by a Khoshut Mongol prince. The Dzungars and Lhasa were not two institutions
in a relationship; they were two arms of one arrangement.

## The lake

His first war went badly — he was beaten by his uncle Shukur — and in 1673 he took
refuge with his father-in-law Ochirtu Khan, and quarrelled with him too.

In 1676 he settled both at once. Ochirtu meant to cross the Talki mountains and fall
on him unawares in the Ili valley; Galdan was told of the plan, forestalled it,
attacked in the passes and broke him, then pursued him to the Zaisan lake and had him
beheaded there. Fifteen thousand prisoners were taken.

The Kalmuks kept a legend about it: that the lake was tinged with blood for six days'
journey and its water was unwholesome ever after. Müller sets against the legend the
flat report of the Russian envoy Unkovsky, who had passed that way and had drunk the
water and cooked with it.

## What he was

The Russians' messenger who came back from his camp in 1679 described him as about
thirty-six, with a severe countenance, and given to drink.

By then he was master of Dzungaria and had subdued not only his own Dzungars but the
Dörbets and the others, and Howorth's explanation of the hold he had over them is
worth keeping: his profession as a lama and his relations with the Dalai Lama made
him a considerable person entirely apart from being khan, and that reputation
survived misfortunes that would have finished anybody else.

He had conquered Turfan and Hami and killed a Torghut chief, and took the title
Boshugtu Khan.

## The invitation

Then the thing that turned a steppe confederation into an empire arrived by letter.

South of the Tian Shan the holy families of Kashgar were split into two factions, the
Black Mountaineers and the White. The khan of Kashgar backed the Black and drove
Afaq Khoja, head of the White, out of the country. Afaq went to Kashmir, then to
Tibet, and ingratiated himself with the Dalai Lama so successfully that the Dalai
Lama sent him on to Galdan with a letter asking him to restore Afaq's authority in
Kashgar and Yarkand.

Galdan took the hint and the country. In 1678 he conquered the whole Tarim basin —
Little Bukharia, as the older books call it — made Afaq his viceroy with Yarkand for
a capital, and carried the deposed khan's family off to Kuldja in the Ili.

He did not try to administer it. The Dzungars left the internal government of the
oasis towns alone and limited themselves to a tribute of four hundred thousand
tangas a month.

It is the oldest bargain on that frontier, and the most durable: the steppe takes the
revenue, the oases keep the administration, and a religious faction gets the ruler it
wanted. The Qing would run the same arrangement in the same towns eighty years later,
having killed everyone who set it up.
