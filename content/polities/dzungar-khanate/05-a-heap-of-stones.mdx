The war between Galdan and Kangxi was decided by supply, and the record of it is
mostly about food, camels and how far men could walk.

## Jao Modo

In 1696 the Qing came out in three columns. The emperor marched west along the
Kerulen with the main army; Gerbillon, who was with it, remembered the quilted silk
doublets of the soldiers and the gold on their cuirasses and the standards making a
magnificent spectacle. Then the provisions ran short and the emperor turned aside to
better country and sent five or six thousand men on after Galdan, who did not catch
him.

Feiyanggu did. Encamped at a place called Jao Modo, he sent a detachment forward to
provoke an engagement; finding the Dzungars too strong it fired a volley and retired,
drawing Galdan after it. Feiyanggu dismounted his troops, put them on a hill, and
waited.

Neither side could retreat. Sustained artillery and musketry and repeated charges
broke the Dzungar ranks; when they gave way Feiyanggu remounted his men, came down
off the mountain and pursued for thirty li. Two thousand Dzungars were left on the
field. The baggage, the arms, the herds and a large part of the women and children
were taken. Galdan's wife was killed by a musket ball in the confusion.

The booty was six thousand oxen, seventy thousand sheep, five thousand horses and
five thousand camels — the material basis of the army, gone in an afternoon.

## The eighty days

What follows is the part worth reading twice.

Galdan fled west with his son, his daughter and a few retainers. Kangxi sent an
envoy offering terms, and the envoy came back with a report.

Only one of the party, a man named Pochi, was allowed an audience, and he had to wait
a long time for it. At length Galdan appeared. He was seated on a heap of stones in
the open plain, and would not let Pochi approach him. He thanked the emperor for his
benevolent intentions, and said that to prove his gratitude he would send one of his
own officers to make his real sentiments known. Then he mounted his horse and rode
away.

Howorth reads it as the guarded behaviour of a desert chief used to surprises and
treacheries, who probably suspected the envoys were assassins. The emperor's offer
was real enough: eighty days' grace, during which the imperial armies would halt and
cease from pursuing him, and after which he would be hunted without ceasing.

Two Dzungars in Qing service were sent next. They were received, and reported to him
on the strength of the empire and how comfortably the prisoners were being kept in
China. He broke off the audience without saying a word to them.

## What was left

An officer who surrendered around that time gave the arithmetic: about four thousand
people still with him, most of them women and children, a thousand of them fighting
men, and those in the greatest distress. A party intercepted on the road to Tibet —
an envoy from the Dalai Lama going to Galdan, and messengers from the Kokonor princes
— said he and his followers had suffered terribly since the defeat, had neither
clothes nor cattle enough, and that he was much broken down by his misfortunes.

His son Sebten Baljur, a name given him by the Dalai Lama, was captured by the chief
of Hami and sent to court. Gerbillon saw him at the audience: a good-looking boy of
fourteen in a cloth coat and a fox-skin cap, with an unhappy embarrassed air, who
fell on his knees and told the emperor how far away his father was.

## The ashes

On the fifth of June 1697 Kangxi received word that Galdan had died six weeks
earlier — taken ill at daybreak and dead the same night — and that his body had been
burnt. The Qianlong emperor, writing later, says he poisoned himself.

Kangxi had a table dressed as an altar in the open plain with perfumes on it, and
with his eldest son and the mandarins and the literati made the triple genuflexion
and touched the ground with his forehead, giving thanks to heaven. Then he sent for
the ashes, to be scattered to the winds as had been done with a rebel's before. He
also ordered the boy Sebten Baljur beheaded and his head set on a stake, and
afterwards revoked it.

The ashes were reduced to dust and scattered on the great parade ground outside the
city walls, in the presence of Manchu, Mongol, Dzungar, Khalkha and Chinese soldiers.

The khanate went on for another sixty years under his nephew and his nephew's
descendants. The ceremony on the parade ground was about something else: an emperor
demonstrating to five armies at once what happened to a man who could not be made to
kneel.
