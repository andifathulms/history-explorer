What was done with the country afterwards is the clearest statement of what had been
destroyed, because it had to be replaced from scratch.

## Repopulation

Dzungaria, Valikhanov writes, having been depopulated by the massacre of half a
million Oirats, was settled by China from the province of Gansu — and, to increase
the population, was converted into a place of exile for criminals.

Manchu soldiers of the green banner were transferred there for protection. Colonies
of Sibe, Solon and Daur were established in the Ili district. Seven thousand Muslim
families were forcibly converted into agriculturists. The remnant of the
exterminated Dzungars were allotted a certain extent of country to roam in.

The beautiful valley of the Ili, which had been the busy centre of a steppe empire,
became a Chinese penal settlement.

## The other half

The Tarim went with it. Within a very short time the oasis towns south of the Tian
Shan that had paid Galdan four hundred thousand tangas a month were incorporated into
the Qing empire — and governed on precisely the terms the Dzungars had used.

A general at Ili with lieutenants at Tarbagatai and in Little Bukharia; garrisons
only in the important towns; pickets on the frontier and post stations for speed;
the internal government left on the same footing as before. The Chinese showed great
caution, Valikhanov notes, because the population had fought with great determination
to assert their independence.

This is the origin of Xinjiang — the New Dominion — and of the frontier question that
has run through Central Asia ever since. A Qing province two thousand miles from
Beijing, created by exterminating the people who had held it, populated with exiles
and garrison colonists, and administered through the same religious families the
Dzungars had installed.

## The scattered relatives

The Oirats did not all die. The Torghuts had gone west to the Volga in Baatur
Khungtaiji's own lifetime and become the Kalmyk Khanate under Russian overlordship —
the same people who broke the Nogai Horde on the Yaik, and who in 1771 attempted the
return march east across the Kazakh steppe and lost most of their number doing it.
The Kokonor Khoshuts remained under Qing supervision. The Dörbet chief who survived
the purge kept his people.

The Torghut march is worth one more sentence, because it is the last act of the
Oirat world and it happened in the country the Qing had just emptied. Eight months
on the road east; two brushes with the Kazakhs on the Ishim; three days crossing the
hundred-and-fifty-verst steppe of Kangarbein Shara Usun, where the only water is
yellow and unwholesome and thirst made them drink it and hundreds died; a two-day
battle with Nurali's Little Horde and Ablai's Middle Horde as they came out of the
yellow waste; another on the shore of Lake Balkhash; then the Kara Kirghiz. They
reached the Qing frontier near the Ili in the middle of 1771. Behind them, wrote one
observer, the Caspian steppes where many thousand families had fed innumerable flocks
for a long series of years were left desolate and unpeopled.

But the confederation was gone, and with it the thing it had been: the last state
raised on the open steppe by people who lived on it, and the last one to make the
empires on either side of it negotiate.

## The last word

It belongs to the man who ordered it, because he wrote it down himself and expected
to be praised for it.

*I uprooted his race. I changed into a desert the unhappy country where the
perfidious Choros formerly dwelt.*

A century and a half later that desert was being resettled again, and it is still
being argued about. The Dzungars left no dynasty and no successor claiming their
name. What they left is a hole in the map that every power since has had to do
something about.
