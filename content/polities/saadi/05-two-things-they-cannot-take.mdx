The Saadis were made by holy men and spent the rest of their existence trying
to get out from under them.

## The persecution of the lodges

In 1551, al-Ifrani records, Muhammad al-Shaykh ordered a persecution of the
members of the zawiyas who claimed the standing of shaykhs and constituted a
danger to the monarchy — since it was precisely through them that he had come
to power himself.

The sentence is al-Ifrani's, and he does not soften the irony. A dynasty raised
to the throne by the religious lodges of the south, on the strength of a
sharifian genealogy the lodges had endorsed, turned on the lodges within two
years of taking Fez.

He was after money as well as authority. He sent to demand the return of
deposits he suspected the zawiyas of having received from the old dynasty. One
of his servants went to collect from a shaykh at Meknes and found him sitting
in a corner of the lodge plaiting palm fibre; a bird flying over let its
droppings fall in front of him; the shaykh barely raised his eyes to it before
the animal dropped dead and its feathers scattered in every direction, at which
the sultan's man fled in terror and went back to his master.

Al-Ifrani reports the miracle straight, and he reports the reasoning of another
shaykh straight too. Asked whether the sultan did not frighten him, the man
answered:

> God alone is to be feared. In any case there are two things they cannot take
> from us, water and the qibla; as for the rest, it must be left to whoever is
> looking for it.

That is the reply of a class of people who had watched dynasties come and go
for eight hundred years and had correctly identified which of them were
temporary.

## What the persecution cost

Nothing immediately. Ahmad al-Mansur's reign after 1578 was the height of the
dynasty: Portuguese ransom money, a modernised army with firearms, an embassy
trade with England, and in 1591 the Niger.

Then he died of plague in 1603, and it turned out that what the government had
been standing on was one man's prestige and a war chest.

## Three sons, and everybody else

The sultanate divided between Marrakesh and Fez and stayed divided, with the
succession contested by whichever brother, son or nephew had an army that
season. Al-Ifrani's later chapters are a directory of the people who took
pieces of the country while the family fought over the title.

In the Sous, a man called Ali ben Muhammad — the son of a venerated saint —
came forward as claimant when the reigning Saadi could no longer hold the
province, gathered the Berbers of the Jazula and Harsa plains and all the
tribes of the Sous, took Taroudant, was thrown out of it by a jurist-marabout,
came back after that man died, took the Draa, then took Sijilmasa, and ruled
from there. He was easy to approach, exemplary in conduct, chaste and little
inclined to shed blood, and he died in 1659 holding the south.

At Marrakesh, after a Saadi sultan was killed, the city rose at the call of
Abd al-Karim al-Shabani, chief of the great tribe of the Shabana, and swore
allegiance to him in 1658. He united the whole kingdom of Marrakesh under his
authority and behaved admirably towards his subjects.

Neither of those men was a sharif of the Saadi line and both of them governed
better than it did by the end. The dynasty that had made sharifian descent the
price of ruling Morocco was being replaced, region by region, by saints' sons
and tribal chiefs — and the country did not appear to mind.

## The famine

Then, in the year that Ali ben Muhammad died in the Sous and while al-Shabani
sat at Marrakesh, came the great famine of 1070 of the Hijra.

The scarcity was such, al-Ifrani writes, that the people, reduced to the last
extremity, came to eat corpses.

That is the state of Morocco a century after Alcácer Quibir, and the Saadi
sultanate had one more year to run.
