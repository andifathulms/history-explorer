Ahmad al-Mansur died of plague in 1603 and left three sons who all wanted the
throne, and the sultanate spent the next twenty-five years divided between
Marrakesh and Fez with the succession contested by whichever brother or nephew
had an army that season.

Dynasties end that way more often than any other. What is distinctive here is
what filled the space, because it is not another dynasty.

Sufi brotherhoods took over large parts of the country — the Dila'iyya of the
Middle Atlas ran a state of their own for decades — and so did marabouts,
holy men whose local authority was religious and hereditary. A corsair
republic at Salé and Rabat on the Bou Regreg, founded substantially by Andalusi
Muslims expelled from Spain between 1609 and 1614, governed itself and raided
the Atlantic as far as Iceland. Provincial commanders and tribal confederations
held what they could. The last Saadi sultan was assassinated by his own uncle
in 1659.

What ended the fragmentation was another sharifian family from the Tafilalt —
the oasis at the end of the western gold road — who took the country in the
1660s and reign in Morocco today. The Saadis are the dynasty that made
sharifian descent the requirement, and the Alawis are the dynasty that has held
it ever since.

Two closing notes for the region.

The gold road ends here too. Every state in the Maghreb had lived off it in
some way, from the Kharijite oasis towns taxing it in the eighth century to the
Genoese buying at Tlemcen to al-Mansur's army at Tondibi. Within a generation
of 1591 the Atlantic trade had made the Saharan crossing marginal, European
ships were buying West African gold on the coast, and the routes that had
financed every polity in the region for eight hundred years stopped mattering.
The garrison at Timbuktu outlived its own purpose.

Nine hundred years of Maghribi history had turned on three questions: who holds
the gold road, who can defend the coast, and who has a claim the scholars will
accept. By 1660 the first had stopped being worth asking, the second was being
settled in Lisbon and Madrid, and only the third was still open — which is why
the answer to it has not changed since.
