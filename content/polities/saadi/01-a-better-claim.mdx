---
title: A better claim
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987

Morocco at the start of the sixteenth century was ruled by a dynasty whose only
claim was possession, and it was visibly losing possession of its coast to
Portugal. What replaced it was a family with the two things the Wattasids
lacked: a genealogy and a war.

The Saadis were *shurafa* — sharifs, claiming descent from the Prophet through
Ali and Fatima — settled since the fourteenth century at Tagmadert in the Draa
valley and later in the Sous, on the southern side of the Atlas. The Idrisids had built the first
state in the far Maghreb on Alid descent seven hundred years earlier, and
sharifian blood had been the strongest credential in Moroccan politics ever
since. This is where it becomes decisive.

Descent alone would not have done it. What made the family a political force
was that in 1510 the tribes of the Sous recognised Muhammad al-Qa'im as their
leader against the Portuguese, who held Agadir and were raiding inland. For
thirty years the Saadis were the war: they organised it, financed it from the
sugar of the Sous, and fought it while the sultan in Fez signed truces with the
same enemy.

That is a legitimacy argument made in the field rather than in a chancery, and
it is the same argument the Almoravids and the Almohads had each made against
the rulers they replaced — that the existing government has failed in the one
duty that justifies a government. The difference is that neither of
those movements had a genealogy and this one did.

Marrakesh was taken in 1525. Agadir was taken back from the Portuguese in 1541,
and the Portuguese then abandoned Safi and Azemmour rather than defend them —
the first reversal of a century of European advance on that coast. Fez fell in
1549 and again, finally, in 1554.

A family with the Prophet's descent that had beaten the Christians where the
government could not was, at that point, the obvious ruler of Morocco. Every
dynasty since has claimed the same descent.
