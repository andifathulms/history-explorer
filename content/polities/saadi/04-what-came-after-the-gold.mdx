Ahmad al-Mansur died of plague in 1603 and left three sons who all wanted the
throne, and the sultanate spent the next twenty-five years divided between
Marrakesh and Fez with the succession contested by whichever brother or nephew
had an army that season.

The pattern is this corpus's most common ending and this site codes it on the
Seljuks, the Mongols, the Ghurids, the Buyids and the Idrisids. What is
distinctive here is what filled the space, because it is not another dynasty.

Sufi brotherhoods took over large parts of the country — the Dila'iyya of the
Middle Atlas ran a state of their own for decades — and so did marabouts,
holy men whose local authority was religious and hereditary. A corsair
republic at Salé and Rabat on the Bou Regreg, founded substantially by Andalusi
Muslims expelled from Spain between 1609 and 1614, governed itself and raided
the Atlantic as far as Iceland. Provincial commanders and tribal confederations
held what they could. The last Saadi sultan was assassinated by his own uncle
in 1659.

What ended the fragmentation was another sharifian family from the Tafilalt —
the oasis this site's Midrarid page is about — who took the country in the
1660s and reign in Morocco today. The Saadis are the dynasty that made
sharifian descent the requirement, and the Alawis are the dynasty that has held
it ever since.

Two closing notes for the region.

The gold road ends here. Every page in this site's Islamic West has been about
it in some way, from the Midrarids taxing it in the eighth century to the
Zayyanid funduk to al-Mansur's army at Tondibi. Within a generation of 1591
the Atlantic trade had made the Saharan crossing marginal, European ships were
buying West African gold on the coast, and the routes that had financed every
polity in this region for eight hundred years stopped mattering. The pashalik
at Timbuktu outlived its own purpose.

And the region as this site holds it closes with this page. Twelve polities
from the Midrarids in 757 to the Saadis in 1659: nine hundred years in which
the same three questions — who holds the gold road, who can defend the coast,
and who has a claim the scholars will accept — were answered in a dozen
different ways by everyone from a Kharijite oasis to a mahdi's caliphate to a
family of sharifs from the Draa.
