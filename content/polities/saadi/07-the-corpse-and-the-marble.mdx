What ended the fragmentation was another sharifian family, from Sijilmasa in
the Tafilalt, and al-Ifrani — who wrote under them — gives them a chapter
heading of their own: *the dynasty of the Hasanid sharifs of Sijilmasa, some
words on their glorious deeds and their brilliant qualities.*

He is a court historian and it shows. But the facts he reports about how the
Alawis established themselves are not flattering, and he reports them anyway.

## What happened to the men who had governed

Abd al-Karim al-Shabani, who had held Marrakesh well for eleven years, died
forty days before Mawlay al-Rashid came to the throne. His son Abu Bakr
succeeded him at Marrakesh, secured his authority over the city and followed
his father's example — until the sultan al-Rashid seized him and his cousins,
had them all put to death, and decimated the tribe of the Shabana.

Then he had Abd al-Karim's body taken out of its grave and thrown on a fire.

In the Sous, the son of the other claimant kept what his father had left him
until he was crushed by the same sultan, and the standard of that house went to
ground in a village and stayed there, powerless to continue the struggle. *The
power belongs to God alone*, al-Ifrani writes, which is what he writes at the
end of every such passage.

## What the marble had been for

Al-Ifrani devotes a chapter to El Badi, the reception palace Ahmad al-Mansur
raised at Marrakesh, and he begins with the motive rather than the marble. The
sultan built it, his authority says, out of a desire to leave a lasting trace
of his dynasty, descended from the Prophet, and to establish its superiority
over the Berber dynasties — the Almoravids, the Almohads and their successors
the Marinids. All those governments had put up monuments to perpetuate their
memory, and until then the sharifian dynasty had done nothing of the kind,
although it was worthier of it than any other on account of its illustrious
ancestor and its ancient nobility. When princes want to recall their glory,
runs the verse he quotes, they do it in the language of buildings.

Then he describes it: a square, with a great dome on each face and other domes
and palaces and apartments grouped around them; onyx of every colour and
marbles white as silver or entirely black; capitals sheathed in melted gold or
gold leaf; floors of polished slabs; walls faced in coloured tile worked like
the embroidery on a cloak; ceilings inlaid with gold; and, when the decoration
was done, the purest water set running through the courts.

It was pulled apart by the dynasty that came next, and what stands now is a
rectangle of red earth walls around a sunken garden.

The dynasty that dismantled it had understood the chapter above perfectly. The
whole point of the building was to say that a sharifian house was worth more
than a Berber one, and the people who took the marble away were sharifs.

## What happened on the Niger

The pashalik at Timbuktu outlived the sultanate that founded it and stopped
belonging to it. The garrison married locally, stopped remitting revenue,
elected its own pashas, and its descendants — the Arma — governed the river
bend as a military caste for another two centuries, with no reference to
Marrakesh at all.

Al-Sadi, writing his chronicle in Timbuktu under those men, is himself the
evidence: a local scholar taking office under an occupation whose original
purpose everyone had forgotten, in a city whose libraries the occupation had
broken up two generations earlier.

## What actually carried

Not the palace, not the province, not the family. What the Saadis established
is the thing that outlasted every one of those, and it is the argument they
made in the Sous in 1510.

Before them, Morocco had been ruled for three centuries by Zenata dynasties
whose claim was that they held the country. After them, no Moroccan dynasty has
ruled on anything but descent from the Prophet. The Alawis who dug up al-Shabani
and burnt him were making exactly the case the Saadis had made against the
Wattasids, and they have held the throne on it ever since.

The Saadis lost their capital, their gold, their province and their line inside
sixty years of al-Mansur's death, and they permanently changed what a Moroccan
ruler has to be.
