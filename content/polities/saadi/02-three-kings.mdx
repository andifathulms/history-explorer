title: Three kings in an afternoon

On 4 August 1578, on the Wadi al-Makhazin near Ksar el-Kebir, a Portuguese army
was destroyed and three kings died in a single day.

The circumstances are a Saadi succession dispute that got out of hand. Abd
al-Malik had taken the throne from his nephew Muhammad al-Mutawakkil with
Ottoman help; the nephew went to Lisbon and asked for an army. Sebastian of
Portugal, twenty-four, unmarried, and personally committed to a crusading idea
that his own advisers regarded as ruinous, gave him one and came with it —
perhaps twenty thousand men, a large part of the Portuguese nobility, and the
treasury behind them.

They were caught between two rivers with the Saadi army in front and cut to
pieces. Sebastian was killed. Al-Mutawakkil drowned in the river. Abd al-Malik,
who had been ill before the battle, died in his litter during it, and his
brother Ahmad took the field and the throne, and afterwards the epithet
al-Mansur, the victorious.

The consequences for the loser are the largest. Portugal had lost its king, its
heir-presumptive prospects, a great part of its nobility and its army in an
afternoon. Sebastian's elderly great-uncle reigned two years and died, and in
1580 Philip II of Spain claimed the throne — the Iberian Union, which lasted
sixty years and put the Portuguese empire under Spanish direction. The belief
that Sebastian was not dead and would return became a durable Portuguese
messianism with a name, *Sebastianismo*, that outlived the union by centuries.

The consequences for the winner are money and standing. The captured Portuguese
nobility were ransomed at enormous sums; al-Mansur struck the proceeds into
gold coin and was called al-Dhahabi, the Golden. He built El Badi at Marrakesh
— a reception palace of imported Italian marble, said to have been paid for
with Songhai gold and Portuguese ransom, and stripped to the walls a century
later by the dynasty that followed. He modernised the army on Ottoman lines
with firearms and artillery. He dealt with Elizabeth of England over saltpetre
and sugar, and the correspondence survives.

This site has recorded gunpowder deciding matters three times on its Ottoman
and Ak Koyunlu pages — Otlukbeli, Chaldiran, Ridaniya. This is the fourth, at
the other end of the Mediterranean, and the army it produced is the one the
next chapter sends across the desert.
