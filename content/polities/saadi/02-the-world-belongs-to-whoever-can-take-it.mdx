Al-Ifrani says that Muhammad al-Shaykh, the man who finished the Saadi conquest
of Morocco, knew the whole Quran by heart and understood it admirably, wrote
marginal glosses on its interpretation, and argued so well with judges about
their own judgements that he often obliged them to admit he was the only one
in the right. He had also memorised the collected poems of al-Mutanabbi, and a
line he quoted often:

> Men resemble one another and circumstances are identical; fate is the same
> for all, and the world belongs to whoever knows how to conquer.

That is not the ordinary register of a holy family from the Draa valley. It is
also, exactly, what the Saadis did.

## The oath at Tedsi

The rise began not with a conquest but with a petition. In 1510 the jurists of
the Masmuda and the chiefs of the tribes sent a deputation to Abu Abdallah
al-Qaim in the Draa, asking him to put himself at their head and offering to
place the conduct of all their affairs in his hands. He agreed, met them at a
village called Tedsi near Taroudant, and received the oath of allegiance from a
people who were, from that moment, of one heart with him and unanimously
disposed to make war on the Christians.

He called on them at once to march against the port the Portuguese held near
Agadir, and they did, and they won.

That sequence is the whole political argument of the dynasty in one paragraph.
A sharifian family is invited to lead; it accepts on condition of a war; it wins
the war; and everyone concludes it was born under a fortunate star.

## Splitting a country between two brothers

Al-Qaim then did something that looks careless and was not. He went north into
Haha with his eldest son, on the invitation of chiefs there who were being
raided by the same enemy, and left his younger son in the Sous with orders to
organise the country, lay the foundations of a state, and harass the enemy
night and day.

Two fronts, two sons, one claim. The elder eventually held Marrakesh and the
younger the south, and in due course the younger took everything from the
elder — which is how sharifian dynasties usually settle their internal
questions, and did not damage the argument at all, because the argument was
about blood and not about which brother had it.

## Agadir

The decisive act came in 1540, when Muhammad al-Shaykh drove the Christians out
of the place called Founti near Agadir and then laid out a port of his own
there. Al-Ifrani calls the founding of that port an act of real perspicacity,
and it was: the point was not to take a fortress but to replace it, so that the
Atlantic trade of the Sous ran through a Moroccan harbour instead of a
Portuguese one.

It worked so well that the Portuguese gave up Safi and Azemmour rather than
defend them. It is the first reversal in a century of European advance on that
coast, and it was managed by a man whose family had no army eighty years
before and no city at all.

## Fez

The last piece took two attempts, in 1549 and 1554, and it was contested by an
Ottoman intervention from Algiers before it stuck.

But by then the case was closed. Every quality the Wattasids lacked, the Saadis
had demonstrated in public over forty years: descent from the Prophet, a
victory over the Christians, ports recovered rather than surrendered, and a
sultan who could out-argue the qadis of Fez on their own ground.

The world belongs to whoever knows how to conquer, the sultan liked to say, and
his own family had spent two generations proving it in the one theatre where
the government of Morocco had refused to fight.
