This site's Songhai record carries a comment in its data file saying that the
polity which destroyed it "is not on this site". This chapter is the reason
that comment can now be deleted.

Everything in this region's history has run on West African gold. The Midrarid
page has the northern terminal at Sijilmasa; the Almoravid page has a Saharan
movement taking both ends of the road before it took Morocco; the Zayyanid page
has the metal changing hands in a funduk at Tlemcen and going north to be
struck as florins; the Mali and Songhai pages have the southern end. For eight
hundred years every North African power had taxed that trade at its own end and
none had ever tried to take it at the source.

In October 1590 Ahmad al-Mansur sent an army south from Marrakesh to do exactly
that. It was a few thousand men — the sources disagree widely on the number —
under a Spanish renegade named Judar Pasha, with arquebuses, light artillery
and thousands of camels, and it crossed the Sahara. The crossing itself killed
a large part of it.

At Tondibi in March 1591 what was left met the Songhai army, which this site's
Songhai page describes as the successor of Mali on the middle Niger, and which
was far larger and had no firearms. The Songhai were broken in a few hours.
Gao and Timbuktu were taken, and Morocco governed the Niger bend as a pashalik
from Timbuktu — nominally for a century and effectively for far less, as the
garrison went native and stopped remitting.

Three things this leaves.

The military point is the one the Songhai page already makes about what
gunpowder did to the balance between numbers and equipment. It is the same
lesson as Otlukbeli, Chaldiran and Ridaniya on this site's Ottoman pages,
transferred to a place where the losing side had never had the chance to learn
it.

The economic point is that it did not work. The gold did not flow as expected;
the sources describe al-Mansur receiving spectacular quantities of gold, slaves
and ivory as display rather than as revenue, and Morocco's Saharan pashalik
became a cost. Taking the source of a trade is not the same as owning the
trade, and the Atlantic was in any case about to make the whole route
irrelevant.

The third is what it destroyed. The scholarly establishment of Timbuktu — the
jurists and libraries this site's Songhai page describes, and the chronicles
written there — was broken up by the conquest, and Ahmad Baba, its most famous
scholar, was deported to Marrakesh in chains and held for years. A Moroccan
sultan who is remembered for building a palace with the proceeds also carried
off the librarian.
