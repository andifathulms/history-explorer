In April 1577 an esquire of Queen Elizabeth's body sailed from London for
Morocco with ten men, three of them trumpeters, and wrote the trip up afterwards.
Hakluyt printed it, and Edmund Hogan's account of his embassy is the best
description there is of what the Saadi sultans and the English were to each
other.

They were to each other an arrangement against Spain, and the terms of it were
saltpetre one way and sugar and safe conduct the other.

## The reception

The sultan was Abd al-Malik, who spoke Spanish and who told Hogan, while showing
him over a galley on an ornamental lake, that he had spent eighteen years of his
youth at that trade. He sent four captains and a
hundred soldiers to the coast to escort the ambassador inland, with one of his
own horses. The four English ships in the road fired off twenty marks' worth of
powder to make a show.

Then, outside Marrakesh, the detail Hogan enjoyed most:

> There met me all the Christians of the Spaniards and Portugals to receive me,
> which I knowe was more by the kings commandement then of any good wils of
> themselues: for some of them although they speake me faire hung downe their
> heads like dogs, and especially the Portugales, and I countenanced them
> accordingly.

The marginal note in Hakluyt is blunter: the Spaniards and Portuguese were
commanded by the king, on pain of death, to meet the English ambassador.

The audience was held in Spanish, which both men had. Abd al-Malik then had one
of his *elchies* — renegades, Europeans in his service — repeat it in Arabic for
the Moors present, and answered in Spanish himself.

## What the sultan said about Spain

He had granted the king of Spain a licence for an embassy of his own, and Philip
had asked him, as part of the request, not to receive any envoy the queen of
England might send. Hogan reports what he was told about that:

> I know what the king of Spaine is, and what the Queene of England and her
> Realme is: for I neither like of him nor of his religion, being so gouerned by
> the Inquisition that he can doe nothing of himselfe.

The Spanish ambassador, when he came, would wait twenty days after delivering
his message. The Englishman was to be treated as *my companion and one of my
house*.

Hogan's own explanation for the favour is confessional and probably half right:
that the sultan was well exercised in the scriptures of both Testaments, and
*beareth a greater affection to our Nation then to others because of our
religion, which forbiddeth worship of Idols* — for which the Moors, he adds,
called him the Christian king.

## What was actually being negotiated

The rest of the account is a merchant's diary, and it is the more valuable for
it.

Hogan presented a great bass lute, and the sultan asked for English musicians and
undertook to keep them well, with provisions, and *to let them liue according to
their law and conscience wherein he vrgeth none to the contrary*. He was given a
case of combs. He was taken into a garden where the sultan lay on a silk bed
complaining of a sore leg, and then onto a new galley on an ornamental water,
where the sultan spent two or three hours on the water.
He watched bulls being baited with English dogs and ducks taken with water
spaniels, and a Morris dance performed by the renegades. He was given a short
dagger from the sultan's own girdle, set with two hundred rubies and turquoises.

And in among all that, in three long night audiences, the substance: English
ships to enter any Moroccan port freely, in trade or for water, and to bring in
prizes taken from the queen's enemies and sell them there; safe conduct along the
whole Barbary coast and through the straits into the Levant, with the sultan's
own letters to the Ottoman sultan and the ruler of Algiers to secure it; and no
Englishman taken captive ever again to be sold anywhere in his dominions. In
return the queen would confirm the trade and supply *such commodities as he
should haue need of* — with the careful English proviso that nothing be required
contrary to her honour and law, or in breach of her leagues with her Christian
neighbours.

Everybody involved knew what the commodity was. Hogan raises it three times, and
the third time it is the last item of business he does before leaving:

> I desired his maiestie to haue special regard that the ships might be laden
> backe againe, for that I found litle store of saltpeter in readinesse.

The sultan promised to send down to Sus for it, and did. The other bills Hogan
put in were for the quiet traffic of the English merchants, and for sugars to be
made by the Jews — for debts past and to come — and for good order in the
*ingenios*, the sugar mills.

## A year

England got saltpetre for gunpowder and a market for cloth. Morocco got English
cannon, English cloth, and a European ally with the same enemy. Both governments
were conducting the relationship in Spanish through renegade interpreters, on the
explicit understanding that the king of Spain was the reason for it.

Abd al-Malik died on the field at the Battle of the Three Kings a little more
than a year after Hogan sailed home. His successor sent his own ambassador to London, and the
English embassy to that court in 1585 is printed in Hakluyt a few pages further
on. The gold from Songhay was a decade away. The alliance outlasted both of them.
