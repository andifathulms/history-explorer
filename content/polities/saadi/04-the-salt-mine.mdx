The best account of the Moroccan conquest of the Niger was written in Timbuktu
by a man whose city it destroyed and whose government it became. Al-Sadi
finished his chronicle about 1655, having held office under the pashas; for the
documents that started the war he says plainly, *I myself have seen the
originals.*

And what those documents demanded was not gold. It was salt.

## Ould Kirinfil

There was a servant of the Songhai prince named Ould Kirinfil, whose master
Askia Ishaq had grown angry with and sent to be confined at Taghaza — the
Saharan salt works, which formed part of the Songhai states and was
administered by them. Fate willed that he escape and get himself to the red
city of Marrakesh.

He wrote to the Moroccan sultan announcing his arrival, gave him news of the
Songhai country, said its inhabitants were in a deplorable state on account of
the baseness of their nature, and urged him strongly to seize the country and
tear it out of the hands of its masters.

The sultan was not in Marrakesh when the letter came. He was at Fez, punishing
the sharifs of that city, having had the eyes of the rebels put out — a good
many of them died of it, and al-Sadi's comment is a formula of mourning
followed by the observation that he did it for purely temporal advantages.

## The demand

Ahmad al-Mansur then wrote to Askia Ishaq to say that he intended to come to
his country, that he was at present far from his capital, and that the enclosed
document would explain his intentions.

The document asked to be given the working of the salt mine of Taghaza, on the
grounds that he had more right to possess it than anybody else, since it was
thanks to him that the country was defended and protected against the incursions
of the Christian infidels.

That is the casus belli, in the sultan's own hand, seen by the chronicler. The
salt of Taghaza was the currency of the Sahara — it went south in slabs and came
back as gold, and whoever held the mine held the exchange rate. Al-Mansur was
not asking for the gold. He was asking for the thing gold was bought with, and
charging for a defence of Islam that the Sudan had not commissioned.

The dispatches reached Gao in December 1589. Al-Mansur went back to Marrakesh
through such heavy snow that he nearly died on the road, and a great many of
his people lost hands or feet to the cold.

Askia Ishaq refused. He answered in violent and insulting terms, and sent with
his reply a bundle of javelins and two iron shoes.

## Three thousand men and a double number of followers

In November 1590 the army left. Al-Sadi counts it: three thousand men-at-arms,
horse and foot, accompanied by twice as many followers of every kind, workmen
of various trades, physicians.

Ten commanders are named, and the striking thing is who they were. Mustafa the
Turk. Ahmad al-Harusi the Andalusian. Ammar al-Feta the renegade. Ahmad ibn
Yusuf the renegade. Ali ben Mustafa the renegade. The right wing under
Ba-Hasan Friro, a renegade; the left under Qasim Waradououi the Andalusian, a
renegade. Over the whole of it, Judar Pasha, who was short and had blue eyes.

A Moroccan army crossing the Sahara to conquer the Niger was commanded, almost
throughout, by European converts and Andalusi exiles. The sultan told them
before they left that the divinatory calculations showed the Songhai country
must cease to be ruled by the Sudanese and that his army would take a certain
part of it.

## What the other side did

Askia Ishaq assembled his generals and the principal men of his kingdom to ask
their advice, and al-Sadi records the outcome in one sentence: every time a
sound piece of counsel was offered, they hastened to reject it.

He follows it immediately with the theological reading — God had decided this
kingdom would disappear and this dynasty collapse, and nobody can push back
what he has decided. A man writing about the destruction of his own country by
a state he later served does not have many ways of saying *we handled it
badly*. That is his.

He also records the last act of the old regime: the Askia arrested the Songhai
agent at Taghaza, who happened to be visiting Gao, on suspicion of spying for
Marrakesh, and threw him in prison with three others.

## The river

The Moroccans reached the Niger near Karabara without going through Arawan.
Judar gave a great feast to celebrate their safe arrival at the river, on
Wednesday 30 March 1591 — because the fact that these men had arrived intact
was itself the omen that the enterprise would succeed.

The desert had been the enemy, and it had lost. At Tondibi a fortnight later a
far larger Songhai army with no firearms met one that had them, and the
outcome had effectively been decided at the feast on the riverbank.

## Then what

Morocco held the Niger bend as a pashalik governed from Timbuktu. The gold did
not flow as expected; what came north arrived as display rather than as
revenue, and the Saharan province turned into a cost. The scholarly
establishment of Timbuktu was broken up, and its most famous jurist, Ahmad
Baba, was taken to Marrakesh in chains.

Within a generation the garrison had stopped remitting anything and had begun
marrying locally, and its descendants ruled the river as a caste of their own.
A sultan who is remembered for a palace paid for with Sudanese gold had in fact
gone to war over a salt mine, and got neither.
