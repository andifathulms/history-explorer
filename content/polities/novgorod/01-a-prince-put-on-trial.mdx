Novgorod was already old by 1136. The city on the Volkhov had been a
princely seat for nearly three centuries, usually held by whichever son
of the Kyivan grand prince was sent north to govern it, a training post
for a young ruler on his way to a bigger throne elsewhere. What made 1136
different was not a new city or a new dynasty. It was a citizens'
assembly deciding, for the first time, that a prince answered to it rather
than the other way around.

## The list of grievances

Prince Vsevolod Mstislavich had ruled Novgorod for years without much
distinguishing himself, and in 1136 the citizens turned on him at once.
They confined him under guard in the archbishop's own palace, with his
wife, children and mother-in-law, and kept him there for close to two
months while they drew up a formal case against him: he had neglected the
poor, he had tried to seize the neighbouring principality of Pereyaslavl
for himself instead of attending to Novgorod, and, worst of all in a city
that lived by its army, he had fled the field at the first sign of battle
against Suzdal the year before. When they finally released him, it was
only to expel him from the city entirely.

## Not a revolution against princes as such

What happened next shows how limited the citizens' ambitions actually
were. They did not abolish the princely office. They sent to Chernigov for
a different prince and installed him in Vsevolod's place. What they had
established was a precedent, not a new form of government fully worked
out in a single year: a prince at Novgorod held his seat only for as long
as the city's own citizens, gathered in the assembly the chronicle calls
the veche, chose to let him keep it.

## What that meant within a generation

The pattern set in 1136 repeated itself for the rest of the century.
Princes arrived, ruled for months or a few years, and were sent away again
whenever they lost the confidence of the assembly or of the wealthier
merchant families who dominated it. One held the seat less than a year.
Another lasted twenty-one months before his own retinue turned against
him along with the city. In 1141 the citizens went nine months with no
prince installed at all, ruling the city through their own elected
officials in the meantime. A prince at Novgorod, from 1136 onward, led
the city's armies and represented it abroad; he did not own the city the
way a prince elsewhere in Rus' owned his patrimony.
