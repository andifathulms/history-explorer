Alexander Iaroslavich was not yet twenty when Novgorod's citizens called
on him to meet a Swedish force that had landed at the mouth of the Neva
in 1240. He won decisively enough to be remembered ever after by the
river's name, and two years later he broke the Livonian knightly order
on the ice of Lake Peipus in a battle fought, by the chronicle's account,
so that the retreating knights' own weight in armour dragged them under
the breaking ice. Between the two victories Novgorod's western frontier
was secured against both of the powers most likely to press it while Rus'
to the south lay broken by the Mongol invasion of the same years.

## The harder fight was diplomatic

Alexander's real distinction was what he did next. Rather than resist the
Mongol demand for tribute and submission the way princes further south
had already tried and failed to, he travelled to the Golden Horde and,
once, all the way to the court of the Great Khan himself between 1246 and
1250, to secure Novgorod's position by accommodation rather than by a
battle he judged unwinnable. The city that had just defeated two Western
powers in open combat submitted to a third power in the east without a
fight, on his advice, and kept both its wealth and its own internal
government intact as a result.

## A death that made him a saint

Alexander died in 1263 returning from another journey to negotiate with
the Horde on the city's behalf. The city's own archbishop, Cyril, is
recorded lamenting his death before the assembled clergy with the words
"the sun of the Russian land has set." He was canonised centuries later
as a saint of the Orthodox Church, but the more immediate legacy, within
Novgorod itself, was the demonstration that the republic could preserve
its own institutions under a foreign overlord's tribute as long as its
prince knew when not to fight.

## What Novgorod did not have to become

The accommodation Alexander negotiated meant Novgorod paid Mongol tribute
alongside the rest of Rus' but was never occupied by a Mongol garrison
and never lost the assembly, the posadnik or the archbishop's civil role
that governed it day to day. The veche kept electing and expelling its
own officials through two and a half centuries of nominal Mongol
overlordship exactly as it had before 1240 - a continuity few other Rus'
principalities of the period could claim.
