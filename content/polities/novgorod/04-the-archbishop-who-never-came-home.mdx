---
title: The archbishop who never came home
drafted_from: forbes-1914

In 1352 the plague that had already devastated Western Europe reached
Novgorod's own lands, and the chronicle's account of its arrival is
one of the most vivid entries in the whole record. Pskov was struck
first, badly enough that its own envoys begged Novgorod's archbishop,
Vasili, to come and bless the stricken city in person. He went without
delay, led a procession through Pskov with crosses, blessed its people
church by church - and was taken ill on the road home, dying within
days at a monastery on the Shelon river before he could reach his own
cathedral again. Novgorod brought his body back and buried him in the
porch of St. Sophia's; that same spring, Feognost, Metropolitan of all
Russia, also died of the same sickness.

## "It passed over the face of all the land"

The plague did not spare the city that had already lost its own
archbishop to it. The chronicle's own description of what followed
reads less like an administrative record than a lament: death came
suddenly and painfully, a man spitting blood and dead within three
days, from Lady Day to Easter, "a countless number of good people"
carried off. The chronicler is explicit that Novgorod was not singled
out - "I believe it passed over the face of all the land" - framing
the epidemic as a shared visitation on Rus' as a whole rather than a
judgment on the city specifically, even as it emptied houses and
overwhelmed the capacity of the living to bury the dead.

## A blow the republic's institutions absorbed anyway

What the plague did not do was break Novgorod's own government. A new
archbishop, Moisei, was installed within the same entries that record
the epidemic's worst months, the veche and the posadnik's office
continuing to function through a demographic catastrophe severe enough
to kill the city's own senior churchman and Russia's own metropolitan
within weeks of each other. It was one more test, alongside Mongol
tribute and foreign princes, that Novgorod's republican institutions
absorbed without themselves collapsing - even as the plague returned
in later decades, a recurring visitation the city's own chronicle kept
recording long after 1352 had passed.
