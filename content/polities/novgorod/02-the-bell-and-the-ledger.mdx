Once a prince no longer owned Novgorod outright, something had to. What
filled that place was an assembly summoned by the ringing of a bell hung
above the marketplace, and a commercial network that reached from the
Baltic to the Ural forests and gave the assembly something worth
governing.

## Who actually ran the city

The veche elected the posadnik, the chief civil officer, from among the
city's leading families, and it was the posadnik rather than the prince
who handled most of the business of governing between crises: negotiating
with neighbouring powers, presiding over the assembly's own sessions,
commanding alongside the prince in war. The office was also, by the
chronicle's own repeated record, one of the most dangerous in the
republic. Posadniks were driven out by mob violence or by rival factions
in 1134, 1146, 1156, 1161, 1167, 1171, 1172, 1175, 1189, 1205 and 1209,
and killed outright in several of those years - the 1209 riots in
particular left the city convulsed for days.

## An archbishop with a civil portfolio

Novgorod's archbishop held a second office beside his religious one,
serving as a check on both prince and posadnik and, in later centuries,
as the keeper of the city's treasury and a signatory to its treaties. The
combination of an elected civil officer and a semi-independent
ecclesiastical one, neither answerable to the prince, is what let the city
survive the frequent and sometimes violent turnover in its princely seat
without its actual administration collapsing along with it.

## Furs, wax and a river system that reached two seas

None of this would have mattered if Novgorod's hinterland had not made it
rich. Merchants operating out of the city drew furs, wax, honey and other
northern produce from a territory stretching toward the White Sea and
east into the Ural forests, and moved it down the same river networks
that had once carried the whole Rus' federation's trade toward
Byzantium and, increasingly as the twelfth century went on, toward the
Baltic and the German trading towns growing along it. The city's wealth,
not the prince it currently employed, was the actual constant across the
volatile politics its own citizens practised on their princely
appointees.
