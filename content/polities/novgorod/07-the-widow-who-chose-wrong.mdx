The figure the chronicle blames for what happened in 1471 is a widow. Marfa
Boretskaya, whose husband Isaac had held the posadnik's office before his
death, led the faction within the assembly that pushed Novgorod toward
Lithuania rather than Moscow, at the exact moment Ivan III of Moscow was
running out of patience with the question.

## A city debating its own extinction

The chronicle, in its introduction, frames 1471 as the year Novgorod had to
decide, in plain terms, whether to be "frankly Muscovite or disloyally
Polish" - the Lithuanian grand duke also held the Polish crown by then, so
the pro-Lithuanian party's position was, from the Orthodox and pro-Muscovite
side that ended up writing most of the surviving chronicle, indistinguishable
from disloyalty to the faith along with the city. Marfa's faction pressed
ahead regardless, and Ivan answered with an army.

## The Shelon

Novgorod's own forces met Ivan's at the river Shelon and were defeated. The
chronicle, by this point thoroughly sympathetic to Moscow's side of the
story, records the citizens receiving their liberty back from Ivan
afterward rather than losing it outright - a settlement, not a sack, and one
that left the veche, the posadnik's office and the city's own administration
formally intact under a prince who was now unambiguously the senior partner
in the arrangement.

## Seven years of a liberty already spent

The settlement did not hold. Renewed friction between the city and Moscow
brought Ivan's army back in 1478, and this time there was no partial
accommodation: the veche was abolished outright, and the assembly bell that
had summoned Novgorod's citizens to session for three and a half centuries -
the physical instrument of the government the city had built in 1136 - was
taken down and carried off to Moscow. What ended at the Shelon was
Novgorod's ability to choose its own foreign patron. What ended seven years
later was the institution that had once been able to make that choice, or
any other, at all.
