For two centuries after Alexander Nevsky's death, Novgorod kept its own
government while the wider Rus' world around it reorganised itself under
Mongol tribute and, gradually, under the rising power of Moscow. The
republic's problem, by the fifteenth century, was no longer the Horde. It
was that it needed a prince from somewhere, and every candidate now
belonged to one of two rival powers that both wanted Novgorod's wealth
more than its independence.

## A city that could no longer stay neutral

Moscow's grand princes increasingly treated the right to install a prince
at Novgorod as their own prerogative rather than a request the city's
assembly could refuse, backing their claim with the growing military
weight that had already made Moscow the strongest of the former Mongol
tributary principalities. Lithuania, whose grand dukes ruled a state that
by then stretched deep into the old Rus' lands to the west, offered
Novgorod's merchant elite an alternative patron with looser demands and
closer trading ties to the Baltic ports the city depended on. Neither
choice preserved what the city had actually built: a government answering
to its own citizens rather than to a foreign court.

## The assembly divides against itself

The veche's own internal politics hardened into something closer to open
faction than the older pattern of expelling one unsatisfactory prince
for another. By the mid-fifteenth century a pro-Muscovite party and a
pro-Lithuanian party contested the city's actual foreign policy, each
backed by different merchant families, and the choice was not a small
one: Novgorod's neighbours to the south had already been absorbed one by
one into the same Muscovite orbit the city was being asked to submit to,
while the alternative meant subordinating an Orthodox city to a
Catholic-ruled state.

## Trade that no longer ran on Novgorod's own terms

The German Hanseatic merchants who had operated a trading post in the
city for centuries increasingly set the terms of that trade rather than
negotiating them, as Novgorod's own long internal preoccupation with the
Moscow-or-Lithuania question left it with less leverage abroad than the
republic had once commanded at the height of its river-borne wealth. A
city whose whole institutional order had been built to manage a prince it
could dismiss had no comparable tool for managing a foreign trading
partner, or a foreign patron, it could not.
