Between Alexander Nevsky's death and the Black Death, Novgorod's own
chronicle records no single ruler and no single crisis worth a chapter
on its own - only decades of small border wars, church-building, fires,
and one moment when the republic's pragmatism was tested against a
neighbouring prince's desperation, and pragmatism won.

## Paying off an army rather than fighting one

In 1293 the Novgorodians learned the price of backing the wrong side
in a Tatar succession quarrel. Prince Andrei had complained to the
Horde against his own brother Dmitri, and the khan sent his brother
Duden with an army that laid waste Vladimir, Moscow, Dmitrov and other
towns before Novgorod's own envoys reached the Tatar camp at Volok
with gifts and a direct request: turn the soldiers back. It worked.
The pattern held for the rest of the period - Novgorod fought the
Swedes and the German Order repeatedly along its own frontiers,
burning a Swedish fort in the Korela land in 1295 and losing another
skirmish to a spring thaw in 1293 - but treated the Horde itself as a
danger to be paid off rather than resisted, a policy that kept Mongol
armies out of the city's own walls for the whole period covered here.

## A prince at the gate, refused

That same calculation was tested most sharply in 1327, when Alexander
of Tver led a massacre of the Tatar envoy Shchelkan's retinue and
touched off a Tatar-Muscovite punitive campaign that burned Tver,
Kashin and the surrounding district to the ground. Alexander sent
envoys to Novgorod asking for shelter. The city refused him. When the
Tatar army's own envoys reached Novgorod afterward, the assembly paid
two thousand silver grivnas rather than risk the same devastation Tver
had just suffered - the same transactional logic that had worked
against Duden's army thirty-four years earlier, applied now against
an army that had just finished demonstrating exactly what refusal
would cost.

## A Muscovite on Novgorod's own throne

The prince who benefited from Tver's ruin was Ivan Kalita of Moscow,
already assembling the reputation for shrewd accommodation with the
Horde that would define his own dynasty. In March 1329 he was enthroned
in Novgorod itself, with the princes of Tver's own defeated house
attending alongside him - a reversal that put the city which had once
sheltered Alexander Nevsky's line under a Muscovite prince within two
years of refusing his rival shelter at its own gates.

None of this made Novgorod's own government or trade network the story
of these decades - the chronicle spends as many lines on burned
churches and rebuilt bridges as on princes - but the choices made in
1293 and 1327 show a republic already fluent in the calculation every
later Novgorodian government would have to make again and again:
which side of a quarrel between Rus' princes actually threatened the
city, and how much silver it took to make sure it never found out the
hard way.
