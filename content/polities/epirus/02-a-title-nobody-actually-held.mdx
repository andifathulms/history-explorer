---
title: A title nobody actually held
drafted_from: nicol-1984
phase: aside
---

Every modern account of this state calls it the Despotate of Epirus, and
the name is, on the evidence of the state's own contemporary sources, a
mistake. Nothing written during Michael I's own lifetime describes him
as holding the office of Despot - a genuine Byzantine court title, ranking
just below the emperor himself, that could only be conferred by an
emperor's own grant. Venetian observers, misled by the coincidence that
Michael's family name, Doukas, resembled the Latin word for duke,
sometimes addressed him accordingly. But no source shows him actually
receiving or claiming the title.

## A name imposed by later, hostile historians

Michael and his brother Theodore did not even use the family name
history now attaches to them. Later Byzantine historians, writing with
open hostility toward the Epirote line, styled them "Angelos" - a name
neither man used of himself. Both preferred to emphasize their
connection to the empire's more prestigious ruling houses, signing
themselves Komnenos Doukas rather than accepting the lesser name their
critics assigned them after the fact. Even the word "despotaton" itself
turns out to be a Latin coinage with a geographical rather than political
meaning, one that crept into Greek usage only later, in the Chronicle of
the Morea - not a term any thirteenth-century Byzantine writer actually
used to describe Epirus's own government.

## A rebellion the emperors at Nicaea never legitimized

What the sources agree on instead is that Epirus's rulers were, from the
Nicaean perspective, simply rebels - governors who had stopped
recognizing any authority above their own and never received, at any
point, a formal grant of the rank later history insists on calling them by.
Theodore himself swore an oath of loyalty to the emperor at Nicaea
before returning to Epirus to help his brother rule, an oath he abandoned
entirely once he judged the moment right. The title "Despot," when it
was eventually used of Epirus's rulers at all, arrived generations later,
applied loosely by writers working from a settled convention rather than
from any actual instrument of Byzantine law - and every ruler who bore
it, down to the fifteenth century, held it without a shred of hereditary
right, because no Byzantine emperor after the initial rebellion ever
bothered granting one.
