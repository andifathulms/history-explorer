Taking Thessalonica settled, in Theodore's own mind, a question that had
been open since 1204: which of the surviving Greek claimants had the
better right to call himself Roman emperor and heir to Constantinople.
John Vatatzes had just taken the Nicaean throne in 1222 as the son-in-law
of its previous ruler - a connection by marriage rather than blood.
Theodore, a genuine member of the Angelos, Komnenos and Doukas
families whose names had filled the imperial throne for generations,
considered his own claim self-evidently the stronger one.

## A coronation the legitimate church would not perform

The obstacle was ceremonial rather than military. The Patriarch of
Constantinople, the only figure with unquestioned authority to crown a
Roman emperor, was already installed at Nicaea and had already crowned
Vatatzes himself. The metropolitan of Thessalonica, unwilling to
encroach on the patriarch's own prerogative, refused Theodore's request
directly. Theodore turned instead to Demetrius Chomatenus, the
autocephalous archbishop of Ohrid - a hierarch who answered to no
patriarch at all, and who crowned and anointed Theodore anyway, with,
according to his own letters, "the general consent" of the senate,
clergy and army in the western territories. Theodore began signing his
own official documents with the full imperial style: "Theodore in Christ
God Basileus and Autocrat of the Romans, Doukas."

## A court convinced its moment had arrived

The letters of John Apocaucus, metropolitan of Naupaktos and one of the
best-documented voices at Theodore's own court, show how seriously the
new title was taken by the clergy who had helped install it. Thessalonica
was openly discussed as a stepping-stone rather than a destination;
Constantinople itself was treated as the near-certain next step, and
several of Theodore's own bishops judged the path there considerably
shorter from Thessalonica than from Nicaea. For a brief window, Epirus
stood as a genuine rival empire rather than a defensive rebel province -
the two Greek claimants to Rome's own inheritance, plus the fading Latin
Empire still clinging to Constantinople itself, forming three separate
courts each convinced it alone would eventually restore Byzantium.

## A rupture that could not be undone by ceremony alone

Theodore's coronation produced an immediate and lasting split - not only
political, between Thessalonica and Nicaea, but ecclesiastical, between
the western Greek hierarchy that had crowned him and the patriarchate
that had crowned his rival. Neither side possessed the strength to force
the other's submission outright, and for six years the two empires simply
coexisted as rivals, each waiting for an opening the other could not
prevent. That opening, when it came, arrived from neither of them.
