Thessalonica, ruled after Klokotnitsa by Theodore's brother Manuel,
lasted only until 1246, when John Vatatzes of Nicaea took the city
outright and ended what remained of the rival empire Theodore had once
built there. Epirus proper submitted to Vatatzes's own suzerainty the
same year - the last of the Fourth Crusade's Greek successor states to
accept that Nicaea, not a western Greek court, would be the one to bring
Byzantium back.

## An alliance that collapsed before the battle started

Epirus's own rulers did not stay quiet under Nicaean overlordship for
long. By the late 1250s, Michael II - governing from Arta - had married
one daughter to Manfred of Sicily and another to William of
Villehardouin, prince of Achaia, assembling a coalition broad enough
that he judged himself ready to challenge Nicaea's own emperor, Michael
Palaiologos, for Thessalonica once again. The armies met at Pelagonia in
1259, and Michael's own coalition disintegrated before serious fighting
even began: his son Nikephoros and Michael himself withdrew by night,
his illegitimate son John deserted to the Nicaean side outright, and his
allies' own cavalry was cut off and captured. Nicaean forces occupied
Epirus itself in the aftermath, driving Michael as far as the island of
Cephalonia and occupying Arta, Ioannina and towns as far north as
Durazzo. Epirote independence looked, to contemporaries, finished for
good.

## A recovery that outran the news of Constantinople's own fall

It was not finished. While Nicaea's own attention turned toward
Constantinople itself - recovered from the Latins in July 1261, restoring
the Byzantine Empire under Michael Palaiologos - Michael II had already
sailed back from Cephalonia, fought his way into Arta, and rallied enough
of his own scattered forces, including reinforcements his son Nikephoros
brought back from Italy, to drive the occupying garrisons out of Epirus
entirely. The inhabitants of Arta and Ioannina, offered a choice between
their own native rulers and incorporation into a restored empire governed
from Constantinople, made their own preference for the former
unmistakably clear.

## A refusal repeated against a legitimately restored emperor

What followed was, if anything, more stubborn than what had come
before. Even after Constantinople's own recovery gave Michael
Palaiologos every formal claim to be the legitimate restorer of Byzantium,
Michael II refused to recognize his authority over Epirus and resumed
open war before 1261 was even out - defeating and capturing the
Nicaean general who had won at Pelagonia two years earlier, and forcing
imperial expeditions against him in both 1262 and 1263 before Nicaea's
own attention was pulled elsewhere by more pressing concerns in Asia
Minor. A polity that had already lost an empire at Klokotnitsa and a war
at Pelagonia still would not accept that either defeat settled the question
of who governed its own territory.
