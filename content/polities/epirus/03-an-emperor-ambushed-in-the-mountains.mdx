Theodore Komnenos Doukas inherited a defensive province from his
brother and immediately began treating it as something considerably
larger. Where Michael had focused on holding what he had against
encroaching Latin and Venetian neighbors, Theodore set his own sights
on driving the Latins out of Greece altogether - beginning with an act
bold enough to announce the change in ambition to all of Western
Europe at once.

## An emperor who never reached his own capital

In 1216 the Latin Emperor Henry of Constantinople died, and the barons
who had crowned him chose his brother-in-law, Peter of Courtenay, to
succeed him. Peter traveled to Rome first, where the pope crowned him
in a church deliberately chosen to avoid any confusion with the
coronation of Western emperors, and then set out to reach Constantinople
by land, landing near Durazzo with his own troops. Theodore ambushed
him in the mountains of Epirus, destroyed the greater part of his escort,
and took Peter himself captive. The Latin emperor died in Epirote
custody without ever setting foot in the capital he had been elected to
rule - the first real blow any Greek successor state had struck directly
against a reigning Latin emperor, and one that spread alarm through
courts as far away as France.

## A kingdom that could no longer defend itself

Theodore's ambitions did not stop at ambushing traveling emperors. The
Latin Kingdom of Thessalonica, established after Boniface of
Montferrat's own death in 1207, had survived on the strength of a series
of energetic rulers, but by the early 1220s it had none left capable of
holding off both of its main threats, Bulgaria and Epirus, at once.
Theodore made war on the kingdom directly and, in 1222, took
Thessalonica itself with little real resistance - ending, after only
eighteen years, the first of the Fourth Crusade's own creations to fall
entirely.

## A base too large to stay merely a province

With his own territory now running unbroken from the Adriatic to the
Aegean and the second city of the old Byzantine world in his hands,
Theodore judged that the modest office his brother had built was no
longer adequate to what he had actually accomplished. What followed was
not a request for recognition from any existing authority, but a direct
claim to stand above all of them.
