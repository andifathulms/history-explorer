When the crusaders sacked Constantinople in 1204 and set about dividing
the Byzantine Empire's provinces among themselves, Michael Komnenos
Doukas - a cousin of two recent Byzantine emperors - joined the army of
Boniface of Montferrat, the crusade's own leader, as it moved to conquer
Greece. He did not stay long. Crossing the mountains into the remote,
defensible country of Epirus in the northwest, Michael deserted the Latin
cause entirely, presented himself to the Byzantine governor already
established at Arta, married the governor's daughter, and made himself
the accepted protector of the region's own Greek population.

<Figure id="epirus-mountains" />

## A province built on other people's inattention

Epirus's own geography - sea on two sides, mountains on the others - had
always inclined the region toward independence, and Michael exploited
that isolation methodically rather than dramatically. He played a double
game with the Latin powers around him: accepting nominal Venetian
overlordship in 1210 to buy time, breaking the arrangement within a few
years once his own position had strengthened, then invading Thessaly and
retaking Durazzo and Corfu from the very Venetians he had just sworn
loyalty to. By the time of his death, probably early in 1215, Michael
ruled unbroken territory from Naupaktos in the south to Durazzo in the
north, governing from Arta while building up Ioannina as a second center
of his own authority.

## A foundation with no one's permission

Nothing about Epirus's own founding carried the sanction of any emperor,
Latin or Byzantine. Michael governed as a self-appointed protector of a
Greek population the Fourth Crusade had left to the mercy of whichever
local strongman could actually hold ground against foreign claimants -
and he held it well enough, and long enough, that the province he built
from nothing outlasted the Latin kingdoms that had expected to absorb
it. His own half-brother and successor, Theodore, would take the modest
foundation Michael left behind and turn it into something considerably
more ambitious than a defensive province.
