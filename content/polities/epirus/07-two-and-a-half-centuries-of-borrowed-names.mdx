title: Two and a half centuries of borrowed names

Michael II's own stubborn recovery bought Epirus another two and a half
centuries of existence, but not two and a half centuries of stability. Two
marriage alliances did more to determine the territory's own fate than
any of its wars did: Michael's own daughter Helena's marriage to Manfred
of Sicily in 1259 signed away Epirote territory to the kingdom of Sicily,
and in 1294 the marriage of his granddaughter Thamar to Philip of
Taranto signed away more of it to the Angevin house of Naples. Neither
union brought the territory any real benefit in exchange - simply new
foreign landlords for towns whose own populations remained Greek
regardless of which distant crown claimed their taxes.

## A title claimed by everyone who could get away with it

The rulers who followed - Greek, then Italian, then Serbian, then
Albanian in turn - continued to call themselves Despot of Epirus, or
some variant of it, long after the title had any connection to an actual
Byzantine grant. A Serbian occupation held Ioannina through the 1340s
and 1350s; a separate Albanian principality controlled Arta for a time
afterward; the Italian Orsini family, then the Buondelmonti, then the
Tocco family in turn claimed the same office from Italy. Carlo I Tocco,
who briefly reunited most of the old Despotate's own scattered territory
in the early fifteenth century, was according to the sources the last
ruler who could plausibly be called a genuine Despot in any sense
connected to the original office - and even his own reunification did not
survive his death, falling apart within a year among his squabbling
heirs.

## Cities falling one by one, in no particular hurry

The Ottoman conquest of what remained of Epirus did not arrive as a
single campaign. Ioannina fell in 1430, Arta in 1449, and Vonitsa, the
last of the old Despotate's own cities still in Christian hands, held out
until August 1479 - by which point the rest of Greece and the Balkans
had already been absorbed into Ottoman rule around it. The fall of the
Ionian islands that followed in the same year brought a documented
cruelty stark even by the standards of the age: officials who had served
the Tocco family were killed outright, and many of the islands'
inhabitants were deported to the Sea of Marmora, where the sultan is
recorded as having forced separated husbands and wives to remarry
across ethnic lines as a deliberate experiment in producing a new
population from the mixture.

## An ending that closed a story two hundred and seventy years long

What had begun in 1204 or 1205 as one deserting officer's refusal to
serve the Latins, and had briefly become, under Theodore, a genuine
rival to the empire eventually restored at Nicaea, ended in 1479 the same
way every other Byzantine successor state in the region eventually did -
absorbed into an Ottoman Empire that had, by then, long since stopped
needing to distinguish between a genuine Despot and a foreign adventurer
who had simply borrowed the name.
