The empire Theodore had built at Thessalonica did not fall to either of
the rivals it had spent six years watching most closely. It fell to an ally.
When the child Baldwin II inherited the Latin throne of Constantinople
in 1228, John Asen II of Bulgaria maneuvered to have himself named
regent - a scheme that, had it succeeded, would have delivered
Constantinople into Bulgarian hands without a single battle. Latin
barons and clergy blocked the plan, but Asen's ambitions did not stop
there, and an alliance formed instead between Bulgaria and Theodore's
own empire, aimed loosely against their shared Latin and Nicaean rivals.

## An ally struck down before the suspicion could be tested

The alliance did not survive its own success. After Bulgarian and
Epirote forces together took Adrianople, Theodore grew suspicious that
Asen's own ambitions in Constantinople threatened his position as much
as any outright enemy's would - and rather than wait to find out, broke
the alliance himself and opened hostilities against Bulgaria without
warning. It was a catastrophic miscalculation. The two armies met in
1230 at Klokotnitsa, and Asen's forces, reinforced by Cuman cavalry,
destroyed Theodore's own army outright. Theodore himself was captured.

## A conqueror's own words, carved in stone

An inscription John Asen II had carved on a marble column at Trnovo
records the victory in his own voice: "I have captured the Emperor
himself, Theodore Komnenos, with all his boyars, and taken all the
countries from Adrianople to Durazzo." The claim was not exaggerated.
Bulgaria annexed the greater part of Theodore's own empire in a single
stroke, and what remained of Greek rule in the region split into two much
smaller pieces - Thessalonica, held afterward by Theodore's brother
Manuel, and Epirus proper, reduced to something closer to Michael I's
own original province than to the empire Theodore had briefly built atop
it.

## A prisoner who could not stay content with captivity

Theodore's own captivity did not end quietly. Treated leniently at first,
he was accused before long of plotting against Asen's own life, and on
the discovery of the plot was blinded - a punishment that ended any
possibility of his own return to power, whatever became of the empire he
had lost. The battle that broke him also broke, for good, the only
realistic chance a western Greek claimant ever had of restoring Byzantium
before the eastern one, at Nicaea, did it instead.
