Jayavarman II's own two immediate successors left almost no epigraphic
trace, and it was Indravarman, who took power in 877 with no clear
blood tie to either of them, who resumed the inscriptional record his
spiritual teacher's own family had kept since the founding. In his
first year he began the Indratataka, a reservoir north of the capital
at Hariharalaya - a reservoir built, on Coedès's reading, for both
irrigation and ritual purposes, and the first of a pattern of
larger and larger water-works every strong king after him would
repeat. In 881 he raised Bakong, the kingdom's first great temple
built in stone, for a royal linga named - by the custom of pairing a
god's name with the founder's own - Indresvara.

## A king who moved the whole city

Indravarman's son Yasovarman, who succeeded him in 889, inherited
both a peaceful kingdom and a father's habit of building on an
ever-larger scale, and within his first years in power he did what
no Khmer king had done before him: he moved the capital outright. The
inscription of Sdok Kak Thom records it plainly - "the king founded
the city of Yasodharapura and took the Devaraja away from
Hariharalaya to establish it in this capital." He raised the temple
mountain of Phnom Bakheng at the new city's center, for a linga
called Yasodharesvara after himself, and dug a reservoir seven
kilometers long north of the new capital, the Yasodharatataka -
what modern maps call the Eastern Baray - bordered by steles
recording his own genealogy and the scale of his own ambition in
Sanskrit verse. Around the reservoir he settled monasteries for every
sect his own religious eclecticism could accommodate, an urban
foundation built, in effect, all at once around a king determined to
outbuild his own father.

## Two obscure reigns, and a city abandoned

Yasovarman's own sons who followed him, Harshavarman I and Ishanavarman
II, left little behind them - a small temple at the foot of Phnom
Bakheng, a handful of inscriptions, posthumous names and almost
nothing else. What happened next was, by Coedès's own reading, a
usurpation dressed in family language: an uncle of the two brothers
left Yasodharapura around 921, "taking the Devaraja with him," and
built an entirely new capital at Koh Ker under the name Jayavarman
IV. There he raised a five-stepped pyramid crowned by a linga the
inscriptions call, in an escalation of the usual formula, "the god
who is the royalty" - a monument on a scale that dwarfed anything
built at Yasodharapura itself. The new capital did not outlast its
builder by much. About twenty years after it was founded, Jayavarman
IV's own line gave way to a cousin's, and the court abandoned Koh Ker
for the city Yasovarman had built, which had never stopped being, in
every sense but the current address of the king, Angkor's real home.
