Angkor was a hydraulic city, and the thing about hydraulic cities is that they
have to be maintained by the state that built them, for ever.

## What Jayavarman VII spent

The reign that looks like the peak is also where the trouble starts.

Jayavarman VII, after retaking the capital from the Chams in 1181, built more
than any Khmer king before or since: the Bayon, Ta Prohm, Preah Khan, Banteay
Kdei, the new walled city of Angkor Thom, and — this is the part usually
skipped — a network of a hundred and two hospitals and a hundred and twenty-one
rest-houses along the roads, each endowed.

The Ta Prohm inscription counts the establishment: some eighty thousand people
attached to that one foundation, including six hundred dancers, and villages
supplying it. Preah Khan's is comparable.

Every one of those is land and labour removed from the ordinary revenue and
assigned to a religious foundation in perpetuity. It is the same instrument that
was dismantling Pagan in the same century, at a larger scale, in a shorter time.

## Then the religion changed

Jayavarman VII's foundations were Mahayana Buddhist. His successors were not,
and the reaction was violent: the Buddha images in his temples were systematically
defaced and recut as Hindu figures, which is visible on the stones today.

Then, over the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, the country became Theravada
— the monastic Buddhism of Sri Lanka, spreading through the villages rather than
imposed from the court.

That mattered institutionally more than doctrinally. The whole Angkorian
apparatus rested on the *devaraja* cult and on temple foundations built and
staffed by a king to generate merit for a king. Theravada does not work that
way: merit is personal, monasteries are modest, and a wooden monastery in a
village does not require a state.

Zhou Daguan, the Chinese envoy who lived at Angkor in 1296 and left the only
eyewitness account of it, describes the Theravada monks in yellow robes as
simply the religious establishment of the place. Within a century of the last
great temple, the ideology it had been built to express was gone.

## The barays

The reservoirs are the other half.

The West Baray, the East Baray, the moats and the channels between them are an
engineered landscape of enormous scale, and what they did is debated — irrigation
in the dry season, flood control, water storage, ritual display, or all four.
What is not debated is that a system like that silts up, and that keeping it
clear requires organised labour every year, indefinitely.

The East Baray was dry by the time Zhou Daguan arrived. Sediment cores and the
excavation of the channels show breaches, patches, and reworkings that get
cruder over time — an infrastructure being repaired by people with fewer
resources than the people who built it.

And then the climate. Tree-ring work from the region indicates severe decadal
droughts in the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries, broken by intense
monsoons — which is the worst possible sequence for a canal network, because
drought starves it and flood tears it apart.

## The direction of the country

Underneath all of it, the centre of gravity was moving.

Maritime trade with China was growing through the fourteenth century, and it
favoured places on rivers that reached the sea. Angkor is a hundred and fifty
kilometres inland, on a plain optimised for rice and ritual rather than for
shipping.

The Khmer court eventually moved south towards the Mekong and Phnom Penh, and
the reasons are commercial as much as military. By the time the Siamese took
Angkor in 1431 the capital had already been losing its argument for existing for
a century, and what looks like a fall is mostly the last stage of a relocation.
