Suryavarman II took the throne in 1113 the way several Khmer kings before him
had: by killing a rival. He fought his uncle Dharanindravarman I in a single
day of battle, and the inscriptions describe him leaping onto the head of his
enemy's war elephant to strike him down in person. A second rival king fell
with him. Suryavarman II had the court Brahmin who legitimised royal
successions crown him the same year, folding two contested claims into one
uncontested reign.

## A soldier-king who mostly lost abroad

He spent much of the next three decades at war. Embassies to Song China opened
within a few years of his accession, but the real business of the reign was
fought closer to home, against Dai Viet to the northeast and Champa to the
east. The results were mixed at best: a 20,000-man invasion of Vietnamese
territory in 1128 was driven back, a fleet sent to raid the Vietnamese coast
achieved little lasting effect, and a renewed campaign a decade later failed
outright. Only against Champa did he manage a real conquest, seizing Vijaya in
1145 and occupying northern Cham territory for four years before a Cham king
retook the capital and ended the occupation in 1149. A final expedition against
Dai Viet the following year collapsed to disease and bad weather before it
ever reached battle.

## What he built instead

It was the building programme, not the wars, that outlasted him. Suryavarman
II endowed temples across the kingdom's older Shaivite foundations, but the
project that carries his name is Angkor Wat itself: constructed during his own
lifetime, on a scale no Khmer temple before or since matched, and built from
the start as his own funerary temple. Where earlier kings had dedicated their
great foundations to Shiva, Suryavarman II built his to Vishnu, and after his
death he was venerated there as a Vishnu incarnation under the posthumous name
Paramavishnuloka — a shift in royal devotion that put Angkor's most famous
monument on the same religious ground as the Vaishnavite courts of contemporary
Java, rather than the Shaivite tradition Angkor's own kings had followed since
Jayavarman II.

## An ending nobody recorded

Nobody wrote down when or how Suryavarman II died. His last surviving
inscription dates to 1145, but he was almost certainly still reigning when the
disastrous 1150 campaign against Dai Viet was launched in his name. His
successor, Dharanindravarman II, was not his son but a cousin — and a
Buddhist, breaking a Hindu orthodoxy at court that had held, with only
individual tolerance for Buddhism alongside it, since the kingdom's founding.
Whether the succession passed peacefully or through the kind of palace
violence that had put Suryavarman II himself on the throne, the silence in the
inscriptions is the only trace left of it.
