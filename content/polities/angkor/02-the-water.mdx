Angkor's plain has a monsoon that delivers most of the year's rain in five
months and almost none in the rest, and a lake — the Tonle Sap — that reverses
the flow of its own outlet river twice a year and swells to five times its dry
season size.

The city built for that. The West Baray, finished in the eleventh century, is a
rectangular reservoir eight kilometres long and two wide, held by earth
embankments, and it is one of four on the plain. Around them ran a system of
channels, moats and dykes that the aerial and lidar surveys of the last few
decades have shown extending across roughly a thousand square kilometres — a
low-density urban landscape of house mounds, ponds and rice fields with the
temples inside it, and a population that no other pre-industrial settlement in
Southeast Asia came close to.

What the barays were *for* has been argued about for a century: irrigation
storage, or ritual replicas of the cosmic ocean, or both, or flood management.
The argument matters less for this record than the fact that whatever they were
for, an enormous quantity of directed labour built them, and the inscriptions
say where the labour came from.

## The field that is not `trade-toll`

This record's `revenue_basis` is `land-tax` and nothing else, and against the
rest of this site that is worth pausing on.

The Khmer temple foundation inscriptions are, functionally, tax records written
from the receiving end. They list what a foundation was given: named villages,
areas of rice land, and people — counted, by category, by the hundred. Reading
them, the state's income is rice and labour, taken from cultivators through the
officials who held the assignment.

Ninety-six records on this site carry `trade-toll` somewhere in that field, and
nine carry it alone — two of the nine, Srivijaya and Melaka, being in this site's
Maritime Asia region, where every record is coded that way or close to it.
Angkor, the biggest polity within a thousand miles of them, has none of it. The
distinction between the two regions is not a matter of geography. It is in the
fields.
