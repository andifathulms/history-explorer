In 1177 a Cham fleet came up the Mekong, crossed the Tonle Sap and sacked
Angkor.

That sentence is the most surprising thing on this region's five pages. The
largest land power in Southeast Asia, forty miles from the nearest navigable
approach and a very long way from the sea, was taken from the water by a string
of coastal principalities with a fraction of its population.

What followed made the polity's reputation. Jayavarman VII drove the Chams out,
took the throne in 1181, invaded Cham territory and held parts of it for two
decades, and then built. The Bayon, Ta Prohm, Preah Khan, Banteay Kdei, a
hundred and two hospitals, a road network with rest houses at intervals — the
largest construction programme in Khmer history, carried out in about
thirty-five years, by a king who was already in his sixties when he began.

The Bayon's outer gallery reliefs show the naval battles against the Chams in
detail: the boats, the rowers, the men going into the water, the fish below
them. It is a monument to a war the polity had lost the first round of, carved
by the side that won the second.

## The conversion

Jayavarman VII made Mahayana Buddhism the state religion of a kingdom that had
been Shaivite for three hundred and eighty years, and the turning point on this
record is typed `conversion` for it.

The influence field it does not touch is `religions_carried`, which reads zero.
Coding rule 2 counts propagation past a prior boundary, and Angkor was on the
receiving end every time: Shaivite from India, Mahayana in 1181, and Theravada
from the Mon and Sinhalese world afterwards, which is what the country has been
ever since. A polity can be the largest religious builder on a continent and
carry nothing, and this record is the clearest demonstration of it on this site.

Nothing on the scale of the Bayon programme was built at Angkor after
Jayavarman VII died. The record's `peak` ruler is the one whose reign is
simultaneously the polity's greatest exertion and the last of it.
