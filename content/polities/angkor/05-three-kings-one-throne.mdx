title: Three kings, one throne

For most of the first decade of the eleventh century, Khmer
inscriptions record three kings at once. Udayadityavarman I, a
nephew of Jayavarman V, took the throne in 1001; a rival, Jayaviravarman,
was reigning at Yasodharapura itself from at least 1003; and a third
prince, Suryavarman - unrelated to the Suryavarman who would take the
same name over a century later - held territory to the east from 1002.
Between 1005 and 1007 Suryavarman led a campaign the inscriptions
describe in unusually candid terms: "he seized the kingdom from a
king in the midst of a host of other kings," damaging sacred sites
along the way. The war lasted nine years, and Suryavarman's own
occupation of the capital dates to around 1010 - though his later
inscriptions, asserting an unbroken legitimacy, backdated his own
accession to 1002, the year his first rival disappeared from the
record.

## A usurper who governed like a founder

Suryavarman I claimed descent from Indravarman on his mother's side
and a marriage tie to Yasovarman's own line through his wife - claims
Coedès treats as at least partly a legitimizing fiction laid over a
victory won by force. Once secure, he ruled like a builder rather than
a conqueror still fighting for his throne: he completed the
Phimeanakas and Ta Keo, raised entrance pavilions for the royal
palace, and had officials swear a formal oath of allegiance to him
carved into the palace walls in long lists of names. He favored
Buddhism enough to earn the posthumous name Nirvanapada, without
interrupting the state cult of the Devaraja his Shaivite predecessors
had maintained - the same syncretism that let a usurper who leaned
Buddhist still keep the priestly family that had served the Devaraja
since Jayavarman II's own founding.

## A gift sent south, against a threat from the sea

Around 1012, feeling threatened by a rival power, Suryavarman I sent
a chariot as a gift to the south Indian Chola king Rajendrachola I -
a diplomatic overture Coedès reads as aimed at Srivijaya, whose own
king at Kataha the Cholas would attack in a major naval expedition
soon after. It was as close as an inland rice kingdom on the Cambodian
plain ever came to entering the maritime politics of the straits a
thousand miles to its south, and it came only because a usurper still
consolidating his own throne needed friends wherever he could find
them.

## The mountain of gold, and the lake that outgrew the last one

Suryavarman I died in 1050 and was succeeded by Udayadityavarman II,
who spent much of his own sixteen-year reign putting down revolts a
general named Sangrama suppressed on his behalf. What he is
remembered for instead is a single temple and a single reservoir: the
Baphuon, a temple-mountain a Chinese visitor two centuries later
would still call genuinely impressive, and the Western Baray - at
eight kilometers by two, larger even than Yasovarman's own Eastern
Baray, which by this point was already showing signs of silting up.
It is this reservoir the water systems of Angkor are still measured
against today.
