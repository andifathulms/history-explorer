This record's `descendant_scripts` count is one, and the item is the Thai script.

The rule it is entered under says an adaptation counts on the record of the
script that was adapted, not on the record of the people who did the adapting.
Nineteen records on this site carry a non-zero count and this is one of them:
Old Babylonian holds the Hittite claim, Neo-Assyrian the Urartian, Byzantium
Cyrillic, Nabataea the Arabic script, and Angkor the Thai one.

The interesting cases are the ones where the rule sends a count nowhere. The
Jawi claim at Melaka, the Swahili one at Kilwa, the Burmese one at Pagan and the
Cham one on the coast all land on parents — Arabic, Mon, a South Indian Brahmi —
with no page here to receive them, and four records in two regions therefore
read zero for scripts they were the making of. Three of those four were written
in the same season as this page.

The Thai script devised at Sukhothai in the late thirteenth century takes Khmer
letterforms and applies them to a language of a completely different family.
Khmer is Austroasiatic and not tonal; Thai is tonal, and tone is not optional
information — it distinguishes words. The adaptation had to invent signs for
something the source script had no concept of, and it did, by pressing Khmer
consonant classes and a set of new diacritics into service.

That is a modification of the system rather than a borrowing of it, which is
precisely the distinction the rulebook draws and the same distinction the
Makuria record's chapter on Old Nubian draws four thousand miles away.

## And what it means for this page

Ayutthaya, the polity that destroyed Angkor, wrote in it. This record's
`successor_claims` counts Ayutthaya for taking Angkorian court ritual and
titulature in 1431; its `descendant_scripts` counts the script that court wrote
those titles in, devised a hundred and fifty years earlier by a different Thai
kingdom.

Two of Angkor's three influence counts point at the same place, and it is the
place that ended it.
