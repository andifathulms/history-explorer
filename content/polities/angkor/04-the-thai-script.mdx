The Thai script devised at Sukhothai in the late thirteenth century takes Khmer
letterforms and applies them to a language of a completely different family.

Khmer is Austroasiatic and is not tonal. Thai is tonal, and tone is not optional
information in it — it distinguishes one word from another. So the adaptation had
to invent signs for something the source script had no concept of, and it did,
by pressing the Khmer consonant classes and a set of new diacritics into service.

That is a modification of a writing system rather than a borrowing of one, and it
is the reason the Thai script is Angkor's inheritance rather than Sukhothai's
invention from nothing.

## And what it means here

Ayutthaya, the polity that destroyed Angkor, wrote in it.

When Ayutthaya took Angkor in 1431 it carried off the court ritual and the
titulature with everything else, and it recorded those Angkorian titles in
letters that were themselves Angkorian, reshaped a hundred and fifty years
earlier by a different Thai kingdom.

What Angkor left behind, it left to the place that ended it.
