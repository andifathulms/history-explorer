Angkor is a rice state on an inland plain, and that fact separates it sharply
from the maritime polities of the archipelago — Srivijaya, Melaka, Aceh,
Ternate — whose power ran on controlling straits rather than territory. Angkor
built at a scale nothing in the islands approached, and for most of its six
centuries the sea route that made those port states rich was something that
happened to other people. Pagan on the Irrawaddy, Dai Viet on the Red River and
Ayutthaya on the Chao Phraya are the same kind of object: inland agrarian
states whose wealth came from taxing a floodplain, not a shipping lane. The
islands and the mainland peninsula were not one Southeast Asian world with a
size difference between them; they were two different economies.

## What the archaeology changed

Coedès called these mainland and island polities alike Indianized states, and
the framework is built into that name: temple, script, Sanskrit, kingship and
law arriving from the subcontinent and being received by societies that had
comparatively little of their own to set against them.

Higham's excavation of the pre-Angkorian Mekong is the main reason that reading
no longer stands on its own. There were substantial, stratified, wealthy
settlements on this ground for a thousand years before any Sanskrit
inscription, with their own bronze industry, their own trade and their own
hierarchies. What arrived from India was a vocabulary in which existing power
could describe itself, adopted by people who already had the power.
