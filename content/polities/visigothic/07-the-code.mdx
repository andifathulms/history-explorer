The Visigothic kingdom ended in 711 and its law did not.

The *Liber Iudiciorum*, promulgated in the middle of the seventh century, was
the kingdom's great achievement: a single territorial law for Goths and Romans
alike, replacing the older arrangement in which each people carried its own, and
covering procedure, inheritance, property, marriage, debt and criminal offences
in something over five hundred titles.

After the conquest it went on being used. The Christian communities of the north
and the Mozarabs living under Muslim rule kept it as their own law, and it was
translated into Castilian in the thirteenth century as the *Fuero Juzgo* and
granted as a municipal charter to reconquered towns.

## Still being cited

Its English translator, writing in 1910, offers a set of concrete claims about
how far the survival went, and they are more useful than his general
enthusiasms, which run well past the evidence.

Some of the laws, he says, were still recognised as binding in the Iberian
peninsula in his own day. He cites a case in the royal chancery court of Granada
in which the Visigothic provisions were ordered to prevail in a dispute over
inheritance, where the property of a dead monk was claimed both by his monastery
and by his relatives.

And the laws were long used in southern France, where the capitularies of the
early kings carry unmistakable internal evidence of having been drawn from the
same source.

A law written for a Gothic king in Toledo was deciding a monastery's claim to a
dead man's estate in a Spanish court more than a thousand years after the
kingdom that made it had been overrun.

## The other survival

The translation itself did something the legislators cannot have intended.

Scott is a partisan and his prose about the Code's beauties should be
discounted, but on this point he is describing a real thing. The Castilian
version — its coarseness, its ambiguities and its errors included — is one of the
earliest substantial bodies of prose in the language, and it shows Spanish being
made: corrupt Latin that had lost its classical grammar and Romance that had not
yet found its own, abbreviated words, crabbed constructions, misquoted
scripture, provincial peculiarities, archaic terms whose meaning was already
lost, and words of Arabic derivation entering the language for the first time as
the names of animals and objects.

## What that adds up to

The Visigoths are usually remembered for the one afternoon at Guadalete and the
eighteen months that followed, which is the shortest possible summary of three
centuries.

What they actually left was a book. Not a claim, not a dynasty, not a church —
the kingdom's Arian church was abandoned by its own king in 589 and the Catholic
one it joined was there already. A book of laws, which outlived the state by
twelve hundred years, was translated into a language that did not exist when it
was written, and taught that language how to say things it had not had to say
before.
