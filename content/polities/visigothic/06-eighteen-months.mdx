In 711 an army under Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed from Tangier. King Roderic marched
south, and somewhere near the Guadalete or the Barbate — the site is not known —
his army was destroyed and he was killed. Within four years the peninsula was
held, and a great deal of it had been taken by treaty rather than by fighting:
the surrender agreement of Theodemir of Murcia survives in an Arabic text,
guaranteeing his people their lives, property and churches in exchange for
tribute.

Almost nothing ends this fast. The Sasanian empire took fifteen years to unwind;
the Vandal kingdom went in a campaigning season and had been under sustained
attack for months. This one had two
and a half centuries of continuous existence, a law code, a functioning church
and a tax system, and it was gone in eighteen months.

## The explanations, and why Collins distrusts them

The traditional accounts are Arabic and Christian and both are late — the earliest
substantial ones are a century or more after the events — and both are
explanations for something the writer already knew had happened.

Treachery is the commonest: a faction of the previous king's family, dispossessed
by Roderic, is said to have invited the invaders in and to have deserted at the
battle. Count Julian of Ceuta opens the straits out of a private grievance. The
Jews of the cities, driven to it by a century of legislation against them, are
said to have opened the gates.

Decadence is the other: a kingdom weakened by luxury, doctrinal quarrels and a
century of contested successions.

Collins's position is that most of these are literary and that the honest answer
involves less narrative. A kingdom whose army was a levy on landholders — an
obligation the seventh-century laws had to keep re-imposing, which tells you how
well it was being met — loses one battle, loses the king and most of the senior nobility in
it, and has no second army and no institution capable of raising one. The
elective succession the previous chapter describes meant there was no
uncontested heir. And a great many cities preferred a treaty guaranteeing their
property to a siege.

None of that is decadence. It is a state with a single point of failure, failing
at it.

## The pages on the other side

A great deal of later history points back at this event.

The Umayyad caliphate counts the conquest of Spain among its campaigns. The
emirate of Cordoba begins with an Umayyad prince arriving in a province taken in
711. Every Andalusi state afterwards sits on the ground this
kingdom lost. The edge is now in the data: `visigothic -> umayyad`, `conquered
by`, 711.

## What claimed it afterwards

One polity claimed to be this one continued, and it is the kingdom of Asturias.

Asturias was founded in the northern mountains within a decade of 711 and from
the ninth century presented itself as this kingdom continued: its chronicles run
the Gothic king list straight into the Asturian one, its kings adopted Visigothic
titles and law, and the reconquest of the peninsula was framed for seven hundred
years as the restoration of a Gothic realm rather than as a conquest.

That is a claim asserted publicly and continuously, and it is the whole
ideological basis of the reconquest: not a new Christian kingdom taking Muslim
territory, but the Gothic kingdom recovering its own. A state that lost a
peninsula in eighteen months was worth claiming to be for the next seven
hundred years.
