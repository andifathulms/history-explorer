In 654 Recceswinth promulgated the *Liber Iudiciorum*, a code of some five
hundred laws applying to every inhabitant of the kingdom without distinction of
descent. It abolished the separate legal treatment of Goths and Romans, which
the earlier Visigothic legislation had maintained, and it went on being used in
Spain — as the *Fuero Juzgo* in Castilian translation — for six hundred years
after the kingdom that issued it had ceased to exist.

It is the most comprehensive body of legislation produced in post-Roman western
Europe, and the contrast with the Lombard record entering this corpus alongside
it is exact and instructive.

## Two codes, opposite answers

Rothari's Edict of 643, eleven years earlier, is three hundred and eighty-eight
chapters of Lombard law in Latin, built on compensation tariffs, binding
Lombards and leaving Romans to Roman law. It is *personal* law: the rule that
applies to you follows your descent.

The Liber Iudiciorum is *territorial*: the rule that applies to you follows the
ground you are standing on. It is built on Roman legal categories rather than on
wergild schedules, and it treats the kingdom as a single jurisdiction.

Two post-Roman kingdoms, a decade apart, both legislating in Latin for a Germanic
minority ruling a Roman majority, arriving at opposite answers to the question of
whether law is a property of persons or of places. Nothing else in this corpus
sets that choice out as clearly, and both records exist now partly so it can be
seen.

## An elective monarchy that nobody wanted

This record's `succession_rule` reads `election`, and it comes from the Fourth
Council of Toledo in 633, which legislated that a king should be chosen by the
bishops and the magnates on the death of his predecessor, that nobody should
seize the throne by force, and that anyone who did was excommunicate.

That is a written constitutional rule, which puts this record in the small group
this site keeps returning to: Telipinu's Edict, Esarhaddon's succession oath, the
Hasmonean decree of 140, the Venetian lot-and-ballot, the Golden Bull of 1356.

The Urartian record's finding — that a state writes a succession law after being
badly hurt by not having one — holds here as squarely as anywhere. The council
legislated in 633 after a century in which Visigothic kings were routinely
murdered by their successors; a Frankish chronicler had already coined a phrase
for it, the *morbus Gothorum*, the Gothic disease.

It did not work either. Kings spent their reigns trying to have their sons
elected in their own lifetimes, which is the manoeuvre the rule exists to
prevent; several succeeded and several were deposed for trying. Between 633 and
711 the throne changed hands eleven times and the transfer was contested more
often than not.

Coding rule 7 takes the rule and this page takes the outcome, and the two are as
far apart here as they are on the Roman record five hundred years earlier — where
the same site codes `nomination, acclamation` for an empire whose army made
twenty emperors in fifty years.

## Four of eight

This record makes the fourth polity in the Latin West carrying `election` in a
succession or legitimation field, after Venice, Genoa and the Holy Roman Empire,
with the Lombard record making a fifth.

The regional observation that produced the note was made when this region and
Rome and Byzantium were one, at six of eight records, and it has survived being
split and then having four post-Roman kingdoms added to one half. Whatever else
the Latin West is, it is the part of this corpus where rulers were most often
made by a body rather than by a birth.
