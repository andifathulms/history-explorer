The Visigothic kingdom that people mean when they say Visigothic Spain was made
by Leovigild, who reigned from 568 and spent it doing what none of his
predecessors had managed: he put down the Suevic kingdom in the north-west,
pushed at the imperial enclave in the south-east, reformed the law, minted in
his own name and made Toledo a capital. Almost none of that reached the one
contemporary historian who was writing about him.

Gregory, bishop of Tours, was interested in Leovigild for a family reason. A
Frankish princess had married his elder son, and the quarrel that followed was
about the faith Gregory shared with her. So the fullest surviving account of the
reign that built the kingdom is an account of a domestic disaster inside it,
written by a hostile Catholic across the Pyrenees, and it has to be read that
way.

## Ingund

Leovigild had married Goiswintha, the widow of a previous king, and Gregory
introduces her as the chief agent of the persecution of Catholics in Spain —
noting with satisfaction that divine vengeance had marked her in return, since a
white cloud had covered one of her eyes and driven the light from it.

Into that household came Ingund, daughter of the Frankish king Sigibert, sent
into Spain with a great train to marry Leovigild's elder son. Goiswintha was her
grandmother, and received her joyfully, and then began with persuasive words to
entice her to a second baptism into the Arian faith.

Ingund refused. It is enough for me, Gregory has her say, to have been washed
once for all from original sin by the baptism that maketh whole, and to have
confessed the Persons of the Holy Trinity one and equal.

> At these words the queen, aflame with fury of wrath, seized the girl by the
> hair of her head and dashed her to the ground; there she spurned her for a
> long time with her feet, and, all bleeding as she was, had her stripped and
> plunged in the piscina.

The piscina is the baptismal pool. A queen who could not argue a thirteen-year-old
into conversion had her rebaptised by force in the font. Gregory adds, in the
careful voice he uses when he is repeating rather than asserting, that as many
maintain, Ingund never suffered her mind to swerve.

## The rebellion

Leovigild gave the couple a city of their own — Seville, on the best
reconstruction — and there Ingund did to her husband what her grandmother had
failed to do to her. She pressed him for a long time, and eventually Hermenegild
renounced Arianism, was baptised a Catholic, and took the name John.

His father began looking for a way to destroy him. Hermenegild learned of it and
went over to the emperor's side, making friends with the imperial commander whose
army was in Spain. Leovigild sent for him to talk; the son answered that he would
not come, since his father was his enemy because he was a Catholic.

What settled it was money. Leovigild gave thirty thousand pieces of gold to the
imperial prefect to withdraw his help, and then marched. When the armies met,
the men who should have supported Hermenegild abandoned him, and he fled into a
church nearby, saying that his father must not come against him, since it is
impious that a father should slay his son or a son his father.

Leovigild sent in the younger brother — Reccared, who would be king six years
later — with an oath that the prince should not be abased, and a promise that if
he threw himself at their father's feet all would be forgiven. Hermenegild asked
that the king be summoned, and prostrated himself. Leovigild raised him up and
kissed him, spoke kindly to him, and walked him back to the camp. There,
disregarding the oath, he made a sign to his men, who stripped the prince of his
own clothing and put vile raiment on him; and at Toledo he took away his son's
servants and sent him into exile attended by one slave.

Gregory records the ending in a single clause, later and elsewhere, as a thing
that had happened: her lord Hermenegild was slain by his father Leovigild.

Ingund never got home. Left with the imperial army when her husband fled, she was
being conducted to Constantinople with her small son when she died in Africa, and
was buried there.

## What the story is doing

Gregory is not a neutral witness, and the shape the story took afterwards is a
martyrdom: a Catholic prince killed by an Arian father for the faith. Gregory
the Great put a full martyr's account of it in his *Dialogues* — Dalton, who
translated the Frankish chronicle, calls that version fictitious and suggests it
came from Leander of Seville, who was in exile at Constantinople while it
happened and did not dare go home to Spain until Leovigild was dead. By the
eighth century Paul the Deacon assumes the martyrdom as a matter of course. The
Spanish chronicle written closest to the events says only that Hermenegild was
killed at Tarragona by a man named Sisbert.

What Gregory wrote down makes the other reading possible, and he wrote it down
without appearing to notice: the thirty thousand gold pieces paid to buy off the
imperial prefect, the imperial army in the field on the son's side, the picked
men, the troops who changed sides. That is a rebellion in alliance with a foreign
empire holding territory on Spanish soil, and the conversion may have served it
rather than caused it. A martyr does not usually need a general.

What is not in doubt is the arithmetic of the outcome. Leovigild won his war,
killed his heir, and died within a year or two of it — some say, Gregory reports
carefully, that he repented of his heresy at the end, forbade anyone to consent
to Arian error, and wept continually for seven days for all the evil he had done
against God, and then gave up the ghost. Whether or not that happened, the son
who inherited was the one who had been sent to carry the false oath, and within
three years of his accession he did in a council at Toledo what his brother had
been destroyed for doing alone.
