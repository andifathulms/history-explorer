The last book of the Visigothic Code is about heresy, and almost all of it is
about Jews.

Its titles run: concerning the wickedness of the Jews; concerning the
extirpation of the errors of the Jews. The provisions convert, in stages across
the seventh century, from disabilities into a programme — forced baptism,
confiscation of property, the seizure of children to be raised as Christians,
the prohibition of observance, and the reduction of the unconverted to slavery.
Those who returned to their own practice after baptism were to be stoned.

The most drastic of these were enacted under Ervig, in the last part of the
seventh century.

## What a law like that indicates

Legislation of this intensity is not a sign of a confident state.

The Visigothic monarchy of the seventh century was elective, and it was elective
in the worst way: a crown that any noble with a following might get, and that
the holder had therefore to defend continuously. Kings were deposed, tonsured,
blinded and murdered with a regularity the councils of Toledo kept trying to
legislate away, binding the nobility by oath to the reigning king and then, when
he was removed, binding them to the next one.

An insecure elective monarchy needs the church, because the church consecrates
the king and the oath is sworn on it. What it can offer the church in exchange is
enforcement. Each round of anti-Jewish law comes out of a council of Toledo and
is issued by a king who has just taken the throne and needs the bishops.

## The argument the translator makes

Scott, writing in 1910, draws a causal line from this to 711, and it is worth
setting out because it is a real argument even though he states it with more
confidence than it can carry.

The Jews of the peninsula, he says, had long-standing relations with their
co-religionists on the north African coast. The oppression, the confiscations,
the seizure of children, the enforced conversion and the prospect of the
annihilation of their community tightened those bonds, established an
understanding between traders and the Muslim conquerors of the west, and thus
invited the conquest of Spain. The harshest laws were passed under Ervig; less
than forty years later the monarchy was gone.

That is a monocausal explanation of 711 and it should not be taken at face
value. What can be said without straining is narrower and worse: a state facing
an invasion had spent the preceding generation making a substantial, literate,
commercially connected part of its own population into enemies by statute, and
had left them no reason at all to prefer it to whatever came next.

Isidore of Seville, who was no friend of Judaism, said of the policy of driving
Jews to baptism that it was *non secundum scientiam* — not according to
knowledge. He said it early in the century, and nobody stopped.
