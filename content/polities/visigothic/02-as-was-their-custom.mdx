The Visigothic kingdom people mean when they say Visigothic Spain was made in an
afternoon in 507, by losing a battle in France.

For the previous ninety years it had been a Gallic kingdom centred on Toulouse,
with Spain as an appendage. Everything that makes it a Spanish story — Toledo,
the councils, the conversion, the law, 711 — happens because of one day near
Poitiers. The account of how comes
from a bishop of Tours writing eighty years later, and it has to be read with
his commitments in front of you: Gregory is a Catholic writing about Arians and
a partisan of the Frankish house, and he does not pretend otherwise.

He begins with a meeting. Alaric II, watching Clovis conquering steadily, sent
envoys proposing that the two of them meet. Clovis agreed. They met on an island
in the Loire near Amboise, in the territory of Tours, and they talked and ate and
drank together, and plighted friendship, and departed in peace.

Then Gregory adds the sentence that tells you what the meeting was worth: *even
at that time many in the Gauls desired greatly to have the Franks as masters.*

## The bishop of Rodez

The next paragraph is the mechanism.

Quintian, bishop of Rodez, was driven out of his city because people said it was
his desire that Frankish rule should extend over the country. A quarrel followed;
the Goths living in Rodez became suspicious at the citizens' charge that their
bishop wanted to submit himself to the Franks; and they took counsel and decided
to kill him. He was warned, got up in the night and left with his most faithful
servants for Clermont, where the bishop took him in and gave him houses, fields
and vineyards.

That is the Visigothic problem in one anecdote. An Arian Gothic garrison
governing a Catholic Gallo-Roman population, in which the local bishop is
plausibly suspected of preferring the Catholic king across the border, and the
garrison's response is to consider murdering him.

## Vouillé

Clovis met Alaric on the plain of Vouillé, ten miles from Poitiers. One army
wanted to fight at a distance and the other tried to close.

Gregory's account of the outcome is four words long and contemptuous: *when the
Goths had fled, as was their custom*, Clovis won the victory by God's aid.

Alaric was killed. Clovis was nearly killed himself, struck at by two lances at
once and saved by his mail and a fast horse. A great many of the Auvergnats who
had come with Apollinaris and the leading senators died there — Roman
provincials fighting on the Gothic side, which is worth noticing against
Gregory's framing.

Alaric's son Amalaric got away to Spain and, Gregory says, wisely seized his
father's kingdom. Clovis wintered at Bordeaux, took all of Alaric's treasure at
Toulouse, and went on to Angoulême, where — Gregory reports without a flicker —
the Lord gave him such grace that the walls fell down of their own accord when
he looked at them.

## What was actually lost, and what was made

Everything north of the Pyrenees except a coastal strip.

The kingdom had been centred on Toulouse and its heartland was the country
between the Loire and the Pyrenees. After 507 it was a Spanish kingdom with a
Gallic remnant, ruled at first from Barcelona, and it never recovered the ground.

The interesting question is why it lost so completely and so fast, and Gregory —
against his own intention — supplies the answer in the first two anecdotes. The
Gothic kings were a small Arian military aristocracy governing a Catholic
population whose bishops were the effective leaders of every city, at a moment
when there was a Catholic king next door. It took a century and a half after
Vouillé for a Visigothic king to convert and close that gap, and by then the
Gallic provinces were gone.
