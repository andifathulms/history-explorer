Malatya fell to Kilij Arslan II in 1178 and there was one Turkish state in
Anatolia instead of two.

There is no battle to date it to and no last stand. The end of the Danishmendids
is a series of towns changing hands over about fifteen years, with the men
holding them transferring their service, and it is a very ordinary way for a
medieval dynasty to disappear. What makes it worth setting down is what was lost
with it, which is not territory.

## The other outcome

For a century Anatolia had two Turkish states and it was not obvious which of
them would end up as the country.

The Danishmendids held the richer half. They had the caliph's investiture and
Konya did not. They beat crusaders, took a prince of Antioch prisoner, fought
Byzantium to a standstill under its most capable twelfth-century emperor, and
were solicited as allies by the empire's own rebel governors. Had the succession
of 1142 gone differently there is no obvious reason the peninsula would not have
been organised from Sivas — in which case the road to the Aegean would have run
the other way, and the frontier principality on the Byzantine march that
eventually produced the Ottomans might never have had a reason to exist.

That is speculation and it should be marked as such. What is not speculation is
that the outcome turned on an inheritance rather than on any of the things the
two states were fighting about.

## What Rum inherited

A working country. The Danishmendid cities — Sivas, Tokat, Amasya, Kayseri,
Malatya — became the eastern half of the Seljuk sultanate and stayed its
economic centre, sitting on the routes east to Tabriz and south to Aleppo. The
Danishmendid administrative and military class became Seljuk officers. The
caravan roads that Rum is remembered for run through territory it took from this
dynasty.

It is the second time this had happened in a century in the same country and by
the same method. The Turkish states in Anatolia were built on top of a Byzantine
one whose towns, taxpayers and roads they kept using, and then one of them was
built on top of the other.

## And a book

One thing outlasted the state, and it was still being read two hundred years
later by people doing the same job on a different frontier.

The *Danishmendname* is a Turkish epic romance, written down in the fourteenth
century out of older oral material, which turns Danishmend Gazi into the
archetypal frontier warrior: a holy fighter on a Christian border, with named
companions, sieges, single combats and conversions. It is not history and was
never offered as history. It is a model of conduct.

Its audience is the point. It was read most enthusiastically in the
fourteenth-century Turkmen beyliks and at the early Ottoman court — men on the
Aegean and Byzantine marches who were doing in their own generation what the
book described, and who wanted a template with a pedigree.

A dynasty that lost its state in 1178 supplied the vocabulary in which the men
who eventually took the rest of Anatolia understood themselves.
