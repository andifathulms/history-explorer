Bar Hebraeus's own family came from Melitene, and his account of how the
Danishmendids took it is the most useful thing written about them by anybody,
because it was written by a Christian who thought the Turkish conqueror had been
the best thing to happen to the city in a generation.

The situation first. Melitene was held by Gabriel, a Greek, and every summer the
Danishmendid amir came over from Sivas, wasted the country, ate the crops and
went home for the winter. Gabriel, unable to stop this, sent three times to the
Franks promising them the city. So Bohemond of Antioch set out for Melitene,
trusting him.

What happened next is a conspiracy nobody in the standard account of the Crusades
is on the right side of. The Armenian lords of the hill country — including one
Bar Hebraeus names as Khoj Basil, glossing the title helpfully as *thief* — were
afraid that if the Franks took Melitene they would be pushed out of their own
places, so they sent secretly to the Danishmendid asking him to lay an ambush.
Gabriel, who had invited Bohemond in the first place, met him at a village above
the city and spun the days out to give the Turks time to arrive. The ambush
worked. Bohemond was put in fetters and sent to Sivas.

Melitene itself held out until two of its own soldiers, enraged past bearing by
Gabriel robbing the people inside the walls, opened it to the Turks on Wednesday
18 September 1102 — a date Bar Hebraeus gives precisely, noting that some Arabic
manuscripts say the year before.

And then the passage that is the reason to read him. When the Turks went into the
city they plundered all its wealth, because the amir had already assigned the
wealth to his troops — *with the exception of the inhabitants.* He did not leave
one soul to perish; he took the people upon himself, sent them back to their own
houses, and brought wheat and oxen and other necessaries from his own country and
gave them to them. Very many blessings came to Melitene in his days. He appointed
a governor over it named Basil, a man who was just and God-fearing.

Gabriel, meanwhile, was handed over to justice by Turks and Christians together,
made to remember the murders he had ordered, and taken in front of a fortress
where his wife was, and killed.

Three things in that sequence are worth holding.

The first is the Armenians' calculation. Faced with a Frankish crusader and a
Turkish amir, the local Christian lords chose the Turk, because the Turk was
raiding their crops and the Frank would have taken their castles. That is the
whole political logic of eleventh-century Anatolia in one decision, and it is why
the Danishmendids lasted eighty years.

The second is the distinction between the wealth and the people. A conquering
army was paid out of the city's goods and forbidden the city's population, and
the population was then subsidised out of the conqueror's own province until it
could feed itself. That is a state behaving like a state, in its second
generation, in country it had entered as nomads.

The third is who says so. Bar Hebraeus had no reason to flatter the family that
took his home town, and the ruler he describes as wicked in this story is the
Christian one.
