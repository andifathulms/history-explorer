The Danishmendids were destroyed by the thing that destroys most of the
polities in this corpus, which is a succession.

Melik Mehmed died in 1142 and the realm divided between two branches, one at
Sivas and one at Malatya. They did not reunite. Each dealt separately with the
Seljuks, with the Byzantines under Manuel Komnenos, and with Nur al-Din of
Aleppo — whose Zangid page on this site describes a ruler assembling northern
Syria in exactly these decades — and each was in turn absorbed. Kilij Arslan II
of Rum took the Sivas branch by 1175 and Malatya in 1178, and the surviving
Danishmendids entered Seljuk service as commanders and provincial governors.

Their afterlife is a book. The *Danishmendname* is a Turkish epic romance,
written down in the fourteenth century out of older oral material, which turns
Danishmend Gazi into the archetypal frontier warrior — a holy fighter on a
Christian border, with companions, sieges and conversions. It was read for
centuries and it was read most enthusiastically in the fourteenth-century
beyliks and the early Ottoman court, who were doing the same thing on a
different frontier and wanted a model for it.

That is the reason this page belongs here rather than as a footnote to Rum.
Anatolia after Manzikert had two Turkish states and it is an accident of
outcome that the corpus remembers one. The Danishmendids held the eastern half
for a century, beat crusaders, minted in Greek, and lost to a rival rather than
to a catastrophe — and the story they left behind was still being used as a
template two hundred years later by the people who took the rest of the
peninsula.
