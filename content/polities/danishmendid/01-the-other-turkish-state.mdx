This site's Sultanate of Rum page describes the Seljuk branch that took
Anatolia after Manzikert, and mentions in one clause that it had
Danishmendids to the east. For a century that clause was the more powerful of
the two.

Manzikert did not hand Anatolia to anybody. It removed the Byzantine frontier
system, and what moved into the country behind it was not an army under a
command but a number of Turkmen groups under their own leaders, each taking
what it could hold. Two of them became states. The house of Seljuk took the
west and Konya. A commander remembered as Danishmend Gazi — whose origins the
sources cannot agree on, making him variously a Turkmen emir, an Armenian, a
kinsman of the Great Seljuk sultan and a veteran of Manzikert — took the
centre and north-east, with Sivas, Niksar, Tokat and eventually Malatya and
Kayseri.

For most of the twelfth century the Danishmendids were the stronger. Emir Gazi
captured Bohemond of Antioch in 1100 and held him three years; a Danishmendid
and Seljuk alliance destroyed the crusading army of 1101; and at their height
they held more of Anatolia than Konya did, with the caliph reportedly sending
Emir Gazi the insignia of a sultan.

What is worth noticing about them is the coinage. Danishmendid coins carry
inscriptions in Greek as well as Turkish and Arabic, and some carry a figure on
horseback spearing a dragon, which is Saint George. A Turkish Muslim dynasty in
central Anatolia was minting in the language of its subjects with the imagery
of their religion, sixty years after arriving. That is what the Turkish
settlement of Anatolia actually looked like at the start — not a replacement of
one population by another but a small armed elite governing Greeks and
Armenians and borrowing the available vocabulary of authority.
