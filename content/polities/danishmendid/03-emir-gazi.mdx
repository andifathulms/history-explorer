---
title: The insignia from Baghdad
drafted_from: peacock-yildiz-2013

Emir Gazi ruled from about 1104 to 1134 and spent thirty years holding off three
powers at once, which is not something a Turkmen principality was supposed to be
able to do.

To the west was the Sultanate of Rum, contesting the same plateau. To the north
and west was Byzantium under John II Komnenos, who campaigned in Anatolia
repeatedly in the 1130s with the serious intention of recovering it and who was
a considerably better soldier than his reputation suggests. To the south were
the crusader principalities of Antioch and Edessa, whose whole northern
frontier ran against Danishmendid country.

Emir Gazi fought all three and lost none of them decisively. He took Malatya in
1103, took Ankara and Kastamonu from the Seljuks, beat a crusading army, held
the Byzantine campaigns to sieges of individual towns, and at his death the
Danishmendids controlled more of Anatolia than Konya did.

The recognition followed. The sources report the caliph in Baghdad sending him
the insignia of a sultan — a diploma, a standard, a robe.

That transaction had been running for two centuries by then, from Baghdad
outward to whoever actually held power in the provinces: the caliph confirmed
what he could not prevent, and the recipient got a piece of paper saying that the
head of Sunni Islam regarded him as lawful. It had reached Bukhara, Ghazna and
Isfahan. Sivas is about as far west as it ever went. A Turkmen emir in central
Anatolia was worth investing, and being invested was worth having.

He is also the last Danishmendid to hold all of it at once. His son Melik Mehmed
held it too, and on Mehmed's death in 1142 the realm divided and never
reassembled. The dynasty's high point is a single long reign, and everything
after it is the same territory administered by people who were not on speaking
terms.
