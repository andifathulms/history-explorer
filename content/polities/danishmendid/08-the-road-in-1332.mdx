A hundred and fifty years after Malatya fell, a Moroccan lawyer walked through
what had been Danishmendid country and wrote down what was there.

He came up from the coast into the interior and went, in order, to Aksaray —
which he calls one of the finest districts of Rum — then Niğde, then Kayseri,
then Sivas, then Amasya, then Sonusa, then Gümüş, then Erzincan, then Erzurum.
Every one of those places is Danishmendid country. Every one of them, he says, is
subject to the king of Iraq: the Ilkhan at Tabriz.

Sivas he found large, and *now the rendezvous of the greater part of the king's
army*. The city Emir Gazi had ruled from, that a Byzantine duke in revolt had
sent to for protection, was the assembly ground of a Mongol force based eight
hundred miles away.

## The brotherhood at Erzurum

What Ibn Battuta actually found holding those towns together was not a state at
all.

In every Turkmen town, he says, there was a brotherhood of young men — the
*akhis* — and no people on earth were more courteous to strangers, more ready to
supply them with food, or more opposed to oppressors. A man of some trade, around
whom the young men of that trade and any destitute strangers collected, would be
made their president; he built a cell and put a horse and a saddle in it; in the
evening the members brought in everything they had earned that day and it was
sold to buy food and fruit for the house. A traveller arriving was taken in and
kept until he left the country.

At Erzurum, Ibn Battuta met one of these brothers who he was told was over a
hundred and thirty years old, still in possession of all his faculties and able
to walk wherever he wished. He took his blessing and went on.

The number is not usable and the institution is. When Anatolia's states failed —
first the Danishmendids, then the Seljuks, then the Ilkhanid administration
itself — what kept the towns functioning was a network of craft brotherhoods that
fed strangers and stood against the powerful, run out of a room with a horse in
it.

That outlived both sultanates. It is also the same answer, in a different key,
as the frontier epic: both were ways of organising men where there was no
government worth the name, and Anatolia spent a great deal of its history in
exactly that condition.
