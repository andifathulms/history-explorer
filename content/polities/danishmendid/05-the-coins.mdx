title: Saint George on a Muslim coin

Danishmendid coins are strange objects, and strange in a way that carries more
information than most of the chronicles about them.

They carry legends in Greek. Not Greek alongside a dominant Arabic, but Greek
as the principal language on many issues — the ruler's name and titles rendered
in Greek letters, sometimes with the Greek word for great emir, sometimes with
Byzantine imperial formulae. Some carry Arabic on the reverse and some do not.
And a number carry a figure on horseback spearing a serpent or dragon, which is
the standard Byzantine image of Saint George.

The obvious reading is that these are coins for Christians to use, and that is
part of it: the Danishmendids ruled a population that was overwhelmingly Greek
and Armenian and had used Byzantine coin for six centuries, and money that
looked unfamiliar would not have circulated.

The less obvious reading is the one the numismatists press, and it is about
authority rather than commerce. A ruler putting Byzantine formulae and
Byzantine saints on his coinage in the twelfth century is not only
accommodating his subjects, he is claiming the thing those images meant —
lawful rule over this country, in the vocabulary the country already had. The
Seljuks of Rum did the same sort of thing from the other direction, taking
Persian throne-names out of the Shahnama while marrying Byzantine princesses and
minting with Greek legends of their own.

Set that beside the two great set-piece images of this period. Manzikert and
the crusades are wars of religion in the sources on both sides. The coins are
what the same decades looked like at the level of a market in Sivas, where a
Muslim Turkish emir paid his troops in money bearing a Christian saint, and
nobody appears to have found it worth remarking on.

It is the reason objects are worth more than chronicles on a frontier. The
chronicles record what people said about each other, in front of audiences who
expected them to say it. The coins record what they handed over for bread.
