Anna Komnene calls him *the Sultan Tanisman*, which is a Greek princess's ear
rendering a Persian word — *dānishmand*, the learned one — that a Turkmen warlord
had taken as a name. She is describing the summer of 1097 and she is describing
a defeat.

The First Crusade had beaten Kilij Arslan outside Dorylaeum and was marching
south-east across the plateau. Near Herakleia, Anna says, Tanisman and Hasan met
them with an army she puts at eighty thousand — her figures are never usable and
her point is that this was the main Turkish effort — and there was a fierce
contest in which neither side would give way, until Bohemond came off the right
wing and rode straight at the sultan, *like a lion rejoicing in his strength, as
the poet says*.

The Turks broke. Anna adds, with the satisfaction of somebody who disliked
Bohemond intensely and disliked the Turks more, that afterwards the barbarian
power collapsed and the survivors scattered, leaving their wives and children
behind, not daring to face the Latins again.

## Three years later

She was wrong about the not daring, and she does not tell the story that proves
it.

In 1100 Bohemond, by then prince of Antioch, was taken prisoner by the
Danishmendids campaigning in the north, and held for three years; the crusade of
1101, raised partly to retrieve him, was destroyed in Anatolia by a Danishmendid
and Seljuk alliance — the two Turkish states cooperating, which they did rarely
and always against somebody from outside. None of that is in the *Alexiad*.
Anna's Bohemond is a figure she needs upright, as her father's great adversary,
and a Bohemond in a Turkish prison for three years is not useful to the book.

The gap is worth noticing on its own account. Within five years, the man she has
riding down the Turkish army like a lion was a captive in Niksar and the western
army sent to fetch him had been annihilated on the road — and the historian
closest to the events, writing in Greek about the Turkish frontier, leaves it
out.

## The rebel who sent for him

The other appearance the Danishmendids make in the *Alexiad* says more about
their standing than either battle.

Gregory Taronites was the Byzantine duke of Trebizond and he rebelled. He
imprisoned the outgoing governor and a number of the leading men of the city;
he wrote the emperor a letter of many pages abusing the Senate, the army and
Alexios's own relations; and when the emperor's nephew came against him with a
force, Gregory withdrew to the fortress of Colonea *in order to call Tanismanes
to his aid*.

That sentence is the measure of the thing. Twenty-five years after the frontier
dissolved, a Byzantine imperial governor in revolt against his own emperor
looked round for a patron strong enough to protect him, and the man he sent to
was the Turkmen emir at Sivas.

He did not get there in time. Alexios's men caught him on the road, knocked him
off his horse with spears, and brought him to Constantinople, where his hair and
beard were shaved to the skin and he was paraded through the Agora and shut in
the tower of Anemas. The emperor's clemency, Anna notes, was such that he was
eventually pardoned and loaded with honours.

## What the empire had lost

Anna's whole book is an argument that her father held the situation together,
and on Anatolia it is an argument she loses in the details.

The Byzantine recovery of the west coast was real and it stopped at the
plateau. What lay beyond was a country in which two Turkish dynasties fought
each other for the interior, took western princes prisoner and ransomed them,
and were solicited as allies by the empire's own provincial governors. The
Danishmendids at this point held Sivas, Niksar, Tokat, Malatya and Kayseri —
which is most of the ground between the Seljuks at Konya and the Armenian and
Georgian marches — and they had got there in a generation.
