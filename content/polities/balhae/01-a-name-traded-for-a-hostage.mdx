In the twelfth year of Silla's King Sŭngdŭk, Kŭl-gŭl Chung-sŭng, leader
of the Song-mal family among the Mal-gal tribes, led his own people
south to settle near Tă-băk Mountain, in what is now called Myo-hyang
San. There he gathered many of the surviving Goguryeo people, who had
lived without a kingdom of their own since P'yongyang fell forty-five
years before, and founded a state he named Chin. The source records it
as an ambitious undertaking from the start: five thousand li in
circumference, with two hundred thousand houses, swelled further by
remnants of the old Buyeo and Ok-jŭ peoples who joined the new kingdom
soon after its founding.

## A tribal name traded for a royal one

Kŭl-gŭl Chung-sŭng sent his own son to the Chinese court as a hostage, a
gesture of submission that brought a return gift more valuable than any
tribute: imperial recognition and the title King of Pal-hă. From that
point, the source notes, the word Mal-gal — the tribal name that had
described his own people — disappeared from Korean records altogether,
replaced by the name of the kingdom he had built. A confederation of
displaced Goguryeo subjects and a Mal-gal ruling family had become, in
Chinese and Korean eyes alike, a single recognized state.

## A neighbor Silla could not ignore

Silla's own court took the new kingdom's founding as "an event of great
significance." A state built from two peoples on the northern frontier,
holding territory Silla itself had never fully controlled after
Goguryeo's fall, changed the strategic picture the moment it appeared —
and Balhae did not wait long before making its own ambitions felt beyond
its borders.
