The source's own record of Balhae's fall is strikingly brief for so
consequential an event: by the time its year-by-year narration reaches
926, Balhae's conquest is already an accomplished fact, mentioned only
in passing as background to a separate piece of diplomacy — "the
Kŭ-ran... tribe, having overcome the Păl-ha tribe, made overtures to
Wang-gön relative to annexation." No siege, no named king, no battle is
narrated; a kingdom that had once landed troops on the Chinese mainland
disappears from the record between one year and the next.

<Figure id="bh-shangjing" />

## Friendship offered, then betrayed

What the source does preserve is the manner of Balhae's fall, recalled
years later at the court of Goryeo: the Kitan had "feigned friendship
for Pal-hă and then treacherously seized her." Whatever diplomatic
overtures passed between the two powers beforehand, they were a
prelude to conquest rather than a genuine peace, and Wang-gön's own
court remembered it that way long after Balhae itself was gone.

<Figure id="bh-brick" />

## A grudge that outlived the kingdom

That memory had teeth. In 942, when Kitan power sent Wang-gön a
friendly gift of thirty camels, he answered not with courtesy but with
punishment: the animals were tied to Man-bu bridge and starved to
death, and the men who had brought them were banished. Balhae had no
king left to avenge her own fall, but the manner of it left a mark on
the one Korean court that remained — proof, if the source's brevity
about the conquest itself left any doubt, that what happened to Balhae
in 926 was remembered as betrayal rather than as an ordinary war
between neighbors.
