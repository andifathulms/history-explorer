title: Four brothers and a governorship

The Samanids do not begin with a conquest. They begin with an administrative
decision taken in Baghdad.

In 819 the caliph al-Ma'mun, having won a civil war largely on Khurasani
strength, distributed governorships in Transoxiana to four brothers — the
grandsons of a Balkh landowner remembered as Saman Khuda. Nuh took Samarkand,
Ahmad took Farghana, Yahya took Shash, Ilyas took Herat. They were not kings.
They were appointees of an appointee, holding provinces under the Tahirid
governors of Khurasan, who in turn held Khurasan under the caliph.

What matters about this arrangement is the shape of it. The Samanids entered
history already inside a chain of delegated authority, and every subsequent
step of their rise was taken without ever formally leaving that chain. There is
no Samanid declaration of independence, no year in which they renounce Baghdad.
There is only a slow accumulation of the things independence is made of, while
the language of subordination stays in place.

That is worth sitting with, because it recurs. Nearly every polity in this
thread acquires power the same way: not by breaking a link but by quietly
becoming heavier than the thing it is attached to.

By the 860s the Tahirid grip on Khurasan was failing, and the family's
Samarkand line had absorbed the others. Nasr I held Transoxiana as a unit. The
paperwork still said governor.
