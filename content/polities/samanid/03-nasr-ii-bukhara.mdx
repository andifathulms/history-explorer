Under Nasr II, from 914 to 943, Bukhara did the thing that makes the Samanids
matter more than their territory suggests.

The court patronised poetry in New Persian and kept its chancery working in
both Persian and Arabic. Rudaki wrote there. Bal'ami produced a Persian version
of Tabari's history, which is to say that the standard historical work of
Islamic scholarship was made available to readers who did not read Arabic, at a
Samanid court, on Samanid initiative. The language that would be the literary
and administrative language of Iran, Central Asia and eventually much of
northern India for the next thousand years was assembled into a working
instrument here.

<Figure id="sm-balami" />

It is worth being exact about what this is and is not. The Samanids did not
invent a script; they wrote Persian in the Perso-Arabic alphabet they had
inherited, unchanged. So they left no descendant alphabet, which is the honest
answer and sits oddly beside the size of what they actually did. Inventing a
script and starting a literature are different achievements, and only one of
them is easy to count.

<Figure id="sm-bowl" />

The other export was religious. Bukhara sat on the frontier with the Turkish
steppe, and Samanid frontier practice combined raiding, trade and missionary
activity in a way that carried Sunni Islam north and east past the limits of
Samanid administration. The Turkish groups that adopted it did not become
Samanid subjects. Some of them, within two generations, would be the people who
ended the dynasty.
