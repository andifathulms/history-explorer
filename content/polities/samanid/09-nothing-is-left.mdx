Not very long after the dynasty ended, a Persian poet of Gurgan named
Sharif-i-Mujallidi wrote a couplet about what had survived of it. Browne quotes
it:

> From all the treasures hoarded by the Houses
> Of Sasan and of Saman, in our days
> Nothing survives except the song of Barbad,
> Nothing is left save Rudaki's sweet lays.

Two dynasties, four centuries apart, each of which governed most of Iran; and
the only thing the poet can find left of either is the music they paid for.
Barbad was the harper of Khusraw Parviz. Rudaki was the harper of Nasr II.

## The song about the road home

The story that made Rudaki famous is the one everybody in the tradition tells,
and the earliest version is Nizami-i-Arudi's.

Nasr II had gone to Herat for the summer and stayed four years. His army wanted
to go home to Bukhara and could not say so. They promised Rudaki five thousand
dinars if he could get the amir to move, and he wrote them a ballad — no
rhetoric in it at all, which later critics found so plain that Dawlatshah
records his surprise that words so simple could have such an effect, and adds
that anyone reciting such a thing at court in *his* day would have been laughed
at.

> The Ju-yi Muliyan we call to mind,
> We long for those dear friends long left behind.
> The sands of Oxus, toilsome though they be,
> Beneath my feet were soft as silk to me.
> Glad at the friends' return, the Oxus deep
> Up to our girths in laughing waves shall leap.
> Long live Bukhara! Be thou of good cheer!
> Joyous towards thee hasteth our Amir!

When Rudaki reached that verse, the account says, the amir got down off his
throne, took the horse of the sentry on duty, and rode for Bukhara without his
boots — they were carried after him for two parasangs before he stopped to put
them on, and he did not draw rein again until he was home. The army paid Rudaki
ten thousand.

## What kind of achievement this is

It is worth being exact about it, because "founded Persian literature" is the
sort of phrase that slides past.

Rudaki was not, on this evidence, a man of letters in the later sense. He was a
harper and a singer and an improviser, closer to the balladeers who still
perform at Persian entertainments than to the elaborate court poets of three
centuries afterwards — which is precisely why the tradition compares him to
Barbad, who had done the same job for a Sasanian king, and who was remembered
for being able to tell Khusraw Parviz things nobody else dared say by putting
them in a song.

So the Samanid contribution was not the invention of a literary language by
scholars. It was a court that would pay a man to sing in Persian, in front of
everybody, about wanting to go home — and would go on paying, until a
generation later a vizier was rendering Tabari into it and a poet at Tus was
beginning the *Shahnama*.

## The measure

The dynasty held Khurasan and Transoxiana for a hundred and eighty years,
governed well, ran the largest slave market in Asia, and was ended by men its
own trade had armed.

Within a lifetime of that, a poet looking for what remained of it could not find
the territory, or the army, or the treasury worth mentioning, and named a song.
He was right, and the song is still known.
