title: What Bukhara was

The identity line at the top of this page says the Samanid court made New
Persian a language of administration and literature. That is the claim this
site rests the polity's importance on, and until now no chapter supported it.

Persian had never stopped being spoken. What it had stopped being, after the
Arab conquest, was written: Middle Persian went out of official use, and for
two centuries the language of administration, scholarship and poetry across
Iran was Arabic. What happened at Bukhara in the tenth century was that a
court began commissioning serious work in the vernacular, in the Arabic script,
and paying for it.

Rudaki, who died in 941, is the figure the tradition puts first — a poet at
Nasr II's court credited with working every major form of later Persian verse
into shape, and remembered as the moment the language acquired a literature
rather than a folklore. Bal'ami, the vizier, produced a Persian version of
al-Tabari's enormous Arabic history, which put the standard account of the
Islamic past into a language a Khurasani could read without Arabic. Daqiqi
began versifying the Iranian national epic and was murdered before he got far;
the poet who finished the *Shahnama* took his thousand surviving lines into it
and presented the completed work to Mahmud of Ghazna, which is on this site's
Ghaznavid page.

Bukhara itself is worth the chapter. It was a city on an oasis fed by the
Zarafshan, with an agrarian hinterland that supported it and a caravan trade
that made it rich, and under the Samanids it acquired the apparatus of a
capital: a bureaucracy of ten specialised *divans*, a library that was famous
enough to be described by the people who used it, and a scholarly establishment
that drew men from across the east. Ibn Sina — Avicenna — grew up outside it,
was given access to the Samanid royal library as a teenager after treating a
member of the family, and said afterwards that he found books there that nobody
else had.

None of that survives as a measurement. This page's rating panel has a reach
figure and a longevity range and three influence counts, and the thing the
Samanids are actually remembered for appears in none of them, because a
literature is not an axis. It is recorded here in prose because prose is the
only instrument the site has for it.
