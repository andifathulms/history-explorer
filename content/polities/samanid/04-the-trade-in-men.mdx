---
title: The trade the whole system ran on
drafted_from: chi-4
phase: peak
---

The Samanid state's principal export was people.

Transoxiana sat on the frontier between the settled Islamic world and the
Turkish steppe, and the Samanids controlled the crossing. Turkish boys and
young men — taken in steppe warfare, sold by their own or by neighbouring
tribes, or acquired as tribute — were brought south into Samanid territory,
where the trade was regulated and taxed: a licence for each slave leaving the
realm, a duty collected at the frontier, and a market at Bukhara and Samarkand
that supplied the entire Islamic world. A tenth-century geographer describes
Turkish slaves arriving in such numbers that the price fell. It was, on the
accounts, the largest single source of the state's income.

Three things follow, and they run a long way past this dynasty.

The first is that the Samanid slave army was not an innovation. It was a
domestic application of an export business: the dynasty was selling *ghulams* to
Baghdad, to Egypt and to the west, and buying some for itself was the cheapest
option available and the one it understood best. The whole institution of the
Turkish slave soldier, which the caliph al-Mu'tasim had built into the centre of
the Abbasid state in the 830s, was fed for the following two centuries largely
from this market.

The second is that the Samanids were arming the frontier they depended on.
Turkish military skill entered the Islamic world through a market the Samanids
ran, and the political consequence arrived on schedule: a Samanid slave-general
took Ghazna in 962 and founded the Ghaznavids, and the Karakhanids who took
Bukhara in 999 were the Karluk confederation from the same steppe, converted and
organised. The two polities that ended this one both came out of the frontier
its economy was built on.

The third is a caution about the map. Two and a half million square kilometres
describes territory, and territory is not what the state was for. What it was
for was to sit astride a set of routes — silver flowing north to the Baltic in
quantities that Scandinavian coin hoards still record, slaves and furs flowing
south — and to tax what crossed. A state like that is better described by a line
than by an area, and drawing it as a shaded region on a map gets the emphasis
exactly backwards.
