---
title: The year the army chose the amir
drafted_from: chi-4
phase: contraction
---

Nasr II reigned for twenty-nine years and this site lists him as the peak
ruler. He was deposed by his own son in 943, at the demand of his own officers,
over religion.

Isma'ili missionaries had been working in Khurasan and Transoxiana for some
time — the *da'wa* described on this site's Fatimid page, operating exactly as
that chapter says it did, inside a rival caliph's territory. They reached the
Samanid court. How far Nasr went is the disputed part: the hostile tradition
says he converted, sympathetic accounts say he protected and listened to them,
and the sources are late enough and partisan enough that the distinction may
not be recoverable.

What is not disputed is what the army did. The Turkish commanders and the Sunni
religious establishment of Bukhara regarded an Isma'ili amir as intolerable,
conspired to kill him, and were forestalled by his son Nuh, who took his
father's abdication instead and then had the missionaries and their supporters
at court executed.

Two things are settled in that transaction and both are bad for the dynasty.

The first is doctrinal and it held: after 943 the Samanid state was firmly,
publicly Sunni and Hanafi, tied to an *ulama* in Bukhara that had just
demonstrated it could veto a ruler. That is the same settlement this site's
Abbasid page describes arriving a century earlier through the mihna, reached
here in one season rather than fifteen years.

The second is the one that ends the polity. In 943 the guard discovered that it
could remove an amir and install another, and be obeyed. Everything in the two
chapters that follow — the slave-general who walked away with Ghazna in 962,
the succession disputes the commanders arbitrated, the officers who simply did
not turn out in 999 — is a consequence of a lesson the army learned here.
