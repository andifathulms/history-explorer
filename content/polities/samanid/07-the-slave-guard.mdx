Samanid armies rested on Turkish military slaves, *ghulams*, bought young on
the steppe frontier, converted, trained, and promoted into command. The system
produced excellent soldiers with no local landholding, no tribal following and
no claim of their own. Their standing was entirely a function of the amir's
favour, which was precisely the point.

It worked until the favour became the only thing holding it together.

In 962 a Samanid Turkic slave-general named Alptigin, having backed the wrong
candidate in a succession dispute at Bukhara, withdrew east and took Ghazna, a
town on the Afghan side of the frontier. He did not rebel in any declarative
sense. He held Ghazna, in the ordinary way a commander held a place, and
the line he founded there outlasted the masters who had bought him.

Read that alongside the first chapter and the symmetry is exact. The Samanids
became a state by being appointees who grew heavier than their appointers. Then
their own appointee did it to them. The mechanism did not change; only the
direction of view.

Through the 970s and 980s the amirs at Bukhara lost the ability to arbitrate
between their own commanders. Real power moved to whichever general held the
capital that season. By the time the external blow came, there was not much
left inside to resist it.
