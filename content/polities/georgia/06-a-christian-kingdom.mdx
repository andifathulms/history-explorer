Look at how the Georgian kingdom was put together and then look at its
neighbours, and the thing that stands out is that it does not resemble them at
all. It resembles France.

## The nobility

The army was the levy of the *didebulni*: hereditary lords holding defined
territories, owing military service by custom rather than by grant, supplying
contingents from their own lands and able to decline. A king in that system does
not create his nobility, cannot dismiss it, and negotiates with it annually.

That is the ordinary arrangement in Christian Europe and in the Christian
Caucasus — Armenia had it under the same name in a different language — and it
is not the ordinary arrangement anywhere else nearby. The Islamic world around
Georgia solved the same problem differently, with slave soldiers who had no kin
in the country, with tribal levies, and with the *iqta*, a revenue assignment
that could be moved or revoked. A Seljuk sultan could take a man's income away.
A Georgian king could not.

David IV's Kipchak settlement is the exception that shows the rule: he had to
import forty thousand families from the far side of the mountains to get an army
that did not belong to somebody else.

## The succession

Primogeniture, with the heir crowned as co-ruler in the reigning king's lifetime
so the question could not be reopened at the funeral. George III did exactly that
for Tamar in 1178 and it worked.

Again: this is a European habit, and the nearest comparison anywhere in the
region is Ptolemaic Egypt fourteen centuries earlier. The states around Georgia
fought a succession war almost every generation, and a good number of them were
destroyed by one.

## The church

And then there is the institution that has no counterpart in the neighbourhood
at all.

The Georgian church was autocephalous — self-governing, answering to no foreign
patriarch, which Vakhtang Gorgasali had obtained or asserted in the fifth
century. It held vast property. It ran the law across large areas of life. It
supplied the kingdom's literacy, a good part of its administration and all of
its ideology. And it maintained foundations well outside the country: Iviron on
Mount Athos, the monastery of the Holy Cross at Jerusalem, Petritsoni in
Bulgaria, and the academy at Gelati that the chronicles call a second Athens.

Nothing in the Islamic states around Georgia does that job. There are ulama and
there are endowments and there are qadis, and none of them is a corporation
owning a third of the country, running a legal system, and maintaining branch
houses in three foreign kingdoms.

## Which is why the country is still there

Put the three together and the answer to a standing question falls out. Georgia
was conquered, garrisoned, taxed and partitioned by Seljuks, Mongols, Timurids,
Ottomans, Safavids and finally Russians, and it kept its language, its alphabet,
its church and its aristocracy through all of it.

That was not because the kings were formidable; mostly they were not. It is
because everything that mattered in Georgia was held by somebody hereditary — a
noble house in its own valley, a bishop in his own see, a monastery with its own
land in another country — and an empire that takes the crown has taken the one
part of the machine that was portable.
