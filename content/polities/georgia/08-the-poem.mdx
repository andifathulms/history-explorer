The kingdom came apart in 1490 and what held the country together for the next
four hundred years was not an institution. It was a poem, and the strangest
thing about it is that Georgia is not in it.

## What is known about the author

Very little. Oliver Wardrop, travelling in Georgia in the 1880s and setting down
what Georgians told him, gives the whole of it: Shota Rustaveli was born in the
village of Rustavi near Akhaltsikhe, was educated at Athens, came home and wrote
his great work, was secretary to Queen Tamar, then became a monk, and died at
the monastery of the Holy Cross near Jerusalem, where his portrait could still
be seen. Tradition adds that he was in love with his royal mistress, and
Wardrop notes that a good many passages in the poem seem to bear it out.

*Vepkhistqaosani* — the Man in the Panther's Skin — is a romance. An old king of
Arabia who has given his throne to his daughter Tinatin goes hunting and sees a
weeping youth of extraordinary beauty dressed in a panther's skin; the stranger
kills several of the guards sent to seize him and vanishes; the king falls into
a depression so profound that Tinatin promises her hand to whoever can explain
it. Avtandil wanders for three years and finds the man, who is Tariel, a knight
of India in love with the king's daughter there.

Arabia and India. Not one of the characters is Georgian, and, as Wardrop puts
it, they are all Georgians to the very finger tips. Tinatin is Tamar, and
everybody has always known it.

## What was done with it

This is the part that matters.

Through what Wardrop calls nearly seven centuries of ceaseless struggle, the
Georgians kept the poem fresh in their minds. Its verses were household words in
cottage and hall. A good many Georgians, and especially the women, knew the
whole of it by heart — and there was a time, he reports, when no woman was
allowed to marry unless she could recite the entire poem.

Consider what that means as a mechanism. A country with no state, partitioned by
treaty between two Muslim empires, whose kings converted and reconverted to keep
their thrones and whose people were being sold as soldiers from Isfahan to
Cairo, made the memorisation of six thousand lines of verse a condition of
marriage. Every household contained the text. It could not be confiscated,
burned, or subordinated to a foreign synod, because it was not written down
anywhere that mattered.

## What a national poem is for

The obvious comparison is the *Shahnama*, which did the same job for Persian
under Arab and then Turkic rule, and the comparison is exact in one respect and
inverted in another.

Firdawsi wrote about Iran, at enormous length, naming it. Rustaveli wrote about
Arabs and Indians and mentioned Georgia not at all, and the poem is more
completely Georgian for it — the manners are Georgian, the code of loyalty and
friendship is Georgian, the queen is Georgian, and every reader for eight
hundred years has known it without being told.

A country that cannot say a thing in public says it in a story about somewhere
else. That is not a defeat. It is the reason the language survived the loss of
the state by four centuries, and had something to say when it got one back.
