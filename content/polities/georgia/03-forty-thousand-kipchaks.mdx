The Georgian chronicle's account of David the Builder is not a list of battles.
It is a list of instruments, and the instruments are what made the century that
followed possible.

He began with the church, and the chronicle is savage about the state he found
it in: the leaders blind and avaricious, and robbers entering the churches
through the walls and windows rather than through the doors, like shepherds
would; bishops ordaining priests for bribes as they had themselves been ordained,
appointing stupid and greedy suffragans and planting lawlessness in the house of
God. David summoned the catholicos, the bishops and the priests, ordered an
election held between the good and the bad, and had the worthless expelled — the
chronicler's image is of the inedible and loathsome being picked out of a net.
The comparison offered is to Constantine. The practical effect was that the one
institution present in every valley of the kingdom answered to the king.

He also stopped paying taxes to the Turks, which the chronicle dates to the
fourth year of the reign and connects, correctly, to the death of Malik-Shah.

Then the army. His wife Gurandukht was the daughter of the Kipchak chief Atrak;
with Kipchak help he subdued the Ossetian kings, took hostages from them, made
peace between Ossetians and Kipchaks, and took the Darial pass and every other
gate through the Caucasus. Having secured the road, he brought forty thousand
Kipchaks with their families onto Georgian land, armed them, appointed
*spasalars* over them, and used them to hold Iran and Turkestan off. He also
kept five hundred Kipchak children at his court and raised them as Christians,
and the chronicle notes others being baptised day by day.

That is a standing army of nomad cavalry, resident, paid, converted and attached
to the crown rather than to any Georgian lordship — which is the thing every
polity in this region spent centuries failing to build, and it was assembled out
of a marriage and a mountain pass.

What he did with it reads, in the chronicle, as a calendar rather than a
campaign. Ten thousand Turks raiding in Trialeti, beaten in a single day by a
king who came up overnight with few troops. Tens of thousands more destroyed in
Tayk. Kapa taken on the thirteenth of February, in the middle of the fast;
Layzan raided on the fifth of May; the winter spent putting Abkhazia in order;
March at Khunan. The point of the dates is that the Turkmen moved with the
seasons — up into the Armenian mountains in summer, down to the warm meadows by
the Kura in winter — and David had worked out that a king with a standing force
could meet them at both ends of the year.

The city came last and came without a siege. A letter reached him from the
leading men of Ani: come, and we will place the city in your hands. He went with
sixty thousand cavalry and had it in three days, then turned and took Shamakhi
and the country round it.

The chronicle ends the reign on two scenes rather than a conquest. David
summoned the Georgian catholicos, a translator, and the bishops and *vardapets*
of Armenia, and had them argue doctrine from morning until evening; when it was
clear that neither side would accept the other he told them that he was unskilled
in such deep words and they were unable to find a way out of them, calmed both
sides in plain language, and dissolved the assembly. And he went to confess to an
elderly Armenian teacher from Haghpat, and sat with his arm round the old man's
neck, and the old man asked to be let go because he was broken and reeked in his
old age — and the king kissed him and said, let this fragrance never cease from
you, reverend father.

A king who could not make the two churches agree and embraced one of them anyway
had understood the country he was assembling better than the theologians had.
