George III had one child and crowned her co-ruler in his own lifetime, in 1178,
precisely so that the question could not be reopened when he died. It worked.
Tamar ruled from 1184 to 1213, and Georgian tradition calls her *mepe* — king,
not queen — which is a fact about the grammar of the office rather than about her.

<Figure id="tamar" />

The kingdom under her is the largest and strongest Christian state between
Byzantium and India. It held the isthmus from the Black Sea to the Caspian
watershed; the Shirvanshahs were tributaries; Armenian principalities were held
by the Zakarids as Georgian vassals; the Muslim emirates of the eastern Caucasus
paid. In 1199 Georgian forces took Ani from the Shaddadids, and the Armenian
highland was administered from Tbilisi.

<Figure id="vardzia" />

Two things she did outlived the kingdom. In 1204, when the Fourth Crusade took
Constantinople, Georgian troops she lent to her Komnenos relatives put them into
Trebizond, which is the entire reason there was a Greek empire on the Black Sea
coast for the next two and a half centuries. And the court she kept produced
Shota Rustaveli's *The Knight in the Panther's Skin*, dedicated to her, which is
the Georgian national poem in the sense that the *Shahnama* is the Persian one:
a work everyone can quote and which fixed the language.

<Figure id="trebizond" />

A court that produces the national poem is usually a court in decline or a
court that never mattered much — the Muzaffarids and Hafez, the Kara Koyunlu and
Haqiqi. Georgia is the other case, where the political weight and the cultural
output peak in the same reign, and that is much rarer than it ought to be.

The other permanent instrument was the church and the monasteries, and they went
a long way outside the kingdom: Iviron on Mount Athos, the monastery of the Holy
Cross at Jerusalem, Petritsoni in Bulgaria, and the academy at Gelati that David
IV had founded, which the chronicles call a second Athens. A small kingdom with
foundations in three foreign countries is buying something, and what it was
buying was standing in a Christian world whose centre was somewhere else.
