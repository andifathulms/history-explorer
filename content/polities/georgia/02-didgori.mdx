title: The army the nobles could not withhold
drafted_from: suny-1994

David IV came to the throne in 1089 at about sixteen, over a kingdom that had
been ruined. The Seljuk incursions of the 1070s and 1080s — what Georgian
sources call the great Turkish troubles — had emptied the eastern lowlands,
turned agricultural country into seasonal pasture for Turkmen herds, and reduced
the monarchy to paying tribute for what it still held.

His answer took thirty years and it was structural rather than tactical.

He broke the great nobles' monopoly on force. The Georgian army had been the
levy of the *didebulni*, hereditary lords who supplied contingents and could
decline to; David built alongside it a royal force that was paid, permanently
under arms and answerable to him — and he manned a substantial part of it by
bringing Kipchak families over the Caucasus from the northern steppe and
settling them with land in Georgian territory in return for service. The figure
the sources give is forty thousand families. He converted them, married a Kipchak
princess, and had an army that did not need the nobles' consent.

This site codes the polity `client-levy` and `standing-professional` together
for that reason, and it is a rare pair. Almost every polity in this corpus that
solved the same problem solved it by purchase — the slave-soldier records run
from Samarra to Cairo to Bukhara. David solved it by immigration, which is a
different instrument with the same purpose, and the corpus should register the
difference.

In August 1121, at Didgori in the hills west of Tbilisi, he met a coalition
assembled against him — Seljuk, and the sources' figures for its size are not
usable — and destroyed it. The following year he took Tbilisi.

What he did with Tbilisi is the part worth keeping. He kept the Muslim
population, guaranteed the mosque, left the qadi's jurisdiction in place, and is
reported to have set the Muslims' taxes below the Christians'. He moved his
capital into the city and it has been the capital ever since. A Christian king
who has just taken a Muslim city after four centuries and governs it by
conciliating its inhabitants is not the ordinary twelfth-century arrangement in
either direction, and it is the reason the kingdom that followed could hold
Muslim vassals without a permanent war.
