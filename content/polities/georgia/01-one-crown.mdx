Georgia before the eleventh century is a set of principalities rather than a
country: Abkhazia on the Black Sea coast, Kartli in the centre with Tbilisi in
it, Kakheti and Hereti in the east, Tao-Klarjeti in the south-west where a
branch of the Bagrationi had built up a principality under nominal Byzantine
protection. Christianity had arrived in the fourth century — the conversion of
Kartli under Mirian III is dated to the 330s, a generation after Armenia's — and
the Georgian church and alphabet are older than any of these polities.

In 1008 Bagrat III inherited both Abkhazia, through his mother, and the
Bagrationi lands of Tao-Klarjeti, through his father, and put them together. He
added Kakheti and Hereti afterwards. That is the founding: not a conquest but a
double inheritance, consolidated by a man who then spent his reign making it
stick against the noble houses.

What he could not take was Tbilisi. The city had been an Arab emirate since 736,
the seat of a Muslim amir in the middle of the Georgian lands, and it stayed one
for another hundred and fourteen years. The kingdom's capital was Kutaisi in the
west, and the country was a Christian monarchy with a Muslim city at its centre
that it could not reduce.

The dynasty's claim is worth stating at the start, because it is unusual and it
was made for eight hundred years. The Bagrationi asserted descent from David,
king of Israel; the sling and the harp on their arms are that claim rendered in
heraldry, and the Georgian chronicles traced the line in some detail. The
Armenian Bagratuni made the same claim through the same tradition, and the two
houses are generally taken to be branches of one family.

Nobody outside the tradition believes the genealogy, and that is a different
question from whether it mattered. A house that tells everyone for eight
centuries that it descends from a king in the Book of Samuel is telling them
something about where its right to rule comes from, and in a Christian kingdom
surrounded by Muslim powers it is not a random thing to have chosen.
