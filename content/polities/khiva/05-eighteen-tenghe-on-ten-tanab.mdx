The most useful pages Vámbéry wrote are the driest: a working account of how the
khanate raised its money, which is also an account of who it could not tax and
therefore who really ran it.

## Salgit

The land tax. For every ten *tanab* of cultivable ground — a tanab being sixty
square ells — the khan received eighteen tenghe, about ten shillings.

Exempt from it were three classes: the warriors, the *noker* or *atli*; the ulema;
and the *khoja*, the descendants of the Prophet.

Read that list again. The men with the weapons, the men with the religious
authority and the men with the hereditary prestige all paid nothing. Everybody else
— the Sart farmers of the delta, the irrigators, the townsmen — paid for the state.
No Khivan khan could ever tax the only three groups capable of removing him, which
is a complete explanation of why the khanship went the way it did.

The kushbegi and the mehter collected it, making a circuit every year through the
main districts and holding the local elders responsible for their departments.

## Zekiat

The customs. Imported goods paid two and a half per cent of their value. Cattle,
camels and horses paid a tenghe a head and sheep half a tenghe, annually — and only
from owners of more than ten, which is what constituted a herd.

The collection was in the hands of a favourite *mehrem* of the khan, who went round
the nomad tribes with a secretary. Since the cattle could not be counted, he simply
taxed each tribe at a rate agreed with its elders.

Vámbéry then delivers the punchline without raising his voice. The principal profit
of this operation found its way into the mehter's sack; the khan had been told the
previous year that the Karakalpak had only six thousand oxen and the Yomut and
Chaudor only three thousand sheep between them; and this, Vámbéry was told, was
about a third of the truth.

## What the numbers describe

A state collecting a fixed tax from a settled population it controlled, and a
negotiated tribute from a nomad population it did not, with two-thirds of the second
disappearing en route.

The settled delta paid and had no weapons. The Turkmen tribes had the weapons and
paid what they felt like. The khan needed the Turkmen for cavalry and could not
coerce them, and the two groups were not the same people and did not like each
other.

That balance is the whole domestic politics of Khiva, and it is why the khanate
could put a hundred horsemen in the field with sacks of heads and could not have
resisted a European brigade for a week. What kept it alive was never its strength.
It was the desert.
