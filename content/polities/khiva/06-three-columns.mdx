In the summer of 1873 the Russians did the thing everybody had said could not be
done, and the khanate ended in a season.

## What had protected it

Peter the Great's expedition under Bekovich-Cherkassky had reached Khiva in 1717 and
been destroyed there. A second attempt, in the winter of 1839, never arrived: the
weather broke the column and it turned back short of the oasis. For a hundred and
fifty years the desert had been a better defence than any army, and the khans had
correspondingly little reason to be careful.

## What changed

Steam, the Aral flotilla, the forts up the Syr Darya, the conquest of Tashkent and
the Bukharan settlement of 1868. By 1873 Russia held the whole northern and eastern
approach and could supply columns from bases instead of from the saddle.

Three of them converged on the delta at once — from Tashkent, from Orenburg, from
the Caspian — and the arithmetic that had protected Khiva since Ilbars stopped
working. The city was taken. The khan kept his throne.

He ceded everything on the right bank of the Oxus, paid an indemnity, opened the
khanate to Russian merchants free of duty, accepted that his foreign relations
would be conducted for him — and abolished slavery, which was the institution the
campaign had been publicly justified by and the khanate's oldest industry.

## Schuyler

Eugene Schuyler was in Turkistan that year, and his account is the reason this
record can be more than a Russian communiqué.

He was an American consul with no stake in the enterprise, and he wrote about how
the campaign was actually conducted — the treatment of the Turkmen, the conduct of
the troops — in terms that were not welcome in St Petersburg and cost him
professionally. A government that has just liberated slaves does not enjoy being
told what its soldiers did on the way.

## The shape of the ending

Set the three khanates side by side and the pattern is exact.

Bukhara lost Samarkand in 1868, signed, and kept its emir until 1920. Khiva lost its
capital in 1873, signed, and kept its khan until 1920. Kokand's khan fled his own
rebels in 1875, and Kokand was annexed outright, wiped off the map and renamed
Ferghana.

The ones that surrendered survived as protectorates. The one that collapsed into
revolt was absorbed. Whether a Central Asian dynasty lasted another half-century
turned not on how hard it fought but on whether it could still control its own
subjects when the Russians arrived.

Khiva could. It had spent three centuries unreachable behind a desert, and it ended
by being the more governable of the two survivors — a khanate of one irrigated
delta, which had lived on a river, a tax on ten tanab of ground, and the people it
caught on the roads.
