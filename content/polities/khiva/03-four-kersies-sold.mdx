An English merchant looking for a trade route to China rode through
Khwarazm in 1558 and found a country that could barely support the
caravan that was trying to sell it cloth. What he saw, and what the
Uzbek chronicles record about the khan he met, are the same story told
from opposite sides of the same market.

## What Jenkinson found

Anthony Jenkinson reached Urgench two days after leaving Sellizure and
paid the customary dues to a khan's brother; the town, he was told, had
been won and lost four times by civil war in seven years, and there
were so few merchants left, and those so poor, that he sold only four
kerseys of cloth in the whole city. Further down the Oxus, at Kait, a
local prince's escort fed itself heartily at his expense for three days
and then demanded an exorbitant payment to go any further; when it was
refused they abandoned the caravan outright, and a banished prince with
a handful of followers attacked it soon after, driven off only by the
guns Jenkinson had brought with him. He describes five brothers,
sons of different mothers - some of slaves - who spent as much energy
destroying each other as ruling the desert route between the delta and
Persia, and a country whose caravans crossed a landscape of feuding
appanage-holders rather than a single administration.

## Hajim Khan

The man whose family Jenkinson was navigating around was Hajim
Muhammad, proclaimed khan at thirty-nine after the extinction of
Bujugha's whole line in a single day of killing. Hajim had already made
his name years earlier persuading Din Muhammad to drive the Bukharans
out of Khwarazm and then personally negotiating the prisoner exchange
that followed; as khan himself he spent decades holding the family's
fractured inheritance together against Bukhara's own repeated
invasions - one of which, under Abdullah Khan II, turned back twice
rather than fight Hajim's relief columns in the field. Jenkinson left
Urgench carrying four of Hajim's own envoys back to Moscow, and in 1595
a further embassy went from Khwarazm to the Tsar seeking the same
friendship on Hajim's behalf.

## What Arab Muhammad inherited

Hajim's son Arab Muhammad succeeded him and opened his own reign the
way the family always had: at war. Within months a hundred Cossack
raiders sacked Urgench itself, killing over a thousand people and
loading a thousand wagons with plunder before Arab Muhammad cut off
their retreat, besieged their improvised riverside fort, and destroyed
what was left of them. A Kalmyk raid followed within the year, then a
palace conspiracy to replace him with a rival prince of Ilbars's own
line, crushed before it could act. It was the same pattern the khanate
had run on since 1511 - a khan's authority proven not by how peacefully
he ruled but by how many attempts to unseat him he survived - and it was
the reign into which Arab Muhammad's own sons, Habash and Ilbars, were
born: the two boys whose 1618 revolt against their father is where this
khanate's own chronicle, written by their younger brother, begins.
