In the summer of 1863 a lame Hungarian in rags, travelling as a dervish returning
from Mecca, was brought before the khan of Khiva and asked to bless him. What he
saw in the days that followed is the most specific account there is of how this
khanate rewarded its soldiers.

## The audience

Vámbéry knew the risk exactly. The khan was in the habit of enslaving strangers of
doubtful character — he had recently done it to a Hindustani who claimed princely
birth and was now dragging artillery carriages — and the khan's cruelty, Vámbéry
notes, was displeasing even to the Tatars.

At the gate the pious handed bread and dried fruit up to them on their camels. In
the bazaar people kissed his hands and feet and the rags hanging off him. At the
customs house an Afghan in the caravan announced loudly that they had brought to
Khiva three interesting quadrupeds and a no less interesting biped, and the words
*spy*, *Frenghi* and *Russian* went round in the whispering.

He got through it by presenting himself to a former Khivan ambassador to
Constantinople who could not afford to admit he did not recognise him, and by
producing an Ottoman passport with the sultan's seal at the top, which the minister
kissed and pressed to his forehead.

The khan was on a dais with his left arm on a velvet cushion and a short golden
sceptre in his right hand. Vámbéry recited a sura, two blessings and a prayer and
everybody stroked their beards. He was offered twenty ducats and a stout ass;
declined the money as sinful for a dervish, took the ass, and pointed out that the
holy commandment prescribed a white one.

Afterwards, alone in his cell, he wrote what he had actually seen: deep-set eyes, a
chin thinly covered with hair, white lips, a trembling voice — the real picture of
an enervated and savage tyrant — and let out a long breath at how well it had gone.

## The coats

While collecting his board money from the treasurer, he found that official sorting
robes of honour to be sent to the army in the field.

There were about four kinds, silk, in staring colours with large flowers worked in
gold. He heard them called four-headed, twelve-headed, twenty-headed and
forty-headed coats. There were no heads on them, in paint or embroidery, so he
asked why.

The plainest coat was the reward for having cut off four enemy heads. The finest was
for forty.

Somebody suggested that if this was not the custom in the land of Rum, he should go
to the main square next morning and watch the distribution.

## The square

He went, and about a hundred horsemen came in from the camp covered in dust.

Each had at least one prisoner with him — among them children and women — bound
either to his horse's tail or to the pommel of his saddle. Each also had a large
sack buckled behind him containing the heads of his enemies, the evidence of his
exploits.

He handed over the prisoners as presents to the khan or some other great man. Then
he unfastened his sack, took it by the two lower corners *as if he were about to
empty potatoes*, and rolled the heads out in front of the accountant, who kicked
them together into a heap with his feet until it was several hundred high. Each man
was given a receipt for the number delivered, and a few days later came the day of
payment.

## What it was for

This was not a pogrom or a massacre. It was a payroll.

The heads were counted by an accountant, recorded on receipts, converted into a
grade of coat and paid out on a scheduled day. The prisoners were transferred as
gifts within the elite. The system was routine enough that a treasurer sorting
garments could explain the price list to a stranger without embarrassment, and that
a bystander could recommend the ceremony as a sight worth seeing.

Vámbéry's own next sentence is the one that has troubled every reader since: that
in spite of these barbarous usages and these startling scenes, it was in Khiva and
its provinces that he passed the most agreeable days of his whole journey, pelted
with gifts by strangers who wanted nothing from him but his blessing.

Both things were true at once, in the same city, in the same week. That is the
hardest thing about the record this khanate left, and the reason for setting it
down exactly as the witness did.
