Ilbars founded a state by massacre and his own grandsons nearly ended
it the same way. For the khanate's first half-century the throne of
Khwarazm changed hands almost as often through a cousin's ambush as
through a natural death.

## What Ilbars actually did

Invited into the delta by conspirators against its Persian governor,
Ilbars took the town of Vezir in a night massacre that left one Persian
alive to carry the news to Urgench, then took Urgench itself and put its
defenders to the sword. He sent for reinforcements from the Kipchak
steppe rather than reward the men who had fought for him - advice one
old Uyghur counsellor warned him against and was overruled on - and by
the time he died, Ilbars and his crippled brother Balbars, who fought
from a chariot and was reckoned a formidable archer despite it, had
pushed the khanate's raiding range deep into Khorasan.

## Three thrones in four years

What followed shows exactly what an appanage state without a strong
khan looked like in practice. Ilbars's nephew Sultan Haji held the
title for a year while his cousin held the real power and the first
mouthful at every meal, then died. Hassan Kuli succeeded him and was
besieged at Urgench for four months by his own jealous kinsmen - a
siege bad enough that a donkey's head, unclean food by any standard,
sold for fifty tengas - and was executed as blood price when the city
finally fell. Sofian Khan, who took the throne next, found a steadier
footing: he collected tribute from the Turkoman tribes by force where
necessary, and when Shah Tahmasp of Persia wanted an alliance against
the Ottomans badly enough to ask for a bride of Chinggisid blood, sent
his own niece to Qazvin in exchange for nine of everything precious a
Safavid court could send back.

## The trick with the branches

The most memorable of Sofian's own successors was Din Muhammad, son of
a khan who had disliked him from birth for the accident of his slave
mother. Captured as a young raider and sent home humiliated in chains
by a rival prince, Din Muhammad grew into the family's most audacious
survivor. When Shah Tahmasp's army captured one of his border towns, he
rode into the Shah's own camp with fifty men, kissed his host's robe,
and asked forgiveness - and the Shah, checking whether the man's heart
was beating faster than normal and finding it was not, pardoned him on
the spot and gave the town back. When Bukhara's Ubaydullah Khan marched
thirty thousand men against an ally of his, Din Muhammad had each
soldier tie branches to his saddle and horse's tail and advance in loose
order across open ground, so that the moving cloud of dust and foliage
looked like an army many times its real size; the Bukharans retreated
without a fight, and Din Muhammad took Merv for himself and reigned
there until his death.

## What sixty years of this bought

Nothing settled, and everything survived. By the time Din Muhammad's
own descendants were fighting Hajim Muhammad's sons over an illegitimate
succession at Merv - the next khan's whole story - the delta khanate
had outlived four assassinated or executed rulers, a dozen appanage
wars among Ilbars's own grandchildren, and every attempt by Bukhara to
absorb it, on the strength of the same desert that had let Ilbars take
it from Persia in the first place: nobody could hold what they couldn't
comfortably reach.
