Nearly everything known about Khwarazm before the eighteenth century comes from a
book written by one of its khans about his own family, and his family behaved
appallingly.

## What he recorded

Abu'l-Ghazi Bahadur Khan was a son of Arab Muhammad Khan, and the story he tells
about his father is not flattering to anybody in it.

Around 1618 two of his brothers — Habash, aged sixteen, and Ilbars, fourteen — rose
in revolt from Khiva and marched on Urgench. Summoned to their father and offered
a governorship, they refused to come until they had gathered a force, and the
adventurous young Uzbeks came in to them. Their father was too weak or too indulgent
to stop it. They raided Khorasan, sent him presents out of the proceeds, and came
back and camped outside his capital.

The khan sent an officer to look at them. The officer came back and reported that
every Uzbek from Darughan Ata to Bakirghan Ata had joined them and that he had been
received with shouts of defiance, and advised retreat to Khiva.

*The Khan was a weak person* — Abu'l-Ghazi's own phrase about his father, and, as
Howorth notes, a fact the historian cannot hide. He did not send to find out whether
the report was exaggerated. He left for Khiva at once.

## The grain

The detail that shows what kind of historian Abu'l-Ghazi was is what comes next.

The whole district from Mizdakhkan to Kuighun, he says, was one large field of
wheat — because the year before he was born his father had cut a canal running by
Tuk and Kuighun into the Aral Sea, closed when the sun was in Libra and opened again
after harvest, and years later more than a bowshot across. In consequence wheat was
very cheap.

The rebels seized the state granaries and won the poor over by distributing the
grain.

That is a man explaining a political collapse by way of hydraulic engineering and
the price of wheat, in the 1660s.

## The end of the family

His father ceded the town of Vezir to the rebels and they lived there quietly for
five years. In the sixth, Ilbars seized Khiva, sent five hundred men to take his
father prisoner on the road home, scattered his treasures — *among the dogs and the
birds* — and deposed his officers.

Abu'l-Ghazi and another brother sided with their father, and argued with him, in
vain, that this was the moment to crush the rebels. He vacillated and leaned on an
atalik who was secretly with the other side. Five months later he changed his mind
and marched on his sons in open daylight instead of surprising them as Abu'l-Ghazi
advised, and was beaten at the canal of Tashli Yarmish and taken.

Habash had him blinded. He was sent to Khiva and then to Qum with three wives and
two younger sons. The next year Ilbars had him put to death, with a brother and two
nephews. Another brother was sent off to Russia, where he died in 1648 and was
buried at Kasimov in a hospice built by his widow — the daughter of a Kasimov khan,
in a Tatar appanage inside the Russian state.

Abu'l-Ghazi went to Bukhara, and then into a long exile in Persia, and came back
twenty years later to take the throne.

## Why the book matters

He had every reason to write a lie and did not write one.

He records that his father was weak, that his brothers were fourteen and sixteen
when they began, that the Uzbeks deserted the khan, that the rebels were more
popular because they gave away the grain, and that the whole thing ended with a
blinded old man dying in Qum. He also records the canal, the dates, the tribal
divisions, the routes and the genealogies, which is why every later account of
Khwarazm — including the one in front of you — is downstream of him.

The rulers of the other two Uzbek khanates left no comparable book. That is the one
respect in which Khiva, the smallest and poorest of the three, is the best
documented.
