Husain Nizam Shah succeeded his father Burhan I in 1553, still only
thirteen, and inherited a kingdom locked in the same running contest
with Bijapur that had occupied his father's own forty-seven-year
reign - sieges and counter-sieges over the frontier forts of Sholapur
and Kulliani, alliances made and broken with the Vijayanagara regent
Ramraj as the balance of power required. Husain lost six hundred
pieces of cannon in one reverse and was besieged in his own capital in
turn, before a chance flood on the Sena river drowned twenty-five
thousand of the besieging army in a single night and forced a peace.

## A confederacy against a common enemy

By 1565 the pattern of shifting alliances had exhausted itself, and
the Deccan's Muslim kings - Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Bidar and Golconda -
united instead against the power all of them had by turns allied with
and fought: Ramraj himself, whose own dominance over the Deccan's
politics had become intolerable to every one of his former partners at
once. Ramraj met the combined armies with a force Firishta puts at
seventy thousand cavalry and ninety thousand infantry, so confident of
victory that he ordered his officers to bring him Bijapur's and
Golconda's kings alive, to be kept in iron cages, and Husain Nizam
Shah's head specifically.

## An elephant's trunk, and an empire undone

The battle turned on artillery: Husain Nizam Shah's own front line,
six hundred guns deep and screened by foreign archers, broke the first
Hindu charge with such slaughter that the field before the cannon was
left "dead close to the muzzles of the guns." When the allied cavalry
exploited the confusion and drove into the center of Ramraj's own
line, the aged Hindu commander was overtaken on foot by one of Husain
Nizam Shah's own war elephants, which seized him in its trunk and
carried him to the Nizam Shahy king - who ordered him beheaded on the
spot. Ramraj's army broke and fled toward Vijayanagara itself, and the
kingdom that had dominated the southern Deccan for two centuries fell
to the four allied armies in the campaign that followed. Husain Nizam
Shah did not long outlive his own victory: he returned to Ahmadnagar
and died there not long after, "of a disorder brought on by excess" -
a court poet marking his death with a single line: "the sun of the
Deccan has become obscured."
