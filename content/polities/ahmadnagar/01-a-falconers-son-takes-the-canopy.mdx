Ahmad Nizam Shah's own father began life as Timapa, a Brahmin boy from
Vijayanagara taken captive as a child by a Bahmani army and raised
among the sultanate's royal slaves under the name Hasan. His talent
won him a place at court, first as a companion to a Bahmani prince and
later, under the nickname Bheiry — a mispronunciation of his own
father's name that stuck as a title — as a provincial governor and
eventually chief minister to the sultanate itself, styled Nizam-ul-Mulk.
His son Ahmad inherited both his father's ambition and his enemies.

## A father's murder answered with a night attack

While Ahmad was campaigning to expand his own family's estates around
Junnar, word reached him that his father had been assassinated at the
Bahmani court at Bidar. He broke off his campaign, returned home, and
took up his father's own titles rather than submit quietly to whatever
faction now controlled the capital. When the Bahmani government sent an
army against him under Jahangir Khan, Ahmad struck first: a night
attack at dawn on 28 May 1490 routed the royal camp entirely, killing
several of its senior officers and scattering the rest.

## A capital built on a battlefield

On the ground where he won that battle, Ahmad built a palace and
garden his successors would later fortify and expand — the beginning of
the city of Ahmadnagar itself. He soon stopped having the Bahmani
sultan's name read in the Friday public prayers and substituted his
own, though when some of his own officers objected to so open a claim
to sovereignty, he backed off just far enough to save appearances,
insisting his royal canopy merely shielded him from the sun. His own
officers pressed him to reverse course again shortly after, and this
time he let his name stand. From Junnar, and then from the new city
bearing his name, Ahmad Nizam Shah spent the rest of his reign
extending his territory into Daulatabad and the Konkan coast,
building an independent kingdom out of the province his own father had
once governed for someone else.
