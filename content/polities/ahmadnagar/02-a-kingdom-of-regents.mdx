Ahmad Nizam Shah completed the conquest of Daulatabad before his death,
persuading its garrison to abandon a Bahmani-appointed governor still
loyal, in name, to Gujarat's king, and folded the fortress permanently
into his own territory. He reduced hill forts across the southern
Khandesh borderlands and compelled the rajas of Galna and Baglana to
pay him tribute, leaving his son Burhan a kingdom considerably larger
than the one he had carved out of his own rebellion.

## A boy-king and the pattern that followed

Burhan Nizam Shah I came to the throne still a child, in his seventh
year, and government passed to a regent minister — a pattern that would
recur throughout the dynasty's history far more often than direct rule
by an adult king in full command of his own court. Ahmadnagar fought
recurring wars with its neighbors Berar and Khandesh over these
decades, winning some engagements decisively and losing territory in
others, but the kingdom's own internal weakness — a young or absent
king, a regent managing rival factions of nobles — proved as
persistent a feature of Nizam Shahy rule as any external threat.

## A princess who became a queen twice over

Into this pattern married Chand Bibi, a Nizam Shahy princess given in
marriage to Ali Adil Shah of Bijapur to seal an alliance between the
two houses. Widowed and without her own children to anchor her at
Bijapur's court, she eventually returned to Ahmadnagar, her own
homeland, at a moment when the kingdom's need for a steady hand at the
center had rarely been greater. What she found waiting for her there
was not a peaceful regency but a kingdom already contested by rival
claimants, a hostile Mughal empire pressing from the north, and, in
time, a test of her own authority that no earlier Nizam Shahy regent
had faced.
