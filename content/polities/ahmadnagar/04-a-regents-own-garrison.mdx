Chand Bibi's regency for the boy-king Bahadur Nizam Shah unfolded
against a Mughal empire steadily tightening its pressure on the Deccan.
She secured help from her own nephew, Ibrahim Adil Shah of Bijapur,
weathered an attempted coup by her own appointed minister, Muhammad
Khan, whose garrison ultimately turned him over to her for punishment
once his secret dealings with the Mughal commander came to light, and
organized a coalition army of sixty thousand men drawn from Ahmadnagar,
Bijapur and Golconda to contest Mughal advances into Berar. That
coalition was defeated at Sonpat, and Mughal forces continued to press
closer to the capital itself.

## An emperor arrives in person

In the beginning of 1599, the Mughal emperor Akbar himself marched
south, unwilling to leave the Deccan campaign to his officers alone. He
laid siege to the fortress of Asirgarh while detaching his son, Prince
Daniel, and his commander Khan Khanan to move directly against
Ahmadnagar. The Nizam Shahy general sent to intercept them was
outmaneuvered on the march and forced to retreat, and Mughal troops
reached the walls of Ahmadnagar itself without further opposition,
beginning a siege with mining operations against the fortifications.

## A council divided, and a queen without allies

Trusting few of the officers left around her after years of betrayals
by her own ministers, Chand Bibi consulted the eunuch officer Humayun
Khan, who urged her to fight on. She judged otherwise: better, she
said, to negotiate the fort's surrender on terms guaranteeing the
garrison's lives and property, and retire with the young king to
Junnar, than to trust men who had already failed her repeatedly.
Humayun Khan ran into the streets proclaiming that the queen meant to
betray the garrison to the Mughals. The soldiers who heard him,
believing it, broke into her private apartments and killed her.

## A fortress taken, a kingdom's independent rule ended

Within days of Chand Bibi's death, Mughal mines breached the walls in
several places, and Akbar's troops stormed the fortress, giving little
quarter to those inside. Bahadur Nizam Shah and the royal family were
taken prisoner; the regalia and treasury were sent to Akbar at
Burhanpur, and the deposed king was confined at the fortress of
Gwalior, where the source notes he still remained as it was written.
Nizam Shahy officers who escaped the fall proclaimed a new king and
held out for a time from Paranda, and in the years that followed an
Abyssinian former slave named Malik Ambar built a fresh resistance
around a series of puppet Nizam Shahy princes — but the sultanate that
Ahmad Nizam Shah had founded at the site of his own battlefield victory
had, in its own original form, ceased to rule itself the moment its
capital fell.
