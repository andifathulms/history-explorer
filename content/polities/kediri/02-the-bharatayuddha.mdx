Jayabhaya reigned at Kediri from about 1135 to 1157, and what makes his reign
the kingdom's height is not a battle or a boundary.

It is the *Bharatayuddha*, an Old Javanese kakawin composed at his court in
1157 by the poets Mpu Sedah and Mpu Panuluh, which takes the war of the
Mahabharata and makes it Javanese. It sits at the head of a body of court
literature — the *Smaradahana*, the *Kresnayana*, the *Hariwangsa* — that this
kingdom produced and that everything written in Java for the next seven hundred
years was measured against. Majapahit's *Nagarakertagama*, composed two
centuries later, is in the same tradition and the same metres.

The *Bharatayuddha* is still performed. The wayang repertoire of Java and Bali
is built on it.

## What a literature is worth

Kediri conquered little, converted nobody, and left no successor claiming to be
it. Singhasari took its territory by force in 1222 and never pretended to be
continuing anything.

By any measure of power it is a middling kingdom on a Javanese river, and it is
remembered because of what was written at its court.

That is a genuinely awkward kind of achievement to weigh. A conquest has a date
and a map. A conversion has a boundary it crossed. A literature has neither: it
is not a thing that happened in a year, it did not travel by an identifiable
route, and its influence is visible only in the fact that everybody afterwards
kept doing it.

But the *Bharatayuddha* is still performed. The wayang repertoire of Java and
Bali is built on it, and Javanese poets were still composing in its metres in
the nineteenth century.
Seven hundred years of a literary tradition came out of
one court in the middle of the twelfth century, and no account of what Kediri
was for can leave it out.
