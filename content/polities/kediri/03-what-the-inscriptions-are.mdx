Almost everything datable about this kingdom comes from copper plates and
stones issued by its kings, and they are nearly all the same kind of document:
a grant of *sima* status.

A *sima* is land whose revenue is assigned away from the crown — to a temple,
to a religious community, sometimes to a village in return for a service
performed. The charter records who granted it, which land, what the boundaries
are, what obligations are lifted, what the ceremony cost, who witnessed it, and
a curse on anyone who interferes.

They are, functionally, the state's tax records written from the receiving end.

<Figure id="kediri-stone" />

Medang was governed the same way three centuries earlier and is known from the
same class of document.

## What that does to what can be known

Almost everything anyone says about how Kediri worked is an inference from that
one genre.

We can say the state lived on land, because the charters exempt land from
obligations that must therefore have existed and are listed by name. We can say
it raised labour and service through territorial officials, because those are
among the obligations being lifted. Neither is stated anywhere as a rule; both
are read off the back of a tax exemption.

How the throne passed is simply not answerable, and one reason among several is
that charters do not discuss succession. They give a reigning king's titles and
his ancestry as he wished them stated, and a king who took the throne
irregularly does not say so in the preamble to a land grant.

## What a civilisation chose to carve

The general point is worth having, because it applies wherever the evidence is
epigraphic.

What survives is whatever that culture happened to inscribe on durable
material, and the genres differ enormously. Angkor carved temple foundations,
so we know how many people were assigned to a particular shrine and what they
were fed. Assyria carved royal annals, so its history reads as an unbroken
sequence of campaigns. Kediri carved tax exemptions, so it comes down to us as
a fiscal system with a literature attached and no politics in between.

None of those is a description of what the kingdom was. Each is a description
of what it wrote on stone.
