Jayabhaya reigned at Daha in the middle of the twelfth century and commissioned
the *Bharatayuddha*. That is the historical claim.

The other thing attached to his name is not historical at all, and it has had
by far the longer career.

The *Jangka Jayabaya* is a body of Javanese prophetic literature, attributed to
him, that circulated in manuscript for centuries and predicted the course of
Javanese history: a sequence of ages, foreign rule, and a *Ratu Adil* — a just
king — who would come and set things right. Its actual composition is much
later than the twelfth century and the texts we have are eighteenth- and
nineteenth-century, but the attribution is to this king, and the attribution is
the point.

## Where it turns up

In the nineteenth century, in Javanese risings against the Dutch, where a
leader identifying himself against the prophecy is a recurring pattern. In
1942, when one of the verses — read as predicting that Javanese rule would
return after a period of yellow men lasting the life of a maize plant —
circulated widely as the Japanese arrived. It is still quoted.

That matters more than it sounds. Javanese rebellion, from the seventeenth
century onward, was rarely framed as a bid for a throne. It was framed as a
restoration — an age turning, a just king arriving, the order coming back — and
the body of text that made it legible in those terms carries Jayabhaya's name
on it.

## Nobody claimed to be Kediri, in the end

No later Javanese dynasty of any permanence asserted that it was continuing
this one. A lord of Kediri did briefly seize Singhasari's throne in 1292 in
the name of the kingdom Ganter had ended seventy years before, but his own
restoration lasted about a year before Majapahit's founder destroyed it in
turn; Majapahit traced its own legitimacy to Singhasari, and the sultanates
after it to Majapahit. The one revival Kediri's name did produce burned out
almost as soon as it succeeded, and no dynasty after it looked back further
than Singhasari.

What Kediri kept instead of an heir was a name that would not stop working.
Nearly eight centuries after Jayabhaya died, in 1942, Javanese were reading a
verse attributed to him about a period of yellow men lasting the life of a
maize plant, and drawing conclusions about the Japanese.

A kingdom nobody claimed to inherit, whose king's name was still being used to
interpret an invasion in the twentieth century. Political succession and being
remembered turn out to be almost unrelated.
