In 1042 Airlangga, who had spent thirty years putting eastern Java back
together after it fell apart, divided the kingdom he had rebuilt between his
two sons and retired to live as an ascetic.

The Javanese account gives the reason as a father's even-handedness. The result
was two kingdoms, Janggala and Panjalu, which fought each other; Panjalu won,
took the name of its capital's district, and became Kediri.

Java's dynastic chain runs from Kediri to Singhasari to Majapahit, and Kediri
is where it starts.

## Nobody knows who founded it

Airlangga is the obvious candidate for founder and he is the wrong one. He
ruled Kahuripan, the kingdom that was divided; Kediri is one of the halves, and
he did not rule it.

The first ruler of Panjalu is named in the inscriptions, and there is nothing
attached to the name. No reign anybody can date, no act, no source that puts
him in a sentence. He is a name on a stone, and that is the whole of him.

That is a more common situation than the tidiness of most dynastic lists
suggests. Srivijaya, Aksum and Champa are all in it — kingdoms with a
documented history and an undocumented beginning — and in each case the
temptation is to promote the most plausible candidate into the empty slot and
move on. A name supplied that way reads exactly like a name somebody wrote down
at the time, which is the whole reason for leaving it visibly empty.
