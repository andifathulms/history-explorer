Jayabhaya reigned at Daha in the middle of the twelfth century and commissioned
the *Bharatayuddha*. That is the historical claim, and this record's second
chapter is about what it means that a literature is the thing this site cannot
count.

The other thing attached to his name is not historical at all, and it has had a
longer career.

The *Jangka Jayabaya* is a body of Javanese prophetic literature, attributed to
him, that circulated in manuscript for centuries and predicted the course of
Javanese history: a sequence of ages, foreign rule, and a *Ratu Adil* — a just
king — who would come and set things right. Its actual composition is much later
than the twelfth century and the texts we have are eighteenth- and
nineteenth-century, but the attribution is to this king, and the attribution is
the point.

## Where it turns up

In the nineteenth century, in Javanese risings against the Dutch, where a leader
identifying himself against the prophecy is a recurring pattern. In 1942, when
one of the verses — read as predicting that Javanese rule would return after a
period of yellow men lasting the life of a maize plant — circulated widely as the
Japanese arrived. It is still quoted.

This corpus's Mataram record's third chapter is about the Trunajaya rebellion of
1677 and the Dutch company becoming a participant in Javanese succession. The
material that made rebellion legible in Java as something other than a bid for a
throne — a restoration, an age turning, a just king arriving — carries this
record's king's name on it.

## Why a chapter rather than a field

Because there is nothing in the schema this touches.

`successor_claims` on this record reads zero, and correctly: nobody claimed to be
Kediri. Singhasari took its territory by force and did not pretend to be it.
`religions_carried` is zero. `descendant_scripts` is zero for the reason the
second chapter gives.

So a king whose name was still doing political work in Java eight hundred years
after he died reads on this site as three zeroes and one battle he was not
present at.

That is the second time this record has had to say that. The site's answer both
times is the one it gives everywhere: the counts were built so they would mean
the same thing on two hundred records, and things that cannot be counted that way
go in a chapter with the absence named.
