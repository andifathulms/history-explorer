The Twelve Cities did not stay confined to the hill country between the
Tiber and the Arno. Dennis records a reach that, at its widest, "embraced
the greater part of Italy" - south into Campania and north over the plains
of Lombardy to the Alps themselves, a span far beyond anything the later,
purely defensive wars against Rome would ever again touch.

## Vulturnum, before it was Capua

The clearest evidence of the southern reach is a city. Dennis records that
the Etruscans held the land of the Volsci and all Campania as far as the
Silarus, on the Gulf of Paestum, and that they themselves founded, around
800 BC, the town they called Vulturnum - the settlement that would later be
known, under a different people's name for it, as Capua. A confederation
whose surviving history is otherwise dominated by its long losing fight
with Rome was, centuries before that fight began, planting cities of its
own far south of its own heartland.

## Lombardy to the Alps

The northern expansion left an even wider footprint. Etruscan dominion, on
Dennis's account, extended over the plains that would become Cisalpine
Gaul, reaching the Alps themselves before Gallic incursions from the north
eventually pushed the Etruscan cities of that region back out. Corsica and
Elba, closer to the Etruscan heartland, were held more directly still.

## A reputation earned on the water

That reach was not built on land alone. Dennis notes that Greek writers,
hostile to Etruria on principle, described its people bluntly as pirates
and robbers - the same accusation, in substance, that Greek writers
levelled at more than one rival maritime power of the archaic
Mediterranean. The same sources that made the accusation, Dennis is careful
to add, also acknowledged what lay behind it: Etruscan power at sea and
Etruscan commercial importance across the western Mediterranean, real
enough that even an enemy's own hostile testimony ended up confirming it.
Rome itself, in Dennis's own account, took real and lasting instruction
from this era of Etruscan reach - lessons, as he puts it, in both the
conveniences of peace and the tactics and equipment of war, absorbed from a
neighbor whose own confederation had, for a time, been the dominant power
of the Italian peninsula rather than merely Rome's rival on it.
