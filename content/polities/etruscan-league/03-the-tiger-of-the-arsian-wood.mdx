When Tarquinius Superbus was thrown out of Rome, he did not simply
accept exile. He appealed to his own kinsmen at Tarquinii and to the
Veientes on the grounds of shared blood, and both cities answered:
Dennis records that "it seemed good to the people of Tarquinii that
their race should reign at Rome," and the two cities sent a combined
army to put him back on the throne by force. The battle that followed,
fought in the Arsian Wood, split down an unexpected line - the
Veientes, who had already lost repeatedly to Roman arms, broke and
ran, but the Tarquinienses, a "new enemy" the Romans had not faced
before, held their ground and even drove the Roman line back. Junius
Brutus, Rome's own first consul, and Aruns Tarquinius killed each
other in the fighting, and the night after the battle, a voice from
the Arsian Wood - attributed by the more credulous ancient sources to
the wood-god Silvanus himself - was said to have declared that Rome
had won by a single life, settling by omen what the battlefield itself
had left too close to call.

## Porsena's siege

The most dramatic act of Etruscan support for the exiled dynasty came
not from Tarquinii but from Clusium, whose king Lars Porsena marched
on Rome with an army large enough that later Roman writers, uneasy
about admitting how close the city had come to falling, wrapped the
episode in legend - Horatius holding the bridge, Mucius Scaevola
burning his own hand rather than flinch before the enemy king.
Whatever actually happened at the gates of Rome, the tradition itself
testifies to how seriously later generations remembered Etruscan
military power at its height: a single Etruscan king, marching under
his own banner rather than the Confederation's combined one, came
close enough to retaking Rome that the memory of it needed heroic
counter-legends to survive at all.
