The Confederation that stopped fielding armies in 283 BC did not stop
existing. Its cities went on paying taxes, furnishing supplies and, when
Rome called on them, fighting under a different flag than their own -
Tarquinii, Dennis notes, was still supplying sailcloth to Scipio's own
fleet during the war against Hannibal, barely half a century after its
last stand at the Vadimonian Lake. Inscriptions Dennis had himself seen
show towns of the old Confederation still flourishing, in an ordinary
municipal sense, under the Antonine emperors - a political defeat in the
third century BC that left the physical cities of Etruria intact for
centuries of Roman rule afterward.

## A conquered people's own priesthood, kept in business

What the political collapse did not touch was the one export Etruria had
that Rome never stopped wanting. Dennis is explicit that Etruscan priests
and augurs had once doubled as princes and military commanders, ruling
through what he calls a triple sceptre of civil, religious and military
power at once - a fusion the Roman state, taking the cities apart
politically, still could not bring itself to discard on the religious
side. Etruscan diviners read the will of heaven, on Dennis's account, in
the entrails of sacrificial victims, in the flight of birds, and in the
paths lightning took across the sky - the body of practice later writers
called the disciplina Etrusca. Rome had its own augurs, drawing on its own
older traditions, but for the specific art of reading entrails and
lightning it kept coming back to Etruscan expertise long after Etruscan
armies had stopped being a threat worth fearing.

## Sons sent to a conquered enemy's own schools

Dennis records the clearest evidence of how seriously that expertise was
taken: Roman families sent their own sons into what he calls "the land of
their hereditary foes" specifically to study Etruscan literature and
language - a conquered people's own learning judged valuable enough that
the conquerors kept paying, generation after generation, to have their
children taught it. A confederation that had lost every field it ever
fought Rome on after Veii kept, for centuries, something no battle could
take from it: a body of ritual knowledge Rome's own religious life
depended on, taught by descendants of cities whose armies had not mustered
together since the third century BC.
