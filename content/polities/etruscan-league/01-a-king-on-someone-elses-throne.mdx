Dennis's own book opens with the problem every historian of the
Etruscans has to admit up front: almost nothing of their own internal
history survives except what Greek and Roman writers chose to record
about them. Tradition held that a hero named Tarchon conquered the
land and founded the Twelve Cities of the Etruscan Confederation - a
league bound less by any central government than by shared religion,
language and a common assembly that met periodically at the shrine
of Voltumna. Dennis records the scholarly dispute over the story
without resolving it, and is equally cautious about the Etruscans'
own origins: whether they migrated from Lydia in Asia Minor, as
Herodotus claimed, or grew up natively in Italy, no contemporary
Etruscan record settles the question, since what survives of their
own writing is almost entirely funerary rather than historical.

## Kings from Tarquinii

What is not in serious dispute is that Etruscan power once reached
directly into Rome itself. The city of Tarquinii gave Rome a
dynasty of kings - the two rulers remembered as Tarquinius Priscus and
Tarquinius Superbus - and Dennis records the argument, made by
Niebuhr and others, that the Roman tradition of an Etruscan royal
house reflects a real period when Rome answered to Tarquinii's own
authority, whatever the precise mechanics of the relationship. Roman
insignia of power - the fasces, the purple-bordered toga - were, by
one ancient account Dennis cites, imports from Etruria by the
Tarquins themselves. It was the kind of intimate, asymmetric
relationship between one Etruscan city and Rome that would define the
whole story of Etruscan-Roman relations for the next two and a half
centuries, long after the kings themselves were gone.
