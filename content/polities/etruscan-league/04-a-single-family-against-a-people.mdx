Porsena's march on Rome did not settle anything permanently between
Veii and its neighbor across the Tiber. Dennis counts fourteen
separate wars between the two cities in the surviving tradition, and
the century after Porsena is where most of them were fought - a
running conflict Veii came remarkably close to winning outright more
than once.

## A war paid for by one family alone

In 479 BC, with Rome pressed simultaneously by the Aequi and Volsci as
well as by Veii, the consul Caeso Fabius made an extraordinary offer
to the Senate: rather than divert a Roman army to watch the Veientine
frontier, his own family would hold it at their own expense. Three
hundred and six men of the Fabii, every one of patrician blood,
marched out under the consul himself and fortified a camp on a
steep hill above the river Cremera, in Veientine territory. For a
year they held the position alone, raiding Veii's own lands and
routing every force sent against them, until Veii sued for peace.

The peace did not last past the following year. Unable to dislodge the
Fabii by open assault, the Veientines turned to a ruse: driving flocks
and herds within sight of the Roman camp as bait. The Fabii, seeing
easy plunder, sallied out after the animals - and were led into an
ambush that killed all three hundred and six men, sparing, by the
tradition Dennis records, only a single boy too young to have marched
with the rest. Veii followed the slaughter with a real victory,
seizing the Janiculum hill within sight of Rome itself and holding it
for months before Roman consuls finally drove them off and extracted a
forty-year truce.

## Fidenae, a client that would not stay one

The truce held only until Veii's own subject town of Fidenae, a
onetime Veientine colony on the Tiber, revolted against Roman
overlordship and declared for Veii instead. Veii backed the revolt
openly enough to have Rome's own ambassadors put to death, and the
combined Fidenate-Veientine army met Rome's forces on the riverbank
below Fidenae - a battle Rome won, killing the Fidenate king Lars
Tolumnius in single combat, but not decisively enough to end the
war. Two years later the same allied army marched up to Rome's own
gates before being turned back, and the war dragged on for years more
before Fidenae itself was finally captured and destroyed, buying Veii
a twenty-year truce of its own.

Veii spent that respite as it had spent every earlier one: rebuilding
for a war its own fortifications and position made unusually difficult
for Rome to finish outright, until Rome, finally free of other fronts,
committed the full ten-year siege that would end the city for good.
