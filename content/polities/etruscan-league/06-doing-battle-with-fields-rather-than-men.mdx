Veii's fall did not end Etruscan resistance - it only removed the
Confederation's own strongest shield. Tarquinii, the city that had
once given Rome its kings, spent the following decades as what
Dennis calls Rome's most bitter Etruscan enemy, joined by Caere,
Falerii, and eventually the whole muster of the Confederation.
Cruelty flowed in both directions: after one Roman defeat, three
hundred and seven captives were sacrificed in the Forum of Tarquinii;
after a later Roman victory, three hundred and fifty-eight captured
nobles were flogged or beheaded in the Roman Forum in return. In 358
BC the allied cities gathered the Confederation's full military
strength and marched on Rome behind priests carrying torches and
serpents - a spectacle that briefly panicked the Roman line before
the soldiers were rallied and the Etruscan force routed. It was the
last time Dennis's own sources show the Confederation fielding a
fully combined army rather than individual cities fighting alone.

## Truces bought with fire

What followed was a war of attrition Rome, not Etruria, was built to
win. By 351 BC, after the Romans had spent a season laying waste
Tarquinii's own farmland - "doing battle," in Livy's phrase that
Dennis quotes, "with fields rather than with men" - the exhausted
city bought a forty-year truce. When that truce expired, the
Confederation tried once more, besieging the Roman-allied town of
Sutrium, only to be routed near the Ciminian forest and to watch a
Roman army, for the first time, cross the wooded frontier that had
long shielded Etruria's own interior. Tarquinii sued for another
forty-year truce after a further defeat at the first battle of the
Vadimonian Lake; Dennis notes that at what precise point she formally
fell under Roman rule is simply not recorded - only that it happened
sometime before the century closed.

## The final stand

The Confederation's last coordinated attempt to reverse its own
fortunes came at the second battle of the Vadimonian Lake in 283 BC -
fought, pointedly, on the same ground where an earlier Etruscan army
had already been broken. Dennis calls it plainly "the final great
struggle for independence." Details of the battle itself are thin;
what survived instead is the outcome, an Etruria that never again
mustered a Confederation-wide army against Rome. The political entity
that had once put a dynasty on the Roman throne and marched a
combined army to the gates of Rome itself did not survive the third
century BC in any form its own contemporaries would have recognized -
though individual cities, and a great deal else besides, outlasted it
by centuries.
