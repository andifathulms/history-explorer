Veii, the closest Etruscan city to Rome and by Dennis's own account
roughly the size of Athens, spent the better part of a century locked
in intermittent war with its neighbour before Rome finally committed
to finishing the contest. The siege that followed lasted ten years -
long enough that a Roman general, according to the tradition Dennis
records, was accused of dragging it out deliberately - and produced
one of the most famous engineering feats of early Roman history. Told
by a captured Etruscan soothsayer, and then by the oracle at Delphi
itself, that Veii would not fall until the waters of the Alban Lake
were drained off rather than allowed to reach the sea, the Romans dug
a tunnel through solid rock to do exactly that - a project Livy
claims was finished within a single year. Camillus, appointed
dictator, took the hint the completed tunnel offered and began a
second one, driven directly into Veii's own citadel.

## Wilt thou go to Rome, Juno?

The stratagem worked where a decade of open siege had not. Roman
soldiers emerged from the tunnel inside the city itself, and Veii
fell - "though beleaguered for ten long years, with more injury to
her foe than to herself, she was at last overcome by stratagem, not
by open force," as Dennis puts it, quoting the elder pattern of
Etruscan cities that resisted Rome longest through sheer defensive
skill rather than open battle. Camillus is said to have asked the
statue of Juno, Veii's own patron goddess, whether she would consent
to move to Rome - and the image, bystanders swore, nodded and
answered in a whisper. Dennis treats the story with the same
skepticism he applies to similar tales from every age and religion,
but the underlying fact needed no legend to matter: the Confederation
had just lost its nearest, most powerful member, and the buffer that
had kept Rome's own ambitions in check for generations was gone for
good.
