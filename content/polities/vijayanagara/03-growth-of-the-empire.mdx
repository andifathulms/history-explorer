Bukka's son Harihara II succeeded him around 1379 and reigned at least
twenty years, the first Vijayanagara king to style himself
Maharajadhiraja - great king of kings - rather than a lesser title.
Early in his reign he expelled the Muhammadans holding Goa on the
western coast, and inscriptions from across the length of the growing
empire - Mysore, Dharwar, Conjeeveram, Chingleput, Trichinopoly - mark
how far Vijayanagara authority now reached, consolidating what Bukka
had only begun.

## A worshipper of Siva, tolerant of everyone else

Harihara II was himself a devotee of Siva under the form Virupaksha,
but an inscription dated 1385 on a pillar of a Jain temple near
Vijayanagara records one of his own generals endowing it - plain
evidence, Sewell notes, of real religious tolerance rather than mere
official indifference. He granted freely to temples of every kind
across his reign, and the ministers and generals named in his
inscriptions - Sayana, brother of the scholar Madhavacharya; the
generals Mudda, Iruga and Gunda - suggest a court settled enough in
its own authority to leave a substantial administrative record behind
it. The Bahmani sultanate to the north stayed largely at peace with
Vijayanagara through most of this reign - Muslim chroniclers
themselves admit an unbroken twenty years of quiet - a stability
Harihara's long and otherwise unremarkable rule seems to have earned
by simple persistence. The latest inscription of his reign is dated
October 1399, and near the very end of his life, whether at his own
direction or his son's, a Vijayanagara army crossed into the disputed
territory between the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers - the opening
move of a war his successors would have to finish.
