Krishna Deva Raya was succeeded in 1530 by Achyuta, a king Nuniz — who
observed his court directly — describes as feeble, selfish, cowardly
and cruel in roughly equal measure. What Krishna Deva Raya's own
generalship had built, Achyuta's weakness began undoing, and by the
reign of Sadasiva, enthroned in 1542, the king himself was little more
than a prisoner of the men who actually held power. Real authority
belonged to Rama Raya, nominally a minister but in practice regent and
commander of the kingdom's armies, ruling alongside his two brothers
Tirumala and Venkatadri.

## A coalition where there had been rivals

For generations the Deccan's Muslim sultanates had fought Vijayanagara
separately and often fought each other just as bitterly. Rama Raya's
own diplomacy — playing one sultanate against another to Vijayanagara's
advantage — helped provoke the one outcome that diplomacy could not
survive: in 1564 four of those sultanates, Bijapur, Ahmadnagar,
Golconda and Bidar, set aside their own rivalries and combined against
the common Hindu power that had grown too dominant for any of them to
face alone.

## An afternoon that ended two centuries

The armies met at Talikota on January 23, 1565. Rama Raya, by
tradition ninety-six years old and directing the battle from a litter,
was thrown to the ground when a war elephant panicked near him,
captured, and beheaded on the spot by one of the allied sultans; his
head was raised on a spear in sight of his own troops, who broke and
fled. The rout became a massacre — a hundred thousand dead by the
victors' own reckoning — and the road to the undefended capital lay
open. Sadasiva was carried south to safety by Tirumala, the last of the
three brothers left alive, along with five hundred and fifty elephants
loaded with treasure; behind them the city was left to five months of
systematic destruction, its temples burned, its statues smashed, its
palaces reduced, in the source's own words, to little more than a heap
of ruins.

## A name a governor did not think worth using

The royal house did not vanish outright. Sadasiva's regents established
a much reduced court first at Penukonda and later at Chandragiri and
Vellore, and kings claiming the Vijayanagara title are attested in
scattered inscriptions for well over a century afterward — one of them
granted the site of Madras itself to English traders in 1639, asking
that the new settlement be named for him. The local governor who
actually administered the place ignored the request and named it after
his own father instead. Vijayanagara's name endured on palm-leaf grants
and temple walls for generations after Talikota; its power to make
anyone act on those grants had ended in a single afternoon.
