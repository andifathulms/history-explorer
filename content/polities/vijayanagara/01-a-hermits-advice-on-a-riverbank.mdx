Two brothers, Harihara and Bukka, served as minister and treasurer to
the Hindu chief of Anegundi, a fortified hill town on the Tungabhadra
river. In 1334 the Delhi Sultanate's ruler marched south against a
rebel nephew who had taken refuge with that chief; the fortress fell,
its ruler died fighting rather than surrender, and — by an account a
contemporary Muslim traveler confirms independently — eleven of his
sons were carried off and converted to Islam rather than killed. The
Sultan installed his own governor over the shattered territory and
withdrew north.

## A restoration that outlasted the conqueror

The new governor found the country ungovernable and the population in
open revolt, and appealed to his own king for guidance. Rather than
commit further troops to holding hostile ground, the Sultan restored
the territory to Hindu rule — elevating, in a decision the source calls
consequential beyond anything its author could have intended, the two
brothers who had served the old chief as minister and treasurer.
Harihara took the title of Raya; Bukka stood as his minister.

## A city built where a hermit pointed

Tradition credits the choice of a new capital to a holy man, Madhava
Vidyaranya, whom Harihara is said to have met on the riverbank after
witnessing a hare turn and attack his hunting dogs — an omen the sage
read as marking the spot for a city. Whatever the truth behind the
legend, a new capital rose on the south bank of the Tungabhadra,
opposite Anegundi, amid a natural fortress of granite hills that would
shelter it for two centuries. Harihara ruled until his death around
1343, and was succeeded by his brother Bukka, from whom, according to
the source, all the kings who followed were in some fashion descended.
