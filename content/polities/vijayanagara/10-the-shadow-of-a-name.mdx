The civil war of 1614-16 was the last time a Vijayanagara succession
mattered to anyone beyond the family fighting over it. Everything the
dynasty had ever governed kept slipping away on its own schedule,
independent of who wore the ring at Chandragiri. The Nayaka governors
of Madurai and Tanjore, appointed to rule those provinces in the
empire's own name, stopped answering to it in 1602; the chiefs of
Ikkeri went on styling themselves under-lords of Vijayanagara out of
habit or convenience until 1650, by which point the title bound them
to nothing at all; and the empire's own viceroy in Mysore governed
that kingdom until 1610, after which his descendants simply ruled it
as kings in their own right, with no further pretense of holding it
for anyone else.

## The last shreds

What little remained standing fell to a newer power altogether. Sivaji
and his Marathas overran the Konkan coast by 1673, and within four
years had swept up what Sewell's source calls the last shreds of
Vijayanagara authority at Kurnool, Gingee and Vellore - the family's
final fortresses, taken not by the Deccan sultanates that had broken
the empire a century earlier but by a Hindu power that owed it
nothing. Sivaji's own brother Ekoji seized Tanjore the year before, in
1674, and founded a Maratha dynasty there that would rule the old
Nayaka capital for a century of its own.

## A title with nothing left to grant

The family did not vanish so much as become ceremonial. In March 1639,
still resident at Chandragiri years after the war that had nearly
ended the line, a king styled Sri Ranga Raya VI granted the site of
Madras to an English trader - a grant the local Muhammadan governor
who actually controlled the ground simply ignored, naming the new
English settlement after his own father instead. The gesture kept
being made anyway. As late as 1792 and 1793, more than two centuries
after Talikota and over a hundred years after Sivaji's Marathas took
the last fortress the family actually held, local grantees in the
south were still recording land gifts to temples "by permission of
Venkatapati Maharaya of Vijayanagara" - a formula with no army, no
treasury, and no territory behind it, repeated on at least eight other
surviving eighteenth-century inscriptions for no better reason than
that the old royal name still meant something to the people making the
grant.

## What was left, writing in 1900

The bloodline itself never broke. By the time Sewell compiled his own
account of the empire, its last two acknowledged male descendants were
living not as kings but as a zamindar family under the Nizam of
Hyderabad - the Rajah of Anegundi, styled Sri Ranga Deva Raya, and his
nephew Kumara Raghava, holding the old family estate on the north bank
of the Tungabhadra, across the river from the ruins the Deccan
sultanates had made of their ancestors' capital three hundred and
thirty-five years before.
