By the early fifteenth century, under Deva Raya II, Vijayanagara's
reach was well established enough to draw a formal embassy from Persia.
Abdur Razzaq, sent by the Persian court, arrived in 1443 after a
diplomatic detour: the ruler of Calicut, though not himself subject to
Vijayanagara, deferred to it enough to forward the Persian ambassador
on at the Raya's own request — a request the source notes was backed by
a claim to three hundred ports each equal to Calicut and territory
requiring three months to cross.

## A city beyond comparison

What Abdur Razzaq found at the capital left a permanent mark on his own
account: "the pupil of the eye has never seen a place like it, and the
ear of intelligence has never been informed that there existed anything
to equal it in the world." He described seven concentric fortified
walls ringing the city, each enclosing the next, with cultivated fields
and gardens in the outer rings giving way to dense markets, jeweled
bazaars selling pearls and rubies openly in the street, and a royal
audience hall raised above every other building in the capital. Roses,
he noted with evident surprise, were sold everywhere — the people, he
wrote, seemed to regard them as necessary as food.

## A kingdom already too large to survey

Abdur Razzaq placed Vijayanagara's dominion as running from the
frontier near Ceylon to the borders of the Bahmani sultanate, with an
army the source records at over a million men and elephants "in their
size resembling mountains." However much these figures were rounded
upward for a foreign audience, the underlying claim — a Hindu power
commanding the greater part of the peninsula against a persistent
Muslim rival to its north — described a state already a full century
away from either its founding or its eventual fall, and a full century
away as well from the reign that later observers would judge its
absolute height.
