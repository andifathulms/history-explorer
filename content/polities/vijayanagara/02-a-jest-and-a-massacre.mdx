Bukka succeeded his brother Harihara I around 1343 and reigned for
thirty-six years. Early in the reign of the Bahmani sultan Muhammad
Shah, officials discovered that Vijayanagara and Warangal were melting
down large quantities of Bahmani gold and silver coin, and the sultan
had merchants put to death over it; Bukka, backed by his ally at
Warangal, answered by demanding the return of disputed territory. The
sultan stalled through diplomacy for a year and a half before finally
attacking Warangal outright, plundering the country up to its capital
before withdrawing on a large indemnity.

## A drunken order, and a king's contempt

The war with Vijayanagara itself began, by Sewell's own telling from
the Muslim chronicler Firishtah, in an almost accidental way. Delighted
by music one evening, Muhammad Shah ordered a gift of three hundred
gold pieces drawn on the Vijayanagara treasury for the musicians who
had pleased him - a jest, his own minister assumed, of a man in his
cups. The sultan insisted the order stood. Bukka, receiving the
demand, had the messenger paraded through the city seated backward on
a donkey and sent him home in open contempt, then mobilized an army
said to number thirty thousand horse, three thousand elephants and a
hundred thousand foot.

## An oath, and half a million dead

Bukka's forces sacked the frontier town of Mudkal and put every
inhabitant to the sword; a single survivor carried the news to the
sultan's capital. Muhammad Shah swore, in Firishtah's account, never
to sheathe his sword until a hundred thousand "infidels" had died in
expiation, crossed the Krishna with a picked force, and caught Bukka's
army by surprise in a night attack, routing it with heavy slaughter.
Firishtah - a Muslim court historian writing two centuries later, and,
Sewell cautions, no neutral witness - put the Hindu dead at half a
million across the campaign that followed; the sultan's own artillery train, unusual for the period if the
account is accurate, helped him press deep into Vijayanagara's own
territory before the campaign wound down. It was, by Firishtah's own
incidental admission, the first time a Delhi-descended Muhammadan
sovereign had led an army in person across the Tungabhadra and into
Vijayanagara's own dominions.
