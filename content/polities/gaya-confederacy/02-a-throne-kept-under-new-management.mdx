By the early sixth century Ka-rak's king, Kim Ku-hyŭng, faced a
calculation his own predecessors had never had to make so starkly:
Silla's power was growing steadily on his own doorstep, and a small
confederacy sandwiched between larger neighbors risked becoming
disputed ground rather than a state in its own right. In 527 he chose
to settle the question himself, surrendering Ka-rak's sovereignty to
Silla outright rather than wait to see which of the peninsula's rising
powers would eventually take it by force.

## A submission, not a conquest

The source's own scanty records give no indication that this was
anything but a peaceful transfer. Silla did not garrison Ka-rak's own
capital or replace its ruling house; instead, Kim Ku-hyŭng was retained
at the head of his former kingdom, now governing it under appointment
from the king of Silla rather than in his own right. A confederacy that
had stood as an independent neighbor to Silla for close to five hundred
years ended not on a battlefield but at a negotiating table, in an act
the source records without a single detail suggesting resistance on
either side.

## An arithmetic the records themselves cannot settle

Even the length of Ka-rak's own existence draws a rare moment of
editorial correction from the source: the records state the kingdom
lasted four hundred and eighty-two years, but the founding date of 41
and the end date of 527 that those same records supply add up to four
hundred and eighty-six. The source lays the discrepancy at the door of
whoever first tallied the figure rather than choosing between the two -
a small case of a source disagreeing with its own arithmetic, left
standing rather than smoothed over.
