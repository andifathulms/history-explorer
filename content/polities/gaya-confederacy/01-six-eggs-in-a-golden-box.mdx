In 41 CE the chiefs of nine districts bearing the shared suffix -gan —
A-do-gan, Yŏ-do-gan, P'i-do-gan, O-do-gan, Yu-su-gan, Yu-ch'ŭn-gan,
Sin-ch'ŭn-gan, Sin-gwi-gan and O-ch'ŭn-gan — agreed to merge their own
territories into a single confederacy they called the Kingdom of Ka-rak,
setting its capital at the site of present-day Kim-hă. Tradition credits
the moment of founding to a banquet on Sung-bong, where the assembled
chiefs saw a strange cloud settle on the mountainside and heard a voice
call down from the sky. Climbing to investigate, they found a golden box
containing six golden eggs, which opened to reveal six boys. One, Kim
Su-ro, became king of Ka-rak itself; the other five were made chiefs of
five further states — So-ga-ya, Ko-ryŭng-ga-ya, Song-san-ga-ya, Tă-ga-ya
and A-ra-ga-ya — that carried the shared name Ka-ya outward from the
confederacy's own leading member.

## A queen who arrived by sea

Kim Su-ro's own queen, tradition says, came to him by boat rather than
by any local match: a woman named Ho, styled Whang-ok or "Yellow Jade,"
sailed from a distant southern kingdom called A-yu-t'a, also known as
Ch'ŭn-ch'uk. She is said to have lived a hundred and fifty-seven years,
with the king surviving her by only one. Beyond this legend and a bare
list of kings, the source states plainly that little else survives of
Ka-rak's own internal history — no wars of conquest, no succession
crises, nothing but a founding and, five centuries on, an end.

## A confederacy not much smaller than its rival

At its own greatest extent Ka-rak reached east to the Wang-san River,
northeast to Ka-ya San, and south and west to the coast and Chi-ri San —
a footprint the source calls "little inferior to Silla in size." For
nearly five centuries the confederacy held this ground as an
independent neighbor to Silla, Baekje and Goguryeo alike, never
absorbed by force and never expanding much beyond the territory its
nine founding chiefs first agreed to share.
