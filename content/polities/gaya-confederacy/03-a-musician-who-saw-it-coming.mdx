Ka-rak's own submission in 527 did not end the confederacy it had
led. The five dependent states founded alongside it in the golden-egg
legend — So-ga-ya, Ko-ryŭng-ga-ya, Song-san-ga-ya, Tă-ga-ya and
A-ra-ga-ya — carried on for decades under their own chiefs, outlasting
the state that had given the whole confederacy its name.

## A master musician reads the omens

In 552 the king of Silla sent three students, Pŭp-ji, Kye-go and
Man-dok, to the Ka-ya country to learn music from a celebrated master
named U Reuk. But U Reuk had already read the direction events were
taking: judging his own homeland doomed, he took his twelve-stringed
instrument and his disciple Ni Mun and moved to the Silla court himself
rather than wait for Silla to come to him. The three students studied
under him there — one singing, one the instrument, one dance — and when
they tried to edit his songs for what they considered excessive
license, the old master refused outright and the plan was dropped. From
this exchange the instrument itself, the kayageum, took the name of the
homeland it came from and remains, under that name, part of Korean
music today.

## An end the source will not date precisely

The remaining Ka-ya states did fall to Silla in the end, in the reign of
Silla's King Chin-heung — "some five hundred years" after the
confederacy's own 41 CE founding, by the source's own reckoning, though
no more exact year or narrated campaign survives to fix the date more
closely. Whatever the precise year, U Reuk's own flight to Silla shows
that Ka-ya's fate was already legible to at least one of its own people
a generation after Ka-rak had given up its sovereignty peacefully.

## A grave robbed, centuries later

Ka-ya's own memory outlived its politics. Tradition holds that when
Silla itself fell in 935, several men who had defiled Kim Su-ro's own
grave died in short order by strange means — one crushed by a falling
beam, one shot by an unseen archer, nine others killed by a serpent
eighteen feet long. Centuries after that, the source adds, Japanese
soldiers who opened the same grave during a great invasion found it
still furnished with gold and jade, and beside the king's own outsized
skull lay the remains of two women whose features held their shape only
until the air reached them.
