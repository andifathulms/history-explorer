At Magnesia in 190 BC Eumenes II commanded the right wing of the Roman army that
destroyed Antiochus III, and it was his charge that broke the Seleucid left. This
corpus's Seleucid record dates its own decline from that afternoon.

The settlement two years later at Apamea handed the Attalids most of Seleucid
Asia Minor — Phrygia, Lydia, Mysia, Lycaonia, Pisidia, the Chersonese — and
turned a city-state with a hinterland into the largest kingdom in Anatolia. The
dynasty's peak, by any measure this site has, is a Roman gift.

## What a gift is

Allen's constitutional history is built from inscriptions rather than from
narrative sources, and the picture that comes out of it is of a state that
governed its new territory competently: royal land let to tenants, cities
handled through negotiated grants and letters rather than by decree, a working
bureaucracy, a considerable building programme, the library.

What it could not do was act independently, and the demonstration came within
twenty years. In the 160s Rome suspected Eumenes II of dealing with Perseus of
Macedon during the Third Macedonian War — the war the Antigonid record beside
this one ends in. Nothing was proved. Rome did not accuse him, invade him or
depose him. It began cultivating his younger brother Attalus, encouraged the
Galatians against him, and when Eumenes travelled to Italy to explain himself the
senate passed a resolution that no king should be received at Rome, and he was
turned back at Brundisium.

A kingdom that exists because of a treaty can be reduced by a procedural
decision. That is the whole content of Attalid foreign policy after 188, and it
is why the record's second and third turning points are a battle Rome won and a
treaty Rome wrote.

## The brothers

The one thing about this dynasty that everybody remarks on is that its family did
not kill each other, which in this corpus's Hellenistic pages is close to unique.

When Eumenes II was ambushed near Delphi in 172 and reported dead, Attalus took
the diadem and married the queen. Eumenes turned out to be alive. Attalus gave
both back. He later ruled twenty-one years as king and regent for his nephew,
Attalus III, and handed over at the end of it.

This record's `succession_rule` reads `nomination` for that reason: designation
within the family, honoured five times running. Set against the Ptolemaic record
in this same region, where the family's dynastic history is largely a list of who
murdered whom, and against the Hasmonean record, which ends in a civil war
between two brothers who both invited Rome in, the Attalids look less like a
dynasty than like a firm.

Which may be what a state founded by a eunuch on somebody else's treasury was
always going to be. The succession had to be designed rather than assumed from
the beginning, and once a family has designed one it appears to be able to keep
using it.
