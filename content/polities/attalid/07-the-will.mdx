Attalus III reigned five years, had no children, and died in 133 BC leaving a
will in which he bequeathed his kingdom to the Roman people.

The ancient sources are unkind to him — Justin has him neglecting government for
gardening, metalwork and poisonous plants, murdering his relatives, and going
about in mourning — and modern accounts are more cautious, because the man who
inherits a kingdom nobody else wants and hands it to the only power capable of
holding it is not obviously mad. The inscriptions show a functioning
administration to the end.

<Figure id="dioscorides" />

What is not in doubt is the document. The senate accepted it. Aristonicus,
claiming to be an illegitimate son of Eumenes II, took the dynastic name Eumenes
III and raised much of the interior; Rome fought him for four years, killed him
in Rome in 129, and organised the province of Asia.

## A value the list does not have, and no request to add one

There is no good word for what happened. A kingdom transferred by testamentary
bequest is not a conquest, not a fragmentation, not a dynastic replacement and
not an absorption. The war against Aristonicus was a war against a pretender:
the kingdom had already ended, at the reading of a document, before it started.

## Aristonicus

Aristonicus claimed to be an illegitimate son of Eumenes II. He took the dynastic
name, struck coins as Eumenes III, and held a substantial part of the interior
for four years against a Roman army — and later sources have him promising
freedom to slaves and calling his followers Heliopolitai, citizens of the city of
the sun, which has attracted a good deal of speculation about a utopian
programme that the evidence will not really carry.

What he was doing is worth naming precisely, because it is not what a successor
does. A successor says: I inherit the legitimacy of the house that was here. A
pretender says: I *am* the house. Aristonicus made the second claim, at the
moment of the dynasty's extinction, and it failed — he was taken to Rome and
killed there in 129.

Nothing afterwards claimed descent from the Attalids or invoked them. The
kingdom that had spent a century and a half arguing in marble that it was the
defender of Greek Asia left no one at all who wanted to be its heir, because
what it had to leave was a province, and Rome already had it.
