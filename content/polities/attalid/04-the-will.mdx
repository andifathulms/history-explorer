Attalus III reigned five years, had no children, and died in 133 BC leaving a
will in which he bequeathed his kingdom to the Roman people.

The ancient sources are unkind to him — Justin has him neglecting government for
gardening, metalwork and poisonous plants, murdering his relatives, and going
about in mourning — and modern accounts are more cautious, because the man who
inherits a kingdom nobody else wants and hands it to the only power capable of
holding it is not obviously mad. The inscriptions show a functioning
administration to the end.

What is not in doubt is the document. The senate accepted it. Aristonicus,
claiming to be an illegitimate son of Eumenes II, took the dynastic name Eumenes
III and raised much of the interior; Rome fought him for four years, killed him
in Rome in 129, and organised the province of Asia.

## A value the list does not have, and no request to add one

This record's `ended.type` reads `gradual absorption`, and it is the least wrong
of six rather than the right one.

The closed vocabulary is `conquest`, `fragmentation`, `dynastic replacement`,
`gradual absorption`, `internal usurpation`, `still contested`. A kingdom
transferred by testamentary bequest is none of them. `conquest` would describe
the war against Aristonicus, which was a war against a pretender rather than
against this record — the kingdom had already ended, at a reading of a document,
before it started.

This site has made a number of vocabulary observations lately and two of them
have produced changes: `investiture` was added to the legitimation list after
nine records in four regions had asked for it, and a `resumes` field was added
after four pairs of same-place records had. Both changes were made on the same
ground, which the coding rules require: a pattern across many records, argued on
the merits.

This is one polity. One case is not a pattern, and the discipline of not
proposing a value for it is the same discipline the Zand record applies to a
ruler who claimed authority on behalf of the governed, and the Hotaki record
applies to a ruler authorised by a jurist. A vocabulary that grows a value every
time a single page is awkward stops being a vocabulary.

So the field says `gradual absorption`, the comment on it says what actually
happened, and this chapter carries the rest. If a second kingdom is ever
bequeathed to somebody, the argument can be made then.

## Aristonicus, and a hole in coding rule 1

The `successor_claims` count on this record is 0, and Aristonicus is the reason
it needs defending.

He took the dynastic name, struck coins as Eumenes III, and held a substantial
part of the kingdom for four years — and later sources have him promising
freedom to slaves and calling his followers Heliopolitai, citizens of the city of
the sun, which has attracted a good deal of speculation about a utopian
programme that the evidence does not really support.

Rule 1 counts a claim when a *later polity* asserts an *earlier polity's*
legitimacy in its own public self-description. Aristonicus does the public part
perfectly. What he is not is a later polity: he was claiming to *be* this
dynasty, at the moment of its extinction, rather than to succeed it.

That distinction is not in the rulebook, because until now nothing in the corpus
required it. A pretender and a successor make different claims — one says *I am
the house*, the other says *I inherit the house's legitimacy* — and counting the
first would put a failed usurpation in the same column as the Ottomans taking
Kayser-i Rûm. The count stays at zero and this paragraph is the reason, which is
the site's standard treatment for a rule that turns out to have an unstated
assumption in it.

## What Rome got

The province of Asia, which was the richest in the empire, and a tax-farming
system in it that made the *publicani* a political force at Rome. Sixty-five
years later, the record beside this one arranged for every Roman and Italian in
that province to be killed on a single day, and the resentment McGing identifies
as the reason the cities went along with it is the resentment of the tax farmers
who arrived because of this will.

The Attalid kingdom's last act reshaped Roman politics more than anything it did
while it existed.
