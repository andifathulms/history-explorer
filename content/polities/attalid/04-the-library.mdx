Pergamon's library was the second of the Hellenistic world and the only serious
rival to Alexandria's, which is to say it was the second of two. The figure
usually repeated for it is two hundred thousand rolls, and it comes from an
allegation made in Rome a century after the kingdom ended.

The two libraries are the clearest case in this region of what Hellenistic
monarchy was for. Neither the Ptolemies nor the Attalids were Greek in any sense
their subjects would have recognised as central; both spent enormous sums
establishing that they were the patrons of Greek learning; and the competition
between them over books, scholars and editions of Homer is a proxy war about
legitimacy conducted in a reading room.

## Parchment

The famous story is Pliny's. The Ptolemies, alarmed at the rival collection,
embargoed the export of papyrus; Pergamon, unable to get writing material, was
forced to develop treated animal skin; and the material took its name from the
city — *charta pergamena*, and so parchment, pergamena, pergamino, Pergament.

The story is almost certainly not true in the form Pliny tells it. Skins had been
written on in the Near East for centuries before the Attalids existed — the
Persian chancery was issuing Aramaic documents on leather long before. What
Pergamon plausibly did was improve the preparation enough to make a superior
product, at a moment when a rival's supply was constrained.

## What the library did instead

It produced Crates of Mallos and a school of Homeric scholarship that argued
with Alexandria for a century. It produced the practice of comparing manuscripts
and noting variants — the habit of recording that different copies disagree,
which is the beginning of textual criticism. And its holdings, wherever they
physically ended up, went into the stream that became the classical canon.

None of that is measurable. A small Anatolian kingdom that spent a Roman subsidy
on scholars leaves no territory, no descendants and no successor state, and the
only reason anybody knows what its library was worth is that the books outlived
the argument about who owned them.
