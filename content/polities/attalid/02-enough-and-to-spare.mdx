The Attalid kingdom doubled in a room in Rome, and the argument that doubled it
was made by somebody else's ambassadors.

Antiochus III had been beaten at Magnesia in 190 and the Senate had to decide
what to do with western Asia Minor. Eumenes II had fought on the Roman side and
wanted the cities. The Rhodians, who did not want a large Attalid kingdom on
their doorstep, argued that the Greek cities of Asia should be free — and
argued it, cleverly, as being in Eumenes' interest too.

Polybius gives their case, and its central image is the one worth keeping. There
was no need to choose between rewarding a loyal friend and honouring Rome's own
proclamation of Greek freedom, because there was plenty for everybody: *as in a
costly banquet, there was enough and to spare for all*. Lycaonia, Hellespontine
Phrygia, Pisidia, the Chersonese and the districts round it were all at Rome's
disposal to give away. A few of them added to Eumenes' kingdom would double its
present extent. All of them, or most, would make it second to no other in Asia.

## What Rome thought it was buying

The Rhodians then told the Senate what its own motives were, and Polybius —
a Greek writing for Greeks about Roman power, from inside Roman custody —
records the flattery without comment.

Other nations went to war to seize cities, wealth or ships. Heaven had arranged
that Rome should need none of those, having already put everything under its
rule. What Rome still had to want was praise and glory among mankind, which is
hard to win and hardest to keep. Look at the war with Philip: Rome had declared
it was fought to liberate Greece, that was the only prize it took, and the glory
of it was worth more than the Carthaginian indemnity. Money is common to all
mankind. Honour is not.

That is the argument a Greek state made to the Roman Senate about how the Senate
should dispose of Asia, and the striking thing is that it half worked and half
did not.

## The list

The settlement commissioners restored the Phocaeans their ancestral city. They
gave the Rhodians Lycia and Caria as far as the Maeander, except Telmissus.

And to Eumenes, over and above what the treaty with Antiochus had already
allowed him, they assigned the Chersonese, Lysimacheia and the border castles,
everything Antiochus had held in Europe, and in Asia: Hellespontine Phrygia,
Great Phrygia, as much of Mysia as he had already taken, Lycaonia, Milyas,
Lydia, Tralles, Ephesus and Telmissus.

Pamphylia was disputed — Eumenes said it lay on the near side of the Taurus, the
Syrian ambassadors said the far side — and the commissioners, unable to decide,
referred it back to the Senate.

## What kind of empire this is

A kingdom founded on a treasury stolen from Lysimachus had, in one afternoon of
someone else's deliberations, become the largest power in Asia Minor.

Not one of those provinces was taken by an Attalid army. They were assigned, from
a list, by commissioners, on the strength of loyalty in a war fought mainly by
somebody else — and the same body that assigned them could, and would,
un-assign them. Eumenes had exactly as much kingdom as Rome currently thought he
should have.

He had twenty years to enjoy it.
