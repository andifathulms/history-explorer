In the winter of 167 Eumenes II sailed for Italy to put his case to the Senate,
and the Senate went to some trouble to avoid hearing it.

Rome had cooled on him. He was suspected of having played both sides during the
war with Perseus, and the senators were, in Polybius's words, thoroughly incensed
with him and entirely fixed in their sentiments. What they were not willing to
do was say so.

The difficulty was one of their own making. They had proclaimed to all the world
that this king was their foremost and most esteemed friend. If they now admitted
him and let him plead, they had two options and disliked both. Answer honestly,
and they advertised their own folly in having singled such a man out for special
honour. Answer politely, and they betrayed the truth and their own interests.

So they found a third way. On the general ground that they disliked visits from
kings, they published a decree that **no king was to visit Rome**.

## The quaestor at Brundisium

Then they learned that Eumenes had already landed at Brundisium, and sent a
quaestor down to read him the decree — with instructions to ask whether he
wanted anything from the Senate, and if not, to tell him to leave Italy as soon
as possible.

Polybius records the whole of the king's reply. He understood perfectly what was
being done to him, said not one word beyond *that he wanted nothing*, and went
home.

## What the decree was for

It was not a snub for its own sake, and Polybius says so plainly: the Senate's
object was to humiliate him in every possible way.

At that moment the Gauls were making war on his kingdom. The visible repulse
did exactly what it was designed to do — his allies were greatly depressed, and
the Gauls were doubly encouraged to press the war.

And there is a final detail that makes the year legible. Every city, prince and
king in the Greek world had sent an embassy to congratulate Rome on the victory
at Pydna, and to all of them the Senate returned a gracious and friendly answer.
It was a general rule about kings, and it applied to one king.

## The shape of the reign after it

The Attalid kingdom had been assembled twenty years earlier out of territory
Rome assigned to Eumenes because he was useful. It was never conquered and never
lost a decisive battle. What happened to it instead was that the power which had
given it the territory stopped wanting it to be large, and began — without a
war, without a treaty and without ever putting anything in writing beyond a
decree about royal visitors — arranging for it to be less so.

Eumenes reigned another eight years. His brother succeeded him and governed
carefully. And his brother's son, twenty-five years after the quaestor came down
to Brundisium, wrote a will leaving the kingdom to the Roman people.
