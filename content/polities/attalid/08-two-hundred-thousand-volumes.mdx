The most famous thing that ever happened to the library of Pergamum is a story
told in Rome, by a political operative, as part of a campaign to destroy a man's
reputation — and the biographer who preserves it says in the next paragraph that
the teller invented most of what he alleged.

It is worth setting out properly, because the Attalid afterlife is largely a
matter of what other people did with Attalid property and said about it.

## Calvisius reads out the charges

In 32 BC, with the break between Octavian and Antony now open, Octavian obtained
Antony's will and read it publicly to the Senate. Many people were scandalised —
by the reading, not the will — thinking it neither reasonable nor decent to call
a man to account for what would not happen until after his death.

Then a dependent of Octavian's named Calvisius brought forward further charges
about Antony and Cleopatra, and Plutarch lists them in order. That Antony had
given her the library of Pergamum, containing two hundred thousand distinct
volumes. That at a great banquet, in front of many guests, he had got up and
rubbed her feet, to settle some wager. That he had let the Ephesians salute her
as their queen. That at public audiences of kings and princes he took amorous
notes written on tablets of onyx and crystal and read them out from the
tribunal. That when a Roman of great authority was pleading a case, Cleopatra
passed by in her chair and Antony walked out in the middle of the argument to
escort her home.

*Calvisius, however, was looked upon as the inventor of most of these stories.*

## What that leaves

So the transfer of the Pergamene library to Alexandria — one of the best-known
facts about ancient libraries — enters the record as item one on a list whose
other items are foot-rubbing and love notes, produced by a partisan in a
propaganda war, and flagged by Plutarch himself as probably fabricated.

Which does not make it false. It makes it unverified, and it makes the reason
anybody repeats it worth noticing: the story was useful because everyone
believed the Attalid library was the kind of thing a Roman might give away to
please a foreign queen. That belief is itself the Attalid legacy. The kingdom
had spent a century and a half making Pergamum the intellectual rival of
Alexandria — the parchment, the collection, the sculpture, the argument that the
Attalids were the defenders of Greek civilisation in Asia — and by the 30s BC
its library was a byword: a possession so celebrated that alleging its
misappropriation could be part of an indictment.

## The rest of the estate

The kingdom itself had gone the same way, by testament rather than by conquest.
Attalus III left it to the Roman people in 133, Rome accepted, and after
suppressing the rising of Aristonicus made it the province of Asia — which
became the richest and most systematically exploited province in the empire, and
the one whose tax-farming contracts did more than anything else to corrupt
Roman politics in the following century.

That is a strange thing for a kingdom to leave behind. Not a successor, not a
claim, not a dynasty in exile: a revenue stream, and a story about a library.
