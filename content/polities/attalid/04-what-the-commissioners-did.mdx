The settlement of Apamea is usually summarised as Rome giving Asia Minor to
Pergamon. Polybius was close enough to the Roman senate to describe what the ten
commissioners actually did in the room, and the detail is more instructive than
the summary.

They began with the cities, and they sorted them by conduct. Autonomous cities
that had paid tribute to Antiochus and stayed loyal to Rome were relieved of
tribute altogether. Cities that had been paying tribute to Attalus were ordered
to go on paying the same tribute to his successor Eumenes. And cities that had
abandoned the Roman friendship and joined Antiochus were ordered to pay Eumenes
the amount of tribute Antiochus had imposed on them.

That third clause is the whole system. A city that backed the losing side did not
have its tribute cancelled or increased; it had the payee changed. The Seleucid
assessment was kept, the Seleucid collector was replaced by an Attalid one, and
the penalty for disloyalty to Rome was to fund Rome's friend at the rate the
enemy had set.

<Figure id="priene-inscription" />

Then the rewards, individually. Colophon, Notium, Cyme and Mylae freed from
tribute. Clazomenae given the island of Drymussa as well. Ephesus given back the
sacred district it had been made to evacuate. Chios, Smyrna and Erythrae given
whatever territory each of them had asked for at the time and claimed was theirs
by right, on account of the loyalty and zeal they had shown during the war.
Phocaea restored to its ancestral city and its old land. Rhodes given Lycia and
Caria as far as the Maeander, except Telmessus.

<Figure id="curetes-street" />

And then Eumenes, who was not content with what the treaty with Antiochus had
already given him. The commissioners added the Chersonese, Lysimacheia and the
forts on those borders, and everything Antiochus had held in Europe; and in Asia,
Hellespontine Phrygia, Great Phrygia, as much of Mysia as he had previously
subdued, Lycaonia, Milyas, Lydia, Tralles, Ephesus and Telmessus.

Polybius then records the detail that says most about the new kingdom. Over
Pamphylia there was a dispute, and Eumenes argued the case himself — his
contention being that Pamphylia lay on this side of the Taurus, and therefore
inside the zone the treaty had assigned to him rather than to Antiochus.

A king who has just been handed the largest territory in Anatolia is standing in
front of a Roman commission arguing about which side of a mountain range a
province is on, because the commission is the only body that can decide it. That
is the Attalid position exactly, at the moment of its greatest extent: everything
the dynasty now held it held because ten Romans had written it down, and the
boundaries of it were a matter for their interpretation rather than his.
