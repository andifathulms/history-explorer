Philetaerus was a eunuch, of obscure and probably Paphlagonian origin, and he
was the officer Lysimachus left in charge of the fortress of Pergamon with nine
thousand talents of treasure in it.

In 282 BC he changed sides. Lysimachus was destroyed the following year at
Corupedium and Philetaerus kept the money, spent the next twenty years spending
it carefully — subsidising cities, dedicating at Delphi and Delos, paying for
walls at Cyzicus, buying the body of Seleucus for burial from the man who had
murdered him — and handed a going concern to his nephew.

This corpus contains states founded on a rebellion, a conquest, a migration and
a revelation. This one was founded on a treasury somebody did not give back.

## Forty-four years without a crown

What the Attalids did with the money is instructive about what money can and
cannot buy in the third century.

They did not take the royal title for forty-four years. Philetaerus, his nephew
Eumenes I, and Eumenes's cousin Attalus all governed Pergamon as dynasts without
calling themselves kings, and in a world where Antigonids, Seleucids and
Ptolemies were all wearing diadems that restraint was a policy. A diadem is a
claim to be somebody's equal, and until about 238 the Attalids were not.

What changed it was the Galatians. Bands of the same Gallic migration that had
killed a Macedonian king in 279 — and whose defeat, on the Antigonid record
entered alongside this one, is how that dynasty got its throne — had settled in
central Anatolia and were extracting protection payments from the Greek cities of
the west. The Attalids had been paying. Attalus I stopped paying, beat them, and
took the diadem and the cult title Soter on the strength of it.

Two kingdoms, thirty-nine years apart, both founded on the same enemy. In the
third-century Aegean world, beating Galatians was how a man who was not a king
became one.

## The argument in marble

The dynasty then spent a century and a half making the point. The Gallic
dedications at Pergamon and the copies at Athens produced the sculpture the
kingdom is remembered by — the Dying Gaul, the Gaul killing his wife and himself
— and the Great Altar's gigantomachy frieze puts the same argument in myth: gods
against giants, civilisation against the formless thing beyond it.

This record's `legitimation` field reads `descent, conquest`, and the second
value is that. Everything about Attalid self-presentation says: we stand between
Greek Asia and the barbarians, and that is why we are kings.

It was a good argument and it had one weakness, which the next chapter is about.
By the time the sculpture was finished the thing standing between Greek Asia and
anybody was Rome.
