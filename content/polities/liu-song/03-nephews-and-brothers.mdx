Liu Shao did not long benefit from killing his own father. His brother
Lieousiun defeated him in the field, had him executed with his family,
and took the throne himself. Eleven years of assured peace followed —
Wei, having just lost Topatao to its own courtiers, showed no interest in
its southern neighbour — and the new emperor gave himself up to hunting
and to excessive eating and drinking, which brought on the apoplexy that
killed him at thirty-five. It was, by the low standard the dynasty was
setting for itself, a good death: no blade, no palace conspiracy, just a
ruler who stopped governing and was allowed to.

## The murders compound

What followed him was not. Fiti, his sixteen-year-old successor, opened
his reign with a wholesale massacre of anyone who fell under his
suspicion and was murdered by one of his own eunuchs before the year was
out. His uncle Mingti, who took the throne next, began by killing
fourteen of his own brother's sons outright, for fear they might one day
be rivals to his own line — a single admitted act of dynastic slaughter
that Boulger records without much need for further comment on the
character of the reign it opened. Having no son of his own, Mingti then
adopted an heir and secured the succession for him by executing his own
remaining brothers as well.

The adopted heir, a second Fiti, proved no better than the first and was
murdered after four years by the general Siaotaoching — who, offered the
throne outright, ostentatiously declined it, and installed a third
adopted son of Mingti's in his place instead, the child Shundi.

## A general takes what a general once won

The refusal did not last. In 479 Siaotaoching deposed the boy emperor
Shundi and took the throne himself, founding the Southern Qi dynasty in
the Song's place — fifty-nine years after Liu Yu's own accession, and by
a method that differed from Liu Yu's own founding chiefly in scale rather
than kind. The Song had never controlled more than a fraction of a
divided China, and after Wenti its own internal history, as Boulger tells
it, was less a story of governing that fraction well than of one murdered
or deposed emperor after another, until the very weakness that had let
Liu Yu seize power in 420 finally caught up with his own descendants.
