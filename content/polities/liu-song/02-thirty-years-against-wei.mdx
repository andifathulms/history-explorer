Kaotsou's heir Chowti lasted barely a year. His only mark on the record was
a disastrous war: the northern king of Wei, Topasse, besieged the fortress
of Houlao for seven months and, when it finally fell, won nothing but a
pile of ruins. Chowti himself gave the war no real attention, and his own
nobles deposed him and had him secretly killed rather than risk his
return.

His brother Wenti took the throne in the murderers' debt and immediately
turned on them, punishing the men who had made him emperor — the first
sign, in Boulger's reading, less of loyalty to a murdered brother than of
a new ruler's fear of men who had already shown they could unmake one. It
did him no lasting harm. Wenti began his reign, by Boulger's account,
"under the fairest auspices," and held the throne for nearly thirty years
— the Song dynasty's longest and most stable reign, and the one real
period this brief, embattled state had to work with.

## Topatao's reply

Wei was ruled through most of Wenti's reign by Topasse's son Topatao, "a
man neither less capable nor ambitious than his father," and the two
courts spent it circling a single question: who held Honan, the province
lying between them.

In 430 Wenti sent an embassy demanding that Topatao hand back the part of
Honan south of the Yellow River. The reply was short:

> I was not out of my teens when I heard it said on all sides that Honan
> belonged to my family. Go and tell your master, that if he comes to
> attack me or mine I shall defend myself; and even if he succeed in
> seizing this province I shall know how to retake it as soon as the
> waters of the Hoangho are frozen.

Topatao then withdrew his garrison without a fight, so that Wenti's
armies took the province they had come for by simply occupying it — and
waited for winter, exactly as promised.

## A general executed for winning

The campaign that followed went badly for Wenti in every particular
except one. His general Tantaotsi — the same officer who had led the
deposition of Chowti — fought thirty engagements in the Tsiho valley
without a defeat, and was still forced to retreat for want of supplies
once Wei's main army had reoccupied Honan behind him. It was the single
competent performance on the Song side in the whole war.

Wenti had him executed anyway. Tantaotsi's success had made him a
dangerous man to a court already uneasy about the class of general who
could depose an emperor, and his death removed the one commander who had
given Wei's army any real trouble. Honan was lost, the campaign's cost in
men and treasure had bought nothing, and pretenders claiming the fallen
house of Jin rose in several provinces at once.

## Hiuy held

Twenty years of uneasy peace followed, each court occupied with its own
governance — Wenti encouraging the study of history and limiting how long
a magistrate could hold one office, Topatao consolidating what Wei had
already taken. It broke in 450, when Topatao crossed the frontier at the
head of a hundred thousand men to settle the question of Honan for good.

The invasion stalled at once, held for months before a minor position
while Wenti's generals gathered their own forces, and Topatao withdrew
short of his goal. Wenti pursued, and the two armies fought twice more to
a standstill before the campaign's one lasting episode: the siege of
Hiuy, where the defender Tsangchi refused every offer to surrender the
fort gracefully and met each of Topatao's engineers with a
counter-device of his own. Topatao had publicly sworn to burn him. He
gave up the siege having lost twenty thousand men to the sword and more
again to disease, and Boulger records that the retreat's disgrace was
compounded by outrages on the towns along its line — gains that mended
nothing.

Topatao was murdered by his own dissatisfied courtiers the following
year, and Wei spent several years absorbed in its own succession before
it troubled its southern neighbour again.

## The reign's own end

Wenti did not outlive him by much. In 453 his son Liu Shao attacked him
in his own palace and had him killed before his eyes, at thirty-five
years old — the single most stable reign the Song dynasty would ever
have, closed by exactly the kind of violence that had opened it.
