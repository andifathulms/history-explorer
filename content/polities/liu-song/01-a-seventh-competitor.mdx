When the general Liu Yu took the imperial title in 420 and proclaimed
himself Kaotsou, founder of the Song dynasty, China was, in Boulger's
own phrase, "still as a house divided against itself." Six kingdoms
already held the northern provinces, each aspiring to be the one that
finally reunited the empire, and Liu Yu's own new state added only a
seventh competitor to their number. Their combined weakness, Boulger
notes, was itself a kind of safety - none strong enough to overrun the
others, all too exhausted for decisive offense - and despite the
grandeur of the imperial title Liu Yu now claimed, "the Songs were
never more than one ruler among many, and their government always
that of only a small section of the Chinese nation."

## A general's habits on the throne

Liu Yu had earned real distinction as a commander under the Jin
dynasty he now replaced, and his own brief reign showed the same
competence in office: frugal, attentive to administration, and loyal
enough to the foster-mother who had raised him that his devotion
became proverbial among his own contemporaries. What he lacked, in
Boulger's reading, was youth - he came to supreme power only late in
life, and the caution age had taught him made him reluctant to risk
what he had already secured by reaching for more. The one notable
event of his three-year reign was grim rather than glorious: the
murder of Kongti, the Jin emperor he had deposed, who refused on
religious grounds to take his own life when custom expected it of a
fallen sovereign, and was killed instead by the guards left to watch
him - the only instance, Boulger notes, of a Chinese ruler ever
refusing that expected exit.
