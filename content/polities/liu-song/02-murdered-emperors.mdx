Liu Yu's heir Chowti lasted barely a year, distinguished mainly by his
own indifference to government and a disastrous war with the
northern Wei kingdom, whose ruler Topasse besieged the fortress of
Houlao for seven months only to win, in Boulger's phrase, "nothing
but a pile of ruins." Chowti's own nobles deposed him and had him
secretly killed rather than risk his return to power - a precedent
the dynasty would repeat with grim regularity. His brother Wenti, who
punished the men who had murdered Chowti even while benefiting from
their act, gave the Song their longest and most stable reign, nearly
thirty years marked by real, if inconclusive, wars against Wei. It
ended in 453 when Wenti's own son Liu Shao (Vouti) murdered him - the
opening act of a second half of the dynasty's history that Boulger
treats as one long demonstration of how thoroughly a throne won by
force could be lost the same way.

## Nephews and brothers

The murders compounded from there. Fiti, Vouti's own successor, is
remembered chiefly for massacres before being murdered in turn.
Mingti, who followed, brought what Boulger calls "small improvement,"
securing his own throne by killing fourteen of his nephews and
brothers outright - a single admitted act of dynastic slaughter that
Boulger records without much need for further comment on the
character of the reign it opened. A second Fiti was murdered after
him, and the throne passed finally to a child, Shundi, too young to
rule in anything but name.

## A general takes what a general once won

The dynasty Liu Yu had founded by force ended the same way. In 479
the general Xiao Daocheng deposed the boy emperor Shundi and took the
throne himself, founding the Southern Qi dynasty in the Song's place
- fifty-nine years after Liu Yu's own accession, and by a method that
differed from Liu Yu's own founding chiefly in scale rather than
kind. The Song had never controlled more than a fraction of a divided
China, and its own internal history, as Boulger tells it, was less a
story of governing that fraction well than of one murdered or
deposed emperor after another, until the very weakness that had let
Liu Yu seize power in 420 finally caught up with his own descendants.
