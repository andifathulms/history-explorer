Hydatius, whose own eyewitness account of the kingdom's early decades
survives in such close detail, stopped writing around 468 or 469, not
long after Remismund's own restoration. What the Suevi kingdom did
with the century that followed - who ruled it, what its internal
politics looked like, how its own religious life developed - went
largely unrecorded by any surviving contemporary voice. The kingdom
did not vanish in that silence. It survived as a functioning Galician
state for another five generations, long enough to outlast the very
chronicler who had once declared it destroyed.

## The second conquest

What ended the kingdom in the end was not a rival Suevi faction or a
fractured succession, but the same neighboring power that had
propped Remismund up in the first place. Leovigild, the Visigothic
king who spent his reign from 568 turning a loose collection of
Iberian territories into something resembling a single kingdom,
finished what Theodoric's campaign of 456 had only interrupted: in
585 he conquered the Suevic kingdom outright, absorbing Gallaecia
into Visigothic Spain for good.

## A conquest without a chronicle of its own

Nobody wrote down the name of the Suevi king Leovigild actually
defeated, or any detail of the campaign itself beyond the bare fact of
the outcome. It is a strange kind of symmetry: a kingdom whose
formation is documented almost day by day by a bishop who lived
through it, and whose actual end, more than a century later, survives
only as a single sentence in someone else's history - the record of
the power that absorbed it, not of the kingdom that was lost.
