Rechila did what his father never had: he turned the Suevi kingdom
into a real military threat to the rest of Roman Spain. With the
forces his father had left him, he opened his reign by destroying a
rival warlord named Andevot in open battle at a river in Baetica,
seizing a large store of gold and silver in the process. From there
the campaigns kept widening. Rechila took Mérida, one of the great
cities of Roman Hispania and a former provincial capital, and went on
to subjugate the Baetican and Carthaginian provinces to his own
authority - territory that had belonged to other barbarian settlers
and to Rome itself, not to any earlier Suevi claim.

## A Roman ambassador taken by siege

The chronicle preserves one especially telling episode from these
years: a Roman count named Censorius, sent to negotiate with the
Suevi as an imperial ambassador, was instead besieged by Rechila's
own forces while staying at a town called Martylis, and was forced to
surrender himself under a peace agreement to secure his own release.
An ambassador sent to talk had ended up a captive, which says as much
about the balance of power between Rome and the Suevi kingdom at this
point as any battle does.

## Dying outside the faith his own son would take up

Rechila died at Mérida in August 448, and the chronicle records his
death in a single unsparing phrase: he died "a heathen." His son
Rechiarius, who succeeded him despite the quiet resentment of part of
the Suevi nobility, was Catholic - the first Suevi king the chronicle
identifies by religion at all, and a sign that the kingdom Rechila had
built by conquest was about to be run on different terms entirely.
