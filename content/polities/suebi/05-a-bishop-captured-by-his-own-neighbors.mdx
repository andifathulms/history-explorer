The decade after Rechiarius's death is, in Hydatius's own chronicle,
a story of a kingdom fracturing into rival factions rather than
reassembling under a single king. One faction chose a man named
Frantanes; another chose Maldras, who promptly plundered Lusitania in
the "usual manner of their perfidy," a phrase the chronicler repeats
often enough by this point to read as a settled judgment rather than
a single accusation. Rival Suevi bands raided Galicia and Lusitania
more or less independently of each other, and Visigothic embassies
came and went without managing to impose any lasting settlement on
the chaos.

## The chronicler becomes part of his own chronicle

The violence eventually reached Hydatius directly. A Suevi warband
under a leader named Frumarius, acting on information from local
informants, seized the bishop himself at the church of Aquae Flaviae
in July of an uncertain year in the early 460s and devastated the
surrounding region. It is a striking moment in the source this whole
record depends on: the narrator of the Suevi kingdom's fractured
politics became, briefly, one of its victims - and the chronicle
itself is the reason this otherwise unremarkable act of local
violence survived to be known at all.

## One king, restored by the power that had once tried to end him

Order of a kind returned only when Frumarius died and a rival
claimant, Remismund, absorbed his forces and "called back all the
Suevi to his authority through regal right." Remismund's own position
rested heavily on Visigothic backing - Theodoric sent him a wife along
with arms and gifts, and Visigothic ambassadors shuttled between the
two courts throughout his reign. The kingdom the same Theodoric had
once declared "destroyed and put to an end" was, within little more
than a decade, functioning again as a single polity - reconstituted,
this time, with the patronage of the very power that had nearly ended
it.
