The Suevi crossed into Roman Spain in 409 alongside two Vandal groups
and a band of Alans, and for two years the four peoples did what
barbarian groups newly arrived in a rich province generally did:
plundered it, without settling anywhere in particular. What changed in
411 was not a conquest but an agreement. According to the chronicle
of Hydatius - a Catholic bishop writing from inside the very province
the Suevi would come to dominate - the barbarian groups had "a change
of heart to enter into peace" and divided the Spanish provinces among
themselves by lot. The Vandals and the Suevi together drew Gallaecia,
the remote, rain-soaked northwestern corner of the peninsula; the
Alans took Lusitania and the Carthaginian province; the Silingi
Vandals took Baetica in the south.

## A king known first for wars against his own neighbors

Hermeric, the first Suevi king the chronicle names, does not appear
doing anything that looks like state-building. The entries that
mention him are almost entirely wars: plundering the middle regions
of Galicia, losing men in the process, restoring an uneasy peace with
the province's Roman and Galician inhabitants through the mediation
of a bishop after taking hostages. Hydatius's own account gives no
sense of a kingdom being organized so much as a war band that had
stopped moving and settled into a long, grinding relationship of
raid and truce with the population it now lived among.

## An abdication, not a death

Hermeric's own reign ended unusually. Rather than dying on the
throne, he was, in the chronicle's words, "weighed down by his
illness" and handed power to his son Rechila years before his own
death - one of the plainer successions the chronicle records for this
dynasty, in contrast to the violent contests that would follow within
a generation. It gave the Suevi kingdom something rare in this
period: an orderly transfer of power at the exact moment a much more
ambitious king was about to take it.
