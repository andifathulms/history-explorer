Trebizond's fourteenth century is the period in which a state this small was,
briefly, one of the more important places in the world economy.

<Figure id="trabzon-hagia-sophia" />

What that bought is visible in the arrangements. Genoese and Venetian merchants
held quarters in the city on treaty terms and quarrelled with each other and
with the emperors over them, occasionally violently. Alum from the Anatolian
interior, needed in quantity by the European cloth industry, went out through
the port. Silver from the Pontic mines went out as coin: the *aspron*, the
Trapezuntine silver piece, which circulated widely enough to be a recognised
denomination in Italian merchant handbooks.

<Figure id="trabzon-hagia-sophia-int" />

Alexios II, who reigned from 1297 to 1330, is where the sources put the height
of it — a ruler who kept the Turkmen at bay in the hills, managed the Italians,
and left a city with walls, churches and a court that produced astronomical
work of real quality, Gregory Chioniades having brought Persian astronomy back
from Tabriz.

It did not last, and what ended it was not conquest. A civil war between court
factions ran through the 1340s, the plague arrived in 1347, and the Ilkhanate's
collapse a decade earlier had already begun to move the trade route.

The city went on. The reason for the city being wealthy went away, and what a
state does when it has lost its economy and still has two hundred and fifty
miles of coast to hold is the rest of the story.
