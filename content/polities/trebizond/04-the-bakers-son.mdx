In the spring of 1404 a Castilian embassy on its way to Timur wintered at
Trebizond, and one of the ambassadors kept a journal. Ruy Gonzalez de Clavijo
had no literary ambitions and wrote down what he was shown and what he was told,
which is why his account is worth more than most.

He describes the city first. It is built near the sea, and its wall rises over
rocks, and on the highest part there is a very strong castle with a wall of its
own. A small river passes the castle and dashes over the rocks, so that on that
side the city is very strong; on the other side it stands on open ground.
Outside the walls are suburbs, and the most beautiful part of the place is a
street near the sea where everything the city needs is sold.

Then, without appearing to notice what he is saying:

> On the shore there are two castles, with strong walls and towers, one
> belonging to the Venetians and the other to the Genoese, and they hold them
> with the consent of the emperor.

Two fortified positions on the emperor's own waterfront, garrisoned by two
foreign republics, held by permission. That is the arrangement this state lived
by, stated as a matter of course by a visitor who thought it unremarkable.

## What the courtiers told him

On the Sunday afternoon two of the emperor's household came to the embassy's
lodging: the *Horchi*, which Clavijo explains means the page who carries the bow
before the emperor, and the *Protovestati*, the treasurer.

The treasurer, he was told, was very intimate with the emperor, who did nothing
in the empire against his advice. And they say, Clavijo adds, that he was of
base lineage, the son of a baker.

Then the rest of it. The young emperor, seeing that his father made so much of
this man and took no notice of the nobles of the empire, was enraged, and rose
against his father, and made war, and besieged the city for three months with
the greatest men of the empire behind him. They came to terms in the end, through
the bow-bearer, who was a friend of the young emperor and of the others who had
risen. And afterwards, Clavijo notes, there came dishonour, trouble and injury
to the emperor, on account of his friendship for this man.

## Why that is the whole state

Because it is the whole state, told in an afternoon, by people who lived in it.

An empire two hundred miles long, with a fortified capital it could not be
starved out of, a waterfront let to two Italian republics, an emperor paying
tribute to Timur, a treasurer of no family who ran the government, and a
succession war fought over him that put the heir outside his own capital for a
season.

Clavijo goes on to describe the Armenians of the city, who have a church and a
bishop and do not put water in the chalice, and the Greeks, whose priests carry
the consecrated bread out on their heads in a white cloth while the people press
their faces to the ground crying that they are not worthy to see it. He is
interested in all of it and condescending about most of it.

He was there because Trebizond was where you changed from a ship to a road. The
embassy was on its way to Samarkand, and this coast was the door.
