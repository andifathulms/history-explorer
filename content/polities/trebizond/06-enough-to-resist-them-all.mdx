In 1438 a Castilian gentleman named Pero Tafur, travelling the East for his own
curiosity and receiving hospitality from most of the rulers in it, landed at
Trebizond and went to see the emperor. He records the town in a line — about
four thousand inhabitants, well walled, fruitful ground, a large revenue — and
then reports the conversation, which is the most useful three paragraphs written
about this state in its last half-century.

The emperor wanted news. He asked about the emperor in Constantinople, how he
had departed for Italy and whom he had taken with him; and about the empress,
who was his sister; and about his own brother, whom he had exiled. He was
working round to one question. Was it true that his brother was betrothed to a
daughter of the lord of Mytilene, and that the lord, the Genoese and the emperor
in Constantinople had given him a fleet to make war on Trebizond?

Tafur told him it was true. The emperor was much cast down, and then replied
that he had enough to resist them all, and many more.

## Who these people were

The man asking was John IV, and Tafur's testimony is the reason historians know
when he came to the throne: his father Alexios IV was long thought to have lived
until 1446, and Tafur's visit shows he was dead by 1437 or early 1438. John had
murdered him.

The brother collecting the fleet was Alexander, and Tafur met him too. Sailing
home past Mytilene he found the exiled brother on the Genoese island, gathering
ships, having married the ruler's daughter to get his help. They asked him how
things stood at Trebizond, and he told them the truth as he saw it: that with
the Grand Turk against them they could do nothing that would either help
themselves or hurt their enemies.

The sister was in Constantinople. When Tafur got back there, the Despot received
him and the empress asked at once whether he had seen her brother in the Black
Sea — and her other brother was standing in the room. A few days later they all
went to hear Mass in Hagia Sophia together: the Despot, the empress, and the man
Tafur calls the real emperor of Trebizond.

So in one year one small dynasty had a reigning emperor who had killed his
father, a claimant brother fitting out a Genoese fleet against him, and a sister
at the Byzantine court hearing Mass beside the claimant, while the thing that
actually decided the question was represented in none of those rooms.

## The argument Tafur had with him

The Castilian did not leave it there. Pressed to stay, he refused, and gave a
reason that was also an insult: that even if his own affairs had allowed it he
could not remain with a man married to the daughter of a Turk, and that harm
would surely come to him from it. The emperor answered that God would show him
grace, because he had married her meaning to make her a Christian. Tafur's reply
was blunt.

> My Lord, they say, rather, that they gave her to you so that she could turn
> you into a Moor, by reason of your expectations from her and the little that
> you have.

The emperor ordered him supplied with whatever food he needed and asked him to
come back.

It is a traveller's set piece, polished for readers at home, and the Christian
moral is applied with a heavy hand. What survives the polishing is the shape of
the predicament: a state whose foreign policy consisted of marriages, whose ruler
knew a fleet was being assembled against him and could only say he had enough to
resist it, and whose two rival princes both understood that neither of them
mattered against the power to the west. Twenty-three years later that power
arrived and the question of which brother was emperor stopped being a question.
