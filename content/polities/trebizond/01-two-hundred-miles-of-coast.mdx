title: Two hundred miles of coast

In April 1204, while a crusading army was in the middle of sacking
Constantinople, two brothers took the city of Trebizond on the far side of
Anatolia with troops lent by their relative Queen Tamar of Georgia. Alexios
Komnenos was twenty-two and was proclaimed emperor that month. His grandfather
Andronikos I had been emperor in Constantinople and had been torn apart by a
mob in 1185.

Three Byzantine successor states came out of 1204 — Nicaea, Epirus and this one
— and all three called themselves the Roman empire. Nicaea won that argument by
retaking Constantinople in 1261. What Trebizond had instead was geography.

The state was a strip: two hundred miles of Black Sea coast from about Sinop to
the Georgian border, with the Pontic mountains rising immediately behind it.
Nothing about that is defensible in the ordinary sense — there was no depth, no
manpower reserve and no field army worth the name for most of the empire's
existence. What the mountains did was make it expensive to reach from the
Anatolian plateau, and what the sea did was make it reachable by anyone with
ships, which meant that whoever wanted the trade had an interest in the place
continuing to function.

The state that resulted is one of the least likely survivals in medieval
history: a strip of shore under a mountain wall, founded by two brothers with
borrowed Georgian troops in the week somebody else's capital was being sacked,
which then outlasted every other Byzantine successor and the Byzantine empire
itself.
