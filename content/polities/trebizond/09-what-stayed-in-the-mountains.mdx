title: What stayed in the mountains

The empire ended in a month. The population it had governed took four and a half
centuries.

Mehmed II did with Trebizond what he did everywhere: deported a portion of the
inhabitants to repopulate Constantinople, took the boys he wanted for the palace
service, installed a garrison, and left the rest. The Grand Komnenoi were
executed. The city became an Ottoman provincial capital and its trade, already
reduced, went on at a smaller scale.

What did not end was the Pontic Greek population of the mountains behind it.

## The reason the coast survived and the empire did not

The geography that had made the state indefensible made its people durable.

A strip of coast with no depth cannot be held against an army. A range of steep
valleys running back into the Pontic Alps, with villages at the heads of them
reachable only on foot, is a different problem — one nobody had any reason to
solve, because there was nothing up there worth the cost of taking. So the
Greek-speaking communities stayed where they were, farming and mining and
speaking a dialect that had separated from the rest of Greek early enough to be
barely intelligible to Athenians.

Some of them converted and went on speaking Greek. That is the strangest
residue: Muslim villages in the Of and Tonya valleys whose language was the
Greek of the empire that had been abolished, a situation that persisted into the
twentieth century and in places past it.

## Ramsay's habit

Ramsay, walking Anatolia in the 1880s to fix the sites of ancient cities, kept
noticing this sort of thing — that at Laranda in the south the Christians were
still using the Roman name of the town four hundred years after the Turkish one
became official, and that the old names survived in the mouths of whoever had
been there longest.

Places remember through the people in them, and the people are removable.

The Greeks of Pontus were removed in 1923, in the population exchange that
followed the Turkish war of independence. Something in the order of two hundred
thousand of them went to Greece, where their descendants still hold festivals
for a place on the Black Sea and dance to a lyre most of them have never heard
played there.

## And a library in Venice

So the empire's inheritance divides cleanly.

The state left nothing: no successor claimed it, no institution outlived it, and
its imperial title had been surrendered a hundred and eighty years before the
end. The coast it governed kept its language for four and a half centuries after
the surrender and then lost it in a single administrative act.

And a cardinal who had been born there, and who understood in the 1450s what was
about to happen to the Greek-speaking world, spent thirty years buying its books
and gave them to a republic on the other side of Europe, where they still are.

Of the three, the books were the only part anybody planned.
