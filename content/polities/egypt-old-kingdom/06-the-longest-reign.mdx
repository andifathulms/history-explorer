Pepi II came to the throne as a small child and did not leave it for over ninety
years. That is the longest reign attested for any monarch anywhere, and the Old
Kingdom did not outlast it by much.

## Ninety years

The traditions disagree only about the details. Manetho has him six years old at
his accession and continuing to his hundredth year. Eratosthenes gives him a full
century. The Turin king-list gives over ninety years, and Breasted saw no reason
to doubt it.

Consider what that means for a state whose entire organising principle was a
living king. Officials appointed in his first decade were dead long before the
last. A nomarch could inherit his father's office, hold it for forty years, and
hand it to his own son without the throne changing hands once. Every arrangement
made in the provinces had ninety years to settle, harden and become customary,
and there was never a succession to interrupt it — which is exactly the process
the previous generation of nomarchs had begun, running now without the one event
that could have checked it.

And the man at the centre, however capable at fifty, was a child at the beginning
and very old at the end. A system that concentrates everything in one person is
most exposed precisely when that person lasts longest.

## Then nothing

What happened next is not obscure because scholars disagree. It is obscure
because the writing stops.

Several brief reigns followed, possibly including a queen called Nitocris, to
whose name later ages attached absurd legends. Two kings, Iti and Imhotep, sent
officials to the Hammamat quarries for stone for their pyramids and statues, and
may belong here or may belong two dynasties earlier. Breasted's summary is
unimprovable: after the death of Pepi II all is uncertain, and impenetrable
obscurity veils the last days of the Sixth Dynasty.

There was no conquest. No foreign army came. What Breasted describes is a
mechanical outcome: the power of the landed barons had become a centrifugal
force which the pharaohs could no longer withstand, the nomes gained their
independence, and the kingdom fell to pieces into the petty principalities of
prehistoric times.

Roughly a thousand years of continuous development from the unification ended
with Egypt in approximately the political condition it had been in before it
began.

## The thing they kept trying to get back

The most striking fact about the Old Kingdom is not how it ended but how long
its ending went on mattering.

Two thousand years later, at the very close of Egypt's independence, priests
were still being appointed to maintain the mortuary cults of these kings.
Breasted's judgement is that in the Twenty-Sixth Dynasty the whole effort of
Egypt's priests and scholars was to restore the religion, life and government
which in their imagination had existed in the Old Kingdom — a period they were
further away from than we are from imperial Rome, and which they were
reconstructing from monuments rather than from memory.

The pyramids were a thousand years old when Tutankhamun was buried. They were
older to him than he is to us. What ended when Pepi II finally died was not
merely a dynasty; it was the only version of Egypt that every later version
would measure itself against and describe as a golden age.
