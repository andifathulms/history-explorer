Before about 2650 BC nobody anywhere had built at monumental scale in dressed
stone. Buildings were mud brick, reed, timber and matting almost everywhere,
including royal buildings, including most of every tomb — the one exception
was a single royal burial chamber, cut in dressed limestone for Khasekhemwy
a few decades earlier, and nothing built since had gone beyond that one
room. <Figure id="step-pyramid" />

Then Djoser's official Imhotep built at Saqqara a stepped structure of
dressed limestone sixty metres high, inside a rectangular enclosure five
hundred metres long, and the enclosure is full of buildings that are not
buildings — solid stone dummies with false doors and no interiors, reproducing
in limestone the reed-bundle columns, timber roofs and matting fences of a
palace complex.

<Figure id="djoser-colonnade" />

The whole thing is an argument about permanence made by translating perishable
architecture into stone. It is also the first building in the world at that
scale in that material, and its architect is one of the very few non-royal
Egyptians of the third millennium whose name was still being said two thousand
years later.

## What it altered

What Djoser's step pyramid altered was the state. A pyramid is not primarily a
building project. It is a permanent apparatus for quarrying, hauling, feeding,
housing, rostering and accounting, running for the length of a reign, absorbing
a large share of the kingdom's disposable surplus, and requiring a bureaucracy
to exist in order to work. From Djoser onward the Egyptian state is organised
around producing one of these per king, and the whole fiscal apparatus — the
assessment on land and herds, the biennial cattle count, the redistribution
through state and mortuary estates — is substantially the machinery that grew
up to supply them.

The ordinary vocabulary of historical turning points does not fit it. The
events that are supposed to change a kingdom are battles, sieges, treaties,
revolts, successions, conversions, and the moving of a capital — nearly all of
them military or dynastic. Egypt's two most consequential centuries consist of
building something that reorganised the state around building it, and there is
no word for that.

## The other Egypt

There is a second Egypt that everybody means when they say the word: the New
Kingdom, 1550 to 1077, ruled from Thebes — Hatshepsut and Thutmose and Amarna
and Kadesh, an empire in Nubia and the Levant, a million square kilometres at
its widest.

The Old Kingdom is five hundred years long, ends fourteen hundred years before
that one begins, is ruled from Memphis, and reaches perhaps four hundred
thousand square kilometres, less than half as much. It is also the Egypt
everybody has heard of. The pyramids, the Sphinx, the mummies of the popular
imagination, and the idea of Egypt as a country preoccupied with death and
permanence are all this one and not the other.

The divergence between how much ground a state held and how large it looms
afterwards is not unusual in this region — Old Babylon looms larger than Ur
III, which was bigger and better organised; Middle Elam supplied two
neighbouring civilisations with the monuments they are now known by and is
remembered by almost nobody. Here it is the two Egypts, and the smaller one
won.
