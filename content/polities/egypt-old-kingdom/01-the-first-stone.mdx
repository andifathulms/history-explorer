Before about 2650 BC nobody anywhere built monumentally in cut stone. Buildings
were mud brick, reed, timber and matting, including royal buildings, including
tombs. Then Djoser's official Imhotep built at Saqqara a stepped structure of
dressed limestone sixty metres high, inside a rectangular enclosure five hundred
metres long, and the enclosure is full of buildings that are not buildings —
solid stone dummies with false doors and no interiors, reproducing in limestone
the reed-bundle columns, timber roofs and matting fences of a palace complex.

The whole thing is an argument about permanence made by translating perishable
architecture into stone. It is also the first building in the world at that
scale in that material, and its architect is one of the very few non-royal
Egyptians of the third millennium whose name was still being said two thousand
years later.

## Why it is coded as a reform

This record's turning point for it uses the type `reform`, not because a law was
passed but because of what coding rule 4.1 asks: what did the event alter?

What it altered was the state. A pyramid is not primarily a building project. It
is a permanent apparatus for quarrying, hauling, feeding, housing, rostering and
accounting, running for the length of a reign, absorbing a large share of the
kingdom's disposable surplus, and requiring a bureaucracy to exist in order to
work. From Djoser onward the Egyptian state is organised around producing one of
these per king, and the administration that this corpus codes in the
`revenue_basis` field — the assessment on land and herds, the biennial cattle
count, the redistribution through state and mortuary estates — is substantially
the machinery that grew up to supply them.

The vocabulary is being stretched and this page would rather say so than pretend.
This site's `turning_points` types are battle, siege, treaty, revolt,
succession-crisis, conversion, capital-move, catastrophe and reform. Six of the
nine are military or dynastic. There is no value for *built something that
reorganised the state around building it*, and this record, whose two most
consequential centuries consist almost entirely of that, has to use the one
general-purpose word on the list.

## The other Egypt on this site

This corpus already carried Egypt once, as the New Kingdom: 1550 to 1077, Thebes,
Hatshepsut and Thutmose and Amarna, Kadesh, the empire in Nubia and the Levant,
1,000,000 square kilometres at its widest.

This record is five hundred years long, ends fourteen hundred years before that
one begins, is ruled from Memphis rather than Thebes, and reaches 400,000 square
kilometres — less than half. It is also the Egypt everybody has heard of. The
pyramids, the Sphinx, the mummies in the popular imagination, and the idea of
Egypt as a place obsessed with death and permanence, are all this record and not
the other one.

That divergence between reach and reputation is now the third instance of the
same shape in this region. The Old Babylonian record has it against Ur III. The
Middle Elamite record has it against everything, scoring zero on every influence
axis while supplying two other records with their surviving monuments. Here it is
the two Egypts. A site that ranks on square kilometres has to keep saying that
the column it sorts on and the reason people arrive at a page are frequently
unrelated.
