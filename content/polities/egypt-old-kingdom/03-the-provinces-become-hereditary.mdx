For most of the Old Kingdom the important people were buried at the capital.
Officials of the Fourth Dynasty have their tombs in the fields around their
king's pyramid at Giza, arranged in rows, facing the monument they had spent
their careers supplying. Where a man is buried is a statement about where his
career and his loyalty belonged, and for two hundred years the answer was: with
the king.

From the Fifth Dynasty and increasingly through the Sixth, the provincial
governors start being buried in their own provinces, in rock-cut tombs in the
cliffs above their own towns, with autobiographical inscriptions about what they
did for their own people. The office of *nomarch* becomes hereditary. Titles that
had been royal appointments become family property.

That is the Old Kingdom's decline, and it happened in the cemeteries before it
happened anywhere else.

## The other half of the arithmetic

Running the other way was the mortuary endowment. Every king's cult was endowed
with land, personnel and a permanent claim on revenue, and endowments were
exempted from tax and corvée by royal decree — a number of these decrees survive,
which is how we know it was systematic. Temples accumulated the same exemptions.

Each dead king therefore removed a portion of the productive base from the
state's reach and kept it removed. Over five centuries and roughly two dozen
kings, on an agricultural base that could not be expanded, the compounding is
not subtle. Kemp's reading is that the Old Kingdom state was progressively
starving itself by a mechanism that was also its central religious obligation and
could not be stopped without stopping being Egypt.

## Pepi II

The last significant king of the Sixth Dynasty is credited by the later king
lists with a reign of ninety-four years, which if true is the longest in recorded
history and would mean he outlived every heir he had. Even the sceptical readings
give him something over sixty.

Whatever the number, the effect is the same: a very long reign at the end of a
long decline, with a provincial nobility that had become hereditary and no
central succession to organise around. This record's `succession_rule` is null,
and the Egyptian material makes the null particularly stark. Egyptian kingship
has one of the most elaborate successional theologies anywhere — the dead king
becomes Osiris, the living king is Horus, and the funeral is the transfer — and
none of it says which son.

## Then nothing central

The collapse after 2181 has no battle in it. The last Sixth Dynasty kings are
followed by a Seventh and Eighth Dynasty at Memphis that the later lists give
seventy kings in seventy days, which is a way of saying that nobody could
remember them. Provincial governors carried on governing, dated by their own
years, and one of them at Herakleopolis and another line at Thebes eventually
fought it out.

`ended.type` on this record is `fragmentation` and `by` is empty. Nobody took
Egypt in 2181; it took itself apart. Five of this region's thirteen records end
that way, with no external agent named at all, and they are worth listing because
the reasons differ: Akkad and the Hittite empire, where a conqueror probably
existed and no source names one; the New Kingdom, at the far end of the Bronze
Age collapse; the Middle Assyrian kingdom, which shrank onto its core and stopped
writing; and this one, which had no enemy in the vicinity capable of anything.
