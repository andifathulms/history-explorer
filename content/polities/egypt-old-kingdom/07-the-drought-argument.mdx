There is a second explanation for the collapse, and it is worth going through
carefully, because what to do with a climatic argument is a methodological
question as much as a historical one.

The claim: around 2200 BC there was a sustained arid episode across the eastern
Mediterranean and the Near East — the event that appears in the palaeoclimate
literature under a decidedly unpoetic name — which reduced the Nile flood for a
run of years. Lower floods mean smaller harvests on a system with no storage
margin to speak of, and famine follows.

The Egyptian evidence adduced for it is textual and comes from the wrong side
of the collapse: provincial tomb inscriptions of the First Intermediate Period
in which governors say, in the first person, that they fed their people in
years of hunger, gave grain to the starving, and kept their nome alive when
others died. Ankhtifi of Mo'alla says the whole of Upper Egypt was dying of
hunger and that men were eating their children.

## Why it belongs on the page as disputed rather than accepted or dropped

The dispute is not about whether the drought happened or when. Nobody serious
denies that the climatic downturn happened. What is disputed is how much
explanatory weight it should carry against the internal mechanism the previous
chapter describes — hereditary provincial office, compounding mortuary
endowments, a ninety-year reign — which is sufficient on its own and does not
need a drought.

And the boasting-about-famine inscriptions are not neutral evidence for famine.
They are a genre. A provincial governor asserting that he alone fed his people
while everyone else starved is making a political claim in a period when the
central state had stopped being the thing that fed anybody, and one would
expect the genre to exist whether or not the Nile was low.

## The general point

It is a rule of this project never to estimate a number, on the grounds that an
estimate stated flatly reads afterwards as a fact. The same hazard applies to
causes. A single-cause climatic explanation is unusually attractive because it
is clean, quantitative-sounding and external, and because the palaeoclimate
record is genuinely good — but the strength of the evidence that a drought
*happened* is not evidence about what it *did*, and running the two together is
the same move as averaging two sources into one figure.

So the downturn stays on the record as a real hinge, dated and impossible to
leave out of how this period ended, with its disputed status carried openly
rather than resolved by omission. The Ur III letters about the fall of Ur and
the Middle Elamite new capital face the same problem and get the same
treatment: a hinge entered, and its status as a hinge marked disputed, rather
than a decision smuggled in by silence.
