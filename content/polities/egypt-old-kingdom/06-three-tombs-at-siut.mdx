After 2181 there is no Egypt to write the history of, and for about a century
and a half the entire contemporary written record of the country consists of
three inscribed tombs cut high into the cliffs above Asyut.

They belong to three successive lords of the Lycopolite nome: Tefibi, his son
Kheti, and another Kheti whose relation to them is unclear. All three carry the
same titles — hereditary prince, count, wearer of the royal seal, sole
companion, superior prophet of Upwawet, lord of Siut — and none of them is a
king.

## The condition of the evidence

Breasted is blunt about what he is working with. The tombs were first copied by
Napoleon's expedition and then neglected for most of a century, during which
they were used as a stone quarry and, in his word, frightfully mutilated.
Griffith produced a careful text between 1886 and 1888, working from the
wreckage and from earlier fragmentary copies.

The language, Breasted adds, is exceedingly obscure and difficult, and together
with the state of the stone this often makes translation quite impossible; his
restorations make no claim to reproduce the lost words but merely indicate the
probable connection.

So the only contemporary account of a century and a half of Egyptian history is
three damaged wall texts that a leading Egyptologist could not always read.

## What Tefibi says about himself

What survives is worth having anyway, because of what these men thought worth
recording.

Tefibi asks everyone who passes the tomb to pray for him. Then he sets out his
government. He was open-handed to everyone, of excellent plans, useful to his
city, of open face toward a petition and toward the widow. He was, he says, a
Nile for his people.

And then the line that says most about the period:

> *When night came, he who slept on the road gave me praise, for he was like a
> man in his house; the fear of my soldier was his protection.*

A ruler's proudest claim is that a stranger could sleep in the open. That is
not what the lords of the Old Kingdom boasted about, because it had not needed
saying.

He also records that his son succeeded him without opposition while still a
child — a child of a cubit high, which is the height of a newborn — and that
the city rejoiced over him and remembered the good his father had done.

## The war

The rest of the inscription is a civil war, and it is the clearest account of
it that survives.

The southern nomes, from Elephantine northward, united against him. He beat
them on the west bank and drove them as far as the fortress of the port of the
South; crossed to the east bank and beat a second army there; broke up a
hostile fleet; and afterwards had the opportunity to promote deserving
officers.

These lords of Asyut were the standing supporters of the weak kings at
Herakleopolis, holding a buffer against the princes of Thebes who would become
the Eleventh Dynasty and eventually reunite the country.

They never name a single one of the Thebans they fought. They name only one of
the kings they served — Merikare.

## What the Old Kingdom left

Not a successor state. A country that had gone on producing, for five
centuries, the largest stone buildings anyone would build for four thousand
years, and which now could not produce a king whose name a neighbouring
province thought worth carving.

What it did leave was the provinces, and specifically the men in them. The
hereditary nomarchs whose rise is the story of the Old Kingdom's last century
turn out to be the only functioning government left when the centre goes, and
one of their lines eventually put the country back together. The institution
that dissolved Egypt is the institution that carried it through.
