Harkhuf was a caravan conductor at Elephantine, on the southern frontier, and
he led four expeditions south into Nubia for two kings. He had the story of
them carved on the façade of his rock tomb opposite Aswan, seven columns of
text on each side of the door, which is where it still is.

The fourth expedition is the one everybody remembers, and it is remembered
because of what he chose to put on the front of his tomb: not his own account
of it, but a letter he received.

## The letter

Pepi II had come to the throne as a child. Harkhuf sent word north that he was
returning from the land of Yam with the army safe and with great and beautiful
gifts — and among them a dancing dwarf from the land of spirits, like the one a
treasurer had brought back from Punt generations earlier. He added, and the
king quotes it back to him, that never before had one like him been brought by
anybody who had visited Yam.

The reply is the earliest surviving letter from an Egyptian king, and it does
not read like a state document.

> *Come northward to the court immediately; thou shalt bring this dwarf with
> thee, which thou bringest living, prosperous and healthy from the land of
> spirits, for the dances of the god, to rejoice and gladden the heart of the
> king.*

Then the instructions, which are the part worth reading twice. When he goes
down into the boat with you, appoint excellent people who shall be beside him
on each side of the vessel; take care lest he fall into the water. When he
sleeps at night, appoint excellent people to sleep beside him in his tent, and
inspect ten times a night.

And then, with no ceremony at all:

> *My majesty desires to see this dwarf more than the gifts of Sinai and of
> Punt.*

The king promises a greater reward than the earlier treasurer got, and adds
that orders have already gone out to every store-city and every temple on the
route to supply the expedition without stinting.

## What the letter is evidence of

Two things, and both are about the state rather than the child.

Harkhuf did not carve his own campaigns on his tomb front and mention the
letter in passing. He carved the letter, in full, including the king's fussing
about the boat. The most valuable thing a frontier official could display,
permanently, to everybody who came to his tomb, was proof that the palace had
written to him personally.

And the postscript about provisioning shows how the machine worked. A single
returning caravan triggered instructions to every storehouse along the Nile to
feed it. That is the same apparatus that quarried and hauled and rostered for
the pyramids, turned outward, and it is the only army Egypt had.

## The other kind of expedition

The same reign supplies the other half of the picture, from the tomb of
Pepi-nakht, another Elephantine nobleman.

He led two campaigns into Nubia. Then he was sent to the Red Sea coast, to
recover the body of a nobleman who had been killed by the Sand-dwellers while
building his ship there for the voyage to Punt.

An official murdered on a beach while assembling a ship, and a second official
sent hundreds of kilometres to bring his corpse home: that is the reach of the
Old Kingdom at its furthest, and its limits, in one commission.
