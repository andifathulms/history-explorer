For most of the Old Kingdom the important people were buried at the capital.
Officials of the Fourth Dynasty have their tombs in the fields around their
king's pyramid at Giza, arranged in rows, facing the monument they had spent
their careers supplying. Where a man is buried is a statement about where his
career and his loyalty belonged, and for two hundred years the answer was: with
the king.

From the Fifth Dynasty and increasingly through the Sixth, the provincial
governors start being buried in their own provinces, in rock-cut tombs in the
cliffs above their own towns, with autobiographical inscriptions about what
they did for their own people. The office of *nomarch* becomes hereditary.
Titles that had been royal appointments become family property.

That is the Old Kingdom's decline, and it happened in the cemeteries before it
happened anywhere else.

## The other half of the arithmetic

Running the other way was the mortuary endowment. Every king's cult was endowed
with land, personnel and a permanent claim on revenue, and endowments were
exempted from tax and corvée by royal decree — a number of these decrees
survive, which is how we know it was systematic. Temples accumulated the same
exemptions.

Each dead king therefore removed a portion of the productive base from the
state's reach and kept it removed. Over five centuries and roughly two dozen
kings, on an agricultural base that could not be expanded, the compounding is
not subtle. Kemp's reading is that the Old Kingdom state was progressively
starving itself by a mechanism that was also its central religious obligation
and could not be stopped without stopping being Egypt.

What that compounding produced, at the very end of the line, is the subject
of the next chapter.
