Uni served three kings and had his career carved on a slab in his tomb at
Abydos. It is the longest secular text to survive from the Old Kingdom, and it
was written to boast — which is why it is useful, because a man boasting tells
you what he thought was worth boasting about.

## Alone with the queen's case

He began as a minor official and rose to be a judge attached to Nekhen. His
account of that period is mostly about intimacy with the king, repeated in a
formula that does not vary: *because the king loved me more than any official of
his, than any noble of his, than any servant of his.*

Then comes the passage that no other Egyptian document has an equivalent for.

Legal proceedings were begun in private, in the harem, against the queen Imtes.
The king had Uni brought in to hear the case alone. *No chief judge and vizier
at all, no prince at all was there, but only I alone* — and Uni, whose office at
the time was merely superior custodian of the domain of Pharaoh, wrote the
record himself with one other judge.

He states plainly why this was extraordinary: *never before had one like me heard
the secret of the royal harem.* Something happened to a queen of Egypt and the
only man permitted in the room was a mid-ranking official chosen for being
trusted. What the case was about, and what became of her, the inscription does
not say, and no other source supplies it.

## An army of many ten thousands

The second half is a war, and it is the earliest detailed account of an Egyptian
army in the field.

The king made war on the Asiatic Sand-dwellers, and Uni says the levy was raised
from the entire country: the whole south from Elephantine northward, both sides
of the Delta, the fortresses — and contingents from Irthet, Medja, Yam, Wawat
and Kau in Nubia, and from the Libyan land of Temeh. Nubians were being
conscripted into Egyptian armies as a matter of course centuries before the
empire that is usually credited with it.

Uni was put at the head of it, and what he wants noticed is not the command but
the discipline. Not one of them, he says, quarrelled with his neighbour; not one
plundered dough or sandals from a wayfarer; not one took bread from any town.
An officer describing his own army's supply behaviour in that much detail is
telling you what usually happened.

The account of the campaign itself is a chant, the same clause repeated with the
damage changed each time:

> This army returned in safety, after it had hacked up the land. This army
> returned in safety, after it had overturned its strongholds. This army
> returned in safety, after it had cut down its figs and its vines.

Then: after it had slain troops there, in many ten thousands.

He was sent five times, because the Sand-dwellers kept coming back. On the last
occasion he loaded the troops into ships and landed them behind a ridge, and
every rebel among them was killed — an amphibious flanking movement, in a text
from the Sixth Dynasty.

## What the document is evidence of

Uni was not a general by training. He was a palace administrator who was handed
an army because the king trusted him, and then handed a quarrying expedition, and
then a canal.

That is the Old Kingdom state at its most capable: it could raise tens of
thousands of men from every province and from beyond its southern frontier, feed
them well enough that their commander thought their restraint worth recording,
send them into Palestine five times, and put them on ships. And it could do all
of that through a man whose qualification was that the king liked him — because
the apparatus was the king's household, and had not yet become anybody else's
property.
