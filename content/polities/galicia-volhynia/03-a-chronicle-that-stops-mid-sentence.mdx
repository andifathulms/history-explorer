Daniel's death around 1265 did not end Galicia's brief golden age
outright. For a time his sons kept the country in close contact with
the West, marrying into western dynasties and maintaining the
building and commerce their father had encouraged. But the political
unity Daniel had held together by force of will did not survive
division among his own heirs. The principality split between several
princes, and a country that had once dictated terms to the Mongols
and crowned a king with a pope's own blessing found itself unable to
resist a new power rising on its own doorstep: Lithuania, whose
dukes, by the century's end, had begun to eclipse Galicia as the
dominant force in the Russian west.

## A Tatar invasion worse than the first

The Mongols had not finished with Galicia either. A Tatar army under
Khan Tulabugha invaded the principality on its way to raid Poland,
laying waste to the country so thoroughly that Mirsky's own account
judges it more destructive than Batu's original invasion a generation
before - the land reduced, for a time, to grazing ground for nomad
herds passing through rather than a functioning state in its own
right.

## A source that simply goes silent

What makes Galicia's final decades so hard to trace is not merely
misfortune but the disappearance of the record itself. The Volhynian
Chronicle - the same remarkable near-contemporary history that
preserved Daniel's own reign and his sons' in such vivid detail -
stops abruptly after 1292. For the half-century that followed,
Mirsky's own account states plainly, there survives practically no
source for Galician or Volhynian history at all. A principality once
documented closely enough to record a Mongol khan offering its king a
cup of wine instead of fermented milk simply falls out of the written
record for two generations, isolated from the other Russian
principalities and left, as its own western neighbors expanded around
it, without a chronicler to say exactly how its own end approached.
