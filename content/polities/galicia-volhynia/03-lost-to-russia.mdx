Daniel died in 1264, closing, in Rambaud's own words, "one of the
most memorable and most chequered careers in the history of Russia" -
a life that had run through the civil wars of a turbulent childhood,
the ruin and exile the Mongol invasion brought in his prime, and
decades of negotiation and war with Poland, Hungary and Rome that
left him, by his own historian's judgment, no rest at any point.
Rambaud's own assessment is unambiguous about what all of it
ultimately achieved: "no prince better deserved to free Southern
Russia, but his activity and talents struggled in vain against the
fate of his country."

## A crown that outlived its own kingdom

The principality did not collapse with its own greatest ruler.
Galitch passed to Daniel's descendants and continued for generations
afterward, still carrying the royal title Innocent IV's legate had
conferred - but Rambaud's own account closes the story with a single
sentence rather than a narrative: in the fourteenth century, Galicia
"was absorbed into the kingdom of Poland and was lost to Russia." No
particular battle or treaty earns a name in this telling; a kingdom
whose founding prince had won his own throne twice over, by election
and then by conquest, and whose son had been crowned by a pope's own
hand, ends in this account as a territorial fact rather than a
dramatic reversal - absorbed rather than defeated, the same quiet way
many of the smaller Rus' principalities disappeared into their larger
neighbors once the age of Mongol overlordship had broken the balance
of power that once let a place like Galitch elect and depose its own
princes at will.
