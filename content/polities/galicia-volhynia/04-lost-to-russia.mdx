Daniel died in 1264, closing, in Rambaud's own words, "one of the
most memorable and most chequered careers in the history of Russia" -
a life that had run through the civil wars of a turbulent childhood,
the ruin and exile the Mongol invasion brought in his prime, and
decades of negotiation and war with Poland, Hungary and Rome that
left him, by his own historian's judgment, no rest at any point.
Rambaud's own assessment is unambiguous about what all of it
ultimately achieved: "no prince better deserved to free Southern
Russia, but his activity and talents struggled in vain against the
fate of his country."

## A crown that outlived its own kingdom, until it didn't

The principality did not collapse with its own greatest ruler.
Galitch passed to Daniel's descendants and continued for generations
afterward, still carrying the royal title Innocent IV's legate had
conferred, even as the surviving record of its own final decades
grows thinner than for any other stretch of its history. What is
known for certain is the outcome: in 1347, a kingdom whose founding
prince had won his own throne twice over, by election and then by
conquest, and whose son had been crowned by a pope's own hand, was
absorbed into the kingdom of Poland - no particular battle or treaty
earning a name in the surviving record, a territorial fact rather
than a dramatic reversal. Its boyar aristocracy went over to Rome and
Polish civilization along with it; the common people of its own
cities, Lviv chief among them, did not, staying Orthodox and Russian
long enough to help carry a Ukrainian revival forward two and a half
centuries later.
