Galitch, in Rambaud's own description, was a genuinely different kind
of Rus' principality - peopled by White Croats and marked, unlike the
rising northeastern power of Suzdal, by a Slavonic character the
Varangian conquest had never fully overwritten. "The prince," a
Russian historian Rambaud quotes puts it, "was a prince of the old
Slavonic type. He was elected by a popular assembly, and kept his
crown by its consent." That assembly was in practice dominated by the
richest men of the country, the boyars, who under Polish and
Hungarian influence had raised themselves into a genuine aristocracy
ruling in the prince's own name. When Prince Vladimir disgraced
himself through drunkenness and the dishonoring of noble wives and
daughters, the boyars simply summoned him to answer for it; Vladimir
fled to Hungary rather than face them, and the throne passed, in
1188, to Roman, Prince of Volhynia - the first time the two
principalities shared a single ruler.

## A crown won twice

The union did not hold. Hungary's own king restored Vladimir by
force, then betrayed him, imprisoning his own protégé and installing
his son instead - a Hungarian occupation the Galicians found harder
to bear than any of their own quarrelsome native princes, and they
expelled it and recalled Vladimir as soon as they could. Only after
Vladimir's own death did Roman make his second and lasting bid,
raising a Polish auxiliary army and conquering Galitch outright rather
than waiting for another election. "This time," Rambaud writes,
"Roman held the crown, not by election, but by conquest" - and he
used that conquest to break the boyar aristocracy that had made and
unmade princes at will, executing and confiscating the property of
enemies with a severity a sympathetic Polish chronicler of the period
called horrifying, even as Russian chroniclers praised the same
ruler as an invincible hero who "flung himself like a lion upon the
infidels."
