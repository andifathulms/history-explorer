Roman's death in 1205, killed in an unequal skirmish during a war
with Poland, left the principality to two sons too young to rule.
Daniel, the elder, was proclaimed prince the same year, but a child
could not govern a country as turbulent as Galitch: the boyars
tortured and hanged princes of Saint Vladimir's own blood, foreign
armies from Poland and Hungary intervened at will, and Daniel was
placed on the throne, expelled, and recalled so many times that
Rambaud describes his whole infancy as "the toy of intriguing
factions." The wandering adventurer-prince Mstislaf the Bold drove
the Hungarians out at one point and married his own daughter to
Daniel, but even that alliance only meant fresh wars, this time
against Poland. It was a rough school, and by the time Mstislaf died
in 1228 and Daniel took the throne of Galitch in earnest, he had
already survived the disastrous Battle of the Kalka against the
Tatars five years before.

## A crown accepted from the horde

The Mongol invasion that followed dwarfed everything before it,
driving Daniel from Galitch entirely and leaving the city in ruins.
He fled to Hungary and worked from exile to rebuild what he could -
inviting Germans, Armenians and Jews to resettle a country the
Mongols had emptied, and granting them privileges that built real
commerce even as they introduced, in Rambaud's own reading, a
distinct and lasting ethnic presence alien to the surrounding
population. Daniel held out against submission to the Mongols longer
than most Rus' princes, and when he finally traveled to make his
peace, the khan Batu greeted him with open approval: "You have done
well to come at last." Batu spared him the usual humiliating rituals
and, noticing his guest's distaste for fermented mare's milk, offered
him a cup of wine instead - a small courtesy Daniel accepted without
ever losing his own resentment of the arrangement it sealed.

## A king crowned for a crusade that never came

Isolated within an Orthodox world now largely subject to the Mongols,
Daniel turned to Rome. He promised Pope Innocent IV his support for
reuniting the Eastern and Western churches and for a crusade against
the Mongols, and in 1254 a papal legate crowned him King at
Drohiczyn - Innocent's own gift of crown and scepter to a ruler he
called his dear son. Neither the church union nor the crusade ever
materialized, and Daniel weathered the reproaches of Innocent's
successor without ever giving up the royal title itself. He spent his
later years fighting in the wars of Central Europe with real
distinction - a Hungarian chronicler Rambaud quotes marveled at the
discipline of his troops, his Tatar-style weapons, and a prince's
"Greek habit embroidered with gold" - and made one last attempt to
throw off Mongol overlordship before superior force compelled him to
dismantle his own fortresses and accept the yoke he had spent decades
resisting.
