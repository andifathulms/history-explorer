Ayutthaya was destroyed in 1767 and the state was reassembled within fifteen
years, forty miles downriver, by people who had been in it — and the
reassembly was deliberate, itemised, and largely successful.

## The interval

The Burmese sack was thorough: the city burned, the population deported or
scattered, the royal family killed or carried off, the archives and the
libraries gone.

Then the Burmese left, because a Chinese invasion of Burma required the army
elsewhere. What was left in Siam was five or six regional warlords, and the one
who won was Taksin — a provincial governor of Chinese descent who had broken
out of the siege with a few hundred men, took the eastern seaboard, then Thonburi
at the river mouth, and had reunified most of the country by 1770.

He reigned fourteen years, went — by the account of the men who deposed him —
mad, claimed to be a stream-enterer on the path to arhatship and had monks
flogged for not prostrating to him, and was executed in 1782 by a general who
took the throne as Rama I.

## Rebuilding on purpose

What the new dynasty did at Bangkok is unusual because it was explicit.

The Grand Palace and the temple of the Emerald Buddha were laid out to
reproduce the Ayutthayan royal precinct. The city was sited on a river bend and
canalised to give it an island quarter, as Ayutthaya had been. The ministries
were re-established with the Ayutthayan names and functions. The law was
recompiled in 1805 — the Three Seals Code — from whatever texts had survived,
with the gaps filled by the memory of officials who had served the old kingdom.

The royal chronicles were rewritten too, and this is the part to be careful
with. The versions of the Ayutthaya story that are standard were compiled or
recompiled in Bangkok, by a dynasty that had replaced Taksin and needed the
sequence to run cleanly from the old kingdom through a regrettable interval to
itself.

Taksin's reputation is the casualty. The madness may be true and it is also
exactly what a successor regime would record.

## The literature

The most striking single act of reconstruction is the *Ramakien*.

The Thai version of the Ramayana had existed at Ayutthaya and was largely lost
in the sack. Rama I convened a commission and had it composed again, in 1797,
running to some fifty thousand verses — a national epic rebuilt from fragments
and memory by royal committee, thirty years after the library burned.

The same was done for the *Traiphum* cosmology, the court poetry, the dance
drama and the mural conventions. The classical Thai culture that exists now is
substantially an early Bangkok reconstruction of an Ayutthayan original, and it
is not always possible to tell which is which.

## What that bought

A century later, when Britain had taken Burma and France had taken Vietnam,
Cambodia and Laos, Siam was the one state in the region that was not colonised.

The reasons are usually given as diplomacy and buffer geography, and both are
real. But the thing being defended mattered: a continuous monarchy with an
articulated bureaucracy, a codified law, a national literature and a court that
could negotiate — all of it demonstrably older than the Europeans in the region,
and much of it rebuilt from ashes by men who had watched the original burn.

Ayutthaya is a ruin and a UNESCO site, and it is also the template the country
next door to it is still built on.
