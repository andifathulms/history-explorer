This record's `revenue_basis` is `trade-toll` and `monopoly`. Its neighbour
Angkor, a hundred and fifty miles away and the source of its court ritual, is
`land-tax` and nothing else. The other three records in this region are
`land-tax` or, at Champa, the coast's own combination.

That coding is the central argument of the book this record is drafted from, and
it is against the standard picture.

Ayutthaya is usually described as a rice kingdom on a fertile delta. Baker and
Pasuk's account is that the Chao Phraya delta was not intensively farmed for
export until the nineteenth century — the great canal-and-paddy landscape of
central Thailand is a later thing — and that what the crown actually lived on
was trade. Customs on the Gulf traffic. And, more importantly, royal monopolies
on what the hinterland produced: sappanwood, eaglewood, hides, ivory, tin, and
the sea products the China trade wanted, bought cheap through the corvée system
and sold through the king's own warehouses to Chinese, Japanese, Persian, Indian
and eventually European buyers.

The king was the kingdom's largest merchant, and for some commodities its only
legal one.

## A land power coded like a port

The consequence for this site is a small structural oddity worth naming.
Mainland Southeast Asia exists as a region because Maritime Asia's blurb says
its polities lived on controlling straits instead of territory, and Angkor,
Pagan and Dai Viet manifestly did not. Ayutthaya is on the mainland, fifty miles
inland, with a corvée labour system and a hinterland empire — and its revenue
fields read like Melaka's.

The region boundary is still right. Ayutthaya's tolls were collected at a river
port it controlled by holding the country behind it, which is not what a strait
polity does. But a reader comparing the two regions on the fields alone would
put this record on the wrong side, and the honest thing is to say so on the page
rather than let the tables imply a cleaner division than exists.
