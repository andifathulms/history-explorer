Ramathibodi I died in 1369 leaving a kingdom whose founder remains,
even in the Siamese chronicles' own telling, almost a blank: no
certain name before his royal title, no certain father, no account of
his life before he arrived at Ayutthaya through his marriage to a
local ruler's daughter. His son and heir, Ramesuen, proved unpopular
within a year - blamed for military incompetence in an earlier war
against Cambodia - and was persuaded by his own ministers to abdicate
in favour of his uncle, Borommaracha, governor of Suphanburi. The
handover was amicable enough that Ramesuen simply returned to his old
post as governor of Lopburi, and Borommaracha I spent the next
eighteen years completing what Ramathibodi had started: the conquest
of the rival Tai kingdom of Sukhothai to the north.

## A seven-day king

Borommaracha I died in 1388 marching to relieve a governor under
attack, and the throne passed to his fifteen-year-old son, Thong Lan.
Ramesuen, still governor of Lopburi and still, by strict right, the
founder's own son, marched on Ayutthaya at once, seized his young
cousin, and had him put to death after a reign of exactly seven days
- restoring himself to the throne he had voluntarily given up eighteen
years before. Five years later he led an army into Cambodia in
retaliation for a border raid, routed the Cambodian forces in the
field, and took the Khmer capital at Angkor Thom itself, installing a
Cambodian client king under a Siamese garrison and carrying off
ninety thousand prisoners - a full generation before the conquest that
would end Angkor's own independence for good.

## Fourteen years of nothing recorded

Ramesuen died in 1395, and his own son, Ramracha, took the throne to
a reign the chronicles pass over almost entirely - fourteen years in
which, as Wood's own sources put it, "nothing whatever is recorded as
having occurred." It ended in 1408 when Ramracha quarrelled with his
chief minister; the minister fled to Suphanburi and appealed to its
governor, Nakhon In, a nephew of Borommaracha I, who marched on
Ayutthaya, forced Ramracha's abdication, and took the throne himself
as Intharacha I.

## Two princes on a bridge

Intharacha I ruled for sixteen years, sending and receiving embassies
from Ming China and intervening in a succession dispute at Sukhothai
without ever fully annexing it. When he died in 1424, his two eldest
sons met on a bridge inside Ayutthaya itself, each mounted on his own
war elephant, to settle the succession by single combat between the
animals - and both princes were thrown and killed in the same
encounter. Their younger brother, left as the only claimant standing,
took the throne unopposed as Borommaracha II - the fifth change of
king in Ayutthaya's first seventy-three years, and the one who would,
within a decade of his own accession, finish what Ramesuen had
started at Angkor.
