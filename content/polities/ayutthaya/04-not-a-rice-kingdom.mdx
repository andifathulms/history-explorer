Ayutthaya is usually described as a rice kingdom on a fertile delta. Baker and
Pasuk's account, which the chapters here follow, is against the standard
picture.

The Chao Phraya delta was not intensively farmed for export until the nineteenth
century — the great canal-and-paddy landscape of central Thailand is a later
thing — and what the crown actually lived on was trade. Customs on the Gulf
traffic. And, more importantly, royal monopolies on what the hinterland produced:
sappanwood, eaglewood, hides, ivory, tin, and the sea products the China trade
wanted, bought cheap through the corvée system and sold on through the king's own
warehouses to Chinese, Japanese, Persian, Indian and eventually European buyers.

The king was the kingdom's largest merchant, and for some commodities its only
legal one.

## A land power that lived like a port

Angkor, a hundred and fifty miles away and the source of Ayutthaya's court
ritual, lived on land tax and almost nothing else. So did its other neighbours
inland. Ayutthaya sat fifty miles up a river with a corvée labour system and a
hinterland empire, and made its money the way Melaka did.

It is worth being careful about what that resemblance means. A strait polity
lives by sitting astride somebody else's route and charging for passage.
Ayutthaya's tolls were collected at a river port it controlled by holding the
whole country behind it — the hinterland was not a route to be taxed but an
estate to be worked, and the monopolies only functioned because the corvée that
supplied them reached deep inland.

Same ledger, then, and a completely different state underneath it.
