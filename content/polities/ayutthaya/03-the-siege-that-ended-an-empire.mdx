Borommaracha II, crowned unopposed after his two elder brothers
killed each other in single combat, proved a warlike and capable
king from the start of his reign, and within a few years he turned
his own army south against Cambodia again. His great-uncle Ramesuen
had sacked Angkor once already, a generation earlier, installing a
client king before withdrawing; Borommaracha II went further. His
army invested the Khmer capital itself and held it under siege for
seven months. Tammasok, the Cambodian king, died during the siege -
whether by illness or by the strain of the campaign, the chronicles
do not say - and when the city finally fell, Borommaracha II placed
his own son, the Prince of Inthaburi, on the Cambodian throne as a
client ruler.

## A throne that did not stay in Siamese hands

The arrangement did not hold. After the Siamese army withdrew, the
young Prince of Inthaburi died - murdered, according to Cambodian
accounts of the same events - and a Cambodian prince took the throne
in his place under the title Borommaracha Thirat Ramathibodi,
apparently without Ayutthaya contesting it. His first act was to
move the Khmer capital away from Angkor entirely, to Phnom Penh - a
relocation driven by simple geography: Angkor sat dangerously close
to the new Siamese frontier that had just proven it could take the
city twice in one generation. Angkor would never again serve as a
Khmer capital.

## A city, not just a treasury

Borommaracha II brought back bronze images and a great number of
prisoners from the campaign - the ordinary spoils of a medieval
siege. But an army that spends seven months occupying a working
capital carries off more than bronze and captives: the men who ran
that capital's own court came north too.
