King Narai's court in the 1680s had gone further toward Europe than any other in
Southeast Asia. His chief minister was a Greek adventurer, Constantine
Phaulkon, who had arrived as an employee of the English East India Company.
There were French troops garrisoned in two forts, at Bangkok and Mergui.
Embassies had gone to Versailles and been received with ceremony. A French
mission in Ayutthaya believed, and reported home, that the king was on the point
of converting to Catholicism.

In 1688, as Narai lay dying, the commander of the elephant corps seized the
palace. Phaulkon was executed. Narai's heirs were killed. The French garrisons
were besieged and then allowed to leave, and the commander took the throne as
Phetracha.

Siam then dealt with Europeans on its own terms for the next century and a half.

## The comparison this site can now make

Set this beside the two records one region over that this site wrote in the same
season.

Melaka, in 1511, was taken by about fifteen ships and twelve hundred men, and
never recovered. Aceh, from 1873, was ground down over thirty years by a Dutch
state that lost its first commander in the first month and still won. Kilwa was
sacked in 1505 by a fleet that abandoned the fort within eight years and had
already destroyed the town's position.

Ayutthaya had European soldiers inside its own capital's defences and removed
them in a palace coup, and the removal held. Ternate expelled the Portuguese by
siege in 1575 and was a Dutch company's vassal a century later; this is the one
that expelled them and then was not.

The difference is not courage or cunning. It is that Ayutthaya was a large
agrarian-labour state with an inland capital and a population it could conscript,
and the port polities were cities on water that could be reached by ships. What
the European companies could take in this region, for two more centuries, was
exactly what could be taken from the sea.
