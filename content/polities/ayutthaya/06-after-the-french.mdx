Simon de la Loubère reached Ayutthaya as Louis XIV's envoy in September 1687
and left in January 1688. Four months later the reign he had been negotiating
with was over, the Greek favourite who had arranged everything was dead, and
the country closed the door he had come through.

His book is the last European description of Ayutthaya before that happened.

## What he saw at court

He is exact about the physical protocol, and the protocol is the political
system.

When the king moves, the guards follow *confusedly and without any order*; the
courtiers walk with their hands joined on their breast; and when he stops,
everyone on foot prostrates themselves *on their Knees and Elbows*, while those
on horses or elephants bow down entirely on the animal.

He notes a category of royal attendants — he transcribes the word as *Scham
mow* — who follow on foot, and specifies that they are *the King's Domesticks,
which are not Slaves*, which tells you what the default assumption was.

He records that revolutions are common at Siam and refers his readers to two
earlier accounts for examples, which is a striking thing to print about a court
you have just concluded a treaty with, four months before it happened again.

## Why the French were there at all

Because of Constantine Phaulkon, a Greek who had come east with the English East
India Company, entered Siamese service, and risen to run the kingdom's foreign
trade and much of its foreign policy.

Phaulkon's project was a French alliance: embassies both ways, French officers,
French garrisons at Bangkok and Mergui, and — the part that destroyed him —
the suggestion to Versailles that King Narai might be converted.

He was not wrong that Ayutthaya wanted a counterweight. The kingdom had spent
the century balancing the Dutch, who had the shipping and were prepared to
blockade the river to get trading terms, against everyone else. What Phaulkon
supplied was a European power with no commercial position in the region and
therefore, in theory, no leverage to use.

## 1688

Narai fell ill in the spring of 1688. Phra Phetracha, the commander of the
elephant corps, took the palace, executed Phaulkon, killed the heirs, married
into the royal house and took the throne when Narai died.

The French garrison at Bangkok was besieged and allowed to sail away. The
missionaries were imprisoned. The treaties were void.

It is usually described as a xenophobic reaction and it was narrower than that.
The Dutch stayed and went on trading. Chinese, Indian, Persian and Japanese
merchants stayed. What was expelled was a specific arrangement — foreign troops
in Siamese forts under a foreign favourite — that a substantial part of the
court had regarded as intolerable for years.

## What the century afterwards looked like

Not decline in the ordinary sense. Trade with China grew through the eighteenth
century and Ayutthaya's Chinese population with it; the rice export to the
Chinese coast became the kingdom's most valuable business.

What did change is that the court stopped taking European powers seriously as
partners and stopped being taken seriously by them, and the succession problem
did not improve. The throne passed by palace violence more often than not, and
the reign that ended in 1767 began with the usual eliminations.

So the kingdom that met the Burmese army in the 1760s was rich, commercially
sophisticated, and had spent eighty years without a stable rule for replacing
its own kings.
