No Chinese, Arab or Turkish record uses the name by which the western
world came to know these people. They called themselves Kipchaks,
after the vast northwestern steppe - now the Kirghiz steppe - their
tribes came pouring out of, pressed westward by the growing Naiman
power on the Irtysh as the crowded Turkic world of Central Asia ran
out of room. Every neighbor gave them a different name: Greeks called
them Comans, Russians called them Polovtsy ("steppe folk"), the
Saxons of Transylvania called them Valvi or Falben ("sallow people"),
and Hungarians used both Kun and Palocz. Only the Arabs and Georgians
used the name Kipchak itself.

## A path opened by two empires' collapse

For generations two great states had held the crowded Turkic hordes
back from Europe: the Samanid emirate on the Oxus and the Khazar
empire on the Volga. Both collapsed around the end of the tenth
century, opening the way south for the Seljuks and north for the
Kipchaks alike. Crossing into the grasslands between the Volga and the
Don, the Kipchaks conquered what remained of the Khazars, drove the
Alans south into the Caucasus, and swept up the Torks and Pechenegs
who had already been broken by the Russians. Their advance moved fast:
they reached the borders of Rus by 1054, crossed the Carpathians by
1070, and stood on the Danube by 1090.

## Allies before they were enemies

The Kipchaks' first major action in Europe was fighting for, not
against, a settled power. Having decisively broken the Pechenegs in
1087, they were called on by the Byzantine emperor Alexius to help
meet a fresh mass of Pecheneg refugees threatening his own European
territory, and shared in his great victory over them at Lebounion in
1091. The gratitude did not last. Having tasted the wealth of the
grasslands and cities beyond their old range, the Kipchaks spent the
next fifty years as one of the most persistent threats the Greek,
Hungarian and Russian frontier provinces faced.
