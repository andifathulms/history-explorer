Enormous numbers of Kipchaks died in the Mongol conquest - the
Franciscan traveler Carpini described bones and skulls strewn the
entire distance from Kiev to the Mongol capital - but the survivors
scattered into a wider range of foreign courts and careers than almost
any defeated steppe people before them. Thousands were sold into
slavery through Crimea by Venetian and Genoese merchants; a large
group took refuge in Bulgaria and were gradually absorbed into the
Bulgarian nation; and the main body, by every account, simply went on
living on its old pasture lands under new Mongol masters, forming the
demographic core of the Crimean and Nogai Tatars for centuries to
come - the name Kipchak itself surviving among the Crimean Tatars, one
Uzbek tribe, and one of the largest Kirghiz-Kazakh groupings.

## A king who shaved his beard for them

Hungary took in the largest organized group of refugees, led by Kotyan
himself, and treated them well enough that one Hungarian king was said
to have "loved the Cumans more than was proper." Their descendants
settled the Tisza valley in such numbers that two counties, Great and
Little Kunság, still carried their name, and their own language
survived there in daily use into the eighteenth century, recorded in
the Codex Cumanicus. King Ladislas IV, himself half Kipchak, was
rebuked by a papal legate for adopting Turkish weapons, speaking
Turkish and shaving his beard in the Turkish fashion - and was
eventually killed by Kipchaks himself, in a brawl, after deserting his
wife for three Turkish women. Kipchak cavalry fought for Hungary at
the decisive battle of the Marchfield in 1278, where the Czech king
Otakar lost his life, and were still serving Poland against the
Teutonic Knights at Płowce as late as 1331.

## Thrones across half of Eastern Europe

Marriage carried Kipchak blood further than conquest ever had. Konchak's
own grandfather Otrok's kinsman Kotyan married one daughter to a
leading Russian prince and another to the king of Hungary, where she
became regent and the mother of Ladislas IV; the Bulgarian adventurer
Tertery married his own daughter to the king of Serbia. By the
thirteenth century, rulers of Kipchak descent sat, at one time or
another, on the thrones of Russia, Hungary, Bulgaria, Serbia, Georgia
and even the restored Byzantine imperial house - a dynasty scattered by
conquest into becoming ancestors of half the royal houses of Eastern
Europe.

## Slaves who became sultans

The most striking Kipchak afterlife of all played out far from the
steppe entirely. Kipchak boys sold into slavery in Egypt rose through
the Mamluk military system to found a ruling dynasty of their own:
Baybars, Qalawun, Lajin and others among them, Baybars greatest of
all - not only one of the most formidable sultans Egypt ever produced,
but the first general in history to defeat the previously invincible
Mongol army in open battle. The Mamluk sultans never severed their own
connection to the steppe, maintaining relations with the Golden Horde's
own court for generations after the conquest that had scattered their
ancestors in the first place - former slaves who ended up ruling an
empire, still looking back toward the grassland their own people no
longer held.
