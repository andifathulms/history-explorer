Driven off the northern grassland by Monomakh's campaigns, the
Kipchaks fell back toward the Black Sea, tightening their hold on
Crimea and the steppes of the Kuban and Kuma and turning south toward
the wealth of the Caucasus instead. A Kipchak khan named Otrok fled to
Abkhazia in the aftermath of Monomakh's victories and, after years in
exile, returned to the steppe as the father of Konchak - the khan who
would come to dominate the next generation's worth of Russian
chronicles. A related figure, Atrak, entered Georgian service outright:
his daughter married King David II, his own followers helped defeat
the Seljuk sultan in battle, and later Kipchak mercenaries went on
serving under George III and Queen Tamara, one of them, a general
named Qubasar, achieving real distinction in both reigns.

## A raid that outlived its own chronicle

In Russia, a succession struggle that ended in Kiev being sacked by
its own Prince Andrew weakened the south for good and normalized
Kipchak involvement in every princely quarrel that followed.
Konchak stepped into the resulting vacuum, threatening the southern
principalities for two decades in a manner the frontier had not
faced since Bonyak's own day. His conflict with Prince Igor of
Novgorod-Seversk, who set out in 1185 hoping to "drink the Don in his
golden helm" as Monomakh once had, ended in catastrophe for the
Russians - a defeat significant enough to produce both a detailed
prose account in the Kievan Chronicle and the great medieval epic *The
Tale of the Host of Igor*. The monkish chroniclers heaped Konchak with
every abusive epithet reserved for a pagan enemy, even as intermarriage
between Turks and Russians continued and the Druzhina class seems to
have regarded him, in practice, as an honorable adversary.

## A frontier that found a new direction

Serious danger from the Kipchaks eased after 1187, whether from
Konchak's own death or from the pull of a new opportunity: in that
same year the chronicle records that "the Polovtsy had gone to the
Danube," drawn by the rise of a new Bulgarian state in which Kipchaks
and their Wallachian subjects played a central role - the later
Bulgarian king Tertery was, by the record, a Kipchak outright. That
southward pull brought Kipchak riders up to the walls of Constantinople
itself, where Western chroniclers like Villehardouin recorded them
scouring the land "up to the gates" during the Fourth Crusade,
"destroying the cities and castles" with a thoroughness the chronicler
claims no one had ever heard the like of.
