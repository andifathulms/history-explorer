Pilliya, whose reign followed Ispudahsu's by an unknown span of time,
is the best-documented king Kizzuwatna ever had - not because any
single account of his reign survives, but because two entirely
separate archives, on two sides of the kingdom, both happened to
preserve traces of it. To the north, a treaty with the Hittite king
Zidanta II placed the relationship with Hatti on record in the same
formal terms Ispudahsu had established a generation earlier. To the
east, an altogether different kind of document shows Pilliya dealing
directly with a Syrian neighbor on his own account.

## A contract between two kings, not two empires

Idrimi, an exiled prince who had spent years as a fugitive before
carving out a kingdom of his own at Alalah, concluded a private
agreement with Pilliya fixing the border and obligations between their
two states. The document reads as a negotiation between equals, with
no overlord named anywhere in its text - even though Idrimi himself
was, at the very same time, a subject of the Mittanian king Paratarna.
Scholars have long debated whether this silence means Pilliya was
also, by implication, under Mittanian authority; the more defensible
reading is that the contract was simply a private arrangement between
two rulers, its Mittanian context relevant to Idrimi's own position
but not spelled out because it did not need to be, since the agreement
itself was not a matter Mittani's own king needed to approve.

## Two archives, one reign

That a single king's own actions should surface independently in a
Hittite royal archive and a Syrian city-state's palace records, without
either apparently referencing the other, says something about the
position Kizzuwatna actually occupied: not a satellite fixed to one
greater power, but a kingdom with real business, and real leverage, on
more than one frontier at once.
