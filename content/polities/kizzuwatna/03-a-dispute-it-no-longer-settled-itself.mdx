A generation after Pilliya, King Sunassura's own reign shows how far
Kizzuwatna's position had shifted. A small legal document from Alalah
records a territorial dispute between Sunassura and Niqmepa, king of
that city, over a town on their shared border - the same kind of
frontier question Pilliya and Idrimi had once settled directly,
ruler to ruler, with no overlord named in the text at all. This time
the case was not settled between the two kings themselves. It was
decided by Saustatar, king of Mittani, sitting in judgment over both
of them.

## What a verdict from Mittani actually meant

Saustatar's own reign is the high point of Mittanian power in
northern Syria, and a Mittanian king arbitrating a Cilician border
dispute is not, on its own, proof that Kizzuwatna had been conquered
or annexed - nothing in the record describes a Mittanian campaign
against Kizzuwatna, and Sunassura continued to rule as a named king
in his own right, not as a Mittanian governor. But a kingdom capable
of settling its own borders, as Pilliya's generation plainly had, does
not submit a boundary dispute to a foreign court unless that court's
authority to decide the matter is already accepted as real. The
verdict itself is the evidence: Kizzuwatna had gone, within two
generations, from a kingdom negotiating its own borders to one whose
border disputes were adjudicated by an outside power.

## An interlude, not a permanent arrangement

Mittanian oversight of Kizzuwatna's affairs did not last as long as it
might have. Saustatar's own verdict is the only firm evidence this
subordination ever produced, and it is Sunassura's own later treaty
with the Hittite king Tudhaliya I, rather than any further Mittanian
document, that closes out his reign in the surviving record. Whatever
the exact terms of Kizzuwatna's dependency on Mittani had been, they
were replaced, not renewed, by a different arrangement with a
different power - a transition the treaty that followed makes plain.
