title: What outlasted the throne

Kizzuwatna's political history ends quietly, with a treaty rather than
a battle, but its religious traditions did not end with it. Kummanni,
the kingdom's own capital, was a major center of Hurrian cult before
the Hittite state ever administered it directly, and that religious
inheritance proved far more durable than the throne that had once
presided over it.

## A conquest that imported the conquered

Once Kizzuwatna passed into the Hittite orbit, its priesthoods, its
ritual texts and its Hurrian religious vocabulary did not disappear
into the wider Hittite state - they were absorbed into it deliberately,
the same acquisitive habit that let Hatti fold in the deities of every
land it touched rather than replace them. Purification and exorcism
rites associated with Kizzuwatna's own ritual specialists were copied
into the archives at Hattusa and continued to be practiced there long
after the kingdom that produced them had stopped being administered as
anything but a Hittite province.

## An influence outlasting an independence

The result is a lopsided kind of afterlife: a kingdom whose kings are
known mostly from a seal, two treaties and a private contract left a
religious footprint in the Hittite record that outweighs everything
its own political history can document. Kizzuwatna stopped being a
place with a king of its own within two generations of Sunassura's
treaty with Tudhaliya I; it did not stop being a place whose priests
and rituals the Hittite court kept drawing on for a very great deal
longer than that.
