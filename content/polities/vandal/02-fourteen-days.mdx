In 455 a Vandal fleet appeared at the mouth of the Tiber. The western emperor had
been murdered a fortnight earlier and his successor was killed by a mob as he
tried to leave the city. Pope Leo met Geiseric outside the walls, and the terms
agreed were that the city would not be burned and the population not massacred.

The Vandals then spent fourteen days removing everything portable. The gilded
bronze roof tiles of the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus. The treasures Titus had
brought from Jerusalem in 71, which had been in Rome for three hundred and
eighty-four years. Statues, plate, and several thousand people including the
empress and her two daughters, one of whom was married to Geiseric's son.

## The word

A French bishop, Henri Grégoire, coined *vandalisme* in 1794 to describe
revolutionaries destroying churches and works of art, and the word has been doing
this kingdom's reputation for two hundred and thirty years.

Merrills and Miles are direct about how unearned it is. Prosper of Aquitaine, the
nearest contemporary source, says the city was not burned and there was no
slaughter. The sack was systematic, negotiated and acquisitive — a fortnight of
organised removal by a state that wanted the contents rather than the destruction
— and by the standards of what happened to cities in this corpus it is
conspicuously restrained. This site's Byzantine record has a crusading army
sacking Constantinople for three days in 1204 with considerably worse results and
no word named after it.

What made the reputation was the confessional quarrel in the next chapter, which
gave Catholic writers a reason to describe the regime as barbarous, plus the
accident of Grégoire needing a term.

## The armada

The kingdom's actual military achievement is thirteen years later and almost
nobody has heard of it.

In 468 the eastern and western empires mounted a joint expedition against
Carthage: over a thousand ships, an army under Basiliscus, and a cost the sources
put at something like a hundred thousand pounds of gold — several years of the
eastern empire's revenue. It reached Cape Bon. Geiseric asked for five days to
consider terms, used them to prepare fireships, and destroyed the fleet at
anchor.

The consequences are on three records in this corpus. The western empire, which
had staked everything on recovering Africa, was finished within eight years. The
eastern empire was bankrupt for a generation, which is part of why this site's
Byzantine record's first chapter describes a fifth century of paying people to go
away. And this kingdom held the western Mediterranean unchallenged for the next
sixty-six years.

It is the most consequential naval battle in this corpus, and this record's third
turning point says so.
