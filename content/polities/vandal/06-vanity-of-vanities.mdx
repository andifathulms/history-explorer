Before the final battle at Tricamarum, Gelimer drew his people up inside a
stockade with everything they owned piled in the middle of it, and made a
speech. Procopius gives it, and its last clause is the one to keep.

If we win, he told them, we shall either live among good things or, dying
gloriously, leave prosperity to our wives and children — *while the name of the
Vandals will survive and their empire be preserved*.

They lost. He fled to a mountain, was besieged there through a winter, and
surrendered.

## The triumph

He was brought to Constantinople and led in Belisarius's procession with the
Vandal treasure — including, Procopius notes elsewhere, the vessels Titus had
taken from the temple at Jerusalem, which had been carried to Rome, then to
Carthage by Geiseric, and were now going to Constantinople.

Procopius watched Gelimer walking in it. Seeing what condition he was in, he
writes, the king neither wept nor cried out, but kept saying over the words of
the Hebrew scripture: *Vanity of vanities, all is vanity*.

At the emperor's seat they stripped the purple off him and made him fall prone
and do obeisance to Justinian. Belisarius did the same, as a suppliant of the
emperor alongside him.

## The settlement

Then the extraordinary part, and it is entirely characteristic of how Rome
disposed of defeated kings when it was in a good mood.

Justinian and Theodora gave the children of Hilderic, and the surviving
descendants of the emperor Valentinian, sufficient sums of money. To Gelimer
they gave estates in Galatia — Procopius says lands not to be despised — and
permission to live there with his family.

He was not, however, enrolled among the patricians. He was offered it and he
would have had to become a Nicene Christian to accept, and he would not give up
the faith of Arius.

That is the last recorded act of the last Vandal king: declining a Roman
senatorial rank, on an Anatolian estate, rather than change his confession.

## What survived

Almost nothing, and the completeness is genuinely unusual.

The Vandals had been in Africa for a hundred and five years. Their fighting men
were shipped east and put into the Persian war; their women married into the
occupying army; their estates went back to the church and to the empire; their
church was dismantled. Within a generation there was no Vandal community in
Africa, no Vandal language, no Vandal law, and no successor claiming the name.

Gelimer had told his people that if they fought well the name of the Vandals
would survive. It survived exactly once more, in French, thirteen centuries
later, as a word for people who smash things — invented by a bishop during the
Revolution, on the strength of the fortnight in Rome, about the one western
kingdom that had made a policy of taking things away intact.
