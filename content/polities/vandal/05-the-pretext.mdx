The Vandal kingdom was destroyed because of a family quarrel that a foreign
emperor was invited to have an opinion about.

Hilderic, great-grandson of Geiseric, came to the throne in 523. He was unlike
his predecessors in two respects that turned out to matter. His mother was a
Roman princess — a daughter of the emperor Valentinian III, taken to Carthage
after the sack of Rome — and he was, in Procopius's account, a very particular
friend of Justinian's, the two having corresponded and exchanged gifts before
Justinian came to the throne.

He was also, Procopius says, unwarlike and not willing even to hear of war, and
he left military matters to a kinsman.

That kinsman was Gelimer, and Procopius describes him as the ablest warrior of
his time and already, in the popular reckoning, second only to the king. He did
not wait. He gathered the Vandals who were dissatisfied, took the throne while
Hilderic was still alive, and put him and his relatives in prison.

## The letter

Justinian wrote to him. Procopius gives the substance of the exchange, and it is
worth noticing what each side thought the argument was.

Justinian's position was that Geiseric had laid down that the throne should
always pass to the eldest surviving male of his line; that Gelimer would in due
course have had it anyway; and that he should therefore leave the old man alone
and be content to wait, holding the power in fact while Hilderic held the title.
Take the reality, in effect, and leave the form.

Gelimer's reply was that he had not seized the throne by violence but had been
given it by the Vandals, because Hilderic was plotting against the house of
Geiseric; that this was an internal matter; and that the emperor of the Romans
would do better to mind his own kingdom.

He then had Hilderic put to death.

## What the correspondence really was

Neither man was writing to persuade the other.

Justinian had spent five years looking for a way into Africa and could not
justify a war of aggression against a kingdom the empire had recognised by
treaty. What he needed was a legitimate Vandal king, wrongfully deposed, whose
restoration could be the object of the expedition. Gelimer supplied one, and
then removed him, which improved the case further.

By the time Belisarius sailed in 533 the war had a rationale that could be
stated in a sentence: the emperor was restoring the lawful heir of Geiseric
against a usurper. That the lawful heir was dead by then was not an obstacle.

## The other weakness

The reason it worked so completely is that the Vandals had spent a century
making enemies of the population they lived on.

They were Arians ruling a Nicene province, and unlike the Ostrogoths in Italy
they had not left the matter alone. Catholic bishops had been exiled, churches
confiscated, and at intervals the persecution had been severe. Hilderic had
begun reversing it — which is part of why the Vandal nobility removed him.

So the invasion of 533 arrived in a country whose landowners had been
dispossessed, whose church had been persecuted, and whose king had just killed
his predecessor. Belisarius landed with about fifteen thousand men, three
months' sailing from home, against a kingdom that had beaten a force ten times
that size sixty-five years before.

He was in Carthage in under two weeks.
