Belisarius landed in Africa in the summer of 533 with about sixteen thousand men,
which is not a large army by the standards of the expedition the Vandals had
destroyed sixty-five years earlier. He beat the Vandal army outside Carthage in
September, took the city, beat it again at Tricamarum in December, and Gelimer
surrendered in the spring. The kingdom lasted one campaigning season.

## The traditional answer and the better one

The traditional answer is decadence: a warrior people softened by ninety-five
years of African luxury, described by Procopius in a famous passage about baths,
banquets, gardens and dancing girls.

Merrills and Miles do not accept it, and this record is coded from them. The
Vandal army of 533 was not obviously worse than the one of 468; what had changed
was that the earlier one was defending a fleet at anchor in home waters against
an amphibious assault and the later one was fighting a first-rate general on
land, having just lost its own fleet and best troops to a revolt in Sardinia that
Belisarius's timing exploited.

The structural answer is in this record's first chapter. A regime that had
dispossessed the provincial aristocracy of the land — rather than taking a share
of the tax on it, as the Ostrogothic record beside this one did in Italy — and
that had spent a century enforcing Arianism against the Catholic hierarchy of a
Catholic province, had no reserve of loyalty anywhere outside its own people.
Procopius has the African cities opening to Belisarius. They had no reason not
to.

That is the same finding this corpus's Visigothic record reaches from the
opposite direction: the one post-Roman kingdom that converted to its subjects'
church lasted a hundred and twenty-two years longer than the ones that did not.

## What the fields say

Three zero influence counts, no reach figure, four coded institutions.

The `succession_rule` is `tanistry`, which is rare on this site, and it is
legislated: Geiseric's own law provided that the throne should pass to the eldest
surviving male of the royal house rather than from father to son. That is a
written succession rule, which puts this record in the small group this corpus
keeps coming back to — Telipinu's Edict, Esarhaddon's oath, the Hasmonean decree,
the Fourth Council of Toledo, the Golden Bull, the Venetian ballot.

It worked, in the narrow sense that the kingdom had orderly successions for
ninety-five years, which is more than the Visigothic or Ostrogothic records
managed. It also meant that the man on the throne in 533 was Geiseric's
great-grandson by seniority, who had taken it in a coup two years earlier from a
cousin friendly to Constantinople — and that coup was Justinian's stated pretext
for the invasion.

A succession law that produces orderly successions and hands the throne to a
usurper at the worst possible moment is a fair note to end this record on.

## And afterwards

Africa was a Byzantine province for a century and a half, and this corpus's
Aghlabid, Zirid and Hafsid records sit on the same ground later. Nobody claimed
the Vandal kingdom's legitimacy: `successor_claims` is zero, and there is no
Vandal afterlife except the word.
