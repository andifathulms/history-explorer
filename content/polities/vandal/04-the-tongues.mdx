Geiseric died in 477, and the son who followed him did the one thing his father
had been careful not to do.

Procopius is brief and unambiguous about Huneric. During his reign the Vandals
had no war with anyone except the Moors — and he showed himself the most cruel
and unjust of all men toward the Christians in Africa. He forced them to change
over to the Arian faith, and as many as he found not readily yielding he burned,
or destroyed by other forms of death.

And then the detail that fixed the reign in memory:

> he also cut off the tongues of many from the very throat, who even up to my
> time were going about in Byzantium having their speech uninjured, and
> perceiving not the least effect from this punishment.

Procopius saw them. He was writing about eighty years after the event and the men
were old, and he does not treat the story as hearsay: they were walking about the
capital, talking. He then adds, in the same breath and without any apparent sense
that it undercuts what he has just called a miracle, that two of them saw fit to
go in to harlots and were thereafter no longer able to speak.

That sentence is the whole of Procopius in miniature. He reports the wonder
because he watched it, and he reports the exception because he heard it, and he
does not reconcile them.

## What the persecution was for

It was not an eccentricity of one king. It was the logic of the settlement
arriving at its conclusion.

The Vandals had taken the land of the African senatorial class rather than a
share of the tax on it, and they were Arians in a province whose bishops were
the effective leaders of every city and whose congregations were the
dispossessed. A regime in that position has two options: leave the church alone
and live with a permanent alternative authority, or try to absorb it. Huneric
tried to absorb it, at a moment when the kingdom was at the height of its power
and had just been left unchallenged by both empires for a decade.

The cost did not fall due for fifty years.

## Cyprian

Procopius tells one more story about Huneric's church policy, and he tells it in
the wrong place on purpose — not in the century of Vandal history he opens with,
but in the middle of the landing of 533, because that is where it pays off.

Cyprian, the third-century bishop and martyr, was the saint the Carthaginians
revered above all others. They had built him a notable shrine outside the city
on the sea-shore, and held a festival there every year called the Cypriana, at a
season so reliably stormy that the sailors named the storm after the feast. In
Huneric's reign the Vandals took the shrine from the Catholics by force, drove
their priests out of it in great dishonour, and thereafter kept the festival
themselves, saying that it now belonged to the Arians.

The Africans were angry and at a loss. But Cyprian, they said, kept sending them
a dream: that there was not the least need for the Christians to be concerned
about him, for he would in time be his own avenger. The story went round the
whole province, and everyone expected some vengeance to come on the Vandals
because of that festival, and nobody could imagine how.

Belisarius landed in the summer of 533. The day after the first battle was the
day of the Cypriana. The Arian priests, with the Vandal army marching out to meet
the invasion and no reason yet to think it would fail, had cleaned the whole
sanctuary, hung up the finest of the votive offerings, made the lamps ready,
brought the treasures out of the storerooms, and arranged everything exactly as
it should be for the feast.

The battle at Decimum went the other way. The Arian priests fled. The Catholics
of Carthage walked into a shrine prepared for them by the men who had taken it,
lit every lamp, and kept the festival as they had always kept it.

Procopius files it among the omens, which is where a sixth-century reader would
want it. It works equally well as a description of what the persecution had
bought: a province in which the population's first act on the arrival of a
foreign army was to repossess its own church, and an Arian clergy that had
prepared the building for them without knowing it.
