title: The last of the three

The Ottoman sultanate was abolished by the Grand National Assembly at Ankara on
1 November 1922. Mehmed VI left Constantinople on a British warship a fortnight
later. The caliphate, which had been separated from the sultanate and left with
a cousin, was abolished in March 1924, and the family was expelled.

Three things end there, and this site records them differently on purpose.

The **state** ends in 1922, and the record types it `fragmentation` with no
agent, because there is none in the vocabulary's sense. No polity conquered the
Ottoman empire. It entered the First World War on the losing side, was
partitioned by the victors under the Treaty of Sèvres, and the partition was
then overturned by a movement out of its own army which established a republic
in its Anatolian core and abolished the monarchy itself.

The **dynasty** ends there too, after six hundred and twenty-three years — the
longest continuous rule by a single family in this corpus, and one of the
longest anywhere. Fourteen generations, no successful usurpation from outside
the house, and a succession that changed its rules twice without ever changing
the family.

The **caliphate** ends in 1924, and it is the third time this corpus has
recorded that office stopping. It ended at Baghdad in 1258 when Hulegu killed
al-Musta'sim; it ended at Cairo in 1517 when Selim removed al-Mutawakkil III to
Constantinople; and it ended in 1924 when a parliament voted. Those three pages
— Abbasid, Abbasid Caliphate of Cairo, and this one — are the whole institutional
history of the office as this site holds it, and the last of the three is the
only one that was not ended by an army.

What this page cannot show is the size of what changed. The rating panel gives
a reach figure for 1683, a longevity range and three influence counts. It has
no field for the fact that the political geography of the modern Middle East,
the Balkans and the Caucasus is substantially the shape this empire's
dissolution left, or that the successor states drew borders the Ottomans had
used as provincial boundaries, or that the argument about the caliphate did not
stop in 1924 and has not stopped since.
