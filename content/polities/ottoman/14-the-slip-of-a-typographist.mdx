Midhat Pasha arrived at the Danube province in 1864 to run an experiment: could
the reforms decreed piecemeal from Constantinople since 1839 — the Tanzimat,
promising equality before the law and regular taxation to subjects who had
mostly never seen either — actually be built, in one province, all at once?
Aali and Fuad Pashas gave him Widdin, Silistria and Nish combined into a single
government and told him to find out.

He divided the province into districts, cantons and communes, and put an
elected council at each level for tax assessment and local administration.
Forced labour was abolished. Fourteen hundred bridges and two thousand miles of
road were built. Brigandage was put down and a local gendarmerie raised in its
place. Agricultural banks were founded to get farmers out from under
moneylenders, funded by bringing waste land under cultivation rather than by a
new tax. Two Ottoman-flagged steamers appeared on the Danube where none had
sailed before, then four. A postal service started. A carriage works at
Rustchuk paid a ten per cent dividend in its first year. Orphan asylums took in
Christian and Muslim children on the same terms and taught them trades.

None of it worked by decree alone. The councils did the property valuations
themselves, which is why the new taxes were paid without the complaints that
had followed every earlier attempt, even though officials' salaries went up and
a long list of vexatious levies came off the books at the same time. Revenue
rose regardless. Within a year the Sublime Porte was congratulating its
governor and ordering every other province to copy what he had done.

## The schools

Midhat's own account of what he thought the Danube reform was actually for
comes through most clearly in the one project that never happened.

Bulgarian families with any ambition for their sons had one real option for a
higher education, which was to send them to the Russian universities at
Odessa, Kharkov and Kiev — and a good number of the young men who came back
from that route came back as organisers for the Panslav committees working to
detach the province from Constantinople altogether. Midhat's answer was to
build the university at home: modern schools in the province's main towns,
Christian and Muslim students together, paid for out of the reform's own
revenue surplus and by subscription, so that the choice of Odessa never had to
be made.

The Russian ambassador, Nikolai Ignatiev, understood exactly what the schools
were for and worked to kill them before they opened, arguing to the sultan that
elected local councils were themselves a step toward the province declaring
independence, as Egypt effectively had. It should not have been enough on its
own — Abdulaziz was not an easy man to move by argument alone. What finished
it, on the account of a man well placed to know, was a single word: the
province's official gazette, describing the vilayet council, printed the
members as *deputies*. A compositor's mistake, corrected the following issue,
was still in circulation long enough for Ignatiev to put it in front of the
sultan as proof of what the councils really meant. Abdulaziz withdrew his
consent, citing the expense.

The reform that had built the roads and the banks and the gendarmerie survived
the refusal. The reform meant to give the empire's own subjects a reason not to
look toward Odessa did not, and no substitute for it was tried again before the
province was gone.
