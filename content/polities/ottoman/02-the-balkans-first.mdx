title: The Balkans first
drafted_from: imber-2002

The standard picture has the Ottomans conquering Anatolia and then crossing
into Europe. They did it the other way round, and almost everything about the
empire follows from that.

Ottoman forces were operating in Thrace by the 1350s and took Edirne, which
became the capital, in about 1369 — eighty years before Constantinople and at a
point when the beylik still held only a corner of north-western Anatolia. The
Balkan campaigns of Murad I and Bayezid I brought in Bulgaria, Macedonia,
Thessaly and Serbia: Kosovo in 1389, where Murad was killed in his own victory,
and Nicopolis in 1396, where a crusading army raised from most of Latin
Christendom was destroyed.

Two things about this order of operations shaped the state permanently.

The first is that the empire's richest and most densely populated provinces
were European for most of its history, and its army, revenue and administration
were built around them. Rumelia — the Balkans — was the senior half of the
empire in Ottoman usage, ahead of Anatolia, and stayed so.

The second is the *devshirme*, which the aside later on this page describes: a
levy of Christian boys from Balkan villages, converted, trained and made into
the sultan's household troops and administrators. That institution required a
large subject Christian peasantry, and the Balkans supplied it. An empire that
had expanded into Anatolia first could not have built it.

The Anatolian beyliks were absorbed afterwards and with more difficulty than
the Balkans, because they were Muslim Turkish polities with their own dynastic
legitimacy and taking them looked like aggression against fellow Muslims rather
than frontier warfare against unbelievers. Bayezid took most of them in the
1390s, and then in 1402 Timur came west, destroyed his army at Ankara, took him
prisoner, and restored the beyliks. The Ottoman state broke into a civil war
between Bayezid's sons that ran eleven years.

It reassembled. That it could reassemble — that a dynasty could lose its
sultan, its army and its Anatolian provinces and be governing all of them again
within twenty years — is the first real evidence that this was a state rather
than a successful warband.
