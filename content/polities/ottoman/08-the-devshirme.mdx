title: The levy of boys

The slave-soldier institution had been running in the Islamic world for six
hundred years by the time the Ottomans built theirs, and it almost always worked
the same way: a ruler buys foreign boys, trains them, arms them, and is deposed
by them within a few generations. Samarra, Ghazna, the Bahriyya. The Ottoman
version was different in three specific ways, and it is the only version that
produced an administration rather than a coup.

The *devshirme* was a levy rather than a purchase. Officials went into Christian
villages in the Balkans and Anatolia at intervals and took boys — a proportion
of households, selected on physical and mental promise, mostly aged between
eight and eighteen. They were converted, taught Turkish, and sorted. The most
able went into the palace school; the rest went to Turkish farming families for
some years to learn the language and the country, and then into the janissary
corps.

The three differences. First, they were the sultan's household in law: legally
his slaves, with no lineage, no property rights independent of him, and no
tribal or provincial constituency — which is the standard design — but the
levy took them from inside the empire rather than off a foreign frontier, so
the system did not depend on a slave market anyone else controlled. Second, it
staffed the government and not only the army: grand viziers, provincial
governors, admirals and treasurers came out of the palace school for two
centuries, so the ruling class of the empire was, by design, a body of
converted Christian peasants' sons who owned nothing and could be dismissed.
Third, it was not hereditary, and enormous effort went into keeping it that
way, because the whole point was a service class that could not become an
aristocracy.

It did not hold. From the later sixteenth century janissaries married, took
trades, enrolled their sons, and turned into a hereditary urban corporation
with property, guild connections and a decisive voice in politics — including
in the deposition of sultans. That is the same ending every other slave
soldiery reached, arrived at about two centuries later than usual.

The moral question does not dispose of itself in a sentence and should not be
made to. The *devshirme* took children from their families
by force and converted them; it was resented, evaded and occasionally resisted;
and it was also, for a Balkan peasant's son, the only route in that world from a
village to the grand vizierate, and some families are recorded trying to get
their boys included. Both of those are true and the sources record both.
