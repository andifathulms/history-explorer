---
title: Two inheritances
drafted_from: imber-2002
phase: peak
---

Mehmed II took Constantinople in 1453 after a fifty-three day siege, ending the
Roman empire eleven hundred years after its eastern half had been separated from
its western one. He was twenty-one. He also took the title *Kayser-i Rum* —
Caesar of Rome — and moved his capital into the city.

That claim is one of the two counted on this page, and it is the paradigm case
in this site's own coding rules for what a successor claim is: not descent, not
conquest, but a ruler publicly taking a predecessor's title as the description of
his own authority. The Ottomans also kept the Greek Orthodox patriarchate in
being under their protection, which was both a practical arrangement for
governing a Christian population and a continuation of an imperial function.

The second claim came in 1517, when Selim I destroyed the Mamluk sultanate and
took Egypt, Syria and the guardianship of Mecca and Medina. The shadow Abbasid
caliph the Mamluks had kept at Cairo since 1261 passed into Ottoman hands with
everything else. The later Ottoman assertion that the caliphate was formally
transferred at that point is largely an eighteenth-century construction, but the
substance was not in doubt: after 1517 the Ottoman sultan held the holy cities
and no rival claimant held anything comparable.

This site's caliphates region ends with the Abbasids because that is where the
office's own line ends. The Ottomans are in Anatolia, where they started, and
their claim on both Rome and the caliphate is recorded as counts and edges rather
than by moving them.
