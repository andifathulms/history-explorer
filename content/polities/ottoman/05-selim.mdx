---
title: Two years that changed what the empire was
drafted_from: imber-2002
phase: peak
---

Selim I reigned eight years and spent two of them acquiring the Arab world.

The sequence is quick and it is worth separating from the wars around it. In
1514 he beat Shah Isma'il at Chaldiran — Ottoman artillery and janissary
firearms against Qizilbash cavalry — which ended Safavid expansion westwards and
fixed a frontier in eastern Anatolia that is still roughly where it is. In 1516
he turned south against the Mamluk sultanate, beat it at Marj Dabiq near
Aleppo, and in January 1517 at Ridaniya outside
Cairo. Syria, Egypt and the Hijaz came in together.

What that acquired was not primarily territory. It was three things the
Ottomans had not previously had.

The holy cities. Guardianship of Mecca and Medina passed to the sultan.
That was the most valuable symbolic ground in the Islamic world, and had been
for six centuries: dynasties in Cairo, Tunis and Baghdad had risen and fallen on
whose name was said in the Friday sermon there.

The caliph. The Abbasid line the Mamluks had kept at Cairo since 1261 was taken
to Constantinople with the rest of the state's moveable assets. The later
Ottoman claim that the caliphate was formally handed over at that moment is
largely an eighteenth-century construction, produced when the empire needed an
argument for authority over Muslims outside its borders. What was not in doubt
after 1517 was that the Ottoman sultan held the holy cities and no rival held
anything comparable.

And a Muslim majority. Before 1517 the Ottoman empire was a largely Christian
state ruled by Muslims; the Balkan and Anatolian populations together made it
so. Afterwards it was the largest Muslim polity in the world and the centre of
gravity of its population, revenue and religious institutions moved
accordingly. The empire that had grown up on a Christian frontier fighting
Christians became the empire that spoke for Sunni Islam against a Shi'i rival
in Iran — and the two things Selim did in those two years are what made that
argument possible.
