The sultanate went in 1922 and the caliphate did not. It was separated off and
given to a cousin, Abdülmecid, who held it for sixteen months as a religious
dignity with no state attached.

On 3 March 1924 the Assembly abolished that too, in a package of measures Kemal
lists together and which are more revealing as a set than singly. The Ministry
for Religious Affairs and the pious endowments were suppressed. Every
educational institution in Turkey, the religious schools included, was
transferred to the Ministry of Public Instruction. And:

> The Caliph is declared deposed and the dignity abolished. All members of the
> deposed Ottoman dynasty are for ever forbidden to reside within the frontiers
> of the territory of the Turkish Republic.

That is the whole of it. An office that had existed since the death of the
Prophet, and had been claimed by the Ottoman sultans for four centuries, ended
in a clause of a bill about schools.

## He was offered it

Twice, and he says so.

Certain persons, he writes, who wrongly believed that the caliphate had to be
maintained for religious and political reasons, proposed at the last moment that
he should take the office himself. He gave a negative reply immediately.

Then a deputy called Rasih Efendi, an ecclesiastic who had been leading a Red
Crescent delegation in India, came back through Egypt and asked for an interview,
and reported that Muslims across the countries he had travelled through wanted
Kemal to become caliph, and that he had been commissioned to say so.

Kemal's reply to him is the most interesting sentence in the whole business:

> You are a Doctor of Religious Right. You know that Caliph signifies Chief of
> the State. How can I accept the proposals and desires of people who are
> governed by kings and emperors?

That is a refusal on a specific ground, and it is not piety and it is not
secularism exactly. It is that the caliphate is a political office; that the
people offering it to him were subjects of the British and Dutch empires; and
that accepting authority over them would make the Turkish republic responsible
for them, which was the last thing it wanted after nine years of war.

The office was abolished, in other words, partly because taking it seriously
would have been an unlimited liability.

## Three endings of the same thing

The caliphate had stopped before. It stopped at Baghdad in 1258, when Hulagu had
al-Musta'sim killed. It stopped at Cairo in 1517, when Selim removed the last of
the shadow Abbasids to Constantinople with the rest of the moveable property of
the Mamluk state.

The third time, in 1924, is the only one that was not done by an army. It was
done by a vote, in a parliament, in the successor state of the empire that had
been holding the office — and the man who could have taken it declined on the
grounds that it would have obliged him to have opinions about India.

## What the dissolution left

The family went into exile and mostly stayed there; the ban on the dynasty was
lifted for women in 1952 and for men in 1974.

What the empire left on the ground is harder to summarise and much larger. The
political map of the modern Middle East, the Balkans and the Caucasus is
substantially the shape this dissolution produced, and a great many of the
borders drawn after 1918 follow lines the Ottomans had used as provincial
boundaries — because the mandate authorities used the administrative
divisions they found.

And the argument did not stop. Every movement since 1924 that has proposed to
restore a caliphate has been arguing with a vote taken in Ankara on a Monday
afternoon, in a bill that also nationalised the schools.
