When the Sultanate of Rum was extinguished in 1308, Anatolia broke into a dozen
Turkish principalities. Osman's, at Sogut, was among the smallest and was placed
on the least promising frontier — facing Byzantium rather than the richer
neighbours to the east.

<Figure id="osman-tomb" />

That placement turned out to be the whole advantage. The other beyliks fought
each other over Anatolian territory; Osman's fought a Byzantine empire that had
been broken by the Fourth Crusade in 1204, had recovered Constantinople in 1261
without recovering its strength, and could not defend Bithynia. Frontier warfare
against Christians also gave the Ottomans a claim on the men and the language of
*gaza*, the raiding war of faith, which drew fighters from the rest of Anatolia
to them rather than to their rivals.

<Figure id="bursa-green" />

Bursa fell in 1326, and the Ottomans were across the straits into Europe by the
1350s — earlier than they finished Anatolia, which is the opposite of the usual
telling. The Balkans, not Anatolia, were the empire's revenue base for its first
century and a half.

The one interruption, in 1402, gets its own telling further on.
