Mahmud II spent years thinning the janissaries before he broke them. Each
campaign in Greece a body of them was embarked on the fleet and landed in small
parties, purposely unsupported, and none came back; only a few thousand were
left in Constantinople when, on 30 May 1826, the sultan issued a decree
announcing the formation of a new victorious army. The janissaries understood at
once why their companions had not returned from Greece. On 15 June they reversed
their soup-kettles, the signal of revolt, and demanded the heads of the ministers
and the revocation of the decree; the sultan had the aga of their own corps, the
Bosphorus garrisons, the gunners and the marines already on his side, and on the
morning of the sixteenth he raised the Prophet's standard in the hippodrome and
had the religious establishment call the people out against them.

Two years later Russia crossed the Pruth. Adolphus Slade, a British naval
lieutenant, arrived in the middle of the war that followed, went to sea with the
Ottoman fleet, and eventually spent seventeen years in its service. He is a
hostile, opinionated, deeply condescending witness who thought the reform was
being done the wrong way, and the detail he collected to prove it is the best
account there is of what the first years of the new army looked like. He calls
those troops by an older name, the *nizam dgedit*, the new order — the label of
an earlier attempt that had cost a previous sultan his throne, and the choice of
word is part of his argument.

## The war the new army had to fight

When the declaration of war reached the capital, the sultan sent couriers across
the empire with pressing orders to the pashas and beys to furnish contingents.
The orders were ill obeyed. Slade's judgement is that if the truth had reached
the sultan's ears it would have shown him the cost of having broken the
*derebeys*, the provincial notables: in consequence, and for the first time, a
Turkish army took the field almost unaccompanied by cavalry.

From Bosnia, full of a warlike Muslim population, the sultan expected serious
help, and ordered its governor to march forty thousand men to the Drina to watch
the Serbs. The governor, afraid of what would happen, sent a subordinate officer
with the mollah to read the decree to the troops in his place. Having heard it,
the men burst into murmurs, then into violence. The officer and the mollah were
shot dead, and the new uniforms that had been brought to dress the troops in
were piled on the spot and burnt. The Porte changed the governor for a milder
man, and no help at all came from Bosnia for the whole campaign; the difficulty
was keeping the Bosnians already garrisoning the Danube fortresses from
deserting.

What the sultan had instead, in Slade's description, was an army of regular
troops — that is, an army of conscript boys, most of them under eighteen,
hastily raised, rawly instructed, without efficient officers, but armed with the
musket and bayonet, which the sultan believed to be the only real advantage
Europeans had over Asiatics. Slade thinks that ill-placed confidence was the
true reason the war was accepted at all.

He also records how the fighting was read at the time. That the sultan could
resist Russia at all, so soon after the disasters in Greece, struck European
observers as extraordinary and was credited to the reform — and even when the
Russian army stood at Adrianople with its Cossacks twenty hours' march from the
capital, parts of Asia Minor in revolt, Albania and Bosnia turning hostile and
the streets of Constantinople running with blood, few of them could bring
themselves to blame reform for anything.

He watched the new troops on parade at Büyükdere when the British ambassador was
received in the imperial tent — about two thousand of them lining the ground
from the tent to the landing-place, the Christian population of the capital
packed in behind, a battery of nine-pounders overlooking the scene — and could
not resist the comparison with the marines of the frigate that had brought the
ambassador: a gratifying contrast, he writes, to the slouching, short,
ill-dressed *nizam*.

## The argument underneath it

Slade's case is not that the janissaries should have been kept. He is savage
about them: lords of the day, ruling Constantinople with uncontrolled insolence,
enormous turbans and bulky sashes full of weapons, thrusting everybody out of
their path without regard to sex or age, pistols let off in sport during the
*bayram* and often followed by a shriek. No man who was not of them and no
property that was not theirs was safe.

His case is about method, and he makes it by comparing Mahmud with Murad, the
sultan Slade credits with creating the janissaries in the first place. Both measures were the same
kind of thing — a mighty innovation, an entirely new military force imposed on
the institutions of the country. But Murad, he argues, had a master mind:
instead of keeping his new army distinct from the nation he incorporated it into
the nation, made it conform in all respects to national usage, and it spread
into a vast national guard. Mahmud, having destroyed the self-appointed
battalions, should have made the rest of that body available to him instead of
outlawing it. By respecting what Slade calls their traditionary whims and social
rights, he says, the sultan could easily have given his subjects a taste for
European discipline.

Then the sentence that carries the whole argument:

> They never objected to it in principle, but their untutored minds could not
> understand why, in order to use the musket and bayonet, and manoeuvre
> together, it was necessary to leave off wearing beards and turbans.

That is a British officer's summary of a foreign reform, delivered with the
usual contempt for the people whose objection he is reporting, and it is still
among the sharpest things said about the whole nineteenth-century Ottoman reform
programme. The drill was not the difficulty. The dress was, because the dress
was the claim: that becoming effective required becoming European, and that the
state's own subjects were the thing needing correction.

He adds one qualification that undercuts his own case, and lets it stand.
Whatever the janissaries were as a bar to the free will of the sultan, he
writes, it is doubtful whether their influence materially affected the empire,
the elements of its decay being deeper seated. He never says what those elements
were. And on his own account the remnant, left alone after June 1826, would have
died a natural death and left only a name; it was the campaign to erase every
token of them, down to Oriental usage in eating and dressing, that turned a
beaten party into a principle worth defending — because trampling on their
principles meant trampling on the nation's.
