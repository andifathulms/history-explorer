Demetrius Cantemir was a Moldavian prince held in Istanbul for some twenty years
as a hostage and a client of the Ottoman court, fluent in Turkish, Persian and
Arabic, on speaking terms with the men who ran the empire, and eventually a
defector to Peter the Great. In exile he wrote the history of the state that had
kept him, and he divided it in two: growth, and then decay. The place he put the
join was the summer of 1683.

Cantemir was ten years old when Kara Mustafa marched on Vienna, so the campaign
comes to him from men who were on it, and he is at his most detailed and least
trustworthy exactly where it matters most. He names his informants, including
his father. He also reports what the grand vizier was privately thinking, which
he cannot have known.

## The council

Before the army moved, the vizier called a council of his pashas and asked them
whether Vienna should be attacked this year or next. Cantemir's explanation of
why he asked is not flattering: so that if anything went wrong it could be
imputed to the authors of the advice rather than to him, and so that the pashas,
gratified at being consulted, would carry out his orders more readily. The
Hungarian rebel Thököly was called in and made to speak first, because he was
supposed to know the ground, and his speech begins with a sentence a
quartermaster could have written — that three things are needed for a great
undertaking: money, men, and above all prudence, which moderates the excessive
ardour of heroic minds.

The advice against the siege was given. Cantemir notes, in a long footnote of
the kind he is good at, that most Christian writers blamed Thököly for urging
the attack, and that this is implausible, since a rebel trying to free his
country from a foreign yoke would naturally use whatever means served his own
faction.

## Why the city was not stormed

Then comes the charge on which the whole of Cantemir's account of the defeat
rests, and it is a charge about greed rather than about arms.

The vizier was convinced the city could not escape him. So he refused to let it
be closely blockaded on every side, and refused to let the army attack the
breaches everywhere at once; he ordered daily assaults by small parties only, so
that the garrison would be worn down by constant watching and forced to
surrender. When the janissaries complained publicly that more of them were being
killed in these skirmishes than a single general assault would have cost, the
vizier let slip why. He knew there were great treasures in that city, and he
could not let it be given up to plunder. He then had it proclaimed in camp that
any troops who got over the walls were to stop there, entrench themselves, and
on no account enter a house until he came and gave fresh orders.

The siege therefore took much longer than expected. The convoys from Hungary
were intercepted by the garrisons downriver, exactly as the army had been warned
they would be, and a thing previously unknown in a Turkish camp happened: ten
drams of bread, without salt, sold for a *rhub*, and everything else rose in
proportion.

The relief arrived. Cantemir's description of what followed is of a rout that
began before the battle did — janissaries and cavalry thinking less of fighting
than of flight — and of Kara Mustafa returning to an empty tent, weeping, taking
up the standard of the Prophet alone and riding after his army. Men who had
complained of the least labour covered fifty hours on foot without food or drink
before they stopped.

Cantemir keeps one story that says more about the aftermath than the casualty
figures do. A Jewish merchant in the vizier's service was asked at Buda whether
he had anything valuable to send the Sultan as a present. He answered that his
best effects were at Belgrade. The vizier offered him five hundred light horse
to fetch them safely through the bands of plunderers on the road, and the
merchant replied that three men would do, because he had a German cap, and the
sight of it was enough to frighten off the largest band.

## What it cost, and what it did not

Kara Mustafa spent the autumn sending rich presents to court as the strongest
argument for his innocence, and for a while it worked: the Sultan confirmed him
in the vizierate and in command of the new army he was raising. Then the order
came the other way. He was seized in his own house at Belgrade, in the presence
of the aga of the janissaries, on the fifth of Muharram, and put to death.

Karlowitz was sixteen years off, and it was there rather than at Vienna that the
empire first signed away territory as a beaten party. But Cantemir put the turn
in 1683, and the reason is worth stating plainly, because it is a claim and not
an observation. He was writing for European patrons, in Latin, about a state he
had left; a history that ran downhill from a date made his own defection look
like foresight. The detail in his pages is first-hand and the shape he gives it
is an argument, and both are his.
