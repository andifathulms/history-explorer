On 28 July 1402 Timur destroyed Bayezid I's army on the plain outside Ankara and
took him prisoner along with one of his sons. Two Castilians were standing in
the field when it happened.

Payo de Sotomayor and Hernán Sánchez de Palazuelos had been sent east by Henry
III of Castile for the express purpose of finding out how much power Timur and
Bayezid each had in the world, and they arrived in time to watch the question
settle itself in an afternoon. Timur learned they were there, treated them
honourably, took them along with him, and sent an ambassador back to Castile
with letters and a present. The second Castilian embassy — the one that reached
Samarkand, and whose journal survives — existed because of Ankara.

That journal is why Ankara can be seen as something other than a date. Its
author sailed down the length of the Anatolian coast in the spring of
1404 and wrote down, port by port, who was in charge. He was not interested in
the Ottoman question. He was making notes on a voyage.

## The coast, twenty months later

North of Sinope the ships put in at a harbour belonging to a Muslim lord named
Isfendiyar, and the journal explains, as a piece of local colour, why he paid
tribute to Timur: Bayezid had killed his father and taken his land, and when
Timur won he gave all of it back. Further along was a town with two castles, one
held by the Genoese and one, with the town, by a Muslim called Chalabi — that is
Süleyman, Bayezid's son, holding a share of his father's inheritance as one lord
among the others. At the next anchorage a Greek lord kept a town on the rocks
and a castle above it with three hundred Turks in it, and he too paid tribute to
Timur.

None of this is presented as news. It is the ordinary furniture of a coastline.
A traveller who had never heard of the Ottomans would have taken from that
passage the impression of a shore divided among a dozen small powers, two
Italian republics, and one distant emperor in Central Asia to whom several of
them sent money.

Inland the same arrangement was being enforced in person. At Erzincan the
ambassadors were eating with the local lord when a Turkish boy of about seven
rode up with ten mounted attendants. He was Isfendiyar's nephew, and he had come
from Timur carrying an order that his uncle hand over half his land to him,
because he was his sister's son. Anatolia in 1404 was a place where a child with
ten servants could arrive at dinner holding a writ that halved a principality.

## How he got there, as the Castilians were told it

The version the journal records is a story about temper. The lordship of
Erzincan was disputed, Timur wrote to Bayezid about it, and Bayezid — believing
there was no man in the world equal to himself — wrote back that he was
astonished anyone could be mad enough to send him such folly, that he would do
as he chose against every other man in the universe, that he would come and find
Timur, and that he would dishonour his principal wife. Timur then marched out of
his winter quarters and besieged Sivas. Bayezid sent his eldest son against him
with what the Castilians were told was two hundred thousand horse, intending to
follow with more. Sivas fell before the relief arrived.

The journal also records the detail that made the disaster complete, and records
it as a grievance of Timur's rather than as analysis. The Genoese of Pera had
promised him one thing and done another: they let the Turks cross from Greece
into Anatolia, and when the Turks were beaten they helped them escape again.
Whatever passed between Timur and the Genoese, the ferry across the straits was
in Italian hands, and it ran both ways.

One more figure walks through these pages. The Castilians were told that
Bayezid's father Murad had been killed on a battlefield by a Christian count
called Lazaro, two thrusts in the breast that came out at his back; that Bayezid
avenged him by killing Lazaro in battle with his own hand; and that Lazaro's son
marched with Bayezid, and now lived with Süleyman. The Serbian prince whose
father the Ottomans killed, and who fought at Ankara on the Ottoman side, was
two years after the defeat attached to the household of the Ottoman claimant in
Europe. The arrangement survived the state that made it.

## What the news was at Chios

Sailing out in the autumn of 1403 the embassy had stopped at Chios, close enough
to the mainland to see it clearly, and picked up the current report: the eldest
son of the Turk whom Timur conquered was dead, and his brothers were fighting
over the succession.

That is the whole interregnum in one sentence of harbour gossip, heard by people
who had no stake in it and were waiting for a ship. It went on for eleven years.
The state that reassembled at the end of it was put back together out of the
pieces the journal describes in passing — the coastal emirates restored by
Timur, the brothers, the Genoese ferry, the Serbian retinue — by men who had
been on the losing side at Ankara and had watched the price of losing collected
port by port.
