---
title: Two years that changed what the empire was
drafted_from: imber-2002
phase: peak
---

Selim I reigned eight years and spent two of them acquiring the Arab world.

The sequence is quick and it is worth separating from the wars around it. In
1514 he beat Shah Isma'il at Chaldiran, which this site's Safavid page
describes from the other side: Ottoman artillery and janissary firearms against
Qizilbash cavalry, ending Safavid expansion westwards and fixing a frontier in
eastern Anatolia that is still roughly where it is. In 1516 he turned south
against the Mamluk sultanate — this site records that polity's ending here —
beat it at Marj Dabiq near Aleppo, and in January 1517 at Ridaniya outside
Cairo. Syria, Egypt and the Hijaz came in together.

What that acquired was not primarily territory. It was three things the
Ottomans had not previously had.

The holy cities. Guardianship of Mecca and Medina passed to the sultan, which
this corpus has repeatedly identified as the most valuable symbolic ground in
the Islamic world — the Fatimid, Ikhshidid and Hafsid pages all turn on who
held the khutba there.

The caliph. The Abbasid line the Mamluks had kept at Cairo since 1261, which
has its own page on this site, was taken to Constantinople with the rest of the
state's moveable assets. The later Ottoman claim that the caliphate was
formally transferred at that moment is largely an eighteenth-century
construction, and this site says so on both pages; what was not in doubt after
1517 was that the Ottoman sultan held the holy cities and no rival held
anything comparable.

And a Muslim majority. Before 1517 the Ottoman empire was a largely Christian
state ruled by Muslims; the Balkan and Anatolian populations together made it
so. Afterwards it was the largest Muslim polity in the world and the centre of
gravity of its population, revenue and religious institutions moved
accordingly. The empire that had grown up on a Christian frontier fighting
Christians became the empire that spoke for Sunni Islam against a Shi'i rival
in Iran — and the two things Selim did in those two years are what made that
argument possible.
