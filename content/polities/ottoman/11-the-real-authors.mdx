Lady Mary Wortley Montagu went to Constantinople in 1716 as the wife of the
British ambassador, stayed until 1718, learned some Turkish, and got into houses
and conversations that no male traveller could enter. Her letters were published
after her death and have been read ever since for the wrong reason — as
descriptions of baths and dress — when the sharpest thing in them is a
constitutional analysis.

## Who governed

She states it in one sentence, from Adrianople, and it is worth quoting exactly
because it is an eyewitness saying in 1717 what historians would take two and a
half centuries to say again:

> The government here is entirely in the hands of the army, the grand signior,
> with all his absolute power, is as much a slave as any of his subjects, and
> trembles at a janizary's frown.

She is not admiring. She goes on about the spies in the coffee-houses and the
house razed to the ground for a careless remark, and she draws the English moral
— that she wishes Parliament would send over a shipload of young passive
obedient men to see arbitrary government in its clearest light, where it is hard
to judge whether the prince, the people or the ministers are most miserable.
When a minister displeases the people, she writes, in three hours he is dragged
from his master's arms; they cut off hands, head and feet and throw them before
the palace gate, with all the respect in the world, while the sultan sits
trembling in his apartment and dares neither defend nor revenge his favourite.

But she does not stop at the soldiers, and this is where she is better than her
contemporaries. She had lodged three weeks at Belgrade with an *effendi*, a
scholar, and came away with a view of where power actually sat. Law and religion
in that empire were one profession; its men held the profitable offices and the
religious revenues; the sultan, though heir to his subjects in general, never
presumed to touch their lands or money, which passed in uninterrupted succession
to their children — a privilege forfeited only by taking a court post or the
title of pasha, and there were few such fools among them. Then the conclusion:

> 'Tis they that are the real authors, though the soldiers are the actors of
> revolutions. They deposed the late sultan Mustapha.

Mustafa II had been deposed in 1703. She was told who did it by the class that
did it, fourteen years later, in a private house.
