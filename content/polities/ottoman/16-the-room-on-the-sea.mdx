General Sir Charles Harington had commanded the Allied occupation forces at
Constantinople for two years when, in the summer of 1922, the war he thought he
was policing turned into one he might have to fight himself.

The Greek army occupying western Anatolia since 1919 had pushed inland toward
Ankara and been held on the Sakarya river by Mustafa Kemal, who spent the
following year building an army out of what Harington calls "peasants and
followers" while the Greek position slowly rotted: bad finance, a war-weary
army, a commander Harington had already told London privately would not stand
if seriously attacked. In August 1922 Kemal struck a lightly held stretch of
the Greek line near Afyonkarahisar. "The whole thing collapsed like a pack of
cards." Harington's liaison officer wired that the Greek army could not be made
to hold; when a second officer confirmed it, Harington wired back the only
question that occurred to him — "Can't someone get on a tub and stop them?" —
and watched what he called one of the great debacles in history end at Smyrna.

Kemal's forces reached the Dardanelles, opposite the small Allied garrison
holding Chanak on the Asian shore under British, French and Italian flags, with
orders to hold the straits at all costs. For several weeks in September the two
armies faced each other across barely a mile of contested ground, and London
came close to authorising Harington to open fire on a Turkish force his own
government had, a year earlier, been arming.

## The conference

He was not made to. A conference of the Allied generals with İsmet Pasha,
Kemal's commander and the man who would shortly be sent to negotiate at
Lausanne, was arranged at Mudania in early October. Harington went by
battleship. The room was, in his own description, "just a bare room on the
sea" — and dead Greek soldiers, recently pushed off the pier by Turkish troops,
were still washing up against it while the generals talked.

He had never seen Kemalist soldiers before. During the lunch recess he asked
an interpreter to put a question to the ones posted outside — had any of them
been British prisoners during the war, and had they been well fed? "Their faces
lit up and they were full of smiles," he wrote; it was evident they had never
eaten so well before or since.

The talks nearly broke down on twenty-eight separate points of disagreement.
A French politician who had recently been to Ankara tried to press his own
settlement on Harington in the recess, offering to have the French general sign
over Harington's head if Harington would not. Harington told him he had never
asked anyone to take his responsibility and was not going to start, went back
to his ship, and returned the following afternoon for what he told İsmet Pasha
plainly was the Allies' final word. It held. A convention was signed on 11
October 1922, restoring eastern Thrace to Turkish administration and requiring
the Allies to treat the Ankara government, not the sultan's, as the authority
they were dealing with.

Three weeks later, on 1 November, the Grand National Assembly voted the
sultanate out of existence. By then the question the vote settled — who in
Turkey actually held the power to make the state's decisions — was one
Harington had already been forced to answer at a bare table by the water,
weeks before anybody in Ankara asked a committee to debate it.
