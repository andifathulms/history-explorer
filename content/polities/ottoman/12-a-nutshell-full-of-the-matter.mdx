---
title: A nutshell full of the matter

Lady Mary Wortley Montagu was writing home from Constantinople about the plague,
which she had just travelled through and which she thought could be rooted out
as easily in Turkey as in Italy or France, when she changed subject: *A propos
of distempers, I am going to tell you a thing that will make you wish yourself
here.* Smallpox, so fatal and so general in England, was harmless there, by the
invention of what they called *ingrafting*.

A set of old women made it their business to perform the operation, every
autumn, in September, when the great heat had abated. People sent to one another
to know whether anyone in the family had a mind to have the smallpox, and
fifteen or sixteen would meet for the purpose. The old woman came with a
nutshell full of the matter of the best sort of smallpox and asked which vein
you would like opened. She ripped it with a large needle, no more painful than a
scratch, put in as much matter as would lie on the needle's head, bound the
wound with a hollow bit of shell, and opened four or five veins in all. Greek
patients often chose the forehead, both arms and the breast, to make the sign of
the cross, which left scars; those who were not superstitious chose the legs or
the concealed part of the arm. The children played together the rest of the day,
kept their beds two days when the fever came, and were well within eight. The
French ambassador said they took the smallpox there by way of diversion, as
people took the waters elsewhere.

She ends by saying that she is satisfied enough of the safety of the experiment
to intend it on her own small son, and that she is patriot enough to take pains
to bring the invention into fashion in England — and then says exactly what she
expects to be up against:

> I should not fail to write to some of our doctors very particularly about it,
> if I knew any one of them that I thought had virtue enough to destroy such a
> considerable branch of their revenue, for the good of mankind.

An empire that Europe was learning to describe as backward was in 1717 running,
as a matter of ordinary autumn routine performed by old women for a fee, a
preventive procedure that England did not have and would resist for a
generation. The letters were written by someone who despised its politics and
said so on the same page.
