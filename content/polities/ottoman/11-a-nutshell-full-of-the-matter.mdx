Lady Mary Wortley Montagu went to Constantinople in 1716 as the wife of the
British ambassador, stayed until 1718, learned some Turkish, and got into houses
and conversations that no male traveller could enter. Her letters were published
after her death and have been read ever since for the wrong reason — as
descriptions of baths and dress — when the sharpest thing in them is a
constitutional analysis.

## Who governed

She states it in one sentence, from Adrianople, and it is worth quoting exactly
because it is an eyewitness saying in 1717 what historians would take two and a
half centuries to say again:

> The government here is entirely in the hands of the army, the grand signior,
> with all his absolute power, is as much a slave as any of his subjects, and
> trembles at a janizary's frown.

She is not admiring. She goes on about the spies in the coffee-houses and the
house razed to the ground for a careless remark, and she draws the English moral
— that she wishes Parliament would send over a shipload of young passive
obedient men to see arbitrary government in its clearest light, where it is hard
to judge whether the prince, the people or the ministers are most miserable.
When a minister displeases the people, she writes, in three hours he is dragged
from his master's arms; they cut off hands, head and feet and throw them before
the palace gate, with all the respect in the world, while the sultan sits
trembling in his apartment and dares neither defend nor revenge his favourite.

But she does not stop at the soldiers, and this is where she is better than her
contemporaries. She had lodged three weeks at Belgrade with an *effendi*, a
scholar, and came away with a view of where power actually sat. Law and religion
in that empire were one profession; its men held the profitable offices and the
religious revenues; the sultan, though heir to his subjects in general, never
presumed to touch their lands or money, which passed in uninterrupted succession
to their children — a privilege forfeited only by taking a court post or the
title of pasha, and there were few such fools among them. Then the conclusion:

> 'Tis they that are the real authors, though the soldiers are the actors of
> revolutions. They deposed the late sultan Mustapha.

Mustafa II had been deposed in 1703. She was told who did it by the class that
did it, fourteen years later, in a private house.

## The thing she took home

The other passage is the famous one, and it belongs here rather than in a
history of medicine, because of what it says about the direction of traffic.

She was writing about the plague, which she had just travelled through and which
she thought could be rooted out as easily in Turkey as in Italy or France, when
she changed subject: *A propos of distempers, I am going to tell you a thing
that will make you wish yourself here.* Smallpox, so fatal and so general in
England, was harmless there, by the invention of what they called *ingrafting*.

A set of old women made it their business to perform the operation, every
autumn, in September, when the great heat had abated. People sent to one another
to know whether anyone in the family had a mind to have the smallpox, and
fifteen or sixteen would meet for the purpose. The old woman came with a
nutshell full of the matter of the best sort of smallpox and asked which vein
you would like opened. She ripped it with a large needle, no more painful than a
scratch, put in as much matter as would lie on the needle's head, bound the
wound with a hollow bit of shell, and opened four or five veins in all. Greek
patients often chose the forehead, both arms and the breast, to make the sign of
the cross, which left scars; those who were not superstitious chose the legs or
the concealed part of the arm. The children played together the rest of the day,
kept their beds two days when the fever came, and were well within eight. The
French ambassador said they took the smallpox there by way of diversion, as
people took the waters elsewhere.

She ends by saying that she is satisfied enough of the safety of the experiment
to intend it on her own small son, and that she is patriot enough to take pains
to bring the invention into fashion in England — and then says exactly what she
expects to be up against:

> I should not fail to write to some of our doctors very particularly about it,
> if I knew any one of them that I thought had virtue enough to destroy such a
> considerable branch of their revenue, for the good of mankind.

An empire that Europe was learning to describe as backward was in 1717 running,
as a matter of ordinary autumn routine performed by old women for a fee, a
preventive procedure that England did not have and would resist for a
generation. The letters were written by someone who despised its politics and
said so on the same page.
