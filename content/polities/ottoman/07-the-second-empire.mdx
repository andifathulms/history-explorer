---
title: The empire that was not declining
drafted_from: tezcan-2010
phase: contraction
---

The standard account of the Ottoman empire after Suleyman is three centuries of
decline: the sultans stop leading armies, the janissaries get above themselves,
the harem interferes, the provinces slip, and Europe pulls ahead until the whole
thing expires in 1922. It is a tidy story, it was written substantially by
Ottoman critics of their own government and then by nineteenth-century European
observers who had reasons to believe it, and modern scholarship has spent forty
years taking it apart.

What actually happened in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was a
transformation, and the direction of it is the opposite of the usual reading.

Power moved out of the palace. The sultan stopped campaigning and became a
sedentary monarch in Constantinople; the grand vizier and the bureaucracy ran
the state; the janissaries became a propertied urban corporation with an
interest in policy; the religious hierarchy acquired independent weight; and
the household of the sultan's mother became a real institution of government
rather than a scandal. Between 1617 and 1622 four sultans were enthroned or
deposed in circumstances that no earlier Ottoman rule would have permitted,
including one deposed and murdered by the janissaries. Tezcan's argument, which
this chapter follows, is that these were not symptoms of collapse but the
constitution of a different state — one in which the ruler was answerable to
identifiable constituencies with the means to remove him, which is a
recognisably early modern arrangement rather than a failing absolutist one.

The empire also went on working. It held Crete after a twenty-four year siege
in 1669, was at the walls of Vienna again in 1683, lost that badly, ceded
Hungary at Karlowitz in 1699 — the first treaty in which the Ottomans conceded
territory as a defeated party — and then survived another two hundred and
twenty-three years, through the loss of the Crimea, Greek independence, the
Tanzimat reforms that rebuilt the administration on European models, a
constitution in 1876, and a young officers' revolution in 1908.

This site records the ending as `fragmentation` in 1922 with no agent named,
and that is right. The empire was not conquered by anyone. It joined a world
war on the losing side, was partitioned by the victors, and the state that
emerged from the war of independence that followed abolished the sultanate
itself.

Six hundred and twenty-three years, on this record, of which this chapter
covers three hundred and fifty. That the last three and a half centuries are
usually summarised in a word is a fact about historiography, and the reason this
page cites a book whose entire purpose is to argue about it.
