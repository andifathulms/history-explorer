Ellis Ashmead-Bartlett was the Daily Telegraph's correspondent accredited to
Abdullah Pasha's army in Thrace when the First Balkan War began in October
1912, and he spent most of it fighting the Ottoman press office for permission
to see anything at all. What he did see, at the three-day battle of Lüleburgaz
and the retreat that followed it, is the empire's presence in the Balkans
ending the way it began: on the same roads, moving the other direction.

## The rout

At five in the morning on 31 October his Ottoman orderly Ismet shook him awake:
*Abdullah and his staff have left. The village has been evacuated. At any
minute the Bulgarians may enter.* Nobody had thought to warn the correspondents.
By the time Ashmead-Bartlett's cart cleared the village the enemy's guns had
opened, and he found himself, in his own words, "amidst a crowd of stragglers
and wounded, ox-wagons, stray batteries of artillery, and all the manifold
debris of a defeated army." Whole brigades had simply come apart. Officers were
scarce — the army had begun the campaign two thousand short of its full
complement and lost heavily among the rest in the battle — and without them
nothing held the fleeing mass together.

Men who had gone three days without food dug up raw cabbages and turnips in
village gardens and ate them where they stood; an old soldier, too weak to do
more than stumble, told Ismet that "a Turkish soldier is not worth the price of
a dog these days" and kept walking rather than abandon his rifle, which almost
nobody did even when they threw away their boots. An artillery major, who had
fought two days at Lüleburgaz under what he called the worst shrapnel fire he
had ever seen, begged Ashmead-Bartlett for brandy and then refused a lift: "No,
I will stay with my guns. You had better push on as fast as you can. For us it
is the end. But what would you? We have no roads, no food, no organisation." He
had eighteen more guns already lost behind him.

## The migration

Mixed into the retreating army were the Muslim inhabitants of the countryside
themselves, driving their households ahead of the front line in ox-carts.
Ashmead-Bartlett watched them for a full day's march and called it correctly:
"It was the migration of a whole people, the return of the Turks to Asia." Three
and a half centuries earlier, Ottoman forces had crossed the other way, taking
Edirne before they had finished conquering Anatolia, and had built the empire's
wealthiest and most populous provinces on that ground. What he was describing
was the same population moving back along the same roads, ahead of the same
kind of army that Murad and Bayezid had once put to flight.

Adrianople held out under siege into the spring; when it fell in March 1913,
the news of the surrender terms triggered a coup in Constantinople and the
murder of the war minister who had agreed to give it up, because the men who
seized power judged — rightly, on the ground the correspondent had just crossed
— that there was nothing left to negotiate with. The lines the retreating army
finally held, at Çatalca, were the last ones before the capital itself. By the
Treaty of London that May, the empire's frontier in Europe had been pushed back
to a strip of eastern Thrace it would fight to keep for the rest of its
existence. Rumelia, the senior half of the empire in its own reckoning for five
hundred years, was mostly gone in eight months.
