Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq arrived in 1555 as the Habsburg emperor's ambassador,
stayed seven years, spent much of that time confined to his house, and used it
to write four long letters to a friend describing what he could see. He was an
enemy. He expected his side to lose. That is what makes the letters useful:
almost everything admiring in them is admiration he did not want to feel.

He was taken to see the army in camp before a campaign, and asked to be shown
the slaughterhouse, to judge the supply from the meat. There were four or five
sheep hanging, for a camp that contained, he reckoned, no fewer than four
thousand janissaries. He said so, and was told that few of them ate meat; most
had their food brought from Constantinople. Somebody pointed out a janissary at
his dinner. He was eating, off a wooden or earthen trencher, a mess of turnips,
onions, garlic, parsnips and cucumbers seasoned with salt and vinegar, and
drinking water, and to all appearance enjoying it as much as if it had been
pheasant.

Busbecq could not stop looking at it. The cloth for their clothes was issued by
the state, given out at nightfall by companies, in packets; twenty-five or
thirty men slept in a tent apportioned so that each had room for his body and no
more; they wore three garments even in summer because they feared cold; the
baggage animals carried armour and tents rather than delicacies; and when the
camp moved, men who had lost their horses stood in a line by the road with their
saddles on their heads, so that the Sultan could see how many of them there were
and make an allowance.

## The passage everybody quotes

Then he wrote the comparison, and the reason it has been quoted for four
centuries is that it is a defeated man's inventory made before the defeat.

> On their side is the vast wealth of their empire, unimpaired resources,
> experience and practice in arms, a veteran soldiery, an uninterrupted series
> of victories, readiness to endure hardships, union, order, discipline, thrift,
> and watchfulness. On ours are found an empty exchequer, luxurious habits,
> exhausted resources, broken spirits, a raw and insubordinate soldiery, and
> greedy generals; there is no regard for discipline, license runs riot, the men
> indulge in drunkenness and debauchery, and, worst of all, the enemy are
> accustomed to victory, we, to defeat.

He adds the qualification that turned out to matter more than the list. The only
obstacle, he says, is Persia, whose position on the Sultan's rear forces him to
take precautions; the fear of Persia gives Europe a respite, but only for a
time. Busbecq's Vienna was safe because Suleyman had a second front, and he
knew it, and he wrote it down.

## What he thought the state actually ran on

The other passage is about who gets promoted. He was in the Sultan's crowded
hall, among the household cavalry and a large force of janissaries, and what
struck him was that in the whole assembly there was not one man who owed his
position to anything but his own service.

> No distinction is attached to birth among the Turks; the deference to be paid
> to a man is measured by the position he holds in the public service. There is
> no fighting for precedence; a man's place is marked out by the duties he
> discharges.

Those who received the highest offices, he was told, were mostly the sons of
shepherds and herdsmen, and far from being ashamed of it they boasted of it,
because they held that high qualities are not inherited any more than a talent
for music or mathematics is inherited, but come partly from God and partly from
training and industry. And then Busbecq turns the knife on his own world: *with
us there is no opening left for merit; birth is the standard for everything.*
He asks his correspondent to treat the remark as strictly private.

It is not a neutral report. It is a European civil servant using a foreign
system to complain about his own, and he is describing the recruited household
at its best moment and not the provincial cavalry, the tax farms, or the
households of the great men where patronage worked much as it did anywhere. But
he was in the room, he counted the sheep, and the thing he says the empire ran
on is not gunpowder or numbers. It is that the men at the top had been selected
and the men in the tents did not expect to be comfortable.

## The part he got wrong on purpose

Busbecq also watched the succession crisis of Suleyman's last years — the
killing of Mustafa, the war between Bayezid and Selim — and reported the Turkish
argument for it as he heard it: that to fall fighting for the chance of empire
is a more honourable lot than to be butchered like a sheep by a brother's
hangman. He offers it as evidence of barbarism. It reads now as evidence of
something else, which is that an empire selecting its servants by merit had not
found any way at all to select its rulers, and that the same household which
produced the janissary with his turnips also produced two princes with armies
who had to fight each other because the alternative was to be strangled.
