title: Fourteen months after

Baldwin was crowned in Hagia Sophia in May 1204. In April 1205 he was captured
alive on a field outside Adrianople by the king of Bulgaria, and the marshal of
Champagne, who was standing in front of one of the city gates when the survivors
came back, wrote down what he saw.

Villehardouin is not a neutral witness — he had argued for the diversion to
Constantinople and spends his book defending the men who made it — and the
account is still devastating, because his method is to list.

The Latins had gone out against a light cavalry army of Cumans and Bulgarians and
been drawn off their ground. Those near the emperor, Villehardouin says, testified
that no belted knight ever fought with greater courage. The fighting went on for
some time, and then some of the Franks broke, and once that started the defeat was
total. The emperor and Count Louis of Blois refused to turn their backs. Louis was
killed on the field. Baldwin was taken alive.

Then the list, in the flat manner of a man writing out a casualty return: Peter,
bishop of Bethlehem; Stephen du Perche; Reginald de Montmirail; the brother of
the count of Nevers; Matthew de Walincourt; Robert de Ronsoy; John de Friaise;
Walter de Neuilly; Ferry de Yerres and his brother John; Eustace de Heumont and
his brother John; Baldwin de Neuville — *and many others.*

What Villehardouin does next is the only creditable thing in the chapter and he
puts himself in it without comment. He was posted in front of one of the gates.
When the fugitives appeared he rode out with everyone he could gather, sent orders
to the officer holding the other gate to follow, collected the men running off the
field around his own banner, and between noon and vespers got the rout stopped.
Others, he adds, were so frightened that they would not stay with their comrades
at all.

Baldwin was carried into Bulgaria and shut in a tower at Tarnovo. Villehardouin's
translator preserves the Greek historian's verdict on him, which is worth keeping
because it comes from the people he had conquered: thirty-two years old, modest
and pious, free of the licence of his countrymen, able to take contradiction
without losing his temper, and given to spending his spare time in prayer or in
relieving the afflicted. Nobody ever learned exactly how he died.

The empire had existed for eleven months when it lost its emperor, its second
greatest lord and the flower of its knighthood in one afternoon to a power nobody
in the council at Venice had considered at all. It went on existing for another
fifty-six years, and never again had a field army capable of taking the offensive
in Thrace.
