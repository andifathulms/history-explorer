Baldwin I was crowned in May 1204. In April 1205, outside Adrianople, the
Bulgarian tsar Kalojan destroyed the Latin field army with Cuman horse archers
who feigned flight and drew the knights into a pursuit. Baldwin was captured and
died in a Bulgarian prison; the doge Enrico Dandolo, who had directed the whole
enterprise, died three months later.

Fourteen months after the conquest the empire had lost its emperor, its army and
the man who had made it, and it never rebuilt a force capable of offensive war.

## What it actually held

Very little, and shrinking.

The partition treaty had assigned the Latin emperor a quarter of the Byzantine
empire and his vassals the rest, but the treaty distributed provinces that had to
be conquered first, and most never were. Bulgaria held Thrace's interior. Nicaea
held western Anatolia within a decade. Epirus held the west. The Greek
territories the crusaders did hold in Greece — the principality of Achaea, the
duchy of Athens — were vassals in name and independent in practice, richer than
the emperor and disinclined to help.

By the 1230s the Latin empire was Constantinople, its immediate suburbs, and a
strip of coast. The emperor was the poorest ruler in his own hierarchy.

## The lead off the roof

This record's `revenue_basis` reads `land-tax`, one value, describing a fiscal
system inherited from Byzantium and applied to almost no territory. The actual
income was something the vocabulary cannot name: the empire sold the city.

Baldwin II, this record's last emperor, spent most of his reign travelling in
western Europe asking for money. He sold or pawned relics — the Crown of Thorns
went to Venetian merchants as security for a loan and was redeemed by Louis IX
of France, who built the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris to house it, and a long list of
others followed. He is reported to have had the lead stripped from the roofs of
the imperial palaces and sold. At one point he pledged his own son Philip to
Venetian creditors as security, and the boy was held in Venice for years until a
Castilian merchant redeemed him.

There is no value in a list of seven revenue bases for *liquidating the contents
of the capital*, and there should not be one for a single record. What can be
said is that this is the only polity in this corpus whose principal income was
the sale of its own predecessor's property, and that a good deal of the treasury
of medieval western Europe — the relics at the Sainte-Chapelle, the bronze horses
at San Marco, reliquaries in a dozen cathedrals — is where it went.

## 1235, and the thing that kept it alive

In 1235 John Vatatzes of Nicaea and Ivan Asen II of Bulgaria besieged the city
together, by land and by sea, and were driven off by a Venetian fleet and a small
Frankish relief force.

That is the last time the empire defended itself successfully, and it
demonstrates what had been true since 1205: the city could not be taken by
anybody who did not control the sea, and Venice did. The Latin empire survived
fifty-seven years because a commercial republic found it convenient, and this
site's Venetian record is candid that the convenience was the three-eighths of
the Byzantine empire the partition had given it.

The last chapter is what happened when the fleet was somewhere else.
