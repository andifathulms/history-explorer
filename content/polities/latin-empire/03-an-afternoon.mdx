In July 1261 a Nicaean commander named Alexios Strategopoulos was passing near
Constantinople with a force of about eight hundred, on an errand that had nothing
to do with it. Local farmers told him the garrison had gone off on a raid against
a Nicaean island and that the Venetian fleet had gone with it. A group got in
through a postern or over the wall — the accounts differ — opened a gate, and the
city changed hands.

Baldwin II left his crown and sword behind in the palace and took ship. Michael
VIII Palaiologos, who was three hundred kilometres away and did not hear for
several days, entered in August and was crowned in Hagia Sophia.

## What this record's ending says

`ended.type` is `conquest`, `by` names the Nicaean record, and the edge is
`latin-empire -> nicaea`, type `conquered by`, dated 1261.

There is a symmetry worth noticing in the two edges on this page. `byzantine ->
latin-empire` in 1204 records a city taken by a fleet and an army after a siege
and a sack, and `latin-empire -> nicaea` in 1261 records a city taken by eight
hundred men who happened to be walking past. The same wall, the same century, and
the vocabulary gives both the same word, which is the vocabulary being a
vocabulary.

## Fifty-seven years of what

This site's Nicaean record carries the same day and draws a conclusion about the
restorers: that recovering Constantinople cost the empire the Anatolian base that
had made the recovery possible, and that the frontier they stopped watching is
where the Ottomans came from.

The conclusion from this side is narrower and worth stating alongside it. A
polity that had survived on somebody else's navy ended on an afternoon when the
navy was elsewhere. Nothing about 1261 was a judgement on the Latin empire's
government; it was a judgement on its position.

Whether the government was any good is the question Van Tricht's book reopened
and this record's first chapter sets out. What can be said with the fields filled
in is that it had a documented constitutional procedure for choosing its head, an
inherited fiscal system it could not apply, an army of enfeoffed knights that was
destroyed in its first year, and a claim on the legitimacy of the empire it had
overthrown which it made in Greek and which this site now counts.

## What it left behind

In the Aegean, rather a lot. The principality of Achaea lasted until 1432, the
duchy of Athens until 1456 under Catalan and then Florentine rulers, and the
Venetian islands until the seventeenth century — Latin states on Greek ground
that outlasted the empire that spawned them by up to two hundred years. None of
them is in this corpus, and a reader who wants to know what happened to the
crusader settlement in Greece will not find it here.

In Constantinople, almost nothing. The Latin patriarchate collapsed with the
empire and left no hierarchy behind, which is why this record's
`religions_carried` count is zero: coding rule 2 asks for durable religious
institutions and means the word. What was left was a city with a quarter of its
former population, no treasury, and its bronze and its relics in Venice and
Paris, which is the state this site's Byzantine record spends its last two
centuries in.
