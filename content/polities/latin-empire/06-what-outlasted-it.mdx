The empire lasted fifty-seven years. Several of the things it created lasted
very much longer, and one of them is still in a treasury in Paris.

## In the Aegean

In the Aegean, rather a lot. The principality of Achaea lasted until 1432, the
duchy of Athens until 1456 under Catalan and then Florentine rulers, and the
Venetian islands until the seventeenth century — Latin states on Greek ground
that outlasted the empire that spawned them by up to two hundred years.

In Constantinople, almost nothing. The Latin patriarchate collapsed with the
empire and left no hierarchy behind: fifty-seven years of a rival church in
Hagia Sophia produced no congregation that survived the day the Greeks came
back. What was left was a city with a quarter of its former population, no
treasury, and its bronze and its relics in Venice and Paris — the condition
Byzantium spent its last two centuries in.

## The titular emperors

The other survival is a claim, and it is a small comedy.

Baldwin II spent the rest of his life as emperor of Constantinople without
Constantinople, and the title descended to his son and then out of the family
altogether — sold, inherited and married into among the princely houses of Italy
and France for the next century and a half. Its holders occasionally raised money
on it. None of them ever went east.

That is the ordinary fate of a title separated from its territory, and it is
worth setting beside what happened to the other imperial claim in the same city.
When Constantinople fell for the second time, in 1453, the conqueror took the
Roman title himself and meant it: Mehmed II called himself Kayser-i Rum and
behaved accordingly. A Turkish sultan's claim on the Roman name outlived the
Latin claim on it, and was taken more seriously in Europe.

## The one that mattered

The lasting consequence was not in Greece or in Paris but in the relationship
between the two halves of Christendom.

Before 1204 the schism between the Latin and Greek churches was a quarrel among
theologians and patriarchs, conducted in letters, periodically patched up. After
1204 it was a memory of the sack of a Christian capital by a crusading army, the
installation of a Latin patriarch in Hagia Sophia, and fifty-seven years of
occupation.

Every subsequent attempt at reunion — and there were several, all driven by
Byzantine emperors who needed western troops — foundered on a population that
would not have it. The best-documented remark of the period, whether or not
anybody actually said it, is that the turban of the Turk was preferable to the
Latin mitre. In 1453 the western help that arrived was a few hundred Genoese.

The Latin empire was a fifty-seven-year state that held a city and a coastal
strip and could not feed itself. What it did was make the reunion of the churches
impossible, at the beginning of the two centuries in which Byzantium's survival
depended on it.
