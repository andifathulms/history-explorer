The Fourth Crusade took Constantinople in April 1204, and the fullest account of
how is by a man who helped organise it. Geoffrey de Villehardouin, marshal of
Champagne, had been one of the six envoys who negotiated the shipping contract
with Venice, was present throughout, and dictated his chronicle afterwards. He
never once suggests the expedition should have gone to Egypt as agreed.

His description of the fleet going in is the passage everybody quotes, and it is
worth quoting because he is describing something he thought beautiful.

The pilgrims embarked on the Thursday after mid-Lent, the horses were carried in
the palanders, each division in its proper vessels, and the whole fleet drawn up
in a line with the ships distinct from the galleys and the transports. *It was a
noble prospect*, he writes, *for the line of battle extended more than half a
French league*.

## The first assault failed

On the Friday morning they came up to the city and attacked. In many places the
crusaders leapt ashore and charged to the walls; elsewhere the scaling ladders on
the ships were brought close enough that the men on them fought the defenders
hand to hand with lances across the gap.

The assault went on in more than a hundred places until nearly noon, and then —
*for their sins*, Villehardouin says — the pilgrims were repulsed, and those who
had come off the galleys and transports were driven back to them by force. The
losses that day were much greater on the crusader side than the Greek, and the
Greeks were greatly elated.

They came back three days later and took the city.

## What the treaty said

The important document is not the assault but the agreement made before it,
because it is the constitution of everything that followed.

The crusaders and the Venetians had settled in advance how the empire would be
divided if they took it. A quarter went to whoever was elected emperor. The
remaining three quarters were split equally between the Venetians and the
non-Venetian crusaders, and a committee of six from each side would choose the
emperor from among the leaders.

They elected Baldwin of Flanders. And the electors' arithmetic had an obvious
consequence which nobody at the time seems to have minded: the emperor of
Constantinople was to hold one quarter of an empire in which his own barons held
half and a foreign republic held the rest.

## What the partition actually distributed

Provinces that had not been conquered.

The treaty assigned the whole of the Byzantine empire — Thrace, Macedonia,
Greece, the islands, western Anatolia, the Black Sea coast — and the crusaders
held a city and the ground their army was standing on. Everything else had to be
taken from Greek lords who were still in it, and much of it never was.

That is why the Latin empire's expansion phase is also, on any reading, its
whole history. It spent 1204 and 1205 riding out to occupy what the parchment
said was theirs, and in April 1205 the field army was destroyed at Adrianople
and the process stopped for good. What had been taken by then was kept for a
while. What had not been taken by then was never going to be.
