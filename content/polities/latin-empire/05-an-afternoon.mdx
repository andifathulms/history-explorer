In July 1261 a Nicaean commander named Alexios Strategopoulos was passing near
Constantinople with a force of about eight hundred, on an errand that had nothing
to do with it. Local farmers told him the garrison had gone off on a raid against
a Nicaean island and that the Venetian fleet had gone with it. A group got in
through a postern or over the wall — the accounts differ — opened a gate, and the
city changed hands.

Baldwin II left his crown and sword behind in the palace and took ship. Michael
VIII Palaiologos, who was three hundred kilometres away and did not hear for
several days, entered in August and was crowned in Hagia Sophia.

## Two conquests of the same city

There is a symmetry worth noticing. In 1204 Constantinople was taken by a fleet
and an army, after a siege, a failed assault, a second assault and a three-day
sack. In 1261 it was taken by eight hundred men who happened to be walking past
and were told by farmers that the garrison was out.

The same wall, the same century, and the word for what happened is the same in
both cases.

## Fifty-seven years of what

The same day looks different from the other side. Recovering Constantinople cost
the restorers the Anatolian base that had made the recovery possible, and the
frontier they stopped watching is where the Ottomans came from.

The conclusion from this side is narrower and worth stating alongside it. A
polity that had survived on somebody else's navy ended on an afternoon when the
navy was elsewhere. Nothing about 1261 was a judgement on the Latin empire's
government; it was a judgement on its position.

Whether the government was any good is the question Van Tricht reopened. What
can be said is that it had a written constitutional procedure for choosing its
head, an inherited fiscal system it could not apply, an army of enfeoffed
knights destroyed in its first year, and a claim on the legitimacy of the empire
it had overthrown, made in Greek.
