The Latin empire had one competent ruler and he got the job because his brother
was captured.

When the news of Adrianople reached Henry of Flanders, he was across the water
in Anatolia holding Atramyttion, which was the one part of the partition treaty
the crusaders had actually made good. He abandoned it and marched for Thrace.

Villehardouin, who was among the survivors Henry was coming to rescue, records
what he brought with him and what he had to leave.

## Twenty thousand people who could not stay

Henry had with him the Armenians who had joined him against the Greeks — twenty
thousand of them, with all their wives and children, *who dared not remain
behind*.

They were foot soldiers encumbered with waggons and families. Henry knew they
would follow, so he left them and went on ahead with the cavalry to reach the
survivors faster.

That is the peak of Latin power in Anatolia described in a subordinate clause: a
Frankish prince with twenty thousand Armenian allies and their households, who
had thrown in with him against the Greeks and now could not be left where they
were — and whom he had to abandon on the road because speed mattered more.

## Two parties, both taking up arms

The detail that stays with a reader is small.

At Kartopolis, Henry's force met another Latin column, a hundred good lances and
five hundred mounted sergeants, coming from the other direction to relieve the
same defeat.

Each party took the other for Greeks and drew their weapons. Then they came near
enough to recognise each other, and did so with great satisfaction, and felt
much safer in their quarters that night for being together.

That is the empire in the summer of 1205: two small groups of Frenchmen in
hostile country, unable to identify each other at a distance, glad to have found
somebody.

## What he did with it

Henry was crowned in 1206 and reigned ten years, and by every account he was the
best ruler the Latin empire had.

He beat the Bulgarians who had destroyed his brother. He came to terms with
Theodore Laskaris in Anatolia and with the Greek lords in Europe, and — the thing
that distinguishes him from everyone else in the enterprise — he governed his
Greek subjects as subjects rather than as a defeated population, kept their
clergy, and was remembered by Greek writers as a just man, which is not a thing
Greek writers said about Latins.

Villehardouin's chronicle breaks off in 1207, which is why the fullest witness
to the conquest is not a witness to what it became.

## And the counter-example

The same passage records the other kind of man the expedition produced.

Five ships slipped their moorings and sailed away in silence — deserters going
home while the army they had come with was being destroyed. Villehardouin names
the worst of them, Peter de Fréville, and says he brought upon himself, from
fear of death, a shame that stayed with him all his days.

It is the only judgement of that kind in the chronicle, and it is worth noticing
what the marshal thought worth condemning: not the diversion of a crusade
against a Christian empire, which he helped arrange and never apologises for, but
leaving early.
