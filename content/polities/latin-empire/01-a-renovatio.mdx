The standard account of the Latin empire is that it was not really a state. A
band of French and Flemish knights held a Greek city they could not administer,
extracted what they could, achieved nothing, and were evicted after fifty-seven
years by the people they had displaced. It appears in most histories as an
interruption in somebody else's narrative — which is how it appears on this site,
in the ninth chapter of the Byzantine record and the first of the Nicaean one,
both of which were written before this record existed.

Van Tricht's book argues against that, and this record is coded from him.

## What they said they were doing

The title is the argument. The Latin emperors did not style themselves kings of
Constantinople or princes of Romania. They used the Byzantine imperial style,
took over the ceremonial, kept a substantial part of the Greek administration
and much of the fiscal apparatus, issued documents in Greek as well as Latin,
married into the Greek aristocracy, and described what had happened as a
*renovatio* — a renewal of the empire, not a replacement of it.

Some of that is what any conqueror claims. What makes the case worth taking
seriously is that the early emperors acted on it: Henry of Flanders, this
record's peak ruler, spent his ten years conciliating the Greek population,
restoring Orthodox clergy where the Latin hierarchy had displaced them, and
recruiting Greek nobles into his administration, against the wishes of the papal
legate and of most of his own barons.

This record's `legitimation` field accordingly reads `election, titulature`, and
the second value has a consequence on another page: this site's Byzantine record
now counts two successor claims where it counted one, the new entry being this
polity, on exactly the test coding rule 1 sets — a later polity asserting an
earlier polity's legitimacy in its own public self-description.

That the assertion was made by men who had just sacked the city does not filter
it out. Rule 1 counts unrecognised claims the same as recognised ones, on the
ground that the count measures whose legitimacy was worth claiming.

## The committee of twelve

The partition treaty provided that the emperor would be chosen by twelve
electors, six Venetian and six from the crusading army, and that whichever party
did not get the emperor would get the patriarchate. The twelve met in the palace
and chose Baldwin of Flanders over Boniface of Montferrat, who had expected it.
Venice took the patriarchate.

That is why this record's `succession_rule` reads `election`: a documented
constitutional procedure set out in a treaty and executed. What followed was a
family — Baldwin, then his brother Henry, then Henry's brother-in-law Peter,
then Peter's sons Robert and Baldwin II — and coding rule 7 takes the constituting
rule rather than the pattern.

It also puts this record in unexpected company. Four polities in this region
carry `election` in one field or another — this one, Venice, Genoa and the Holy
Roman Empire — and the Roman and Byzantine records carry it in `legitimation`
besides. Six of the eight records here have an elective element somewhere, which
is not true of any other region on this site and is not a coincidence.
