The standard account of the Latin empire is that it was not really a state. A
band of French and Flemish knights held a Greek city they could not administer,
extracted what they could, achieved nothing, and were evicted after fifty-seven
years by the people they had displaced. It appears in most histories as an
interruption in somebody else's narrative.

Van Tricht's book argues against that reading, and this account follows him.

## What they said they were doing

The title is the argument. The Latin emperors did not style themselves kings of
Constantinople or princes of Romania. They used the Byzantine imperial style,
took over the ceremonial, kept a substantial part of the Greek administration
and much of the fiscal apparatus, issued documents in Greek as well as Latin,
married into the Greek aristocracy, and described what had happened as a
*renovatio* — a renewal of the empire, not a replacement of it.

Some of that is what any conqueror claims. What makes the case worth taking
seriously is that the early emperors acted on it: Henry of Flanders, the
strongest ruler the empire had, spent his ten years conciliating the Greek population,
restoring Orthodox clergy where the Latin hierarchy had displaced them, and
recruiting Greek nobles into his administration, against the wishes of the papal
legate and of most of his own barons.

So the Latin emperors claimed to be the legitimate continuation of the empire
they had just destroyed, and made the claim in Greek, to Greeks, using the
Greeks' own formulas.

That the claim was made by the men who had sacked the city does not make it less
of a claim. What it measures is whose legitimacy was worth having — and the
answer, to a Flemish count sitting in Constantinople in 1204, was obvious enough
that he did not consider an alternative.

## The committee of twelve

The partition treaty provided that the emperor would be chosen by twelve
electors, six Venetian and six from the crusading army, and that whichever party
did not get the emperor would get the patriarchate. The twelve met in the palace
and chose Baldwin of Flanders over Boniface of Montferrat, who had expected it.
Venice took the patriarchate.

So the empire had a written constitutional procedure for choosing its head, set
out in a treaty and actually executed. What followed in practice was a family:
Baldwin, then his brother Henry, then Henry's brother-in-law Peter, then Peter's
sons Robert and Baldwin II. The rule was election and the outcome was a
dynasty, which is the usual relationship between the two.

It also puts the Latin empire in company it would not have expected. An
elective element turns up almost everywhere in this part of the world: at Rome,
at Byzantium, at Venice, at Genoa, in the Holy Roman Empire. The states that
were straightforwardly hereditary — the Carolingians, the Nicaean emperors — are
the exceptions.

That is not a claim about the Mediterranean being unusually republican. It is
what happens downstream of an empire whose own emperors held their powers by a
law voted in the senate. Everybody who inherited the argument inherited the
vocabulary for it, including a Flemish count elected by a committee of twelve in
a palace his army had just taken.
