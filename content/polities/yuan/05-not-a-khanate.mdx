This corpus carries four polities out of the Mongol division: the Ilkhanate, the
Chagatai khanate, the Golden Horde, and this one. The Mongol record's third
chapter is about the division that produced them.

Three of the four took a share of somewhere. The Yuan took a settled empire
entire — the whole of China, north and south, for the first time in three and a
half centuries — and that is the difference this record is written around.

It shows up in `legitimation`, which carries `conquest` alongside `descent` and
`divine-sanction`. The Ilkhanate's rulers eventually became Muslim sovereigns of
Iran and stopped needing to say they had conquered it. The Yuan never got there.
Its own legal system sorted the population into four ranked categories with
Mongols at the top and the southern Chinese — the population of the Song, the
majority of the empire's people — at the bottom, and it kept the examinations
suspended for the first thirty years and quota-restricted thereafter.

A dynasty that claims the Mandate of Heaven while running a legal hierarchy in
which the people it claims it over are ranked last is making two arguments that
cannot both be load-bearing.

## What withdrew

`successor_claims` on this record reads one, and the item is the Northern Yuan.

In 1368 the court did not fall. It left — north, with the seals, the archives and
the reign titles — and its successors went on issuing them from the steppe for
two more centuries. That is why this corpus's Ming record has a turning point at
Tumu in 1449, where a Ming emperor was captured alive by a Mongol force, and a
whole chapter about a wall.

Read together the two records describe the same fact from opposite sides: a
dynasty that says it is still the dynasty, and a dynasty that has to keep an army
in the field about it for two hundred years. Neither `ended.type` can carry that.
This record's says `conquest`, `by: [ming]`, at 1368, and it is true about the
territory and wrong about the claim.
