title: A reign with nothing left to do

Temür Khan took the throne in 1294 the moment his grandfather Kublai died,
secured against rival claimants by the general Bayan's own prompt action,
and then spent thirteen years doing something almost none of his predecessors
had managed: ruling without launching a single major war of conquest.

## A reign built for peace by circumstance as much as choice

Part of that was deliberate. Temür governed, by his own contemporaries'
account, with a visible desire to win the sympathy of his Chinese subjects -
abolishing the arbitrary power of life and death the Mongol princes and
Genghisid nobility had been exercising in their own allotted territories, and
decreeing that no execution could proceed without his own express authority.
Part of it was simple exhaustion: Kublai had already done so much conquering
that there was very little obvious left for a successor to add, and a severe
famine in the northern provinces early in the reign made the preservation of
peace less a policy choice than a plain necessity. When the famine passed it
left banditry in its place, bands that could raid with near impunity in small
groups but collapsed the moment they tried to combine into anything large
enough for Mongol garrisons to engage directly.

## The one war he could not avoid

Not every front stayed quiet. Kaidu, the Ögödeid prince who had spent
decades contesting the Great Khanate itself, kept his own rebellion alive on
the northern steppe throughout the reign - a war that produced real Yuan
victories under the general Changar and one real disaster in 1298, when
Temür's own son-in-law Kolikisse, his force surprised, chose an honorable
death over surrender. Kaidu's own death from chagrin after a defeat in 1301
did not end the fighting, though it steadily drained the urgency out of it.

## The war he chose, and regretted

The one campaign that was genuinely Temür's own doing went badly enough to
prove why his reign otherwise avoided them. A flattering minister persuaded
him that conquering the remote southwestern territory of Papesifu would cost
little and add cheaply to his own reputation as a ruler. The resulting
expedition lost a third of its twenty-thousand-man force to climate before
it even arrived, provoked a rising across Guizhou and Yunnan when its
commander's exactions became too much for the local population to bear, and
spread as far as Burma, Laos, Annam and Tonkin before Temür had to strip
garrisons from three provinces to put it down. The rising was eventually
broken and its leaders executed, but the whole affair added, in the record's
own judgment, neither territory nor reputation to the man who had authorized
it - a rare failure inside a reign otherwise defined by restraint.

## What thirteen quiet years left behind

Temür fell ill in 1306 and died the following year, leaving no direct heir.
Chinese chroniclers praised his character in terms stronger than they gave
most Mongol rulers, crediting him with having made the dynasty visibly more
Chinese in its own habits of government without any of Kublai's own costly
wars to pay for it. Whatever else can be said of the reign, the balance sheet
it left behind was a genuinely rare one for this dynasty: when Temür died,
Mongol authority in China stood no weaker than when he had inherited it -
the one Yuan emperor after Kublai of whom that could be said without
qualification.
