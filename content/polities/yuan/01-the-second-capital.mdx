The Yuan begins with a disputed election and ends up, for that reason, as a
Chinese dynasty rather than a Mongol one.

## 1260

When the Great Khan Möngke died on campaign in 1259, the succession should have
been settled by a *quriltai* — an assembly of the Mongol nobility, held in
Mongolia, with the princes of the whole empire present.

Kublai did not wait for one. He held his own assembly at Kaiping in Inner
Mongolia, among the commanders of the armies he had been leading in China, and
had himself proclaimed. His younger brother Ariq Böke held the proper kind of
assembly at Karakorum, with the proper people, and was proclaimed too.

The war between them lasted four years. Kublai won it because he held north
China and could feed his army from it while Karakorum, which had never been able
to feed itself, depended on grain convoys from the south that he cut.

That is the whole argument of the dynasty in one campaign. The brother with the
farmland beat the brother with the steppe, and the empire's centre of gravity
moved with the result.

## What winning that way cost

It cost the empire. Kublai's election was procedurally indefensible and the
western khanates said so; the Golden Horde and the Chagatai ulus effectively
stopped treating the Great Khan as anything but a distant relative, and the
Ilkhans in Iran, who did recognise him, were four thousand kilometres away.

After 1260 there was no single Mongol empire in any operative sense. There were
four states with a common ancestry, of which Kublai's was the largest, richest
and most administratively developed, and which spent the next century minding
its own business.

## Becoming a dynasty

He then did something no previous Great Khan had done: he took a Chinese
dynastic name.

In 1271 the state was proclaimed the Great Yuan — the name taken from the *Book
of Changes*, meaning the primal origin — and with it came the whole apparatus of
Chinese legitimacy: a dynastic title rather than a people's name, ancestral
temples with posthumous imperial titles conferred on Chinggis and his
successors, a court calendar, and the compilation of dynastic histories for the
Liao, the Jin and eventually the Song.

He built a capital to match at Dadu, on the site of the Jin capital and the
future Beijing: a walled rectangle on a grid, laid out on classical principles
by a Chinese official, with the palace at the centre and the ancestral temple
and altars of soil and grain in their canonical positions.

And he kept Shangdu — Kaiping, where he had been elected — as a summer capital,
and went there every year with the court.

Two capitals, one for each half of what he was. The word travelled to Europe
through Marco Polo, and six hundred years later Coleridge wrote a poem about it
under the name Xanadu.

## What he never did

He never became Chinese, and the state never pretended he had.

The Mongols did not adopt the examinations for another forty years and never
relied on them. The population was formally divided into four ranks — Mongols,
central and western Asians, northern Chinese, southern Chinese — with the last
two excluded from the senior posts. Mongolian was the language of the court;
Persians, Turks, Uyghurs and at least one Venetian held offices that Chinese
scholars expected to hold.

A dynasty with a Chinese name, a Chinese capital, a Chinese calendar and a
foreign ruling caste that would not let the Chinese govern is a contradiction
that lasted ninety-seven years, which is roughly how long such things last.
