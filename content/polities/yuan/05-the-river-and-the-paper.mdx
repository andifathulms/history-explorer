The Yuan was killed by a currency and a river, in that order, and the second
would not have been fatal without the first.

## The money

The Yuan ran the first economy in history in which paper was the only legal
currency. Not paper alongside coin — paper instead of it. Silver and copper were
called in; the notes were backed by a silver reserve, exchangeable, and legally
required for all transactions.

For about forty years it worked, and it is one of the genuinely remarkable
administrative achievements anywhere. Marco Polo gives it a chapter, and cannot
quite get over it:

> The Emperor's Mint then is in this same City of Cambaluc, and the way it is
> wrought is such that you might say he hath the Secret of Alchemy in
> perfection, and you would be right!

He then describes the process exactly: the fine white bast between the wood and
the outer bark of the mulberry, made into sheets, cut into denominations from
half a *tornesel* up to ten bezants of gold, each signed by a succession of
officials and finally stamped in vermilion by the Khan's own deputy — *the
Money is then authentic. Any one forging it would be punished with death.*

Two of his observations are the whole fiscal system. First: the Khan has this
made every year in a quantity *which costs him nothing, that it must equal in
amount all the treasure in the world.* Second: foreign merchants arriving with
gold, silver, gems or pearls are forbidden to sell to anyone but the emperor,
who has twelve experts appraise the goods and pays for them in paper — and the
merchants accept readily, because nobody else pays as well and nobody else pays
as promptly.

A Venetian looking at that saw alchemy. What he was actually describing is a
state buying the world's luxury imports with money it printed, and a bullion
monopoly enforcing the currency at the point where bullion entered the
country.

Then it did what unbacked paper does. Military campaigns, the court's spending,
the subsidies paid to Mongol princes, and the loss of revenue from a countryside
in trouble were all covered by issuing more, and the reserve stopped matching
the issue. There were currency reforms in 1287 and again in 1309, each of which
devalued the existing notes against a new issue. The last, in 1350, was
catastrophic: prices rose beyond the ability of the state to pay its own bills
in its own money, and people went back to barter and to hoarded coin.

A government whose entire fiscal system is a promise loses everything the moment
the promise is not believed, and by the 1350s it was not.

## The river

The Yellow River carries more silt than any large river in the world, builds its
own bed above the surrounding plain, and periodically breaks out and changes
course by hundreds of kilometres. It had done so before and would again.

In the 1340s it did it repeatedly, flooding the north China plain, wrecking the
Grand Canal and cutting the grain supply to a capital that could not feed
itself. Famine followed, and plague — the same pandemic that reached Europe in
1347 was moving through Asia in the same decades, and the Chinese records
describe severe epidemics in the 1330s and 1340s.

In 1351 the government did the responsible thing and mobilised, by some accounts,
a hundred and fifty thousand conscripted labourers to return the river to its old
channel.

The work succeeded. The mobilisation destroyed the dynasty. A hundred and fifty
thousand hungry men from a ruined province, working under corvée for a
government whose money had stopped being worth anything, was the largest
concentration of grievance in China, and the White Lotus preachers were already
among them. The Red Turban rising began that year.

## What the court was doing

Fighting itself. Between 1307 and 1333 there were nine emperors; two were
murdered, one reigned for fifty-three days, and the succession was decided by
whichever faction of Mongol princes and commanders had the palace guard.

That instability has a structural cause and it is the one the dynasty was
founded on. The Mongols never adopted a fixed succession rule, because their own
tradition was election by assembly and the assembly had been abandoned in 1260.
What replaced it was neither: not a *quriltai*, not primogeniture, just the
strongest faction at court. Every reign began with a purge and ended with one.

## The last twenty years

By 1360 the Yuan controlled the north, the canal and not much else, while its
generals in the south fought the rebels and each other, and the court in Dadu
argued.

In 1368 a Ming army came north. The emperor Toghon Temür left the capital
without fighting for it, and rode back to Mongolia with his court.

That is the least discussed fact about the fall. The dynasty did not end at
Dadu; it went home, kept the dynastic name, kept the seals, and went on
claiming to be the legitimate government of China from the steppe for another
two centuries. The Ming called them the remnant Yuan and campaigned against
them for two hundred years without settling it, and in 1449 one of those
campaigns lost a Ming emperor.
