This record's second chapter asks why the Yuan lasted less than a century when
every other dynasty that held the whole of China lasted two or three. It had no
institutions block to answer with. It has one now, and the answer is in
`succession_rule`.

The value is `tanistry`: a successor raised by an assembly of the eligible, from
among all the eligible.

## What that is for

On the steppe it is a good rule. A confederation whose survival depends on the
competence of the man leading it cannot afford to be handed a child, and an
assembly of the leading families choosing from a pool of adult claimants will
usually produce somebody who can command. It is why this corpus's Mongol record
exists at the scale it does.

In a settled empire it is a war. The pool of eligible Chinggisids is large, every
one of them has a following, the assembly can be packed or convened twice, and
the loser of a disputed succession has an army rather than a monastery.

The Yuan never replaced it. Kublai's own accession in 1260 was decided by two
rival assemblies and four years of fighting his brother. Between 1307 and 1333
the throne changed hands eight times; one emperor was murdered by his guard
commanders at the summer camp in 1323, which is this record's second turning
point.

## Why they did not fix it

This is the part worth putting on a page rather than in a field.

The Chinese answer to the problem — a designated heir apparent, named in
advance, with the rule written down — was available, well documented, and being
used by every dynasty the Yuan's own historians were compiling records of. This
corpus's Ming record shows what it looks like when it is codified, and this
corpus's Han record shows it working for four centuries at the cost of a great
many child emperors.

Adopting it would have meant conceding that the emperor of China was something
other than the Great Khan — that the throne descended by a Chinese rule rather
than being conferred by Mongol nobles. `legitimation` on this record carries
three values, `descent`, `divine-sanction` and `conquest`, and they are three
different constituencies: the Chinggisid princes, the Chinese bureaucracy, and
the population being ruled. Fixing the succession would have meant choosing among
them.

They did not choose, and got eight emperors in twenty-six years, and then a
rising among conscripted labourers on the Yellow River which is this record's
third turning point and the beginning of the Ming.
