---
title: Why it lasted less than a century
drafted_from: franke-twitchett-1994
phase: end
---

The Yuan held all of China for eighty-nine years, which is short. The Liao
managed two hundred and nine on a fraction of the territory, the Jin a hundred
and nineteen, the Qing later two hundred and sixty-eight.

Several reasons compound. The Mongol elite was small and its legal privileges
made it visibly foreign in a way the Khitan and Jurchen arrangements had softened.
The suspension of the examinations for most of the dynasty cut off the route by
which Chinese literati normally attached themselves to a regime, leaving a large
educated class with no stake in it. Revenue was farmed to Central Asian
financiers whose methods were efficient and detested. Paper currency was issued
against no reserve and inflated.

Then the Yellow River changed course in the 1340s, the conscripted repair works
gathered hundreds of thousands of labourers in one place, plague arrived, and the
rebellions began in the exact populations the dynasty had least reason to trust.

The ending is unusual for this corpus. The Yuan court was not destroyed; it
withdrew to the steppe in 1368 and continued as the Northern Yuan for two more
centuries, still claiming the mandate. Almost every other conquest dynasty here
ended where it stood. The Mongols had somewhere to go back to, and that is a
consequence of never having fully come inside.
