In the early 1270s two Nestorian Christian monks left Khanbaliq — Kublai's own
capital — to walk to Jerusalem, and the account one of them left behind is the
only description of the Yuan's western frontier written by someone who actually
crossed it on foot while the empire was at war with itself.

## Where they were from

Rabban Sauma was born in Khanbaliq itself, the son of a Nestorian nobleman of the
city, ordained a priest there and later a hermit in the hills nearby. His
companion Markos was a younger monk from Koshang, a town of Önggüd Mongols further
west who had been Christian for generations. Neither man was Chinese, and neither
was a stranger to the court: the community they belonged to had held office and
standing in the Mongol capital since Chinggis's day, and Sauma's own account takes
that standing for granted rather than explaining it.

They decided to go to Jerusalem to pray at the tombs of the martyrs, gave away
their belongings, and went to the capital to find a caravan.

## Kublai's sons-in-law

Word of two respected monks leaving the country reached Könchi and Ay-buqa, sons-
in-law of Kublai Khan himself, who had the pair brought before them and tried,
with real affection, to talk them out of it.

> Why are ye leaving this country of ours and going to the West? For we have
> taken very great trouble to draw hither monks and fathers from the West. How can
> we allow you to go away?

Sauma's answer is the whole of a monk's case in one line: *we have cast away the
world; as long as we live in the society of men there will be no peace to us.*

Failing to change their minds, the princes did what a Mongol grandee did for
anyone travelling far on his goodwill: they loaded them with riding animals, gold,
silver and clothing, over the monks' own objections that they needed none of it.
*Accept these gifts as a loan*, the princes said. *If necessity does not arise,
and ye arrive safe and sound, distribute them among the monasteries that are
there.* This was, in effect, a Mongol travel warrant made of goods instead of
paper — the same function a paiza or yarlikh performed for an official envoy,
extended informally to two men of no rank at all, because they were going to pray
for the khan's own western Christian subjects.

## What the road actually looked like

The Yuan's western reach was not a line on a map. It was a country being fought
over, and the monks walked straight through the middle of that fight.

They came to Tangut — the old Xi Xia country, by then a Yuan province — and were
welcomed with gifts by a population Sauma describes as ardent believers. Then two
months across a waterless desert to Khotan, with drinkable water found on only
eight days of the whole crossing. And there they arrived in the middle of an
actual war: Kublai's forces against a rebel commander who had fled into the
country and, in Sauma's plain words, *destroyed thousands of men therein*. The
roads were cut. Grain could not be found. People were dying of hunger around them.

They waited six months for it to pass, then went on to Kashgar and found the city
*empty of its inhabitants, because it had been already plundered by the enemy.*

## Kaidu

Then they reached Talas, and the camp of the man Sauma's editor correctly
identifies as Kublai's own cousin: Qaidu, the Ögedeid prince who had refused to
recognise Kublai's succession in 1260 and had been at open war with him, on and
off, for the whole of the monks' lifetime.

They went into his presence, blessed his kingdom, and asked for a safe-conduct
through his territory. He gave it to them.

Two monks travelling on gifts and goodwill from Kublai's own sons-in-law walked
into the camp of the man Kublai's empire had been fighting for a decade, and were
received, blessed, and sent on their way with the same courtesy the Yuan court
had shown them. Whatever Qaidu's war with Kublai was actually about, it was not
a war on travellers, and it was certainly not a war that recognised borders the
way a modern reader expects a war to.

## Why this belongs beside the ortogh and the paper money

Because it is evidence of the same thing from a completely different direction.

The Yuan financed a merchant aristocracy that ran silver the length of the empire
regardless of which khanate it was crossing. It printed a currency an Ilkhanid
observer's cousin marvelled at. And it produced two monks who could walk from
Beijing to Khorasan through a live civil war, unarmed, protected at every stage
not by soldiers but by a network of obligation — Mongol courtesy to travellers,
Christian hospitality from Tangut to Talas to Maragha, and a rebel prince's
willingness to honour a blessing from men who served a court he did not
recognise.

The empire that could not agree on who its Great Khan was could still move two
harmless men four thousand miles through the middle of the argument without
either side laying a hand on them. That is not a small thing to be true of a
civil war, and it says something about what the word empire still meant, even
when it had stopped meaning one government.

They reached Khorasan with most of what they had been given already spent or lost
on the road, and went on to Maragha, where the story of one of them ends and the
story of the Ilkhanate's church resumes it.
