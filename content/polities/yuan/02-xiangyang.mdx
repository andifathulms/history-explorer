The Southern Song took forty-five years to conquer, which is longer than the
Mongols spent on anything else, and the reason is water.

## Why it was hard

Everything that had made the Mongol armies unstoppable across Eurasia worked
less well south of the Huai. The country is wet, cut by rivers and canals,
terraced, and in summer it is hot in a way that killed both the horses and the
men. The Song had the largest navy in the world, walled cities in enormous
numbers, gunpowder weapons, and a state that — whatever its failures against
the Jin — could still put very large armies into the field.

Möngke had died in 1259 on that campaign, of dysentery or wounds depending on
the account, which is the fact that produced the succession war and the
division of the empire.

## The siege

Xiangyang and Fancheng faced each other across the Han river, and between them
they were the lock on the middle Yangtze. Everything the Mongols needed to do
depended on getting past them.

The siege lasted from 1268 to 1273 — five years. The Mongols built a ring of
forts, blockaded the river, and were still unable to take the walls. Song relief
convoys got through by river more than once.

What broke it was artillery, and the artillery was imported. Kublai sent to his
nephew the Ilkhan in Iran for engineers, and two Muslim specialists — the
sources name them Ismail and Ala al-Din — arrived and built counterweight
trebuchets of the Western type, larger and far more powerful than anything in
Chinese use. The Chinese sources call them *Huihui pao*, Muslim catapults.

Fancheng fell first. Xiangyang surrendered shortly after, its commander given
terms and taken into Yuan service.

That is the Mongol empire working exactly as designed: a siege in central China
broken by Persian engineers summoned from Tabriz, because one family held both
ends of the road.

## The last six years

After Xiangyang it went quickly. Bayan took the Yangtze in 1275, Hangzhou
surrendered in 1276 and the child emperor and the dowager empress were sent
north, and the court that would not surrender kept moving south by sea with a
fleet and two more child emperors.

It ended at Yamen, off the Guangdong coast, in 1279: a naval battle in which the
Song fleet was chained together and destroyed, and the chief minister Lu Xiufu
put the last emperor — seven years old — on his back and jumped into the sea.

The accounts say tens of thousands went into the water after them. Whatever the
number, it is the only Chinese dynasty that ends in a mass suicide at sea rather
than an abdication ceremony, and Chinese memory has never treated it as anything
else.

## What was acquired

For the first time since 907, one government held everything from the Amur to
Hainan. It was also, in that moment, the richest and most populous territory on
earth, with the most advanced economy — printing, paper money, iron production,
canal transport, ocean shipping — and it had all been captured intact by a
regime that had spent seventy years learning not to destroy such things.

Kublai's two failed invasions of Japan, in 1274 and 1281, both broken by storms;
the failed expeditions to Vietnam, Champa and Java — those are what happened
when the same machine kept going after the useful conquest was finished. The
sources for the Java expedition of 1293 record it being outmanoeuvred by a
Javanese prince who used it to destroy his own rival and then turned on it.

An empire that had just absorbed the wealthiest state in the world spent the
next fifteen years losing fleets in the tropics, which is what a conquest
machine does when there is nothing left worth conquering.
