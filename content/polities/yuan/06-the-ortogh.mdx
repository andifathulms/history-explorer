The Yuan was funded by a Chinese land tax and the salt monopoly, which supplied
the largest single share of the money. Both of those would be true of any
Chinese dynasty of any period.

What they leave out is the part that belonged to nobody else.

## Merchant partnerships with imperial capital

The *ortogh* were associations of merchants, largely Central Asian and mostly
Muslim, financed directly by Mongol princes and by the imperial household. A
prince put up silver; the partnership traded with it across the length of the
empire and beyond it; the profits were divided.

They also farmed taxes. A partnership would advance the state a sum against the
revenue of a district and then collect it, at whatever it could extract. And they
lent — to peasants, at rates the Yuan's own edicts periodically tried to cap,
against the security of land and people.

So the imperial family was simultaneously the government, the largest investor in
the empire's long-distance trade, the creditor of its taxpayers, and the
beneficiary of the tax-farming contracts. Venice and Genoa were states run by
merchants. The Yuan is the inverse: merchants run as an arm of the ruling
family's private capital.

## Why it disappears from the summaries

Ask where a pre-modern state's money came from and the answers available are
land, heads, tolls, tribute, plunder, mines, monopolies. They are all categories
of *extraction* — of taking a share of something somebody else produced.

The ortogh are not extraction. They are finance: the ruling house putting silver
into a trading partnership and taking a return on it, with the state's own
courier network, tax farms and coercive power available to the partnership as
working capital. There is no ordinary way to describe a government whose rulers
were also its principal investors, which is why the arrangement tends to vanish
from any short account of how the Yuan was funded.

A land tax and a salt monopoly are not a wrong description of Yuan finance. They
are simply the description that fits every other dynasty too, applied to the one
government in Chinese history whose treasury was entangled with a commercial
partnership running to Tabriz.
