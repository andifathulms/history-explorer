There are more published administrative tablets from this hundred and eight
years than from any other comparable stretch of the ancient world. The number
usually given for the Ur III corpus is somewhere well over a hundred thousand,
and it is still rising, and none of it is literature. It is receipts.

A dead sheep is recorded, with the date, the animal's condition, the official
responsible for it and the official who received the carcass. A day's labour by
a named woman in a milling establishment is recorded, with her ration. Reed
bundles, fish, silver, barley, hides, the movement of oxen between provinces —
all of it entered, all of it dated to the day by a system in which every year
has an official name, the names are listed in order on tablets kept for the
purpose, and the sequence has no gaps.

## The bala

What the receipts show is a fiscal system unlike anything else attested for two
thousand years. The Ur III state ran an arrangement called the *bala*: each
province delivered into a central pool during its assigned months of the year
and drew from that pool during the others. It is a redistributive rotation
rather than a straightforward tax, and it is reconstructed entirely from the
delivery records themselves — nobody in antiquity described it. Beside it, the
*gun mada*, a levy in cattle and sheep owed by the militarised zone on the
eastern frontier.

It is the earliest tax system anywhere that can be described in operational
detail, by more than a thousand years, and the next one that can be described
at this resolution is Neo-Babylonian, at the far end of Mesopotamian history.

What the receipts do not supply is a figure for how much ground the dynasty
held. The standard tabulations of ancient empires give extents for Akkad, for
Mitanni, for Hatti, for Egypt's New Kingdom, for both Assyrias and for
Neo-Babylon, and they do not carry Ur III at all.

So the state whose provincial deliveries can be counted sheep by sheep is one
of the few in the region with no published size. Documentation is not size, and
the thing this dynasty is remarkable for is not the thing empires are usually
measured by.

## Shulgi

The reign that built the apparatus is the second and longest, forty-seven
years. Standardised weights and measures, a single state calendar imposed
across the provinces, a reformed accounting system with a standard year of
three hundred and sixty days for the bookkeeping, the scribal schools, a road
system with staging posts, a reorganisation of the army, and the conversion of
the eastern frontier into a belt of garrisoned provinces.

None of that can be dated to a year; the reforms are spread across decades of
one reign. What can be dated is the thing that came with them: in his
twenty-first year Shulgi was made a god.
