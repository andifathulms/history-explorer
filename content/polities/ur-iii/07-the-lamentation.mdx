Sumerian literature produced a form for exactly one purpose: mourning a
destroyed city. The lament for Ur is the fullest of them, and it is a poem about
the fall of this state, written afterwards, in the language of the people it
happened to.

## The storm

The catastrophe is not narrated as an invasion. It is narrated as weather.

Enlil, having taken the good storm away from Sumer, calls down another: an evil
storm, a storm that annihilates the land, the great storm of heaven, an
afflicting storm, a destructive storm. It is helped by evil winds, by fire, by
darkness and by unbearable heat. Kramer notes that of the thirty-two lines of one
song, only two mention any destructive agent that is not an element.

The enemies are there — Sutians and Elamites, breaking up the Ekishnugal with
pickaxes — but they are almost incidental to the way the poem understands what
happened. What struck Ur was decided in a divine assembly, and the men with the
axes were the instrument.

## What was in the streets

Then the poem becomes specific, and stops being figurative at all.

The people of Sumer lay on the walls of their cities and their dead filled the
gates. Those killed by weapons lay unburied and untended; those who escaped the
weapons were brought down by the storm. Weak and strong alike died of famine.
Parents who stayed in their houses were caught by the fire. Suckling infants were
carried off by the water. The breasts of nursing mothers were pried open.

Judgment and counsel perished in the land. Parents abandoned their children and
husbands their wives, and everything they owned lay scattered.

## The goddess who has nowhere to go

The centre of the poem is Ningal, Ur's own goddess, and what makes it unbearable
is that she is treated as a displaced person rather than as a deity.

Her city has been cursed by Anu; Enlil has turned hostile to her house. Dust has
gathered in her rivers and the sparkling water does not flow. There is no grain
in her fields and the field worker is gone. Her palm groves and vineyards have
brought forth mountain thorn. Her possessions have been carried off upriver and
down; her gold and stone and lapis lazuli lie scattered, and her ornaments are
now on the bodies of people who do not know what precious stone is. Her sons and
daughters have been taken away captive. She is no longer queen of Ur.

Where, the poem asks on her behalf, shall she sit down, and where shall she stand
up? She goes out from the city and finds no rest; she goes out from the house and
finds nowhere to live. *She is a stranger in a strange city*, and curses and abuse
are heaped on her there.

She goes to her husband to weep about the house and city that have been
destroyed. And then:

> Like a fallen ox she will lie down beside the ruins of her city and her house
> and will not rise up.

Kramer's rendering of the poem's own verdict on the destruction is that it was
*attacked without cause*.

## What it was for

This was not a private grief. Laments of this kind were liturgical, composed and
performed for the rebuilding of temples, and the copies that survive were made by
scribes in the kingdom of Isin — the state whose kings had taken Ur back from the
Elamite garrison and presented themselves as its restorers.

So the poem is doing two things at once. It records, in detail nobody had any
motive to invent, what happens to a city when the state that fed it stops
working. And it is also an instrument of the successor regime, because a city
mourned properly is a city whose restoration means something.

The most complete account of the end of this polity was written by the people who
profited from it, for use in a ceremony.
