An Elamite coalition took Ur, sacked it, and carried Ibbi-Sin away east to
Anshan, where he disappears. A garrison was left in the ruins and stayed for
several years until Ishbi-Erra of Isin drove it out — which allowed the kings of
Isin to present themselves as the restorers of Ur, and is part of why this
record's `successor_claims` count is 1.

This record's `ended.by` is empty. Elam is not in this corpus, and this is now
the third record that has to work around the fact: the Elymais page in Iran
before Islam calls Elam one of this region's larger absences, the Neo-Assyrian
page has Ashurbanipal destroying it in the 640s, and this one ends with it. A polity that three separate records
point at, across sixteen hundred years, is a gap of a specific and recognisable
kind, and this site has learned to say so on the page rather than in a list
somewhere.

## The lament

What survives from the moment is not a chronicle. It is a poem — the Lament for
Ur, and there are several others for Sumer, for Nippur, for Uruk, for Eridu — in
which the goddess Ningal pleads with the assembly of the gods for her city and is
refused, and the city is destroyed because the gods had decided it would be.

> The people, like broken shards, littered the streets.

The laments were composed, or given their surviving form, in the scribal schools
of the kingdom of Isin, by the successors of the men this dynasty had trained.
They were copied for centuries afterwards as school texts and as liturgy, which
is how they survive. So the fall of Ur reaches us in the literary form the fallen
state's own educational apparatus had developed, produced by the institution that
outlived the institution.

That is worth putting beside the previous chapter. The administrative record —
receipts, deliveries, rations, prices — is contemporary, disinterested, and stops
abruptly. The literary record is retrospective, theological, and never stops. On
this site the first is what fills the `institutions` fields and the second is
what fills the chapters, and they answer different questions and cannot check
each other.

## What the dynasty left

The Sumerian language stopped being a spoken language of government within a few
generations, and after this every regime in Mesopotamia is Akkadian-speaking and
most of them are Amorite. This is the last time the political centre of the Near
East is in the far south.

What did not stop was the writing. Ur-Nammu's law collection — the earliest
surviving one in the world, two centuries before Hammurabi's, with the same
structure of prologue, conditional provisions and epilogue — was copied in
schools long after the dynasty; Roth prints it alongside Hammurabi's and the
Hittite laws in a single volume, which is a fair picture of how this region's
legal writing actually travelled. The hymns and the laments and the king list
went the same way.

And the succession field on this record is null. Five kings of one family, a
hundred and eight years, not one disputed accession, and nothing in the whole
enormous archive states the rule. The Old Babylonian record in this region
carries the identical null for the identical reason across eleven generations.
Coding rule 7 takes the rule and not the outcome, and Mesopotamia — which
legislated the price of hiring an ox, the penalty for a badly built house and the
compensation for a slapped face — did not legislate this, or did not write it
where anyone has found it.
