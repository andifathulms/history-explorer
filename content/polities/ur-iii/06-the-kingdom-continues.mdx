Elam took Ur in 2004, carried off the last king and the statue of the moon god,
and left a garrison in the ruins. Then, as far as anyone can tell, they went
home.

That is the first surprising thing about the fall. Leonard King, surveying the
period in 1910, states it flatly: no period of Elamite control in Babylonia
followed the fall of Ur. The people who destroyed the most thoroughly
administered state in the world did not take over its administration, and made
no attempt to.

## What Isin did instead

What followed in southern Mesopotamia was continuity of a kind that makes the
word "fall" awkward.

The kings of Isin, beginning with the officer who had walked away from Ibbi-Sin
with an army and a grain contract, called themselves king of Sumer and Akkad.
They used Ur's titles, kept the divine determinative in front of their names
for another century, dated by year-names in the same style, and went on issuing
law in the same forms — the collection of Lipit-Ishtar of Isin survives, and it
sits in the same tradition as Ur-Nammu's before it and Hammurabi's after.

They also rebuilt Ur, restored the temple of the moon god and installed their
own daughters as his priestesses, which is what the kings of Ur had done.

King's judgement is that the kingdom of Sumer and Akkad was continuous across
2004. A dynasty ended. The state did not obviously stop.

## Why it fell, on the evidence available

The other thing King's survey offers is a diagnosis, and it is the one an
administrator would reach.

He attributes the decline of Ur to what happens when a highly centralised
administration is combined with a complete delegation of authority by the king:
provincial governors accumulating several offices at once, and misgoverning the
provinces they held. Not invasion, and not climate — the failure mode of an
over-organised state whose organisation depended on the centre paying
attention.

That is a reading from 1910 and much has been added since. It has the merit of
being drawn from the same documents the dynasty is known by. The state that
recorded every sheep is the state whose collapse can be read in the recording.

## What actually survived

Not the territory, which broke up; not the dynasty, which ended in captivity in
Elam.

What survived was everything the scribal schools had written down. The Sumerian
literary corpus was fixed under Ur III and copied for the next fifteen hundred
years by people who did not speak the language. The lament for Ur's own
destruction became a literary form, and was reused for other cities. The year
formulas, the weight standards Shulgi had reformed, the calendar, the shape of
a law collection and the practice of dating documents by an event all continued
without interruption into Isin, into Larsa, and into Babylon.

Ur III is the earliest state whose bureaucracy is fully legible, and the reason
it is legible is that its own successors kept the system running and its own
schools kept teaching the texts. A hundred thousand receipts survive because
nobody ever decided to stop writing them that way.
