From his twenty-first year Shulgi's name is written with the cuneiform sign that
marks a divine name. Hymns are composed to him in the first person in which he
runs from Nippur to Ur and back in a day, plays every instrument, reads any
tablet put in front of him and knows five languages. Offerings are made to him in
temples. His statues receive a cult. He is not a king favoured by gods; he is
listed among them.

Naram-Sin of Akkad had done it first, three centuries earlier, and this site's
Akkadian record carries the fact. What is different here is that it stuck: every
king of this dynasty after Shulgi was divine, and the practice continued under
the kings of Isin for another century. The `legitimation` field on this record
reads `divine-sanction, descent, titulature`, and the first value is doing more
work than the same value anywhere else on this site. Everywhere else in this
corpus `divine-sanction` means a ruler claims to govern with a god's approval.
Here it means the ruler is one.

## Why

The honest answer is that nobody knows, and the good arguments run in two
directions. One is that the deification is a response to a governing problem:
this state was assembling a bureaucracy across provinces that had been
independent city-states with their own gods and their own loyalties, and a
divine king is a focus of allegiance that outranks a local temple. The other is
that it is a continuation of much older Sumerian ideas about the king's ritual
marriage to the goddess Inanna, formalised.

What can be observed is the timing. The deification arrives with the
administrative reorganisation, not before it and not after, and the hymns that
celebrate the king as a god are also the texts in which the scribal schools that
the reorganisation created taught boys to write.

## The wall

Two reigns later, Shu-Sin built a wall. It is called *muriq-Tidnim*, "the one
that keeps Tidnum at a distance", it ran for something like two hundred and
seventy kilometres between the Tigris and the Euphrates, and it was directed
against the Amorites — pastoralists moving into Mesopotamia from the west, whom
the Ur III texts describe in a set formula as people who do not know grain, who
do not bury their dead, who eat raw meat.

It is not a turning point on this record and it belongs in a chapter, because
nothing about the state's trajectory turned on the year it was finished. It is
here for what it says about the state's own reading of its position. A hundred
years after Ur-Nammu, the most centrally administered polity in the world was
building a fortified line against people who had no state at all, and within
thirty years an Amorite would be a king in Mesopotamia, and within three hundred
the dynasty this region's Babylon record begins with would be Amorite too.

The wall did not work. Neither does the explanation that the Amorites brought
this dynasty down: the last chapter is about what the tablets actually show,
which is more complicated and considerably worse.
