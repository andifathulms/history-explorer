An Elamite coalition took Ur, sacked it, and carried Ibbi-Sin away east to
Anshan, where he disappears. A garrison was left in the ruins and stayed for
several years until Ishbi-Erra of Isin drove it out — which allowed the kings
of Isin to present themselves as the restorers of Ur.

It is worth being exact about who took the city, because "the Elamites" covers
three different things. The Elam of the fourteenth and twelfth centuries and
the Elam of the eighth are later kingdoms with their own histories. The
coalition that took Ur in 2004 belongs to the Elam of Awan and Shimashki, six
hundred years earlier, a federation with its own dynasties and a succession
that passed from brother to brother and then to a sister's son, which is a rule
almost nothing else in the ancient Near East used.

And they did not stay. Nobody ruled Babylonia from Elam after 2004. The
conquerors took the king, took the statue of the moon god, sacked the city and
went home.

## The lament

What survives from the moment is not a chronicle. It is a poem — the Lament for
Ur, and there are several others for Sumer, for Nippur, for Uruk, for Eridu —
in which the goddess Ningal pleads with the assembly of the gods for her city
and is refused, and the city is destroyed because the gods had decided it would
be.

> The people, like broken shards, littered the streets.

The laments were composed, or given their surviving form, in the scribal
schools of the kingdom of Isin, by the successors of the men this dynasty had
trained. They were copied for centuries afterwards as school texts and as
liturgy, which is how they survive. What that authorship actually meant for
the poem, and for how much its account of the fall can be trusted, is worth
taking slowly on its own.

Put that beside the archives. The administrative record of receipts,
deliveries, rations and prices is contemporary, disinterested, and stops
abruptly. The literary record is retrospective, theological, and never stops.
They answer different questions and cannot check each other.

## What the dynasty left

The Sumerian language stopped being a spoken language of government within a
few generations, and after this every regime in Mesopotamia is
Akkadian-speaking and most of them are Amorite. This is the last time the
political centre of the Near East is in the far south.

What did not stop was the writing. Ur-Nammu's law collection — the earliest
surviving one in the world, two centuries before Hammurabi's, with the same
structure of prologue, conditional provisions and epilogue — was copied in
schools long after the dynasty; Roth prints it alongside Hammurabi's and the
Hittite laws in a single volume, which is a fair picture of how this region's
legal writing actually travelled. The hymns and the laments and the king list
went the same way.

And nobody wrote down how the throne passed. Five kings of one family, a
hundred and eight years, not one disputed accession, and nothing in the whole
enormous archive states the rule; Old Babylon is the same across eleven
generations. Mesopotamia legislated the price of hiring an ox, the penalty for a
badly built house and the compensation for a slapped face. It did not legislate
this, or did not write it anywhere that has since been found.
