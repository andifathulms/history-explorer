Ibbi-Sin's reign is twenty-four years long and the archives fail during it, which
means the collapse of the best-documented state in the third millennium is
documented in the act of failing.

The provincial deliveries stop, one province at a time, and the sequence can be
followed. Eshnunna's last dated tablet is in Ibbi-Sin's second year; Susa's in
his third; Umma's in his fifth; Girsu's in his sixth or so; and by his eighth
the *bala* system has effectively ceased and the year-names have stopped
mentioning anything but Ur itself. And in his sixth year the price of grain at
Ur, which the ration records let us track, goes up by something like a factor of
sixty.

## What caused it

This is genuinely argued about and this page will not settle it. The candidates:
Amorite movement cutting the roads; a failure of the irrigation system in the
south, possibly from salinisation, possibly from a shift in the Euphrates
channel; a climatic downturn; and the possibility that the *bala* itself was the
fragility — a rotation in which every province depended on every other one is
efficient until one link fails, at which point the failure propagates.

What the tablets show without ambiguity is the shape rather than the cause: a
central redistribution failing outward from the periphery, and a capital paying
sixty times the normal price for barley while its own accounting system is still
issuing correctly formatted receipts.

## Ishbi-Erra

The one narrative source for this is a set of letters between Ibbi-Sin and his
officials, and it is the most vivid material on this record and the least
trustworthy. Michalowski's edition is the reason both halves of that sentence can
be said.

The story they tell: Ibbi-Sin sent an officer named Ishbi-Erra north with a large
quantity of silver to buy grain. Ishbi-Erra bought it — twenty *gur* of barley,
the letter says — and then wrote to say that Amorites had entered the country, he
could not get the grain through, and would the king make him governor of Isin and
Nippur so that he could hold the region. The king's reply is furious. Within a
few years Ishbi-Erra was a king at Isin, holding the north, and Ibbi-Sin held Ur.

Michalowski's warning, which this record carries as `contested: true` on the
turning point, is that these letters survive only as school copies made
centuries later, in multiple recensions that disagree with each other. They are
literary compositions about the fall of Ur before they are correspondence, and
the events in them may have been shaped by scribes who already knew how the story
ended. Something in the outline is almost certainly true — Ishbi-Erra was real,
he did take Isin, the dates fit — and no individual sentence in the letters can
be relied on.

Which is a familiar condition on this site's older pages and an unfamiliar one
here. Every other claim on this record comes out of an administrative document
that had no motive to lie. This chapter's central episode comes out of a school
text, and the difference is worth feeling.
