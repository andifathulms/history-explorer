The Akkadian record on this site ends around 2154 BC, and what came next is
conventionally a dark century: the Gutians, a people out of the Zagros, held
some part of Babylonia, and the later Sumerian tradition treated them as a
catastrophe visited on the land for Naram-Sin's impiety. How much of that is
true and how much is a literary construction written three hundred years
afterwards is very much open.

What is not open is what happened at the end of it. A governor of Uruk named
Utu-hegal defeated the Gutian king; a governor of Ur named Ur-Nammu, who may
have been his brother, then made himself king of Ur, and within a few years held
southern Mesopotamia from the Gulf to the latitude of modern Baghdad. The line
he founded lasted a hundred and eight years and five kings of one family, with
one throne passing to a brother rather than a son and not one disputed accession
on record.

## What they claimed to be

Ur-Nammu did not present himself as a new thing. He took Akkadian titles — king
of Sumer and Akkad, and later king of the four quarters, which was Naram-Sin's —
and he kept Akkadian in the administration alongside Sumerian. That is the
oldest edge in this corpus: `akkadian -> ur-iii`, type `claimed legitimacy of`,
dated 2112 BC.

It is worth being precise about what the edge asserts, because it does not assert
a succession. A century separates the two records, the Gutians are in between,
and nothing passed from Akkad to Ur in the ordinary sense. What passed was a
vocabulary of kingship. Coding rule 1 counts exactly that — titulature publicly
asserted, whether or not anybody was in a position to object — and it counts it
on Akkad's record rather than on this one, which is why the Akkadian page's
`successor_claims` went from `null` to 1 when this record was written.

## Sumerian, one last time

The dynasty is called a Sumerian revival, and the label needs handling. Sumerian
was the language of the royal inscriptions, the hymns, the law collection and the
overwhelming bulk of the administrative record. It was almost certainly no longer
the language most people in southern Mesopotamia spoke; Akkadian had been
spreading for centuries and would be the only one left within a few generations
of the dynasty's fall.

So this state wrote in Sumerian on a scale nobody had before and nobody would
again, and one of the things it was doing was preserving. The scribal schools it
founded are where the Sumerian literary corpus was fixed — the hymns, the
laments, the debate poems, the king list — and everything later Mesopotamia knew
about Sumer, it knew because this dynasty's schools copied it. The last chapter
of this record is about a lament for the fall of Ur, composed by scribes trained
in the institutions that fell.
