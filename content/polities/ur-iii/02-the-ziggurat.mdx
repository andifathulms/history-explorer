The founder of the dynasty is best known through masonry. Ur-Nammu — read
Ur-Engur by the excavators of King's generation — left his name stamped into
kiln-baked bricks across southern Mesopotamia, and the largest thing he left is
still standing.

## The tower

The great temple-tower at Ur is his.

<Figure id="ziggurat-ur" /> He faced it with baked brick carrying his
own name and inscription, and it is the form of ziggurat that everything later
copies.

But the excavation showed something more interesting than a new building. Inside
the structure were the cores of earlier and smaller towers — one of them raised
by Naram-Sin, and others from before the Akkadians altogether. Ur-Nammu did not
invent the ziggurat. He built a larger one around the ones already there, which
is a different act and a more revealing one.

He reworked the whole precinct to match. He raised the level of the inner court
above Naram-Sin's pavement. He straightened the line of the outer wall, using
Naram-Sin's wall as a foundation wherever the two crossed, and extended the
circuit to take in the mounds where much of the temple archive was later found.

## What building on top of somebody meant

A ruler who encloses his predecessor's monument inside his own is making a claim
that no inscription has to state.

The Akkadian empire had ended less than a lifetime before, and the Gutian
interval between was the thing the Sumerian tradition would spend centuries
explaining as divine punishment. Ur-Nammu's programme answers it physically. The
old pavement is not destroyed, it is buried under a higher one. Naram-Sin's wall
is not demolished, it is used as footing. The Akkadian tower is not replaced, it
is inside the new one.

The message is continuity rather than conquest: this is the same holy place, run
properly again, by someone who belongs in the sequence.

## The long view from the same trench

The site keeps a record of everyone who did this after him.

Above Ur-Nammu's level, Kassite-period buildings across the water held the temple
archives in their turn. Samsu-iluna — Hammurabi's son, four centuries later —
left a clay cone in the debris of the temple court recording that he had dammed
the Euphrates and cut it a new channel, probably to bring water to the part of
the city that had grown up on the far side.

Ur-Nammu's ziggurat outlasted his dynasty by about sixteen centuries as a working
building, and it is the reason a state that governed for barely a hundred years
is visible on the ground at all. The hundred thousand tablets tell you how it
was run. The tower is what it was for.
