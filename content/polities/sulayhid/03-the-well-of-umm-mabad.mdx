At the top of his career Ali al-Sulayhi sat still. He had taken the highlands
out of the Haraz mountains, taken Zabid off the Najahids on the coast, and taken
the amirate of Mecca; and then he returned to Sana'a and remained there twelve
years without moving from the city.

The Yemeni kings he had beaten lived at Sana'a too, in houses he had given them.
That is the arrangement at its height: a country of fortress-holding lords,
brought down off their mountains and settled where the conqueror could see them.

## What he had been told about himself

The strangest thing about him is documented in an account of his childhood.

His father was a Sunni judge in Haraz with authority over forty thousand men.
The Isma'ili chief missionary of the district set out to cultivate him — riding
regularly to his house, persevering for years — but the son was the target. He
worked on the boy before he had reached puberty, without the father's knowledge,
and told him that he was described in one of the treasured books of the imams,
the *Book of Delineations*, which set out the greatness reserved for him and the
career he was to fulfil. When the missionary died he left the boy his writings
and his learning.

So the founder of the Isma'ili state in Yemen was recruited as a child, by a
man who showed him a book about his own future, in a house belonging to a Sunni
judge. He grew up learned in law and in the interpretation of the Qur'an, led
the pilgrimage caravan for several years, and rose, in his biographer's phrase,
from lowliness to exaltation and from poverty to wealth.

There is a story from the poor end of that. Travelling in the Tihama as a young
man, unable to raise the extravagant bride-price demanded for his cousin Asma —
her mother wanting her married to one of the kings of Sana'a — he spent a night
in the mosque of an Abyssinian at Zabid named Faraj, a man who made a practice
of asking after strangers himself rather than through his servants. Faraj found
him reading the Qur'an, asked whether he had eaten, and got a couplet of
al-Mutanabbi for an answer. He took him home, heard the story, and gave him
twice what the marriage required.

Asma bore him the son who succeeded him. She was famous in Yemen for her
learning and her spending, and one of the court poets wrote that when people
magnify the throne of Bilkis, Asma has put out the loftiest of the stars.

## The road to Mecca

In the year he decided to go on pilgrimage again he took the whole apparatus
with him: Asma, the settled kings, and a hundred and seventy princes of his own
family — travelling with him, according to the account, precisely because he did
not trust them to stay behind.

Najah's sons had been driven off the mainland to an island in the Red Sea years
earlier. Two of them came back, raised seventy men with no horses and no weapons
but nails on palm-sticks, and came down the coast road after him.

When the raiders were recognised and his brother shouted at him to mount, Ali
answered that he was fated to die nowhere except at ad-Duhaym and the well of
Umm Ma'bad — meaning the famous well far to the north, where the Prophet had
halted on the flight to Medina. He had evidently carried the prediction a long
time and found it reassuring, since neither place was anywhere near him.

Then one of his own men said: defend yourself, for that is the well of Duhaym
ibn 'Abs, and that mosque stands where the tent of Umm Ma'bad stood.

He never moved from the spot again. They killed him where he stood, with his own
sword.

The man who had been told as a child that a book foretold his life died because
he believed a prediction about the place of his death, in the one place in
Arabia where it could be made true by a coincidence of names.
