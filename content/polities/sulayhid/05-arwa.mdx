Arwa al-Sulayhi took the government of Yemen in 1086, handed to her by her
husband al-Mukarram, and held it until she died in 1138 at about ninety.
Fifty-two years is longer than almost any reign of the medieval Islamic world, and
she spent them governing a state that was smaller every decade and outlasting
everyone who expected to take it.

Her first substantial act was to move. Sana'a was the capital her father-in-law
had conquered from and was indefensible: exposed on the plateau, contested by
the Zaydi imams to the north and reachable from the Tihama, where the Najahids
who had killed Ali al-Sulayhi were rebuilding. In 1087 she moved the seat to
Jibla, a small town in the southern highlands on a spur between two valleys,
and built it up — a palace, a congregational mosque she endowed and is buried
in, roads and cisterns. The state stopped trying to hold all of Yemen and held
what it could.

<Figure id="arwa-mosque" />

That is the decision the record marks as a turning point, and it is the reason
the polity outlived its founder by seventy years. A conqueror's capital is
chosen for reach; hers was chosen for survival, and the difference bought five
decades.

<Figure id="jibla-town" />

The rest of the reign was management. She governed through appointed
lieutenants, most consequentially the Zurayids at Aden, who paid her a fixed
tribute for the port and its customs and eventually became a dynasty of their
own. She kept the Najahids in the Tihama at a distance, sometimes by war and at
least once by arranging the assassination of their ruler. She corresponded
directly with Cairo, and her correspondence with the Fatimid caliphs — some of
which survives in the Isma'ili collections — is addressed to her as a religious
superior addresses a subordinate of rank rather than as a court addresses a
provincial governor's widow.

She had no surviving heir with a claim anyone would follow. The Sulayhid state
fragmented at her death, and what she had built inside it did not.
