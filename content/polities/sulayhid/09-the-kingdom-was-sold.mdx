title: The kingdom was sold

Arwa died at Jibla in 1138, and left the kingdom of the Sulayhids by bequest to
Mansur ibn al-Mufaddal, a lord of Himyarite descent whose family had spent two
generations acquiring castles in the southern mountains.

Fifteen years later he sold it.

The fortress of Ta'kar, which commanded al-Janad; the fortress of Habb; the
strongholds of Mikhlaf Ja'far with their attached districts — the whole southern
core of what had been an Isma'ili state stretching from Aden to Mecca went to
the prince of Aden for money, in the year 1152. The chronicler who records it
does the arithmetic in the same breath: reckoning from 1038, the Sulayhid
sovereignty had lasted a hundred and nineteen years.

Mansur lived at the fortress of Ta'izz until he died. He is remembered for one
other thing: he was the first to take up Tha'bat as a place to relax, and used
to go down and spend several days there.

## Where the rest of it went

What he had not sold was taken.

The surviving Sulayhid family strongholds, each with a wide territory and large
revenues attached, were among the properties that ended up in the treasury of
Ali ibn Mahdi when he came down off the mountain in the 1150s and destroyed the
coastal dynasty. A man of Jibla told Umara that the wealth of twenty-five Yemeni
dynasties finished in that one hoard.

Then in 1173 an Ayyubid army out of Egypt took the whole country in a single
campaign — the coast off Ibn Mahdi's son, the mountains off whoever was holding
them — and the hundred and fifty years in which no power in Yemen could finish
any other came to an end because a fourth party arrived with more men than any
of them.

## What did not get sold

Two things survived the auction.

The first is the religious organisation. The decision Arwa made in 1130 —
recognising an infant imam and declaring him hidden — detached the Yemeni
mission from Cairo and put it under a line of chief missionaries she appointed
herself. That structure outlived the state by centuries and moved east, and the
community it produced is still there.

The second is her reputation in her own country, and it is worth being precise
about its shape. The Yemeni sources are not admiring in a general way; they are
specific. She was tall and fair and inclined to stoutness, with a clear voice;
she was well read and a skilful writer, her memory stored with history, poetry
and the chronology of past times, and nothing could surpass the interlinear
notes on grammar and meaning that she wrote in the margins of books. Her
father-in-law used to tell his wife to show the girl respect, because she would
be the preserver of the family and the guardian of its crown for whoever of the
dynasty was left.

He was right in a way he cannot have intended. She outlived him by seventy-one
years, held what he lost, chose the succession that mattered, and was the last
of them. The crown she guarded was sold by the man she left it to, and the thing
she actually preserved was not the kingdom at all.
