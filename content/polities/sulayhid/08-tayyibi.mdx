---
title: What she detached from Cairo
drafted_from: daftary-2007

The Fatimid caliphate produced two succession disputes in thirty-six years and
the Yemeni mission had to answer both.

The first was 1094. Al-Mustansir died, a vizier put the younger son al-Musta'li
on the throne over the designated elder son Nizar, and the mission split. The
eastern network went to Alamut under Hasan-i Sabbah and became a state of its own
in the Persian mountains. Yemen recognised al-Musta'li.

The second was 1130, and it was worse. The caliph al-Amir was assassinated,
leaving — by the account the Yemenis accepted — an infant son called al-Tayyib.
A cousin took the throne in Cairo instead and was recognised there. Arwa, as
*hujja*, declined to follow Cairo. She recognised al-Tayyib as imam, declared
that he had gone into concealment, and reconstituted the Yemeni mission under a
*da'i al-mutlaq* — an absolute missionary — holding authority on the hidden
imam's behalf and appointing his own successor.

That is the decisive act of the reign, and it is a religious one, taken by a
woman who was also the head of state, without reference to the caliphate that
had made her dynasty.

The consequences are radically out of proportion to the polity. The Sulayhid
state came apart at Arwa's death in 1138, ninety-one years after it began, with
no successor and no claimant. Nobody conquered it; it simply stopped being
governed as one thing. The Tayyibi da'wa she had constituted
did not fragment. It continued in Yemen for four centuries, sent missionaries to
Gujarat, and when conditions in Yemen deteriorated the seat of the mission moved
to India, where the Bohra communities that descend from it still are, still
under a da'i al-mutlaq in a line that runs back to the one she appointed.

A state of ninety-one years, in a corner of Yemen, whose ruler's decision about
a disputed infant in Cairo is still the constitutional basis of a religious
community on another continent nine hundred years later. Nothing else the
Sulayhids did comes close, and it was not a political act at all.
