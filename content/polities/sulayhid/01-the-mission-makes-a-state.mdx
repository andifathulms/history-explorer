The Fatimid caliphs of Cairo ran a missionary hierarchy — the *da'wa* —
that operated overwhelmingly outside their own territory, in the business of
persuading a rival caliph's subjects that their caliph was a usurper. Most of
what it produced was congregations. In Yemen it produced a state.

<Figure id="haraz" />

Ali al-Sulayhi was the son of a Sunni judge in the Haraz mountains who
converted after a long association with a Fatimid missionary, and in 1047 he
rose in the mountains with a following. Within fifteen years he held most of
Yemen, and for a period he controlled the appointment of the amirs of Mecca,
which meant a Fatimid client was administering the holy cities on behalf of a
caliph in Cairo whose name was preached there. That is the high-water mark of
the Fatimid claim, and it was reached through a mission rather than an army.

The Sulayhids ruled as Fatimid vassals and did not pretend otherwise, and the
relationship was unlike the ordinary one between a dynasty and a caliph. Most
provincial houses of the age held a diploma from a caliph they acknowledged in
the Friday prayer and otherwise ignored. The Sulayhids held theirs from a caliph
whose missionaries had made them, who remained their religious superior, and
whose orders on matters that had nothing to do with government were expected to
be obeyed.

Ali was ambushed and killed on the road to Mecca in the 1060s and his wife was
taken captive; his son al-Mukarram recovered the position and rescued her, and
then, in 1086, handed the government to his own wife. What follows from that is
most of the reason anybody remembers the dynasty.
