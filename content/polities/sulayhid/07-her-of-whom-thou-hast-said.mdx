title: Her of whom thou hast said

Cairo eventually decided that the queen of Yemen was too old to be left in charge
of the Yemeni mission, and sent a man to take it off her. The account of what
happened next is Umara's, and it is the best evidence there is for how Arwa
actually governed.

The man was Ibn Najib al-Dawla, sent as *da'i* with full powers. He began well
enough to be worth describing well: he respected the property of the inhabitants,
dealt righteously with them and maintained the laws, and Umara says the queen's
reputation was enhanced through him and the neighbouring Yemeni powers were
constrained to stop coveting her outlying provinces. He took three hundred
horsemen of the Banu Himas and Sinhan into his service under a Hamdanite
commander, and when the Fatimid vizier who had appointed him died in 1121 his
successor confirmed him, sent letters delegating to him the fullest powers over
the people, and reinforced him with four hundred Armenians and seven hundred
black archers. He set up his residence at Janad, in the centre of the country.

The kings of Yemen endured this impatiently, and the record of the next four
years is a man with Egyptian troops losing fights to local ones.

In 1124 he attacked Zabid, held by the Najahid vizier. Ten of his own archers had
come to an understanding with the townspeople; as the armies engaged one of them
put an arrow into the muzzle of his horse. He came off it, the army broke, every
one of his black troops was killed and fifty of the four hundred Armenians got
away. Fifteen Hamdanite horsemen fought over his body until one of them got him
up behind his own saddle. His horse disappeared from the battle at midday prayer
on the Friday; he was in Janad — four days' journey, three at a forced pace —
early on the Saturday morning. By that evening Jibla had heard he was dead.

The next campaign went the same way. He made war on the Zurayids, took a lance
thrust, and — being, Umara notes in passing, a bad horseman — fell off again, and
was saved by the same Hamdanite chief, who killed the slave coming for him. He
left his breastplate on the ground, and the Zurayid prince wrote a poem about it:
*he fled forgetting his breast-plate, in terror of the Banu Yam, lest their
lances should pierce him; but flight saveth not from death.*

In 1125 he moved against the queen. Her mind, he said, had become weakened, and
in his opinion it was necessary to place her in seclusion.

What happened then is the whole of Arwa's career in one manoeuvre. The six Yemeni
kings — the same men who had spent four years fighting each other and him — came
to her, and asked her permission to besiege him at Janad. She gave it. They
brought three thousand horse and three thousand foot and surrounded the walls,
inside which he had four hundred picked Hamdanite horsemen and a handful of
individual fighters Umara says were each worth a hundred men, and the siege
pressed him hard.

Then she wrote, as was her custom, to her own tribal ally, who came and camped at
Jibla. She sent to the tribal chiefs and distributed ten thousand Egyptian dinars
among them. And she instructed the men carrying the money to put a rumour about
among the ordinary soldiers of the besieging army: that Ibn Najib al-Dawla had
distributed ten thousand Egyptian dinars among their own commanders.

The soldiers demanded their share of the Egyptian gold or they would go home. The
kings made promises. When night fell the kings left, each for his own country,
and in the morning the army found itself without leaders, struck camp and
abandoned the siege.

Umara gives the line somebody said to Ibn Najib al-Dawla afterwards, and it needs
no help: *perceivest thou the artifice accomplished by her of whom thou hast said
that her mind is weakened?*

He rode to Jibla and tried to justify and excuse himself to her. He was arrested
not long afterwards and sent back towards Egypt, and did not arrive.

The queen was by then somewhere past seventy and had been governing Yemen for
about forty years, and the incident is the clearest statement of what her
authority rested on. It was not Egyptian troops, because the Egyptian troops had
just been destroyed at Zabid. It was that she could buy six rival kings for ten
thousand dinars and then buy their soldiers away from them for nothing, using the
same money twice.
