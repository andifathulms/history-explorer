---
title: Two women, and what the ruler fields cannot show
drafted_from: daftary-2007
phase: aside
---

Across every dynasty this kind of survey ordinarily asks about a founder, a
ruler at the height of the state, and a last ruler, the answer has almost
always been a man. That is worth examining rather than noting.

Some of it is the period and some of it is the sources, which record women as
mothers, wives and regents where they record them at all, and which — being
mostly written by men attached to courts — treat a woman exercising authority as
an irregularity to be explained. And some of it is the question itself: asking
who formally held the sovereign office is a question about a title, and
in most of the medieval Islamic world women exercised power without holding one.
Shajar al-Durr does not answer to the title of Mamluk sultan in the ordinary
telling of that dynasty, though she was
proclaimed sultan and ruled for eighty days. Gawhar Shad ran the Timurid state
for a decade without ever being named its ruler. Zenobia of Palmyra is
remembered mainly as a regent who never claimed the throne in her own name.

Yemen has two women and neither needs that kind of accommodation.

Asma bint Shihab, Ali al-Sulayhi's wife, is recorded as having sat in the
council unveiled, as having had her name read in the khutba alongside her
husband's and her son's, and as having governed while her son was on campaign.
Having a woman's name in the Friday sermon is not a courtesy; it is the
formal announcement of who is sovereign, and it was done.

Her daughter-in-law Arwa is the one who actually held the title outright. She governed for about
fifty years in her own name, moved the capital, conducted the diplomacy, ran the
wars, and her name was in the khutba too. And she held religious office — the
Fatimids recognised her as *hujja*, the senior rank of the mission in Yemen,
which is a position of doctrinal authority over a network of missionaries, and
which no other woman held anywhere in the Isma'ili hierarchy.

Political authority delegated to a woman is unusual anywhere in this period. Religious
authority is close to unique, and it is what mattered when Cairo produced a
disputed succession: a *hujja* was competent to decide what the Yemeni mission
would do about it, and she did.
