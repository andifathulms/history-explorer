The Sulayhid state was broken in a single morning on the coastal plain, and the
fullest account of it was written by the man who did the breaking.

Ali al-Sulayhi had spent twelve years at Sana'a without moving from the city.
Then he decided to go to Mecca, and took with him his wife Asma and the Yemeni
kings he had defeated and settled at Sana'a — a hundred and seventy Sulayhid
princes travelled in the party as well, all of them, according to the account,
men he did not trust to leave behind him.

Najah's sons came out of hiding at Zabid and caught the column at a place called
ad-Duhaym, by the well of Umm Ma'bad.

## What it cost

Ali was killed where he stood, beheaded with his own sword. His brother Abd
Allah, whom the account calls the bravest of Arab horsemen, charged the raiders
and was speared. Both heads were mounted and carried to the mosque where Asma
was.

Then Jayyash ibn Najah advised his brother to let the rest of the party live —
Asma, the hundred and seventy princes, thirty-five Qahtani chiefs — and to send
Asma home to her son with a message that the feud was settled. He argued it on
the merits: do that, he said, and the tribes will not contest your rule over the
Tihama; refuse, and their sense of wounded honour will bring them down on you.

Sa'id al-Ahwal refused, and the Sulayhids were killed to the last man. Jayyash
saw an old man among them try to shelter behind his son and one spear go through
both.

Asma was taken to Zabid and lodged in a house, and the heads of her husband and
her husband's brother were set up facing her window.

## What was left of the state

The effect on the highlands was immediate and it is the reason this counts as
more than a defeat.

The governors Ali had appointed to the mountain fortresses simply kept them. The
authority of his son al-Mukarram, Umara writes, was all but destroyed. On the
plain the opposite happened: Sa'id's position became so strong that he sent
buyers to Abyssinia for twenty thousand spearmen.

Al-Mukarram had no news of his mother at all. He eventually raised what he could,
came down and stormed Zabid and got her out — and then had to leave, and the
Najahids came straight back and expelled the governors he had installed.

That is the shape of the rest of Sulayhid history in one sequence. The state
could still mount a campaign and win it. It could not hold what it took. And
Sa'id al-Ahwal, who had made it so, was killed at the castle of Sha'ir some
years afterwards by a stratagem contrived not by al-Mukarram but by his wife.
