William the Conqueror's own sons could not hold what he had won
together. Normandy passed to his eldest son, Robert Curthose, while
England went to a younger son, and the two brothers spent the
following years at war with each other until the youngest of the
three, Henry, held both England and, after 1106, wanted Normandy as
well. Before the armies met at the castle of Tinchebray, Henry
offered his brother easy terms - half the duchy's revenue for life,
in exchange for giving up its government entirely - and Robert,
"foolish to the last," refused.

## A battle fought on foot

The armies that met at Tinchebray were unevenly matched in kind: Duke
Robert stronger in footsoldiers, King Henry stronger in knights, with
the flower of the Norman nobility fighting on his side against their
own duke. Both leaders fought on foot in the English style. When
Robert's first charge drove back Henry's front line without breaking
it, Henry's only mounted reserve, Breton and Manceaux horsemen under
Count Elias, swept in on the flank and cut down two hundred men in a
single charge; Robert of Belleme fled the field with his own
followers the moment he saw the battle turning, and the duke's army
dissolved with him. Henry himself described the victory plainly: the
battle had delivered into his hands "the duke of Normandy, the count
of Mortain... some four hundred knights, ten thousand foot - and the
duchy of Normandy." The date was Michaelmas Eve, 1106 - forty years
to the day since William the Conqueror had landed at Pevensey to
begin the conquest of England. Robert Curthose never regained his
freedom; he remained Henry's prisoner until his own death, nearly
three decades later.

## Geoffrey of Anjou completes a second conquest

Henry I died in 1135 with no surviving legitimate son, and the
succession he had extracted a triple oath from his barons to honor -
his daughter Matilda, married to Geoffrey of Anjou - was disputed
almost as soon as he was in his grave. Geoffrey spent much of the
following decade winning Normandy by force from supporters of the
rival claimant, Stephen, town by town and castle by castle, until in
January 1144 he crossed the Seine and camped before Rouen itself. The
citizens opened their gates to him the next day; only the castle held
out, for three months, before hunger forced its surrender. One last
holdout, the fortress of Arques, fell the following summer after its
Flemish commander was killed by a stray arrow. Geoffrey held the
duchy afterward by his own right of conquest, formally recognized by
the king of France - not in his wife's name, and not yet in his
son's, though it was that son, Henry, who would carry Normandy into
a still wider Angevin realm stretching from the Scottish border to
the Pyrenees.
