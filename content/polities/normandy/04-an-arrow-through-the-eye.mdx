By the afternoon of the 14th of October, 1066, Duke William's army
had spent hours failing to break the English shield-wall on the
ridge above Senlac, and the fight showed no sign of ending before
dark. William's answer was a new tactic: archers ordered to shoot
upward, so their arrows would drop like bolts from the sky onto the
men still packed around King Harold's standard, while twenty
handpicked knights swore an oath to reach the standard itself and
bring it down.

The volley found its mark in the worst possible way. One arrow struck
King Harold through the right eye. He fell beside his own standard,
and although most of the twenty knights sent to finish the work died
in the attempt, four of them reached the wounded king and killed him
where he lay, carrying off both his personal banners. Around him his
household troops fought on without him rather than flee or surrender
- by the record's own account, not one of the king's chosen men
either ran or was taken prisoner - until the last of them fell at
their posts as darkness closed over the field.

## Not yet a king

William did not call himself king of England that evening, or for
more than two months afterward. He held only a corner of Sussex when
the battle ended, and it took him years to bring the rest of the
country under his own control. But Hastings had already decided the
question that mattered most: no English army would ever again meet
him in a pitched battle for the crown. When William offered Harold's
own mother the weight of her son's body in gold for the right to bury
him with proper rites at Waltham, he refused outright, judging Harold
a perjured man unworthy of Christian burial, and had him interred
without ceremony on the coastal rocks near the battlefield instead -
a small, deliberate cruelty from a duke who, within three years, held
the whole kingdom that single afternoon at Senlac had made his to
take.
