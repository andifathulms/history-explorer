Rollo's grant at Saint-Clair-sur-Epte was only a beginning. He
enlarged it himself with the addition of Bayeux and its territory,
and his son William Longsword, who ruled from 927 to 943, added the
Cotentin peninsula and the land of Avranches to its south - the
addition that finally gave the duchy the whole north coast of what
had been the duchy of France, cutting Paris off from the sea by way
of Rouen. Rollo ruled the land he had won firmly enough that the
story was told of a jewel he hung up in a tree, which no man in his
territory dared to steal.

## A pagan revolt and a captive duke

Under Rollo and William Longsword the Normans settled around Rouen
gradually became Christian and left off their own Scandinavian
tongue for French; by the end of William's reign, French alone was
spoken there. But in the more recently won western lands, Danish
speech and the old gods still held on, and when William was
murdered, the heathen party got hold of his young son and successor
Richard and forced him to join their own worship for a time. Duke
Richard - later called Richard the Fearless - outgrew that crisis
and went on to commend himself to Hugh the Great, duke of the
French, binding Normandy and France into a close alliance; it was
Richard who had a chief hand in giving the Frankish crown itself to
Hugh's son, Hugh Capet.

## Gentlemen on horseback

Richard the Fearless was followed by Richard the Good, whose reign
from 996 to 1026 saw a great revolt of the Norman peasantry, likely
drawn largely from the duchy's Celtic population, against a landed
class that was by then entirely Norman. The revolt was put down, and
Richard began drawing a new distinction among his own subjects,
favoring the men called gentlemen - the word appears for almost the
first time here. By now the Normans had transformed themselves
completely from the seafaring raiders of Rollo's day into the best
horsemen in the world, mounted men with kite-shaped shields and long
lances who went out, in the same restless spirit as their Viking
forebears, to seek fighting wherever it could be found - helping the
Christians of Spain against the Moors, and, in the reign of Richard
the Good, sending the private adventurers whose descendants would
carve out whole principalities of their own in southern Italy and
Sicily.
