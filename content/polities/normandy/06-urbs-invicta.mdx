By the end of 1203, King John had already left the duchy his ancestor
had won England from, and Philip Augustus had spent months driving a
wedge between its eastern and western halves. The garrison at
Vaudreuil, well provisioned and apparently prepared for a determined
defense, surrendered without a stone being cast against it, a
collapse so sudden that contemporaries blamed treachery among the
castellans rather than any failure of the walls themselves.

What Vaudreuil gave up without a fight, Chateau Gaillard did not.
Built by King Richard as the key to the whole Seine valley, the
fortress held out under siege for six months, from the autumn of
1203 until Roger de Lacy, its constable, finally surrendered on the
6th of March, 1204. The length and difficulty of the siege - the only
substantial resistance Philip's campaign met anywhere in the duchy -
gave it a place in the chronicles that the rest of the war never
matched.

## The unconquered city falls

Rouen was the duchy's own seat, and its own defenders knew what
Chateau Gaillard's fall meant for them. On the 1st of June its
barons and citizens agreed an armistice with Philip: they would
surrender within thirty days unless help arrived from England, terms
that amounted, in substance, to a treaty of surrender already
signed. Help did not come, and the city did not wait out the full
month. On the 24th of June, the feast of St John the Baptist, Rouen
- urbs invicta, the unconquered city - capitulated, along with
Verneuil and Arques on the same day. Resistance may have continued in
scattered places afterward, but no record of it survived; Philip's
own officials moved quickly to fold the duchy into the French royal
administration, and the state Rollo had extracted from a French king
at the point of an insult very nearly three centuries earlier ceased,
in that single summer, to answer to anyone but the crown it had once
refused to kneel to.
