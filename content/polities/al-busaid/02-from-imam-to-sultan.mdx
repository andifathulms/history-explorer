---
title: An elective office becoming a family's
drafted_from: risso-1986
phase: aside
---

Ahmad bin Sa'id was elected imam. His son Sa'id bin Ahmad was elected imam after
him. His grandson was not: by the 1790s the rulers of Oman had stopped seeking
election, dropped the title of imam, and called themselves sayyid and then
sultan — a word with no Ibadi content at all — and the office passed from father
to son.

That is the same thing that happened to the Zaydi imamate of Yemen after 1636,
on a page two entries away in this region, and the parallel is close enough to
be worth stating as a finding rather than a coincidence.

Both offices were doctrinally elective and explicitly not hereditary. Both were
held by one family for long enough that the family became the obvious candidate.
Both families then found the doctrine inconvenient, because an office open to
any qualified claimant gives every qualified claimant a standing right to rise
against the incumbent. The Zaydi answer, on the Yemeni page, was to revise the
jurisprudence. The Omani answer was simpler: stop calling it an imamate.

The consequence was the same in both places, and in Oman it is still visible on
a map. The interior did not accept it. The Ibadi scholars of the interior went on
holding that the imamate existed and that Muscat's sultans were not imams, and
they reconstituted the office when they could — in 1868 briefly, in 1913
seriously, with a treaty in 1920 recognising an imam at Nizwa alongside a sultan
at Muscat, and a war in the 1950s that ended it. The last chapter of this record
returns to that.

The site's `succession_rule` field is null here and reads `election` on the two
Omani records before it, and the null is doing work: it is the only way the
schema can indicate that the arrangement changed into something the sources do
not describe as a rule. A field that records the arrangement in force for most
of a polity's life cannot show a polity in the act of abandoning one.

There is a second, smaller limitation on this page and it should be admitted in
the same place. The span ends in 1861. The dynasty did not end in 1861 and has
not ended; the Al Bu Sa'id are the ruling house of Oman today. What the span
measures is the empire — from the election of 1744 to the partition that
separated Zanzibar from Muscat — because the empire is the thing with a
beginning and an end. The corpus has no way to record a polity that is still
running, and every entry in it stops.
