Lobsang Gyatso is the only Dalai Lama besides the seventh whom
Tibetans remember by an honorific rather than simply a number - "The
Great Fifth," a title that has outlasted every criticism contemporary
foreign visitors leveled at him. The Jesuit Johann Grueber, who
reached Lhasa in 1661 with the Belgian Albert d'Orville - the first
Europeans to enter the city - described the ruling Dalai Lama in
openly hostile terms, but Bell's own judgment is blunt about how
little that mattered to the verdict of history: "nothing succeeds
like success." The Fifth Dalai Lama did not personally administer
the government he now headed for long; following established custom,
he withdrew to religious matters and left secular affairs to his
Chief Minister, Sang-gye Gyatso, a man Bell calls one of exceptional
talent.

## A capital rebuilt and centralized

Under that arrangement the government of Tibet was centralized in
Lhasa and made genuinely more orderly - old regional forts, no longer
needed as centers of independent power, were gradually abandoned and
left to fall into ruin. The Great Prayer Festival, still the largest
and most striking event of the Lhasa calendar, was established on its
lasting basis during this reign, trebling or quadrupling the capital's
own population for three weeks each February and March. Sang-gye
Gyatso completed the Potala Palace in its present form a few years
after his own master's death, giving the new Gelug government a
physical monument to match its consolidated authority. Bell's own
assessment of the era is double-edged: the Fifth Dalai Lama and his
minister were men of real exceptional ability, but the growing
influence of Buddhism itself had softened Tibetan martial vigor, so
that from this point onward Tibet's own weight in regional affairs
would be religious rather than military - a shift the early Manchu
emperors of China, already courting the Dalai Lama's support to help
manage their own Mongol subjects, were quick to exploit.

The Fifth Dalai Lama died in 1680. His own Chief Minister concealed
the death for a time, ruling in his name while the Chinese court,
Bell notes, quietly went along with the concealment because it let
them keep citing his authority in their own dealings with the
Mongols - a convenient fiction on both sides that could not, in the
end, be maintained indefinitely.
