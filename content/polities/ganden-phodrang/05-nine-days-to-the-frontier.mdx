The flight from Lhasa in February 1910 was, by any measure, a
remarkable feat for a court accustomed to leisurely travel: the Dalai
Lama, his ministers and roughly two hundred soldiers covered two
hundred and seventy miles in nine days, crossing three high passes
and the Tsangpo river with Chinese troops in close pursuit, before
riding into safety across the Sikkim frontier and on to Darjeeling.
The soldiers left behind at the river crossing to hold off the
pursuit did so at real cost, killing a number of the pursuing
Chinese before scattering across the country themselves. For a
second time in six years, Chinese assertion of authority over Tibet
had driven its own government into exile rather than secured it.

## A revolution nobody in Lhasa had to fight

What finally reversed the occupation was not Tibetan arms but a
collapse in China itself. As the Qing dynasty's own authority
disintegrated in the revolution of 1911, the Chinese garrison
occupying Tibet lost the political backing that had sent it there in
the first place; large numbers of its soldiers were captured and
deported to Sikkim, where Tibetan captors - by British report -
treated their former occupiers with a humanity that startled British
observers. By June 1912 the Chinese held no effective power left in
central Tibet at all, and the Dalai Lama returned from his second
exile to a country his own officials were already reclaiming without
him.

## A garrison sent home rather than destroyed

The remaining Chinese troops garrisoned in Lhasa itself surrendered a
few months after the Dalai Lama's return and were escorted out across
the Indian frontier, given the same considerate treatment as the
soldiers deported earlier - a telling contrast with the violence the
Chinese occupation had itself employed. Ten-gye-ling monastery, which
had collaborated with the Chinese administration and long held the
privileged right to supply a regent during a Dalai Lama's minority,
was subdued, its monks expelled and its estates confiscated by the
Ganden Phodrang's own government. What had begun in 1750 as a
tightening of Qing oversight after the murder of the Ambans ended,
within the same institution's own lifetime, in the complete expulsion
of Chinese officials and troops from central Tibet - not a change of
government, but the same government the Great Fifth's regent had
built two and a half centuries earlier, ruling its own country again
without a foreign garrison inside it for the first time in generations.
