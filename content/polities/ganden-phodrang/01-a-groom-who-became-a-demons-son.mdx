Tibet's own government had passed through two prior eras of
priest-kings before the Dalai Lamas ever took secular power. The
Sakya hierarchs ruled with Mongol backing from about 1270 to 1345,
until King Chang-chub Gyal-tsen broke Sakya power and founded what
Bell calls the Second Monarchy - a dynasty that discarded many of the
Chinese and Mongol innovations the Sakya lamas had introduced, even
as Mongol and Chinese influence kept pressing on Tibet from outside.
The Sitya dynasty that followed ruled for three centuries in relative
peace and prosperity, until 1635, when it was overthrown by the king
of Tsang - a ruler popular tradition, Bell records, credited to
trickery and a demon mother, since he had reportedly risen from a
stable groom to supreme power through sheer cunning.

## Subdued a few years later

The Tsang king's own triumph proved short-lived. Gushi Khan, chief of
the Oelöt Mongols, subdued him only a few years after his victory and
installed the fifth Dalai Lama, Lobsang Gyatso, on the Tibetan throne
in his place - a conquest, not an election, that gave the Gelug
school of Tibetan Buddhism the secular authority it had never held
outright before. The fort the Tsang king had built at Shigatse
survived him: local tradition holds that the fifth Dalai Lama's own
Chief Minister later copied its design for the rebuilt Potala Palace
in Lhasa, so that the very architecture of the new Gelug government
carried a trace of the rival dynasty Gushi Khan's own conquest had
just destroyed.
