In due course a successor, Tsang-yang Gyatso, was installed in the
Pontifical Chair - a young man of real intelligence who devoted
himself to composing verse and enjoying the pleasures of the capital
rather than to religious duty, in a way that left many Tibetans and
Mongols doubting whether he could genuinely be a true incarnation at
all. That doubt gave China its opening. In 1700 Chinese forces
occupied Tachienlu, a strategic post on the road from Lhasa to
Sichuan, and annexed part of eastern Tibet outright; in 1706 the
Chinese and their Mongol allies removed the sixth Dalai Lama and put
him to death, declaring him no true incarnation and installing their
own twenty-five-year-old nominee in the Potala instead. Tibetans
refused to accept the substitute. Reports that Tsang-yang Gyatso had
already reincarnated at Litang spread through Lhasa, and with the
help of a fresh body of Oelöt Mongols who pushed through to the
capital, the Tibetans held their ground against the Chinese-appointed
rival even as the crisis let China tighten its own grip on the
country regardless of who actually sat in the Potala.

## An emperor who changed his mind

The Kangxi Emperor found nearly every Mongol tribe backing the
Tibetans' own choice and regarded his own nominee as widely seen as
an impostor - a dangerous split that raised the specter, in Bell's
own reading, of a combined Mongol-Tibetan force uniting against
China outright. Kangxi's first army, sent in 1718, was defeated by
the Oelöts and Tibetans together. Rather than press a losing hand, he
abandoned his own candidate, adopted the boy the Tibetans themselves
had chosen, and sent a second, larger army to Lhasa in 1720 -
announcing, this time, that it came to enthrone the Tibetans' own
pick rather than to impose a rival. Tibetan resistance evaporated
once the goal aligned with their own choice, and the Chinese army
entered Lhasa unopposed, leaving a garrison of two thousand men
behind and keeping the road to Sichuan open with permanent
detachments - a Chinese military presence in the capital that had not
existed before the crisis over Tsang-yang Gyatso's own death began.

## Massacre and restoration

Chinese oversight deepened further three decades later. In 1750 the
Chinese Residents at Lhasa, known as Ambans, murdered the sitting
Tibetan Regent; the people of Lhasa rose and massacred the Chinese in
response. The Qianlong Emperor dispatched an army that restored
Chinese ascendancy and left the Amban system stronger than before -
a cycle in which every Tibetan attempt to assert independent
authority against Chinese oversight ended, in Bell's own telling,
with that oversight more deeply entrenched than it had been before
the crisis began. The government Gushi Khan's conquest had installed
in 1642 endured through all of it, but by the middle of the
eighteenth century it endured as something the Qing court now
expected to supervise rather than merely to court.
