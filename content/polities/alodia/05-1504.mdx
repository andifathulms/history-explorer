The traditional date for the fall of Soba is 1504, to a coalition of the Funj and
the Abdallab Arabs, and the Funj sultanate of Sennar took the country.

The date comes from later Sudanese chronicle traditions rather than from anything
contemporary, and the process it stands for was longer than a year. There is no
contemporary account of a siege, no letter, no inscription. What there is, is a
tradition remembered by the people who ended up holding the ground, dating the
end of the last Christian kingdom on the Nile to a year that is probably close
and cannot be checked.

## Nine hundred years

Christian Nubia lasted from the sixth century to the sixteenth.

That is longer than the interval between the fall of Rome and the Norman
conquest, and it happened on a river a thousand kilometres above a Muslim Egypt
with which these kingdoms were, for most of the period, formally at peace under a
treaty older than almost any other in the world.

With Soba it ends. Makuria had gone a hundred and thirty-nine years earlier. What
disappeared was not just two states but an entire branch of Christianity — with
its own liturgical language, its own script, its own architecture, a body of
literature in Old Nubian that survives in fragments, and nine centuries of
bishops.

It was not martyred and it did not apostatise under pressure in any dramatic
way. It ran out of clergy and was married into, over about two hundred years,
until there was nobody left to be the church of.

## Nobody wrote it down

The most striking thing about the end of Christian Nubia is how little anybody
seems to have noticed.

There is no lament from Alexandria for the loss of two ancient sees. There is no
crusading appeal, though Europe had been hearing about a Christian kingdom
somewhere south of Egypt for three hundred years and had spent a good deal of
energy looking for it — the Prester John of the letters was chased into Asia and
then into Ethiopia, and the actual Christian kingdoms on the Nile went under
while people were looking for a legendary one.

A civilisation that built four hundred churches, or however many four hundred
stands for, ended without an obituary.
