The conversion of Nubia is one of the few episodes in this corpus where an
imperial couple ran competing foreign policies against each other and the sources
say so.

Justinian was Chalcedonian. Theodora was Miaphysite. In the 540s both sent
missions south to the Nubian kingdoms, and John of Ephesus — a Miaphysite bishop
writing not long afterwards — tells the story from his own side: Theodora's
missionary Julian reached Nobatia first because she had written ahead to the
governor of the Thebaid instructing him to delay the emperor's party, and did.

Nobatia became Miaphysite. Makuria, the middle kingdom, took the Chalcedonian
mission and went the other way for a time. And Alodia, furthest south, was
converted around 580 by Longinus — Theodora's man again, working after her death.

## Why it mattered which

Because a church is a supply line, and the alignment decided where the bishops
came from for the next nine hundred years.

Miaphysite Nubia took its clergy from Alexandria. When Egypt became Muslim in the
640s the Coptic patriarchate continued under Muslim rule and went on consecrating
bishops for the Nubian kingdoms, and this corpus's Aksum record makes the same
observation about Ethiopia: two Christian establishments cut off from the rest of
Christendom, each supplying something the other needed, for a thousand years.

Chalcedonian Nubia would have been supplied from Constantinople, which after the
640s could not have reached it.

The alignment that looks in the sixth century like a quarrel about the nature of
Christ turns out in the seventh to be the difference between a church that can be
staffed and one that cannot.

## And when the supply failed

This corpus's Makuria record's last chapter dates the middle kingdom's collapse
partly to that: Nubian bishops were consecrated in Alexandria, the Coptic
patriarchate under Mamluk rule came under pressure of its own, and the sees went
unfilled.

Alodia outlasted Makuria by a hundred and thirty-nine years and went the same way
in the end. This record's own last chapter is about the Funj arriving to find a
kingdom whose church had already run out.

## What is left of it

Churches, mostly, and they are what the Soba excavations produced.

Three of them at the capital, one of them large, with granite columns, painted
plaster and fragments of window glass. Ibn Selim al-Aswani's four hundred is not
a number anybody can check and is not obviously absurd for a kingdom of this
extent — Nubian Christianity built at village scale as well as at royal scale,
and the churches along the Makurian Nile that have been excavated are numerous
and small.

And a bronze hanging lamp, an inscribed bishop's tombstone, imported amphorae,
and enough Egyptian and Aswan pottery to show the trade continuing through the
whole period.

None of it answers a single one of this record's four institution fields, which
is the subject of the first chapter and of the last.
