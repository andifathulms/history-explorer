Ibn Selim al-Aswani went to Nubia in the 970s as a Fatimid envoy — the mission's
business was the tribute arrangement this corpus's Makuria record carries as the
*baqt* — and he went on past Dongola to the kingdom beyond it.

His account does not survive as a book. It survives in quotation, mostly in
al-Maqrizi four hundred years later, and that is the ordinary condition of Arabic
geographical writing about places nobody went twice.

## What he says

That Alodia is larger than Makuria and its king more powerful. That its territory
is more extensive. That the capital, Soba, has fine buildings, large monasteries,
churches with much gold in them, and gardens. That there are four hundred
churches. That the country produces horses and camels, that the people have
gardens and grain, and that the king can raise more men than the Makurian one.

He also says the Alodians are Jacobites — Miaphysite — that their books are in
Greek which they translate into their own language, and that they have a
substantial Muslim minority living among them.

## How much of it to keep

The comparative claims are the load-bearing ones and they are also the ones a
visitor is least equipped to make.

He had been to both kingdoms, which is more than almost anybody else who wrote
about either. He had a diplomatic reason to assess a foreign ruler's strength
accurately, which is a point in favour. And he was a Fatimid envoy describing the
southern flank of a Muslim empire's frontier, which is a reason to notice
strength rather than to invent it.

Against that: four hundred churches is the kind of round number this corpus has
learned to read as *many*, exactly as al-Bakri's two hundred thousand men on the
Ghana record is read as *many* and Ezana's tribute lists are read as *a lot of
cattle*. And "larger and richer than the neighbour" is a comparison that flatters
the further place in almost every travel account ever written.

## What the archaeology says about it

Not much, and what it says is not contradictory.

Soba's excavated area shows a substantial settlement with monumental churches,
imported goods across the whole period, and enough scale to make "the largest of
the three kingdoms" credible without confirming it. There is no royal palace
identified with certainty. There is no archive.

The site is under the eastern suburbs of Khartoum and was quarried for bricks for
centuries before anybody dug it, which is the ordinary situation for a medieval
African capital under a modern city and is the reason this record's first chapter
says the problem is not that nobody has looked.

## One visitor, and the shape of the page

This corpus's Ghana record is built on al-Bakri, who never left Córdoba. Its Mali
record has Ibn Battuta, who was there and disapproved of most of it. Its Kilwa
record has the same man, who liked it. Its Great Zimbabwe record has nobody at
all.

This record has one man who went, quoted by another man four centuries later, and
a suburb.

That is why the four institution fields are empty and why this page is short. It
is not that Alodia was a small or unimportant kingdom — the one witness who saw
both says it was the larger of the two — but that a corpus which codes what a
state's army rested on and where its revenue came from needs somebody to have
written that down, and for this kingdom nobody did.
