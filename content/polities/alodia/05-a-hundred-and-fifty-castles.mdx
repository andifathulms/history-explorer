Two descriptions of the same country survive from either side of its
disappearance, three centuries apart, and reading them together is the closest
anybody can get to watching Christian Nubia end.

## What was there

The first is a thirteenth-century Egyptian compilation, which gives the southern
kingdom four lines.

> Town of 'Alwah. Here there are troops and a large kingdom with wide districts,
> in which there are four hundred churches. The town lies to the east of the
> large island between the two rivers, the White Nile and the Green Nile. All its
> inhabitants are Jacobite Christians. Around it there are monasteries, some at a
> distance from the stream and some upon its banks. In the town there is a very
> large and spacious church, skilfully planned and constructed, and larger than
> all the other churches in the country; it is called the church of Manbali.

Troops, a large kingdom, wide districts, four hundred churches, monasteries on
and off the river, and one cathedral bigger than any other building in the
country, with a name. The crops depended on the Nile flood and the rain.

Nothing in that paragraph is the description of a state in difficulty.

## What was left

The second comes from the chaplain of a Portuguese embassy that spent six years
in Ethiopia in the 1520s, and Evetts's co-editor prints it in a footnote because
it describes the same place.

Francisco Alvares never went to Nubia. He questioned a Syrian from Tripoli who
had, and wrote down what the man told him:

> There are in it a hundred and fifty churches, which still contain crucifixes
> and effigies of our Lady and other effigies painted on the walls, and all old.
> These churches are all in old ancient castles which are throughout the country;
> and as many castles, there are so many churches.

A country of abandoned fortified towns, each with a church in it, each church
still holding its crucifix and its wall-paintings, and nobody using any of them.

## The delegation

Then Alvares reports something he did see.

> While we were in the country of the Prester John, there came six men from that
> country to the Prester himself, begging of him to send them priests and friars
> to teach them. He did not choose to send them.

The reason he gives is the mechanism of the whole disaster, stated by the one
party who could have broken it and would not. The Ethiopian emperor's own
metropolitan came from the patriarch of Alexandria, who lived under Muslim rule;
he had his bishop from that source and could not manufacture clergy himself. How
then could he give priests and friars, *since another gave them?*

Alvares adds what he was told about how it had happened. In ancient times these
people had everything from the patriarch; it was a very long time since a bishop
of theirs had died, and on account of the wars they could not get another, *and so
they lost all their Christianity*. He notes, because he is writing for a court
that would want to know, that the Nubians say there is much fine gold in their
country.

## What the pair shows

A church without a bishop cannot ordain. A church that cannot ordain runs out of
priests in one generation and out of sacraments in two, and there is nothing the
laity can do about it except send six men a very long way to ask somebody else.

Between the two passages lies the whole of it: a kingdom with four hundred
churches and the largest cathedral in the country, and then a hundred and fifty
buildings still standing with the paintings on the walls, in castles nobody lives
in, in a country whose Christians had walked to another kingdom to beg for
teachers and been turned down for a reason of ecclesiastical procedure.

Neither writer was there. The first says nothing about his sources and the second
is repeating a Syrian traveller. That is the quality of evidence available for
the end of a Christian kingdom that lasted the better part of a thousand years,
and it is why so much of what can be said about Alodia has had to be dug up
instead of read.
