In the 970s a Fatimid envoy called Ibn Selim al-Aswani went up the Nile past
Makuria to the kingdom beyond it, and wrote a description. Alodia, he says, is
larger than Makuria, its king more powerful, its territory more extensive, and
its capital at Soba has fine houses, churches with much gold, and gardens. He
gives the number of churches as four hundred.

That paragraph is a substantial fraction of everything known about this kingdom,
and the third chapter is about how much of it to believe.

## What is not known

Nobody disputes that Alodia existed. There are churches excavated at Soba, Greek
and Old Nubian inscriptions, imported ceramics, a royal seal, and an envoy's
account of a functioning court.

What there is not is any evidence about how it worked. Not what the army rested
on, not where the revenue came from, not who inherited, not on what ground a
king claimed the right to rule. Makuria, a few hundred kilometres downstream,
shared the religion, the script, the architecture and probably a great deal else,
and a good deal more is known about it — but a neighbour's arrangements are not
evidence, and filling the gaps from downstream would produce a kingdom that
never existed anywhere except in the reconstruction.

So the gaps stay.

## Soba

The capital is under the eastern suburbs of Khartoum.

Parts have been excavated — three churches, a large building that may be a
palace, cemeteries, a great deal of pottery — and a good deal of the rest is
built on. The site was mined for bricks for centuries.

That is the ordinary situation for a medieval African capital that happens to sit
where a modern city grew, and it is worth stating because the alternative
explanation for how little is known is the one a reader may reach for. It is not
that nobody has looked. It is that the looking has to be done between houses.

## Where it sits on this river

Four polities held the middle Nile in succession, and this is the least known of
them.

Kush, from the eighth century BC, ruling Egypt and then Meroë, writing a script
that can be read aloud and not understood. Then three centuries nobody can fill.
Then Makuria and Alodia, both Christian, from the sixth century — Makuria
holding the Egyptian frontier by treaty for six hundred years, this one upstream
and, on the only account by somebody who saw both, richer. Then the Funj
sultanate of Sennar, Muslim, which lasted until an Egyptian army arrived in
1821.

Nine hundred years of Christian kingdoms on a river a thousand kilometres above
a Muslim Egypt, at peace with it for most of that time. It is one of the more
surprising facts in African history and one of the least written about, and the
reason is simple enough: for the southern kingdom there is a suburb, a scatter
of excavated churches, and one visitor's paragraph.
