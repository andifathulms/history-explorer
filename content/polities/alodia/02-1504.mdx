The traditional date for the fall of Soba is 1504, to a coalition of the Funj and
the Abdallab Arabs, and the Funj sultanate of Sennar took the country.

The date comes from later Sudanese chronicle traditions rather than from anything
contemporary, and the process it stands for was longer than a year. Arab groups
had been moving into the Gezira for generations; the church was collapsing for
the same reason it collapsed in Makuria, because Nubian bishops were consecrated
in Alexandria and Alexandria under Mamluk rule could not supply them; and there
are indications the kingdom had already fragmented before the Funj arrived.

## Nine hundred years

Christian Nubia lasted from the sixth century to the sixteenth. That is longer
than the interval between the fall of Rome and the Norman conquest, and it
happened on a river a thousand kilometres up from a Muslim Egypt with which it
was, for most of that time, formally at peace.

This corpus's Makurian record ends in 1365 and this one in 1504, a hundred and
thirty-nine years later. With Soba the last Christian state on the Nile above
Egypt disappears, and the Christian tradition on this river ends after nine
hundred years — not conquered in a war anybody records, but ground down by a
demographic change, a bishop shortage and a sequence of raids.

## The Funj, and the fifth polity

The Funj sultanate of Sennar ruled the middle Nile from about 1504 to 1821, when
Muhammad Ali's Egyptian army took it.

It is not in this corpus. Neither are the Abdallab. So this record's `ended.by`
is empty and the edge that ought to run from this page to the next one on the
same river cannot be drawn — which makes it the seventh African record here
ending against a power the site cannot name.

That is now a pattern rather than a series of accidents, and this corpus's
Kanem-Bornu record's last chapter sets it out: six of nine records in this region
had an empty `by` before this one, and the reason in almost every case is that
the polity on the other side has no page.

## What this record is for

It is thin. Two chapters, no coded institutions, two turning points, no reach
figure, and three influence counts of zero.

It is here because this corpus's Makurian record named it as an absence on its
own river in its own last chapter, and because a site that carries the middle
kingdom of three and not the southern one is telling a reader that the middle one
was the important one, which Ibn Selim al-Aswani did not think.

Hard rule 8 says rankings include every polity, threaded or not, region or none,
and that measurement does not require a predecessor. The corresponding obligation
is that a record with nothing in most of its fields still gets written, and gets
written as thinly as the evidence supports rather than padded to look like the
pages around it.

This is what that looks like.
