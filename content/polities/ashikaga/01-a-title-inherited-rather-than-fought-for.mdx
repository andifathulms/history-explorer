Ashikaga Takauji, descended from the same Minamoto line Yoritomo's own
shogunate had come from, founded a new military house on the ruins of
the one he had just destroyed - installing a compliant emperor in
Kyoto and receiving from him the same office, Sei-i Tai Shogun, that
had governed Japan on the throne's behalf since 1192. He died in 1356,
still fighting a war whose real resolution belonged to his grandson.

## An office that no longer needed a fight for each holder

Under the Hōjō, the shogunal title had been filled by imperial
appointment, however hollow the appointment had become in practice.
Under the Ashikaga, the office simply became hereditary in the family
itself, passing from father to son without the outside confirmation or
internal power struggle that had marked its transfer under Kamakura.
This was, in its own way, a genuine consolidation - and it did nothing
to guarantee that the man who inherited the title would actually wield
the power attached to it. In nearly every generation that followed
Takauji, the shogun himself remained a figurehead, no more capable of
independent rule than the mikado he nominally served.

## A boy shogun who ended his family's war

That pattern of hereditary transfer without guaranteed personal
authority did not stop one holder of the office from becoming, in
substance as well as name, the most powerful ruler Japan had. Takauji's
grandson Yoshimitsu became shogun at the age of ten and grew into a
celebrated warrior across southern and western Japan. It was under his
rule that the fifty-six-year schism between northern and southern
imperial courts - the war his own grandfather's rebellion had opened -
was finally settled, in 1392, giving the Ashikaga house a single throne
in Kyoto to answer to rather than a divided one.
