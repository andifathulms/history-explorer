The war that broke Kyoto ran for ten years, 1467 to 1477, and settled
almost nothing except that the country was no longer governable from
the city where it was fought.

## A capital of half a million, fighting itself

Kyoto in 1467 held something like a hundred and eighty thousand
households - perhaps nine hundred thousand people, a population few
contemporary European cities came within a tenth of matching. Hosokawa
Katsumoto could put sixty thousand of his own retainers into the field
at a word, with twenty-one thousand more from collateral branches of
his house; Yamana Sōzen commanded comparable numbers. One of the war's
first great battles was fought for possession of the Zen monastery of
Sōkokuji, founded by Yoshimitsu himself as the Ashikaga house's own
mortuary temple, and was followed by a still fiercer battle for
control of its ruins. Neither side ever achieved a decisive victory
over the other. What they achieved instead was ten years of fighting
inside and around a city that had never been built to withstand a
war.

## What the fighting cost

By the time it ended, the samurai who had fought it abandoned the
capital outright and went home to their own provinces, and the
imperial court that Yoshimitsu and Yoshimasa had spent fortunes
patronising was left destitute in every sense a court can be.
Sōkokuji, temples, palaces and mansions across the city went up in
flames; the emperor himself spent thirteen years confined within the
narrow grounds of the shogun's own Muromachi compound because there
was nowhere else safe to hold court, and when ceremonies finally
resumed they were abridged versions, stripped down because there was
no money left to hold them properly. When one emperor died in 1500 it
took forty-four days to raise the funds for his funeral; his successor
could not afford a coronation until 1521, two decades into his reign.
Between 1465 and 1585 no emperor abdicated at all - not from any new
respect for the office, but because the ceremony an abdication
required cost money nobody had.

## What replaced the government that failed

The war's deeper damage was administrative rather than architectural.
Imperial decrees and shogunal instructions alike came to be ignored
everywhere outside Kyoto and the single province of Yamashiro, and it
was soon recognised as pointless to issue them at all. Ordinary
taxation on peasants, craftsmen and traders continued to be collected
- it simply stopped reaching the shogun's own treasury, because the
great military houses that collected it had made themselves
independent in substance while the Ashikaga name remained nominally
above them. European missionaries arriving a century later described
local warlords as "Kings" - the King of Bungo, the King of Satsuma,
the King of Hirado - and Murdoch's own judgment is that the term was,
if not technically correct, practically accurate: within his own
domain, even the smallest of these lords held army, law and taxation
entirely in his own hands, owing nothing upward to anyone. This is the
condition later Japanese historians call the Sengoku Jidai, the Age of
the Country at War, and its opening decades ran directly out of the
fighting in Kyoto's own streets rather than starting from nothing once
the Ōnin War happened to end.

## What the war did not destroy

Oddly, it did not destroy the shogunate's own cultural patronage.
Yoshimasa, driven from the political stage as thoroughly as any
Ashikaga ruler before him, retreated after the war into exactly the
kind of building projects that had helped provoke it in the first
place - the Silver Pavilion chief among them - and the Zen-affiliated
school of painters his and his grandfather's patronage had sustained
went on producing what is still counted among the high points of
Japanese pictorial art, largely because the great monasteries that
housed the artists were among the few institutions the general anarchy
left more or less alone. A country whose central government had
stopped functioning in every practical sense kept producing, in its
capital's own ashes, some of the finest art it would ever make.
