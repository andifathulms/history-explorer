title: A king of Japan by a foreign emperor's grant
drafted_from: griffis-1883

Yoshimitsu, having reunited the imperial courts and built himself a
luxurious palace in Kyoto, reached a height of personal power no
Ashikaga shogun before or after him would match. He styled himself in
the manner of the mikado, dressed his court to match, and, for a man
already the most powerful figure in the country, wanted only one thing
his own countrymen could not give him: the outward form of a crown.

## An embassy that bought a title at a real cost

In 1401 Yoshimitsu sent a mission to the Ming court bearing gifts that
amounted, in substance, to an acknowledgment of Chinese overlordship,
and accepted in return the style Nippon Ō - King of Japan. To later
generations of Japanese readers, this was not diplomacy but a
humiliation without justification: a ruler who already held every real
form of power available to a subject, seeking foreign validation for a
rank his own country's own institutions had no mechanism to grant him,
and doing so at the price of Japan's own dignity before a neighbour
already regarded as arrogant.

## A precedent nobody else would repeat until the reckoning came

No mikado, one nineteenth-century chronicler insists, ever accepted
tribute status from China in the whole of Japanese history; only this
one shogun did, and the insult attached to his name for it rather than
to the office he held. A much later shogun who styled himself "Tycoon"
before American treaty negotiators, in similarly self-aggrandizing
terms, would pay for the same impulse far faster - in war, exile and
the final abolition of the shogunate itself, rather than in a slow
accumulation of posthumous blame. Yoshimitsu's own reign, by contrast,
closed a year after his greatest honours with a retirement to a
Buddhist monastery, the humiliation of 1401 left for history rather
than his own contemporaries to punish.
