Yoshimitsu's son and grandson governed without his commanding
authority, and the shogunate's decline shows most plainly in what
happened to the sixth shogun, Yoshinori - a former priest who governed
with real vigour for thirteen years and was murdered at his own host's
dinner table for trying to use it.

## A banquet with the doors shut

In 1441 Yoshinori moved to break the power of the Akamatsu family by
partitioning its provinces among rival vassals. The Akamatsu chieftain
said nothing, and instead invited the shogun to a banquet at his own
Kyoto mansion. When the feast was at its height, two of his retainers
loosed every horse in the stables into the courtyard, where the
uproar covered the sound of the doors being shut. One retainer seized
the shogun by the hands; another came up behind him and took his head
off with a single stroke. Akamatsu fled to his own castle with the
head as a trophy, was besieged there by three rival houses eager for a
share of his confiscated domains, and took his own life along with his
leading vassals when the castle fell.

It was the second time in two and a half centuries that a sitting
Japanese ruler had been murdered by his own subject at a banquet held
in his honour, and it left the shogunate to a nine-year-old heir who
drank himself to death within two years, and then to his younger
brother Yoshimasa - a child of eight, patented as shogun at fourteen,
whose long personal rule would do more damage to the house than any
assassination had.

## An aesthete with an empty treasury

Yoshimasa inherited his grandfather Yoshimitsu's taste for magnificence
without any of his capacity for governing, and kept building as though
the state's finances were still what they had been in Yoshimitsu's
day, when in fact they were not. He is recorded pawning his own armour
to cover the costs of his consort's childbirth in one breath and
lavishing fortunes on incense-parties, poem-competitions and tea
ceremonies in the next - refined pastimes, in the record's own
judgment, considerably less innocent once they were the main
occupation of the man responsible for governing an empire sliding
toward ruin. He is recorded holding a banquet in his own palace in the
middle of one of the fiercest battles of 1467, enjoying, by one
chronicler's comparison, the spectacle of the world burning around him
much as Nero was said to have done.

Real administration passed by default to his consort, the Lady Tomi,
and to her brother Hino, whose appetite for bribes was constant and
unappeasable at a court so impoverished that other nobles could no
longer afford robes to attend what functions still occurred. When Tomi
had the old toll barriers at Kyoto's gates rebuilt, ostensibly to fund
repairs to the imperial palace, and diverted every coin of the revenue
into her own purse instead, the city mob rose, tore the barriers down
again, and had to be pacified with a debt-cancellation edict rather
than a reckoning.

## An heir undecided

What turned this ordinary misrule into a war was Yoshimasa's own
failure to settle who would succeed him. Having no son for years, he
adopted his own brother Yoshimi as heir; when a son, Yoshihisa, was
finally born to him, he could not bring himself to choose cleanly
between the two, and each claimant found patrons among the great
military houses already competing for influence at a weakening court.
Two of the shogunate's own senior deputies, Hosokawa Katsumoto and his
father-in-law Yamana Sōzen - the "Red Monk," nicknamed for his flushed
complexion - had already spent years managing rival successions inside
the Shiba and Hatakeyama military houses from opposite sides of every
quarrel. By 1467 each commanded tens of thousands of his own retainers
and had built, in effect, two competing shadow governments inside the
one the Ashikaga name was still nominally attached to. The dispute
over who would succeed Yoshimasa was not the cause of what followed.
It was simply the occasion the two men, already primed for open war
with each other, had been waiting for.
