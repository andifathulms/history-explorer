Oda Nobunaga first appears in the record in 1542, inheriting from his
father a modest stock of arms, land and feuds typical of any regional
warrior family scrambling for territory in a fractured country. Over
the following decades he absorbed one province after another - Suruga,
Mino, Ōmi, Mikawa, Ise, Echizen - through a combination of battle and
alliance, until he held Kyoto itself and could make or unmake the
occupant of its shogunal office.

## A kingmaker who became his own king's enemy

Nobunaga used exactly that power in 1568 to put Ashikaga Yoshiaki on
the shogunal throne, backing his claim with the same military weight
that had already won him half a dozen provinces. The partnership did
not last. Within six years the two men had quarrelled, and Nobunaga
answered the quarrel the way he answered every obstacle in his path:
he arrested Yoshiaki and deposed him outright.

## An office ended, not merely vacated

The deposition in 1573 closed a shogunal line that had held power,
nominally or in fact, for two hundred and thirty-eight years. No new
Sei-i Tai Shogun would be appointed for three decades afterward.
Nobunaga himself, despite commanding more territory and more soldiers
than any Ashikaga shogun had wielded in generations, never claimed the
title for his own house - by one account because the office had long
been the customary preserve of Minamoto descendants, and Nobunaga
traced his own line to the rival Taira instead. He took the rank of
Naidaijin, inner great minister, and governed in the mikado's name
without ever becoming, in title, anything more than the emperor's own
subject.

## A power built on war that could not survive peace

Nobunaga proved, like Yoritomo four centuries before him, a skillful
and determined soldier - but unlike Yoritomo, he never subdued Japan's
great regional clans completely, and lacked the administrative
patience to consolidate in peace what he had won by force. He met his
own death in Kyoto in 1582, betrayed by one of his own captains and
burned alive in a temple he had chosen, by chance, as his lodging that
night. What his conquests had already accomplished, though, could not
be undone: the office the Ashikaga had held for nearly two and a half
centuries was gone, and the country's next unifier would build on
foundations Nobunaga's own campaigns had laid rather than start again
from the fractured landscape he had first inherited.
