title: The help that stopped coming

Granada's survival strategy had two legs. One was managing Castile. The other
was North Africa, and it was cut off in a single afternoon in 1340.

The Marinids of Fez — a dynasty this site's Islamic West region will describe
separately — were the last North African power to take the Iberian frontier
seriously, and for most of the fourteenth century Granada used them. Zanata
Berber volunteers, the *ghuzat*, crossed the strait to serve in the emirate's
armies under their own commander; Marinid armies crossed when the emirate could
persuade them that Islam in the peninsula required it; and the mere possibility
of a Marinid landing was a card Granada played in every negotiation with
Castile.

It was also a danger. The ghuzat were a foreign corps with its own shaykh and
its own loyalties, and more than one emir found them backing his rivals. This
site has recorded that arrangement failing on the Abbasid, Samanid, Ayyubid,
Buyid and Ziyarid pages; here it failed less catastrophically and for the same
reasons.

In October 1340, at the Salado river near Tarifa, a combined Castilian and
Portuguese army with volunteers from across Europe destroyed a joint Marinid
and Granadan force. Alfonso XI followed it by taking Algeciras in 1344. The
Marinids never mounted another serious expedition to the peninsula, and after
Rio Salado no North African power did.

The consequence took a century and a half to arrive and was decisive when it
did. From 1340 Granada was alone: one small state, on one frontier, against a
neighbour four or five times its size, with no possibility of a second front and
no ally who could reach it. That it lasted another hundred and fifty-two years
is the strongest evidence for how well the first leg of the strategy worked.

The other thing that changed is harder to date and shows in the fifteenth
century. Granada's internal politics became lethal — the Banu Sarraj faction
and its rivals, emirs deposed and restored, sultans blinded, the court divided
into parties that each sought Castilian backing. A state whose entire security
rested on unified diplomacy toward one neighbour began negotiating with that
neighbour in two or three voices at once.
