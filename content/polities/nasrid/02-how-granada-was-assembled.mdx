Al-Maqqari, writing in the seventeenth century, gives the founding of the
emirate in Ibn Khaldun's words, and what the passage records is not a conquest.
It is forty years of a man changing sides at the right moment.

The family were from Arjona, a castle in the district of Cordoba. Their
ancestors had been army officers, known locally as the Banu Nasr; they traced
their genealogy to Sa'd ibn Ubada, a Companion of the Prophet and lord of the
Khazraj. When the Almohads began to fail, and members of the Almohad royal
family were handing their castles to the Christian king, Muhammad ibn Yusuf had
himself proclaimed sultan of al-Andalus in 1231.

Then he had the Friday prayer said in the name of somebody else.

## Four allegiances in five years

The name he ordered mentioned in the public prayers, as Commander of the
Faithful, was that of Abu Zakariya at Tunis — the Hafsid who had just kept
Ifriqiya out of the Almohad wreck. A man with a castle in Andalusia was
borrowing the credit of a governor at the far end of North Africa who had
himself seceded eighteen months earlier.

Jaén and Jerez submitted the following year. In 1233, hearing that his rival
Ibn Hud had received a favourable answer from Baghdad, Ibn al-Ahmar sent in his
allegiance to Ibn Hud instead. Then Seville revolted from Ibn Hud under a local
notable, and Ibn al-Ahmar allied with him, gave him a daughter in marriage,
promised to defend him on condition of vassalage — and entered Seville as its
lord in 1234. In due course he had the man seized and put to death, through an
agent he sent for the purpose. One month later Seville went back to Ibn Hud and
threw his troops out.

Meanwhile he had made an alliance with Ferdinand III of Castile against Ibn
Hud, and received occasional Castilian troops. Ibn Hud, to break that up,
offered Ferdinand thirty castles on his own western frontier if the king would
drop Ibn al-Ahmar and help him take Cordoba. The offer was accepted and the
castles handed over. Cordoba fell to Castile in 1236 — *may God restore it to
Islam*, the text adds — and Ibn al-Ahmar co-operated with his forces at the
siege of Seville in 1248.

## What he was assembling

Granada itself came by conspiracy rather than by siege. A friend of his inside
the city raised the citizens against Ibn Hud, got Ibn al-Ahmar proclaimed, and
rode to Jaén to offer him their allegiance. He sent an advance force, followed
with the rest of the army, took up residence, and built the fortress of the
Alhambra to live in.

Málaga followed soon after. Almería came in 1245, handed over by Ibn Hud's own
vizier, who had seized it. Lorca sent its allegiance in 1264. Every one of
those acquisitions is a defection managed, not a town stormed.

By the end the Muslim population of Spain had been driven onto the coastal
strip between Ronda in the west and Almería in the east — twenty stages long,
Ibn Khaldun measures it, and one stage or less from the sea to the northern
frontier. Ibn al-Ahmar, the passage says plainly, then grew angry and tried to
recover the rest of the island, and found it too difficult.

## The thing he built instead

What he had instead was North Africa on call. In 1261 the sultan of the far
Maghreb sent three thousand warriors under a standing agreement; when they went
home others crossed in their place, continually, until Ibn al-Ahmar died in
1272.

That is the machine. A strip of coast one day's march deep, a genealogy running
to a Companion of the Prophet, a Castilian alliance renewed whenever Castile
was the greater danger, and a rotating corps of Berber volunteers paid for out
of silk. It held for two hundred and fifty years, and every part of it was
assembled by a man from a small castle who was never, in any year of his life,
the strongest party at the table.
