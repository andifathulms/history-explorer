The emirate paid tribute to a Christian kingdom for most of its existence and
was, for much of that time, richer per head than the kingdom it paid.

The reason is silk. The sierras behind Granada and Almería were the largest
silk-producing region in Europe, and the emirate's economy was built on it:
mulberry, worms, spinning and weaving in the towns, and export through Málaga
and Almería to Genoese buyers who had permanent factories in both. Granada
exported dried fruit and sugar as well, and the ports handled the North African
trade. The state taxed all of it, and the tribute to Castile — which was
frequently in arrears and periodically renegotiated — was payable out of
customs rather than out of the peasantry.

<Figure id="n-silk" />

The politics were played with equal care. Granada survived by being useful and
by never being worth the cost of conquering. It fought for Castile against
Muslims when required and against Castile when Castile was distracted; it
invited North African intervention when it needed leverage and expelled the
North Africans when they became a threat; and it exploited every Castilian
minority, succession dispute and civil war for two and a half centuries. The
emirs were also, several of them, personally close to Castilian kings —
Muhammad V spent part of his exile at the court of Pedro the Cruel and returned
with Castilian help, and the Alcázar Pedro built at Seville was decorated by
craftsmen from Granada in the Granadan manner.

<Figure id="n-pedro" />

The high point is the second reign of Muhammad V, from 1362 to 1391. Diplomacy
that kept both Castile and Fez at arm's length, a court that produced Ibn
al-Khatib — vizier, physician, historian, and one of the finest prose writers
in Arabic — and his student and successor Ibn Zamrak, whose poems are carved
on the walls of the Alhambra.

<Figure id="n-muh5" />

This is the part of the emirate's history the phrase "the last remnant" makes
invisible. Granada was not a survival waiting to end. It was a functioning
state with a specialised export economy, a professional diplomatic service and
a cultural output that its neighbours imitated, and it behaved like one for ten
generations.
