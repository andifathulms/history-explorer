The historians of Granada, al-Maqqari says, recorded a great many warnings, and
the ruin of the kingdom was announced more than once. He is not endorsing the
omens. He is doing what a seventeenth-century Maghribi scholar does with a
catastrophe in the recent past: collecting what the people who lived through it
said about it afterwards.

## The talisman

He had read, in the handwriting of a historian from Guadix who lived at
Tlemcen, an account he says he vouches for. A jurist told the historian that he
had been present when they took down the talisman known as the weathercock from
the top of the old citadel at Granada, during repairs, and had seen it with his
own eyes. It was heptagonal, and carried a verse:

> The palace at fair Granada presents to the eye of the observer a talisman
> turning round with the succession of time. The horseman on its weathercock,
> although a solid body, turns with every wind. This, to the wise man, reveals
> many a mystery. Indeed, after subsisting a short time, a calamity will come
> which shall ruin both the palace and its owner.

A city that told itself its own fortress was inscribed with a prophecy of its
fall was a city that had understood its position. The verse is not a prediction
of 1492. It is a description of a state that had lived for two centuries on
turning with every wind.

## The review, and the river

Abu'l-Hasan Ali came to the throne after a civil war with his brother
al-Zaghal, made war on the Christians, took several castles, and was feared and
sued for peace. His armies grew and he decided to review them. He had his
architects build him a platform outside the Alhambra, and the troops passed in
front of him daily from the twenty-fourth of March 1478 until the review was
finished a month later — armour polished, silk robes, fast horses, swords and
spears and shields inlaid with gold and silver.

One day while the sultan sat under his pavilion and the troops filed past, with
the slope and the crest of the Sabika hill crowded with people who had come out
to watch, the rain came down in torrents and the Darro burst its banks. The
flood came off the mountains carrying boulders and whole trees, and took houses,
shops, mills, inns, markets, bridges and garden walls with it. The water reached
the square where the great mosque stood. Nothing like it had been seen in that
country, and the people took it for the harbinger of what was coming.

Set the two halves of that day beside each other and the fifteenth century is
in them: the largest army the emirate could put on parade, and a river that did
not care.

## What actually went wrong

Al-Maqqari's source is unsparing about the rest, and it is not weather. The
Castilian nobles fell out among themselves, one seizing Cordoba, another
Seville, another Jerez — and instead of profiting from it, the sultan gave
himself to pleasure, handed the government to his viziers, hid himself from his
subjects, let the army lose its discipline, imposed new taxes, and had several
of his best generals executed on the theory that the Christians were too busy
to attack him. Then the king of Castile finished his civil war, brought the
rebellious nobles to heel, and was free.

The last thing to go was the family. Abu'l-Hasan had two sons by his cousin and
wife, and another wife he doted on, a Christian, who had also borne him
children. The court divided into a party for each set of sons. The truce ran
out while they were arguing.

Everything in that paragraph had been survivable before. Granada had spent ten
generations trading on the fact that Castile was always divided and always
distracted. In the 1470s Castile stopped being either, and the emirate chose
that decade to be both.
