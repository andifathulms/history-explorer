Al-Maqqari finished his great book on al-Andalus in Cairo in the 1630s, a
hundred and forty years after the country he was writing about ceased to exist.
He was born at Tlemcen. He had never seen Granada and never would. The last
pages of the work are the nearest thing there is to a report on what happened to
the people afterwards, written by someone who went and looked at part of it.

## Boabdil at Fez

The terms of the surrender assigned the deposed sultan the Alpujarras, and he
went to live at Andarax. Some time later the king of Castile used a stratagem
to get him out of Spain: he pretended that Abu Abdallah had expressed a wish to
leave, and wrote to the governor of Almería instructing that nobody hinder
Muley Abu Abdallah from sailing to whichever African port he liked best, and
that everyone who read the letter should facilitate his passage and observe the
treaty towards him.

He took the hint, embarked at Almería, and landed at Melilla. He had meant to
settle at Marrakesh, and on landing heard that the country there was suffering
famine and pestilence, so he went to Fez instead. There, complaining of his luck
and regretting the kingdom he had lost, he settled with his family, and built
himself some palaces in imitation of those of Granada.

Al-Maqqari saw them. *Which we ourselves saw and visited during our residence
in that city.*

He died at Fez in 940 of the Hijra and was buried outside the gate of the law. He left
two sons. When al-Maqqari was in Fez in 1037 of the Hijra he was acquainted
with some of their descendants, who were living on the charitable allowances made to the poor
out of the funds of the mosques, and who were, he writes, nothing more than
mere beggars.

## The hundred and twenty years in between

The Capitulations guaranteed the population its religion, its law, its property
and its customs. Within seven years the Christians had begun infringing them
one by one, and by 1498 were compelling conversions — the most plausible
pretext being that their priests had written books on the propriety of forcing
Christians who had become Muslims back to their former religion. Then the
argument was extended: *thy ancestor was a Christian, although he made himself
a Muslim; thou must also become a Christian.*

The Albaicín rose and killed its magistrates, which supplied the excuse for the
next measure: a law condemning to death anyone who revolted against his
magistrates, unless he immediately became a Christian. Every Muslim in Granada
and its neighbourhood was ordered to convert within a fixed time. Those who
refused took up arms in the villages and were hunted down and almost
exterminated, except for a group who fortified themselves on a mountain, won a
battle there and killed the lord of Cordoba, and were granted terms: they could
cross to Fez with their families and their movable property, but might carry no
more money than the journey required.

Those who stayed were Christians in appearance and not in their hearts. They
prayed and performed their ablutions at the proper hours in secret, and were
watched with the greatest vigilance, and many were discovered and burnt. They
were forbidden weapons, and then any sharp instrument made of iron, down to
small knives.

## Where they went

The final expulsion, al-Maqqari writes, happened in our own time, in 1610. Many
thousands went to Fez; thousands crossed from Oran to Tlemcen; the greater part
took the road to Tunis.

Few reached where they were going. They were set upon and robbed on the road,
especially on the roads to Fez and Tlemcen. Those who made for Tunis mostly
arrived safely, and repopulated empty towns and districts there, and also
Tetouan, Salé and the plain of the Mitidja near Algiers. Some took service with
the sultan of Morocco, who formed them into a body and gave them the port of
Salé — where, al-Maqqari notes, they have since made themselves famous by their
naval expeditions, and have fortified the castle and built themselves houses,
palaces and baths, and are living there still. A few went on to Constantinople,
Egypt and Syria.

God, the passage ends, is master of all lands and dominions, and gives them to
whomever he pleases.

## What was left in the ground

The Alhambra was not destroyed. The Christian king ordered repairs to it and to
the other fortresses immediately after the surrender, came up to it daily during
the work, and went back down to his camp every night until he stopped being
afraid of the townspeople. It has been maintained by whoever held Granada ever
since, which is why the best-preserved medieval Islamic palace in the world is
in a country that spent the next century removing every other trace of the
people who built it.
