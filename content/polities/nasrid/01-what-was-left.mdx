title: What was left

The middle of the thirteenth century removed al-Andalus. Cordoba fell to
Castile in 1236, Valencia to Aragon in 1238, Seville in 1248; Portugal took the
Algarve. Within about twenty years, five centuries of Muslim rule over most of
the peninsula was reduced to one emirate in the south-east corner.

That emirate is this page, and the manner of its founding is the thing to
understand about it.

Muhammad ibn Yusuf ibn Nasr, called Ibn al-Ahmar, was a minor commander from
Arjona proclaimed in 1232 in the confusion after the Almohad collapse that this
site's Almohad page describes. He was one of several claimants in a fragmented
country, and what distinguished him was neither descent nor doctrine — he
claimed descent from the Ansar of Medina and made no caliphal claim at all —
but judgement about what was possible.

He moved his capital to Granada in 1238 because it was defensible, distant from
the Castilian frontier and near the sea. In 1246 he accepted vassalage to
Ferdinand III of Castile and agreed an annual tribute. And in 1248 he sent
Granadan troops to help Ferdinand besiege Seville, which was held by other
Muslims.

That is a hard sentence and this page does not soften it. It is also the reason
there was an emirate at all. The alternatives available in 1246 were
submission on terms or conquest without them, and every other Andalusi ruler who
chose differently was gone within a generation. Ibn al-Ahmar bought two hundred
and fifty years with a tribute and a contingent of troops.

What he bought them for is the rest of this page. The territory — roughly the
modern provinces of Granada, Málaga and Almería — was mountain country with a
fertile coastal strip, hard to invade and easy to hold, and it filled up with
refugees from everywhere else. A small state became one of the most densely
populated in Europe, perhaps three hundred thousand people, because it was the
only place left to go.
