The Alhambra is the best-preserved medieval Islamic palace complex in the
world, and it was built by a tributary state in its last century and a half.

Ibn al-Ahmar began the citadel on the Sabika hill in the 1230s. The buildings
that draw four million visitors a year are later: the Comares palace and the
great gate under Yusuf I in the 1330s and 1340s, and the Court of the Lions
under Muhammad V after 1362. It is a complex of courts, chambers and gardens
built of brick, timber, plaster and tile — cheap materials, worked to an
extraordinary standard — with water running through it as an organising element
rather than an ornament.

<Figure id="n-justicia" />

The feature that belongs in a political history rather than an architectural
one is the writing. The Alhambra is covered in inscriptions: Qur'anic verses, the
Nasrid motto, and — unusually — a great deal of original poetry, much of it by
Ibn Zamrak, the court poet of Muhammad V, composed for the specific room it is
carved in. The fountain in the Court of the Lions has a poem on its basin that
describes the fountain. The walls of a palace state, in verse, what the palace
is for and who built it.

<Figure id="n-lions" />

Two things follow.

The first is about scale, and it is sharper here than almost anywhere. The
emirate was a tributary of a larger kingdom for two hundred and fifty years and
never held more than a corner of one peninsula. Measured by territory, by
population, by armies, by anything a chancery would have counted, it is
negligible — and it produced the building that more people now travel to see
than anything else the medieval Islamic world put up.

The second is about what the building was doing. A dynasty that paid tribute,
sent troops to its overlord's sieges and could not have won a war against
Castile in any year of its existence built a palace whose entire vocabulary is
sovereignty: the throne room facing the Christian frontier, the inscriptions
naming the emir as victorious, the motto — *wa la ghaliba illa Allah*, there is
no conqueror but God — repeated thousands of times across the walls.

That motto is usually read as piety. Read it in the political situation it was
carved into and it is also an argument: that the emirate's weakness relative to
Castile settles nothing, because conquest is not what confers right. It is the
most articulate thing a small state ever carved into a wall about being one.
