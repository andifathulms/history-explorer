In 1350 the most travelled man in the world came to Granada, and the king would
not see him.

Ibn Battuta had been to Mecca, Iraq, Persia, the Swahili coast, Anatolia, the
Golden Horde, Constantinople, India — where he sat as a judge for the sultan of
Delhi — the Maldives, Ceylon, Bengal, Sumatra and China, and had lived through
the Black Death at Damascus on the way home. He crossed from Gibraltar into what
was left of Muslim Spain, and his account of the last Islamic kingdom in Europe
is four paragraphs long.

## What he says about the city

> I then went to Granada, the chief city of Andalusia, which, for its structures
> and suburbs is unequalled in the whole world.

It is divided by the river, he writes, and besides the river there are many other
streams, and cisterns, gardens, orchards and palaces surrounding it on every
side. That is the whole description. A man who had written pages on Delhi and
Kilwa and the Maldives gives the Alhambra's city one sentence of praise and
moves on.

## What he says about the court

> The King of Granada was at this time Abu'l-Walid Yusuf ibn Nasir. I never met
> him, on account of a disease under which he then laboured. His noble and
> excellent mother, however, sent me some dinars for my support.

Yusuf I was the sultan who built the Comares palace and the Gate of Justice, and
he is the reason the Alhambra looks the way it does. He was assassinated four
years after this, and his son Muhammad V finished the work.

What Ibn Battuta records is a sick king who did not receive the most famous
traveller in Islam, and a queen mother who sent money to the visitor anyway. That
is a functioning court doing the correct thing with a diminished head, which is
roughly the Nasrid method for two and a half centuries, seen for a moment from
the doorway.

## The poet from the desert

The thing he actually wanted to write about was a young man he met among the
scholars of the city.

Abu Ja'far Ahmad ibn Ridwan al-Judhami had been brought up in a desert and had
never studied or given himself any trouble about learning, and yet produced poetry
so good as scarcely to be equalled by the most accomplished writers. Ibn Battuta
copies out a specimen, and the translator's English of it is Regency rather than
Andalusi, but the conceit survives:

> Friend, from whom 'tis pain to part, / Take thy station in my heart. / Through
> my eye, its lucid door, / View the structure o'er and o'er. / There enthroned
> thou'lt always see / Every chamber filled with thee. / But when from thee, with
> pain distrest, / I feel the void within my breast, / My vacant eyes too well
> declare / Their favourite inmate is not there. / But, when thy charms my
> spirits fill, / I close my lids to keep thee still.

Then he left, went down to Gibraltar, took ship to Ceuta, and was across into
Africa. From Granada he travelled to Marrakesh, then to Fez, and then straight
out into the Sahara for Mali.

## Why the brevity is the point

Granada in 1350 was not a frontier curiosity to a man of Ibn Battuta's world. It
was an ordinary Muslim city with a sultan, scholars, a mosque and a court that
paid its respects to travellers, and he treats it as such — one stop on a coastal
itinerary whose real destinations were elsewhere.

Nothing in the passage suggests that he thought he was visiting a survival. The
kingdom he passed through had a hundred and forty-two years left, and to a
Moroccan of the fourteenth century the interesting question about al-Andalus was
not whether it would last but who in Fez would be paying for its defence next
season.
