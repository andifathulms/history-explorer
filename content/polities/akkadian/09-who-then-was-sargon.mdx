---
title: Who, then, was Sargon?
drafted_from: king-1910

For most of the time since Akkad fell, Sargon existed only as a story.

The empire collapsed around 2150 BC. The name survived in Mesopotamian writing
for another two thousand years, but what survived was not a record of a reign;
it was a body of legend, omen and moral literature about a king who had once
ruled the world, copied and recopied by scribes who had never seen an Akkadian
inscription and who were working a millennium and a half after the events.

## How he came back

Leonard King, writing in 1910 as keeper of the Babylonian antiquities at the
British Museum, sets out the recovery in order, and the order is the point.

Sargon's name turned up first in explanatory religious and astrological texts
from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh — a figure in Assyrian heroic mythology,
called king of justice and proclaimer of favours. In 1867 Rawlinson announced
the famous *Legend of Sargon*, in which the king tells his own birth and
boyhood in the first person, and made the shrewd suggestion that the Assyrian
king Sargon, Sennacherib's father, may have been styled "the later Sargon" to
distinguish him from a hero of romance whose adventures were better known to
Assyrians than any history. George Smith translated the Legend in 1872, and
with it the *Omens of Sargon and Naram-Sin* — a genre in which a great king's
career is boiled down to a list of portents and their outcomes.

All of that is later tradition. The first object actually made in Sargon's own
kingdom to reach a museum with a royal name on it came much later: a cylinder
seal belonging to one of his officials, described in 1877, and then in 1883 a
mace-head dedicated at the temple of Shamash at Sippar, which King calls the
first actual inscription of that king to be found.

Two and a half thousand years of reputation, and then a piece of stone.

## The argument King could not settle

And then the awkwardness, which he states without flinching.

The contemporary inscriptions gave a royal name that was read at the time as
Shar-Gani-sharri. Whether that man was the Sargon of the legends was disputed
throughout the period King is describing. One scholar read the name differently
again and put him in the nineteenth century, three hundred years too late, and
took him for an earlier king of Akkad altogether. Others assumed the
identification as certain "in spite of some dissentients". Naram-Sin, by
contrast, presented no difficulty at all: his name was on a vase found at
Babylon and lost afterwards in the Tigris, and matched what Nabonidus had
written about him.

So the position in 1910, sixty years into the study of Mesopotamia, was that
Naram-Sin was solid and his grandfather might be two people.

## The first archaeologist

The other thread in that paragraph is the one worth ending on.

The reason Naram-Sin's identification was secure is that Nabonidus, the last
king of Babylon, had dug for his foundation deposit in the sixth century BC and
recorded what he found, including a date. He was excavating a building that was
already seventeen hundred years old when he came to it, in order to restore it
properly, and his inscription is one of the reasons a nineteenth-century
Englishman could put a number on an Akkadian king.

That is the actual afterlife of Akkad. Not a successor state — nothing
succeeded it — but a permanent position in other people's understanding of what
a king is for, maintained by scribes copying omens, by a Babylonian antiquarian
digging in his own ruins, and finally by philologists arguing over whether the
man in the legend and the man on the mace-head were the same person.
