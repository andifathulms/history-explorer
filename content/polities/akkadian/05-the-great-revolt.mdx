Naram-Sin inherited an empire built in two generations by his grandfather and
his uncle, and very nearly lost the whole of it in his first years on the
throne. A copper statue base found at Bassetki, north of where Akkad itself
is thought to have stood, carries his own account of what happened, cut in a
tone closer to relief than triumph:

> Naram-Sin, the mighty, king of Agade: when the four quarters together
> revolted against him, through the love which the goddess Ishtar showed him,
> he was victorious in nine battles in one year, and the kings whom they had
> raised against him, he captured.

## Who rose, and against what

This was not a border rebellion or a single disaffected governor. Later
copies of the events, and the inscriptions of the campaign itself, name a
coalition drawn from across the length of the empire's Mesopotamian core:
Iphur-Kish, a king raised up at Kish; Amar-Girid, raised at Uruk; a rebel
leader at Nippur; and cities further out — Kutha, Sippar, Kazallu among
them — joining what the inscriptions describe as "the four quarters," the
same phrase Akkadian kingship used to describe the whole world it claimed to
rule, turned back against the throne that claimed it.

Whatever the precise trigger, the shape of the crisis is legible without one:
a young king, newly succeeded to a throne two men before him had built and
held for barely two generations combined, faced nearly every major city his
family had conquered rising at once rather than waiting to test him
individually. An empire that recent had no deep reservoir of habitual
obedience to draw on. It had only the army Sargon and his sons had built,
and whether that army would hold for a third ruler in a row was, in these
opening years, a genuinely open question.

## Nine battles, one year

It did hold. Naram-Sin's own count — nine battles fought and won within a
single year, the rebel kings taken captive rather than left to regroup — is
the kind of claim royal inscriptions routinely inflate, but nothing in the
aftermath suggests exaggeration on the scale that would matter: no second
rising is recorded, no lost city stays lost, and Naram-Sin goes on to reign
for another three and a half decades over a state that, by every later
measure, is more secure rather than less.

The revolt outlived its own suppression in a different way. Old Babylonian
scribes centuries later were still copying literary accounts of it — *Naram-
Sin and the Enemy Hordes*, a text describing a coalition of seventeen kings
massed against him — numbers and details growing with retelling in exactly
the way a single dramatic year of survival tends to, once it has had a
thousand years to become a story rather than a memory.
