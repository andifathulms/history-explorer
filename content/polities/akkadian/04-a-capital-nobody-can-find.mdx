---
title: A capital nobody can find
drafted_from: van-de-mieroop-2015
phase: aside
---

Akkad, the city the empire is named for and ruled from, has never been located.
It is mentioned constantly in Mesopotamian texts for two thousand years and no
excavation has identified it; the likeliest explanations are that it lies under
alluvium, or under a later city, somewhere near modern Baghdad.

That absence sets the terms for everything else. The dynasty is known from
royal inscriptions, from administrative tablets at other sites, and from later
literary tradition — including the *Curse of Agade*, composed centuries
afterwards, which explains the empire's fall as divine punishment for Naram-Sin
desecrating a temple. That text is theology about a political event, written by
people with their own reasons, and it is one of the main narrative sources.

Modern accounts often add a climate explanation: a sharp arid episode around
2200 BC, argued from sediment cores, coinciding with the collapse. The
correlation is real and the causal claim is contested, and no source names
anybody who conquered Akkad.

So the capital is a place nobody can point at, the fall has no agent, and the
dates carry a century of slack. That is the evidential position for the first
empire in history, and it is worth stating plainly rather than writing around,
because everything asserted about Akkad rests on it.
