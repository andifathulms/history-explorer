title: A capital nobody can find
drafted_from: van-de-mieroop-2015
phase: aside
---

Akkad, the city the empire is named for and ruled from, has never been located.
It is mentioned constantly in Mesopotamian texts for two thousand years and no
excavation has identified it; the likeliest explanations are that it lies under
alluvium, or under a later city, somewhere near modern Baghdad.

That absence sets the terms for everything on this page. The dynasty is known
from royal inscriptions, from administrative tablets at other sites, and from
later literary tradition — including the *Curse of Agade*, composed centuries
afterwards, which explains the empire's fall as divine punishment for Naram-Sin
desecrating a temple. That text is theology about a political event, written by
people with their own reasons, and it is one of the main narrative sources.

Modern accounts often add a climate explanation: a sharp arid episode around 2200
BC, argued from sediment cores, coinciding with the collapse. The correlation is
real and the causal claim is contested, and this site records the ending as
`fragmentation` without an agent because that is what the record supports.

So this record's `capitals` field names a place nobody can point at, and its
turning point is dated by a convention with a century of slack in it. That is
the evidential position for the earliest polity in the corpus, and the site
prints the fields anyway because the alternative — leaving the page out — would
imply the corpus begins somewhere it does not.
