Shar-kali-sharri, Naram-Sin's son, spent his reign fighting on every frontier
at once and after him the king-list records a period it summarises with a
question: who was king, who was not king? Akkad held a shrinking territory for
another half-century under rulers about whom almost nothing is known, and
around 2154 BC it stops.

<Figure id="ak-brick" />

Nobody conquered Akkad. It came apart rather than falling to anyone, and
there are three explanations in circulation for how, none of which excludes
the others.

The first is the Gutians, a people from the Zagros, whom the Mesopotamian
tradition blames comprehensively. The blame is worth discounting: "Gutian" in
later Mesopotamian writing functions as a general term for highland barbarians
and is applied to almost anyone from that direction, and the evidence that they
brought the empire down rather than moved into a vacuum is thin.

The second is the *Curse of Agade*, a Sumerian literary composition written a
century or more afterwards, which explains the fall as divine punishment:
Naram-Sin, denied a favourable omen, sacked the temple of Enlil at Nippur, and
Enlil brought the Gutians down on him. It is one of the principal narrative
sources for this period and it is theology about a political event, composed by
people with their own reasons, and it is almost certainly wrong about
Naram-Sin, who is well attested as a builder of temples including that one.

<Figure id="ak-curse" />

The third is climate. A sharp arid episode around 2200 BC — the 4.2 kiloyear
event — is argued from sediment cores in the Gulf of Oman and elsewhere, and
coincides with disruption across the region. The correlation is real. The
causal claim is contested, the dating is not precise enough to tie it to a
reign, and the same event has been used to explain collapses in Egypt and the
Indus at the same time, which is either a significant finding or a sign that a
single explanation is doing too much work.

No single cause is named here because none can be, honestly, from what
survives: naming one would mean choosing between three arguments the
scholarship itself has not closed.

What Akkad left is the precedent. Every later Mesopotamian ruler who wanted to
describe a large state described it in Sargon's terms; his titles, his
grandson's divinity, and the memory of a single authority over the whole land
were still being invoked when Nabonidus dug for Naram-Sin's foundation deposits
seventeen hundred years later. The empire lasted about a hundred and eighty
years and remained useful to other people for two thousand.
