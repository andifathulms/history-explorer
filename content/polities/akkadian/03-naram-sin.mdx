---
title: The king who became a god
drafted_from: foster-2016
phase: peak
---

Sargon's grandson Naram-Sin ruled for about thirty-seven years and is the peak
on this record for two reasons, one military and one that had no precedent
anywhere.

Early in his reign most of the cities of Sumer and Akkad revolted at once. His
inscriptions say he fought nine battles in a single year and won all of them,
and captured the kings who had led the rising. That is this record's only
turning point, and the date attached to it is conventional in the way the first
chapter describes.

What happened afterwards is the thing. The citizens of Akkad, according to an
inscription he had cut, asked the gods to make Naram-Sin the god of their city,
and the gods agreed. He began writing his own name with the divine determinative
— the cuneiform sign that marks a word as the name of a god — and in the great
victory stele now in the Louvre he climbs a mountain over his defeated enemies
wearing the horned crown, which no king had worn before because it belonged to
gods.

This site's `legitimation` field on this page reads `divine-sanction` and
`conquest`, and the first value is being used more literally here than anywhere
else in the corpus. The rulebook glosses it as the mandate of heaven, divine
kingship, or a claim to be mahdi or imam — a right that comes from above rather
than from a lineage or an army. Naram-Sin's version is that the king is the
thing above.

He also took the title *king of the four quarters*, meaning the whole world, and
that title and the divine determinative were both reused by Mesopotamian rulers
for the next two thousand years. This corpus's Achaemenid record has a chapter
on the formula "by the favour of Ahuramazda I am king", which is the opposite
move made by people who knew this one; and its Neo-Assyrian record has kings
campaigning as the vice-regents of Ashur. The Akkadian innovation was the
maximal version, and nobody in Mesopotamia went that far again.

The empire reached its widest under him — from the Gulf to the Mediterranean and
into Anatolia, by the claims of the inscriptions, which are claims — and it
began coming apart under his son.
