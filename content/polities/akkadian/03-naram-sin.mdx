---
title: The king who became a god
drafted_from: foster-2016
phase: peak
---

Sargon's grandson Naram-Sin ruled for about thirty-seven years, and the empire
is at its height under him for two reasons, one military and one without
precedent anywhere.

Early in his reign most of the cities of Sumer and Akkad revolted at once. His
inscriptions say he fought nine battles in a single year and won all of them,
and captured the kings who had led the rising. The date is conventional, in the
way all these dates are.

What happened afterwards is the thing. The citizens of Akkad, according to an
inscription he had cut, asked the gods to make Naram-Sin the god of their city,
and the gods agreed. He began writing his own name with the divine
determinative — the cuneiform sign that marks a word as the name of a god — and
in the great victory stele now in the Louvre he climbs a mountain over his
defeated enemies wearing the horned crown, which no king had worn before
because it belonged to gods.

Rulers claiming a sanction from above are ordinary: the mandate of heaven,
divine kingship, a claim to be the awaited imam. All of those locate the
authority somewhere else and route it through the king. Naram-Sin's version is
that the king is the thing above.

He also took the title *king of the four quarters*, meaning the whole world,
and that title and the divine determinative were both reused by Mesopotamian
rulers for the next two thousand years. The Achaemenid formula "by the favour
of Ahuramazda I am king" is the opposite move, made by people who knew this
one; the Neo-Assyrian kings campaigning as the vice-regents of Ashur. The
Akkadian innovation was the maximal version, and nobody in Mesopotamia went
that far again.

The empire reached its widest under him — from the Gulf to the Mediterranean
and into Anatolia, by the claims of the inscriptions, which are claims — and it
began coming apart under his son.
