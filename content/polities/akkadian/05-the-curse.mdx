title: The curse of Agade
drafted_from: foster-2016

---

Shar-kali-sharri, Naram-Sin's son, spent his reign fighting on every frontier at
once and after him the king-list records a period it summarises with a question:
who was king, who was not king? Akkad held a shrinking territory for another
half-century under rulers about whom almost nothing is known, and around 2154 BC
it stops.

The site types the ending `fragmentation` with no agent named, and there are
three explanations in circulation for what happened, none of which excludes the
others.

The first is the Gutians, a people from the Zagros, whom the Mesopotamian
tradition blames comprehensively. The blame is worth discounting: "Gutian" in
later Mesopotamian writing functions as a general term for highland barbarians
and is applied to almost anyone from that direction, and the evidence that they
brought the empire down rather than moved into a vacuum is thin.

The second is the *Curse of Agade*, a Sumerian literary composition written a
century or more afterwards, which explains the fall as divine punishment:
Naram-Sin, denied a favourable omen, sacked the temple of Enlil at Nippur, and
Enlil brought the Gutians down on him. It is one of the principal narrative
sources for this period and it is theology about a political event, composed by
people with their own reasons, and it is almost certainly wrong about Naram-Sin,
who is well attested as a builder of temples including that one.

The third is climate. A sharp arid episode around 2200 BC — the 4.2 kiloyear
event — is argued from sediment cores in the Gulf of Oman and elsewhere, and
coincides with disruption across the region. The correlation is real. The causal
claim is contested, the dating is not precise enough to tie it to a reign, and
the same event has been used to explain collapses in Egypt and the Indus at the
same time, which is either a significant finding or a sign that a single
explanation is doing too much work.

The site records the ending as `fragmentation` without an agent because that is
what the record supports and because naming one would be choosing between three
arguments the scholarship has not closed.

What Akkad left is the precedent. Every later Mesopotamian ruler who wanted to
describe a large state described it in Sargon's terms; his titles, his grandson's
divinity, and the memory of a single authority over the whole land were still
being invoked when Nabonidus dug for Naram-Sin's foundation deposits seventeen
hundred years later. The empire lasted about a hundred and eighty years and was
useful for two millennia, which is the argument for having the earliest page in
the corpus be this one.
