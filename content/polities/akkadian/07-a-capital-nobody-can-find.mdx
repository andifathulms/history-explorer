---
title: A capital nobody can find
drafted_from: van-de-mieroop-2015
phase: aside
---

Akkad, the city the empire is named for and ruled from, has never been located.
It is mentioned constantly in Mesopotamian texts for two thousand years and no
excavation has identified it; the likeliest explanations are that it lies under
alluvium, or under a later city, somewhere near modern Baghdad.

That absence sets the terms for everything else. The dynasty is known from
royal inscriptions, from administrative tablets at other sites, and from later
literary tradition — including a Sumerian composition written a century or
more after the fall, which turns the empire's collapse into a story about
divine punishment. What that story explains, and what else has been proposed
in its place, is a question worth taking up properly once the fall itself is
being told; the point to establish here is only that a text this late, and
this interested in making a theological argument, is nevertheless one of the
main narrative sources for what happened.

So the capital is a place nobody can point at and the dates carry a century
of slack. That is the evidential position for the first empire in history,
and it is worth stating plainly rather than writing around, because
everything asserted about Akkad rests on it.
