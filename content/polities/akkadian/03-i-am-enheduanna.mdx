Sargon's appointment of his own daughter as high priestess of the moon god
at Ur was, by itself, an ordinary act of statecraft — a political office
dressed as a religious one, of a kind rulers went on installing relatives
in for the next four thousand years. What makes Enheduanna worth a chapter
of her own is not the appointment. It is what she is recorded as having
done with it.

## A name attached to a voice

A body of Sumerian religious poetry survives naming her as its author,
including hymns to the temples of forty-two cities and a longer composition
known from its opening words, *Nin-me-sara* — "The Exaltation of Inanna" —
in which the narrator describes being driven from her office by a rebel
who seized power at Ur, and appeals to the goddess Inanna to restore her.
Partway through, the text names its own speaker directly:

> I am the high priestess, I am Enheduanna.

No comparable moment survives from anywhere earlier in the written record.
Royal inscriptions before this point speak in a king's voice about a
king's deeds, composed by scribes who leave no trace of their own
identity. This is a named individual, describing her own situation in her
own first person, in a text copied for well over a thousand years
afterward — well over a hundred manuscript copies survive from schools at
Nippur, Ur and elsewhere, most of them centuries later than Enheduanna's
own lifetime.

## What the ground gave back

Confirmation that a person by this name actually held the office comes
from outside the poetry entirely. Leonard Woolley, excavating at Ur in
1927, recovered a broken alabaster disk that names her directly: "wife of
Nanna, daughter of Sargon," standing in a scene of worship. Seals bearing
her name and title were found alongside it. Whoever wrote the poetry,
somebody named Enheduanna certainly held this office, in this city, in
this reign — the disk fixes the person to the record independently of
whatever is claimed about her pen.

## What is not settled

Whether Enheduanna herself composed the works copied in her name is
disputed, and the terms of the dispute are worth stating rather than
smoothing over. The surviving manuscripts are Old Babylonian school
copies, made several centuries after she would have lived, with none of
the linguistic traces an original Old Sumerian composition would show.
Some Assyriologists treat the attribution as essentially reliable; others
argue that naming a text after a revered figure of the deep past was
itself a way of investing it with authority, regardless of who actually
composed it centuries closer to when the copies were made.

Both readings agree on the same underlying fact, which is what actually
matters here: within a few centuries of Sargon's reign, Mesopotamian
scribal tradition had settled on his daughter, by name, as the person a
major body of temple poetry belonged to — the first time authorship of
that kind attaches to anyone, real claim or invented one, anywhere in the
written record.
