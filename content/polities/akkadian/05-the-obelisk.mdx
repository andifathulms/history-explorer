De Morgan's first season at Susa, in the winter of 1897-8, turned up a four-sided
block of stone carrying sixty-nine columns of Akkadian. It is not a victory
monument and not a law code. It is a property deed, and it belongs to Sargon's
son.

## What it records

Each face of the obelisk deals with one tract of farmland. The first is the Field
of Baz, three estates near the city of Dur-Sin; the second the Field of
Baraz-sirim, near Kish, which was Manishtushu's capital; the third three estates
together called the Marsh of Ninkharsag, near Marad; the fourth the fields of
Shad-Bitkim and Zimanak near a city whose name is still uncertain.

The text is enormous because of what it insists on recording. For every estate it
gives the size, the value and the position. It names every proprietor the land
was bought from, with their parentage. It names the associates who held an
interest — mostly relatives who had put capital into cultivating or improving the
ground. It names the overseers who were dispossessed, and the overseers installed
in their place, and repeats the incoming names on all four faces before the
purchase formula.

## Bought, not taken

The thing worth noticing, and the reason a document like this exists at all, is
that the king did not confiscate.

He bought. The area of each estate was measured first. Its value was reckoned in
grain and then converted to silver at a fixed rate — one *bur* of land counted as
sixty *gur* of grain, or one *mana* of silver. On top of the purchase price the
owners were paid a further tenth, or three-twentieths. And beyond that they
received presents from the king — animals, garments, vessels — scaled according
to the recipient's rank and to how large a share of the property had been theirs.

King's summary is that Manishtushu acquired the land legally, *as though he were
merely a private citizen or large land-owner*.

That is a considerable statement about the first empire. A king who could
certainly have taken the fields instead assembled a royal estate across four
cities by measured valuation, above-market payment, compensation to secondary
interest-holders and a written record naming every party — and then had the whole
transaction carved on stone and set up in a temple, which is what you do with a
document you want to remain enforceable after you are dead.

## Where it was found

It was not found in Babylonia. It was at Susa, because the Elamites carried it
there as plunder.

They took Hammurabi's law stele in the same way, and it was dug up on the same
site. The two most informative Mesopotamian monuments about how a state handled
property and justice both survive because a foreign army thought them worth
stealing, and both re-entered the world through French excavations in Iran within
four years of each other.

There is no version of Mesopotamian history that is not partly a history of what
Elam took.
