The name is not a name. Sharru-kin means "the king is legitimate" or "the true
king", and a ruler who has to say that in his own throne-name is telling you
something about how he got there.

The legend says he was a gardener's foster-son, set adrift as a baby in a reed
basket sealed with bitumen and drawn out of the river — a story that reaches the
Hebrew Bible in another form a millennium and a half later — and rose to be
cupbearer to the king of Kish before taking the throne himself. The text that
records it is centuries later than Sargon and is a literary composition, not a
document; what it establishes is that the tradition remembered him as a man
without a claim.

What he did is better attested. Around 2334 BC, by the conventional chronology,
he took the Sumerian city-states of the south — Uruk, Ur, Lagash, Umma — and
then kept going: north up the Euphrates towards Syria, east into Elam and the
Zagros. He ruled it from a capital called Akkad, which has never been located,
and which is the subject of this record's fourth chapter.

This corpus opens with him. Akkad is the earliest polity on the site by seven
hundred years, which makes this the page where several of the site's habits get
their first test.

The dates are the first. The chronology of the third millennium is a
reconstruction with an uncertainty of roughly a century in either direction —
Assyriologists work with a high, middle and low chronology and the middle one is
a convention rather than a finding. Every date on this page is conventional, and
the site prints them because a cited convention is not an invention, and says
here that they are conventions.

The second is the reach figure of 650,000 square kilometres, which is the
smallest in the corpus and the oldest. Placing it in a ranked table beside the
Mongol empire's twenty-four million is arithmetically simple and historically
empty, which is what the era-normalised scale on the rankings view is for: a
share of the world under any state's control in 2300 BC is a different quantity
from the same area in 1300 AD.
