title: The first, conventionally

Sargon took the Sumerian city-states of southern Mesopotamia around 2334 BC and
ruled them from a capital, Akkad, that has never been located. The state lasted
roughly a hundred and eighty years.

It is conventionally called the first empire, and the qualifier matters: it is
first by a definition — one authority ruling multiple previously independent
polities of different language and identity, through appointed officials rather
than tribute — that was constructed afterwards, partly to make it first. What is
not in doubt is that it was treated as the precedent. Mesopotamian kings for the
next two thousand years described themselves in Sargon's terms, and Naram-Sin's
claim to divinity and to the title king of the four quarters were reused for
centuries.

This is the earliest polity on this site, and the reach figure — 650,000 square
kilometres — is the smallest. Placing it in the same ranking as the Mongol
empire's twenty-four million is arithmetically straightforward and historically
close to meaningless, which is what the era-normalised scale on the rankings view
exists to address: a share of the world under any state's control in 2300 BC is a
different quantity from the same area in 1300 AD.
