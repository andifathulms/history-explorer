Seleucus founded a capital on the west bank of the Tigris and named it after
himself, three centuries before Christ. The Arabs, arriving nine hundred years
later, found the place under a different name and called it something else
again: Al-Mada'in, the Cities, plural, because by then Seleucia and Ctesiphon
had run together into one sprawl on both banks and nobody could sensibly call
it one town.

That is the Seleucid afterlife in a phrase. The dynasty is gone, the language
is gone, and the sites are still the sites.

## What the Parthians and Sasanians did with them

Neither of the empires that took Mesopotamia from the Seleucids built a new
capital in empty country. They built next to Seleucia. Ctesiphon went up on the
opposite bank and became the seat of the Parthian and then the Sasanian kings,
and when Ardashir had taken the empire he refounded the western city under his
own name — Bih-Ardashir, the good town of King Ardashir, which the Arabs
shortened to Bahurasir. The successor of Seleucus's capital is a Persian town
called after the man from Fars, standing on the same bank, opposite the palace
of the kings who had beaten the Parthians.

There is a stranger episode still. In AD 540 Khusraw I took Antioch — the
Seleucid capital of Syria, the other great foundation of the dynasty, together
with the Seleucia that stood on the Orontes — and did what Persian monarchs did
with a
captured population: he moved it. The Antiochenes were resettled in a new
suburb across the Tigris from the ruins of the original Seleucia, and the
suburb was called Rumiyah, the Roman town, and was reported to have been laid
out on the plan of Antioch itself.

So in the sixth century AD there were, within sight of one another on the
Tigris: a city founded by Seleucus, a city refounded by the Sasanian who ended
the Parthians, and a copy of the Seleucid capital of Syria populated by
deported Syrians. Eight hundred and fifty years after Ipsus, the map of that
stretch of river was still being drawn around Seleucid foundations by kings who
had never heard a word of Greek spoken at court.

## And then it emptied out

The end is undramatic, which by now should be no surprise.

When al-Mansur began building Baghdad, seven leagues upriver in the eighth
century, he proposed to demolish the great arched hall of the Sasanian palace
and cart the materials north. His Persian vizier Khalid the Barmecide argued
against it and lost the argument; the caliph was obstinate. What saved the arch
was arithmetic. When the order came to be carried out, taking the thing down
proved to cost more than the bricks were worth, and it was left standing. It is
standing now.

Baghdad drew everything to itself. In the tenth century Al-Mada'in was still a
small populous town with a Friday mosque from the conquest and markets built of
burnt brick. By the fourteenth, Mustawfi reports both Al-Mada'in and Rumiyah as
uninhabited ruins, with only the villages on the west bank still holding
people.

Seventeen centuries after Seleucus chose the site, what is on it is a field, an
arch, and a name in the plural. The dynasty that could not find a claim to the
land, and answered by covering it with its own family's names, was in the end
outlived by the places rather than by the names — except in one case, which is
the era. The count that began with a general coming back to Babylon with eight
hundred men had no ancestors in it and needed none, and it is still running.
