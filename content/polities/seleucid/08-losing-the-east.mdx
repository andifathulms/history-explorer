Around 250 BC the satrap of Bactria, Diodotus, stopped acknowledging the king
and struck coins in his own name. Within a few years the Parni, a nomadic people
from the steppe east of the Caspian, moved into the satrapy of Parthia and took
it, and out of that seizure came the dynasty that would rule Iran for the next
four and a half centuries.

<Figure id="se-diodotus" />

Neither event was a catastrophe at the time and neither was treated as one. The
Seleucids were fighting the Ptolemies over Coele-Syria, which was closer, richer
and contested by an equal; the eastern provinces were four months away, and the
imperial answer to a satrap who stopped remitting was an expedition when one
could be spared.

Antiochus III spared one. Between 212 and 205 he conducted the great *anabasis* —
east through Media, into Parthia, over the Elburz, into Bactria where he
besieged Bactra for two years, then over the Hindu Kush to renew the treaty with
an Indian king, then back through Arachosia and Carmania and up the Gulf. He
obtained submissions, tribute, elephants and the title Great King, and he
changed nothing structural: Diodotus' successor kept Bactria and the Parthians
kept Parthia, both as acknowledged clients.

That is the shape of the loss. The empire never lost a decisive eastern battle,
and it never got the east back either; it converted secession into vassalage and
then the vassalage lapsed. It was not a conquest at any stage. It was a chain of
Greek cities that stopped being connected to one another, beginning at the far
end.

Then Antiochus turned west, met Rome, and lost at Magnesia in 190. After the
indemnity there was no money for anabases. Mesopotamia went to the Parthians in
the 140s; a Seleucid army sent to recover it in 130 was destroyed and its king
killed; and the empire's eastern frontier ended up at the Euphrates, where some
version of it would sit for the next six hundred years.
