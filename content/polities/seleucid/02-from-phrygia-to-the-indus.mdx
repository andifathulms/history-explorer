Seleucus got his kingdom back with about eight hundred men and ended up ruling
more of Asia than anyone since Alexander. Appian, writing his *Syrian Wars* four
centuries later, gives both the scale of it and the way it was assembled, and the
two do not match in the way you would expect.

## Getting Babylon

He had been satrap of Babylon and had lost it to Antigonus, fleeing to Egypt.
Ptolemy sent him back with a small force — a thousand foot and a few hundred
horse — and with that he took Babylonia, and *in this way Seleucus became king of
Babylonia*.

Media came next, and Appian's phrasing is worth keeping: he acquired that kingdom
*slaying with his own hand in battle* the satrap Antigonus had left there.

Then Ipsus in 301, where Antigonus commanded in person and fought in person
although he was over eighty, and was killed. The victors divided his territory,
and Syria from the Euphrates to the sea, with inland Phrygia, fell to Seleucus.

## The list

What follows in Appian is not a narrative but an inventory, and the inventory is
the point:

> Always lying in wait for the neighbouring nations, strong in arms and persuasive
> in council, he acquired Mesopotamia, Armenia, the so-called Seleucid Cappadocia,
> the Persians, Parthians, Bactrians, Arabs, Tapyri, Sogdiani, Arachotes,
> Hyrcanians, and other adjacent peoples that had been subdued by Alexander, as
> far as the river Indus.

*Strong in arms and persuasive in council.* Appian puts the two in one clause and
does not say which did more, because in this empire they were the same
instrument: most of these peoples had been Alexander's, had spent twenty years
deciding whose they now were, and were acquired by a mixture of marching and
argument.

The boundaries, Appian says, were the most extensive in Asia after Alexander's.

## The Indian frontier, settled by marriage

He crossed the Indus and went to war with Chandragupta, and Appian describes the
outcome in a single clause that is one of the most consequential in the book:
they came to an understanding with each other *and contracted a marriage
relationship*.

That is the eastern limit of the Hellenistic world being fixed by treaty between
two men who had both taken their kingdoms in the confusion after Alexander. The
Seleucids gave up the Indus provinces and got five hundred war elephants, which
turned up at Ipsus.

## An empire named after a family

The other method of possession was nomenclature, and Seleucus used it on an
industrial scale. Appian counts the foundations: sixteen Antiochs after his
father, five Laodiceas after his mother, nine after himself, four after his
wives — three Apameas and a Stratonicea.

The effect, Appian notes, is that towns across Syria and the barbarian regions of
upper Asia carry Greek and Macedonian names: Beroea, Edessa, Pella, Amphipolis,
Larissa, Chalcis, Arethusa, Apollonia; in Parthia a Hecatompylos and a Calliope;
in India an Alexandropolis; in Scythia an Alexandreschata.

## The Magi and the hour

The best story Appian tells about any of it concerns Seleucia-on-the-Tigris, and
it is a story about local people trying to stop the empire and failing.

The Magi were ordered to name the propitious day and hour to begin the
foundations. They falsified the hour — *because they did not want to have such a
stronghold built against themselves.*

Seleucus waited in his tent for the appointed moment and the army stood ready. And
then, suddenly, at the true hour, the men seemed to hear a voice ordering them on,
and sprang to work with such alacrity that the heralds sent to stop them could
not.

Afterwards, granted immunity, the Magi confessed:

> That which is fated, O King, whether it be for better or worse, neither man nor
> city can change... We feared lest it should be a stronghold against ourselves,
> and falsified the appointed time. Destiny is stronger than crafty Magi or an
> unsuspecting king.

They were right about what the city was for. Seleucia-on-the-Tigris drew the
population of Babylon into itself and became the great city of Mesopotamia for
four hundred years — a Greek foundation planted forty miles from Babylon,
deliberately, to replace it.
