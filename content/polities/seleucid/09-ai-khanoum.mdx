At the confluence of the Oxus and the Kokcha, in what is now northern
Afghanistan, French archaeologists excavated a city with a gymnasium, a theatre
seating several thousand, Corinthian capitals, and an inscription recording that
a man named Klearchos had copied the Delphic maxims and brought them from Delphi
to set up in the sanctuary. It is roughly four thousand kilometres from Delphi.

<Figure id="ai-khanoum-capital" />

Ai-Khanoum is the Seleucid settlement policy made visible. Greek and Macedonian
colonists were planted across the empire in dozens of foundations, holding land
in exchange for military obligation, running their own civic institutions, and
functioning as garrisons, markets and administrative anchors at once. Where the
network held, the empire held.

It also shows the limit. The city was abandoned around 145 BC, after Bactria had
seceded and nomadic incursions from the north had begun, and the Greek presence
in Central Asia continued afterwards as a series of increasingly local kingdoms
minting increasingly Indian-looking coins. Distance was the enemy: a Greek city
on the Oxus depended on a chain of such cities reaching back to the
Mediterranean, and when the chain broke the far end did not survive as Greek for
long.

The Buddhist sculpture of Gandhara — Greek drapery, Greek proportion, an
artistic vocabulary from the Aegean applied to Indian subject matter — is the
residue of
that failure. It outlived the political project that carried it east by several
centuries, and it is what a chain of Greek cities leaves behind after it stops
being a chain.
