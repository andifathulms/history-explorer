---
title: Governing Iran in Greek
drafted_from: chi-3
phase: contraction
---

Seleucus took the largest and most difficult share of Alexander's inheritance:
Syria, Mesopotamia, the Iranian plateau and, briefly, the eastern approaches to
India, which he traded to Chandragupta Maurya for five hundred war elephants.

The structural problem was that a Macedonian-Greek ruling class of a few tens of
thousands had to govern Iranian and Mesopotamian populations of many millions.
The answer was urban: dozens of Greek city foundations planted as garrisons,
administrative centres and settlement points, from Antioch on the Mediterranean
to Ai-Khanoum on the Oxus. It worked in Syria and Mesopotamia and progressively
failed further east, where distance made the cities isolated rather than
connected.

Bactria seceded around 250 BC and the Parthians took Iran across the following
century. By the time Rome finished the dynasty in 63 BC the Seleucid kingdom was
a piece of northern Syria. The empire is the clearest case on this site of a
polity that contracted continuously for two hundred years without ever being
decisively beaten.
