Most of what survives about the empire's lost provinces comes from Greek
writers looking at the Seleucids from Rome's side of the story, or from the
dry attrition of administrative silence — a satrap who stops sending letters.
Judaea is the one province whose loss is told by the people who did the
losing, in their own book, written a generation after the events by somebody
who had no interest in being fair to the king.

## What Antiochus did

Antiochus IV needed money and a compliant Jerusalem, and he had both problems
solved for him by rival claimants to the high priesthood who outbid each other
for his backing. He sold the office twice in a few years, to men the book calls
Jason and then Menelaus, and when a false report of his death reached Jerusalem
and the city moved against his man, he took it as revolt.

He came in person, and 1 Maccabees does not soften what happened next. He
entered the sanctuary and took the golden altar, the lampstand and all its
vessels, the table of the showbread, the cups and censers of gold, the veils
and the crowns, and stripped the gold ornament from the front of the temple; he
took the hidden treasures he found, and carried it all away to his own country,
*having made a great massacre, and spoken very proudly*. Two years later he
sent a tax collector who repeated the massacre on a Sabbath, when the city
would not defend itself, and then fortified the city of David as a garrison
over the temple.

Then the decree. Every nation in the kingdom was to become one people, and each
was to abandon its own laws. Sacrifice was forbidden in the sanctuary and
required to idols instead; circumcision was forbidden on pain of death; copies
of the law were torn up and burned; and an altar to Zeus was set up on top of
the temple's own altar, on the twenty-fifth of the month, and unclean sacrifice
offered on it. The book calls it *the abomination of desolation* and gives the
date.

## What that cost people specifically

Most of the chapter that describes the persecution is a list of individuals,
which is not how evidence about the empire's other lost provinces usually
survives.

Two women who had circumcised their children were led round the city with the
infants hanging at their breasts and then thrown down from the wall. People who
kept the Sabbath in secret in caves were found and burned where they hid,
*because they would not defile themselves*. The book states this as fact and
does not pause on it, which is itself informative about how ordinary the
information had become to write down.

## The revolt

Mattathias, a priest at Modein, was ordered to make the required sacrifice
first, as an example. He refused publicly, killed the Jew who stepped forward
to comply in his place and the king's officer overseeing it, pulled down the
altar, and fled to the hills with his sons, calling out that whoever was
zealous for the law should follow him.

What follows is guerrilla war conducted by a family, in terrain they knew,
against an empire that could not spare the force it would have taken to finish
it — the same problem Antiochus III's anabasis had solved for Bactria by
negotiating rather than conquering, except here nobody on the Seleucid side was
willing to negotiate the actual demand away until the demand had already cost
them the province. Mattathias died within a year. His son Judas, called
Maccabaeus, beat successive Seleucid columns sent against him, retook the
temple in 164 and purified it — the origin of Hanukkah — and the fighting went
on, with Roman recognition sought and eventually granted to his brothers, until
Simon's own settlement of 140, the constitutional document of the Hasmonean
state that resulted.

## Why this loss reads differently

Every other Seleucid province had to be reconstructed from silence or from a
Greek source with its own interests. This one is recorded by name, by date, by
the specific vessels taken from the sanctuary and the specific women thrown
from the wall, because the people who survived it kept the count and had
every reason to keep it precisely.

It took eight years to fail to suppress, and it produced something the empire's
other lost provinces did not: a state that told its own founding as a refusal
rather than a secession, a text still read for reasons that have nothing to do
with the Seleucids at all, and a festival still kept, twenty-two centuries
later, by people who trace no other part of their history back to this king.
