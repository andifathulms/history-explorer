title: What a Greek city was for
drafted_from: chi-3
phase: aside
---

The city foundations were the empire's administrative machinery and its military
reserve at once, and the second function is the one that kept the dynasty alive.

Settlers — Macedonian and Greek, and later others enrolled on the same terms —
were given plots of land in and around a foundation on condition of military
service. The holding was heritable so long as the obligation was met. When the
king called, the katoikoi turned out as the core of the phalanx, and between
campaigns they farmed, voted in their own assemblies and worshipped their own
gods.

Land for service is one of the oldest arrangements there is — the Mitanni had a
version of it a thousand years earlier, and Byzantium, the Seljuks and the whole
apparatus of the iqta' would have versions of it long afterwards — but the
Seleucid and Ptolemaic ones are the same answer to the same question, adopted
within a decade of each other by men who had served in the same army. How does a
small conquering elite garrison an enormous conquered country without paying for
a standing army in cash? It settles the army on the country and lets the country
feed it.

Hired troops were the other half and were used continuously rather than
exceptionally. Seleucid armies at Raphia and Magnesia contained Greek, Thracian,
Galatian, Arab and Iranian contingents on wages, and paying for them is a
substantial part of why Magnesia's indemnity was ruinous.

What the system could not do was reach. A katoikia works if it is one link in a
chain of them running back to the centre, and the empire's eastern chain broke.
The next chapter is about that, and the one after it is about the far end of the
chain after it broke.
