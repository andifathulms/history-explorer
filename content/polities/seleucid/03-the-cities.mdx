title: What a Greek city was for
drafted_from: chi-3

The city foundations were the empire's administrative machinery and its military
reserve at once, and this record's `military_basis` field is coded from them.

Settlers — Macedonian and Greek, and later others enrolled on the same terms —
were given plots of land in and around a foundation on condition of military
service. The holding was heritable so long as the obligation was met. When the
king called, the katoikoi turned out as the core of the phalanx, and between
campaigns they farmed, voted in their own assemblies and worshipped their own
gods.

This site codes that `land-grant`, and the value is the same one it carries for
the Ptolemaic kleruchy in Egypt, for the Mitanni maryannu a thousand years
earlier, for the Byzantine and Seljuk arrangements, and for the iqta' that
half the Islamic corpus rests on. The Seleucid and Ptolemaic versions are the
two halves of one Macedonian answer to one problem — how a small conquering
elite garrisons an enormous conquered country without paying for a standing army
in cash — and they were adopted within a decade of each other by men who had
served together.

Mercenaries are the second value and were used continuously, which is
unremarkable for the period and is why the corpus records it as a basis rather
than as an event: Seleucid armies at Raphia and Magnesia contained Greek,
Thracian, Galatian, Arab and Iranian hired contingents, and paying for them is a
substantial part of why Magnesia's indemnity was ruinous.

What the system could not do was reach. A katoikia works if it is one link in a
chain of them running back to the centre, and the empire's eastern chain broke.
The next chapter is about that, and the one after it is about the far end of the
chain after it broke.
