title: The last piece of Syria
drafted_from: chi-3

After Magnesia the dynasty had a hundred and twenty-five years left and spent
most of them fighting itself.

The pattern from about 150 BC is continuous dynastic war: rival Seleucids, some
of them genuine and some of them produced by Ptolemaic Egypt or by Rome or by
the cities, holding pieces of Syria against each other, with the eastern
provinces gone, Judaea independent, and the Parthians on this site's next record
taking Mesopotamia. Demetrius II was captured by the Parthians in 139 and held
for ten years. Antiochus VII went east to recover Iran in 130, won two battles,
and was killed with his army the following year — the last serious Seleucid
attempt to be an empire.

By the 90s there were sometimes four claimants at once in a kingdom the size of
Lebanon. In 83 the Syrians invited Tigranes of Armenia to take it, which he did
and held for fourteen years — this corpus's Greater Armenia record is about the
man who accepted, and his fourteen years in Antioch are on that page as the far
end of an empire that lasted fifteen.

Pompey ended it in 64. He declined to recognise the surviving claimant, annexed
Syria as a Roman province, and the dynasty stopped without a battle.

This record's own first chapter calls it the clearest case on the site of a
polity that contracted continuously for two hundred years without ever being
decisively beaten, and that judgement holds after five more chapters. There is no
Seleucid catastrophe. There is Magnesia, which cost Anatolia and a great deal of
money; there is the loss of the east, which happened while nobody was looking;
and there is a hundred and fifty years of a family that could not stop producing
claimants.

What it left is on four other pages of this corpus. The Parthians and then the
Sasanians governed Mesopotamia through cities the Seleucids had planted. The
Ai-Khanoum chapter traces the Greek presence in Central Asia to the Kushan
record's Gandharan sculpture. The Maccabean revolt produced a Jewish state and a
body of literature. And the era they invented is still counting.
