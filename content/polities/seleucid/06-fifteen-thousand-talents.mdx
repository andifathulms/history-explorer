Twenty years after the anabasis, the same king signed a treaty at Apamea that
took away his navy, his elephants, half of Asia Minor and the next twelve years
of his revenue. Appian preserves the terms, and they are worth reading in full
because they are the most thorough dismantling of a great power in the ancient
record that did not involve a conquest.

## The lecture

The terms were delivered by Scipio, and Appian gives the speech, which is
essentially a moral character study of the defeated king.

> The grasping nature of Antiochus has been the cause of his present and past
> misfortunes. While he was the possessor of a vast empire, which the Romans did
> not object to, he seized Coele-Syria... Then he invaded Europe, which did not
> concern him, subjugated Thrace, fortified the Chersonese, and rebuilt
> Lysimacheia. He passed thence into Greece and took away the liberty of the
> people whom the Romans had lately freed.

Even after Thermopylae, Scipio says, he did not give up his grabbing propensity;
he did not seek peace until the Romans had crossed the Hellespont; he rejected
the terms offered; he raised another army and fought on *determined to come to
an engagement with his betters*, until he plunged into this calamity.

Rome, he adds, would be entitled to punish him more severely, but is not
accustomed to abuse its own prosperity.

## The terms

Then the list.

Abandon Europe altogether, and all of Asia this side of the Taurus. Surrender
every elephant, and keep none in future. Surrender ships, and in future keep only
the number allowed — twelve warships, enough to police his own subjects, and more
only if he is attacked himself. Give twenty hostages of the consul's choosing;
among them was Antiochus, the king's younger son. Surrender all prisoners and
deserters. Restore to Eumenes of Pergamum what had been taken from his father.

And pay. Five hundred Euboic talents immediately, two thousand five hundred more
when the Senate ratified, and **twelve thousand over twelve years, each yearly
instalment delivered in Rome.**

Fifteen thousand talents. The annual instalments had to be carried to the
creditor's capital.

The written treaty added the details left indefinite: the boundary fixed at the
promontories of Calycadnus and Sarpedonium, beyond which he might not sail for
purposes of war; no recruiting mercenaries in Roman territory and no sheltering
fugitives from it; the hostages exchanged every third year — except the king's
son, who stayed.

It was engraved on bronze and deposited in the Capitol, where such treaties were
kept.

## What it actually removed

Look at the list as an inventory of capability rather than of humiliation.

The elephants were the dynasty's strategic arm and had been since Seleucus got
five hundred of them from Chandragupta; Antiochus had refreshed the stock in
India twenty years earlier. Gone, permanently, by treaty.

The fleet had made the Aegean coast and the Levantine ports usable as a single
system. Reduced to twelve hulls.

Asia Minor beyond the Taurus was where the Greek cities and much of the money
were. Gone.

And fifteen thousand talents, payable annually to Rome, out of a revenue that had
just lost its richest province.

A state cannot be a great power without ships, elephants, Anatolia and money, and
the treaty removed all four in one document — without Rome occupying a single
acre of what was left.

## Afterwards

Antiochus went east to find money and was killed, according to the tradition,
robbing a temple in Elymais.

The dynasty lasted another century and a quarter and never again fought a war it
chose. What is usually described as Seleucid decline begins here, in a set of
clauses, and the clauses are more informative than any battle: the empire was not
defeated out of existence. It was contractually reduced, and then it declined
because the terms of the contract made anything else impossible.
