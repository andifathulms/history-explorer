title: The last piece of Syria

After Magnesia the dynasty had a hundred and twenty-five years left and spent
most of them fighting itself.

The pattern from about 150 BC is continuous dynastic war: rival Seleucids, some
of them genuine and some of them produced by Ptolemaic Egypt or by Rome or by
the cities, holding pieces of Syria against each other, with the eastern
provinces gone, Judaea independent, and the Parthians taking Mesopotamia. Demetrius II was captured by the Parthians in 139 and held
for ten years. Antiochus VII went east to recover Iran in 130, won two battles,
and was killed with his army the following year — the last serious Seleucid
attempt to be an empire.

By the 90s there were sometimes four claimants at once in a kingdom the size of
Lebanon. In 83 the Syrians invited Tigranes of Armenia to take it, which he did and held
for fourteen years — an Armenian king in Antioch, invited in by the inhabitants,
because a foreign occupation was preferable to another round of the family's
own.

Pompey ended it in 63. He declined to recognise the surviving claimant, annexed
Syria as a Roman province, and the dynasty stopped without a battle.

It is difficult to find the catastrophe. Nearly two centuries of continuous
contraction, and no single day on which the empire was destroyed:
there is Magnesia, which cost Anatolia and a ruinous quantity of money; there is
the loss of the east, which happened while nobody was looking; and there is a
century and a half of a family that could not stop producing claimants. A state
can be beaten to death slowly enough that no historian can name the blow.

What it left, other people used. The Parthians and then the Sasanians governed
Mesopotamia through cities the Seleucids had planted, and Seleucia-on-the-Tigris
sat opposite Ctesiphon for centuries. The Greek presence the foundations put
into Central Asia turned into the sculpture of Gandhara. Judaea's own revolt,
described earlier, produced a state and a body of literature that is still
read. And the era they invented, counting from a general's return to Babylon
with eight hundred men, is still counting.
