title: Making the land mean something
drafted_from: kosmin-2014
phase: peak
---

Kosmin's argument, and the reason this page is drafted from him rather than only
from a volume published in 1983, is that the Seleucid problem was ideological
before it was military, and that the dynasty's characteristic activities are
all attempts to solve it.

They founded cities, in numbers no other Hellenistic dynasty approached — dozens
of them, from Antioch and Seleucia-on-the-Tigris to Ai-Khanoum on the Oxus,
planted as garrisons and markets and administrative anchors. And they named
them: Seleucia, Antioch, Laodicea, Apamea, over and over, after the founder, his
father, his mother and his wife, so that a traveller crossing the empire in any
direction passed through the dynasty's family repeatedly. A landscape that
belonged to nobody in particular was overwritten with one that belonged to them.

They moved constantly. The Seleucid king was rarely in a capital; the *anabasis*
— the royal progress east through the provinces, receiving submissions,
appointing, campaigning and being seen — was a governing instrument rather than
an emergency measure, and Antiochus III's seven-year version of it is the reason
he is the peak ruler on this record.

They negotiated their edges rather than claiming everything. The treaty with
Chandragupta gave away territory Alexander had taken, in exchange for something
usable, and Kosmin reads the frontier arrangements and the commissioned
geography as an attempt to define a bounded and therefore defensible thing
rather than an unbounded and therefore fictional one.

And they counted from a fixed point, which is the subject of this page's first
turning point and is the most durable thing they did. The Seleucid Era runs
continuously from 312 BC with no restarts — the first such count anywhere, a
century and a half before Rome had one and eight hundred years before the hijri
calendar. A dynasty that could not date by the reigns of ancestors it did not
have dated by arithmetic instead, and the arithmetic outlived the empire by two
thousand years: Syriac Christians still use it.
