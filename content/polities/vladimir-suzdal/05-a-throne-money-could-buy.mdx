By the early fourteenth century, the contest for the Suzerain throne had
narrowed to two junior branches of Vsevolod's own line: the princes of
Moscow, an appanage so minor it had not even existed as a separate
holding under Vsevolod's own sons, and the princes of Tver, carved from
the old appanage of Pereiaslavl and considerably better placed by
seniority and reputation alike. On paper, Tver's claim was the stronger
one. In practice, the two branches went about pursuing the throne in
almost opposite ways, and only one of those ways actually worked.

## Valor against cunning

Tver's princes met the situation as men still holding to an older idea of
what a Rus prince owed his own people. Early in the century, Alexander
of Tver urged his fellow princes to stand together against Tartar
demands rather than submit to them - counsel he gave from hiding at
Pskov, having just joined his own citizens of Tver in massacring a party
of Mongol emissaries sent to the city. Moscow's princes never
entertained anything like open resistance. They saw, correctly, that the
Horde could be managed far more reliably through steady tribute and
flattery than through defiance, and Ivan Kalita in particular made
himself a fixture at the Khan's own court - never arriving without gifts,
and consequently never leaving without favor.

## A punishment carried out with total thoroughness

That favor turned decisive when the Khan entrusted Kalita with punishing
Tver for its own resistance. Kalita executed the assignment so
completely that he and his Tartar allies "made all the Russian land
desolate," in the chronicle's own words - laying Tver's entire
principality waste while leaving Moscow itself, conspicuously, untouched.
Michael of Tver, executed at the Horde not long after in a dispute
Moscow's own princes had helped provoke, was returned to Rus as a
mutilated corpse thrown down naked before his rival's tent - a detail the
chronicle records as shocking even the Tartars present, whatever it
said about the state of feeling among the Rus princes themselves by
then.

## A reward that never went back

For carrying out Tver's punishment so thoroughly, Kalita received the
Suzerain throne of Rus in 1328 - and from that year forward, the title
never left Moscow's hands again. The Khans, satisfied that a Muscovite
Suzerain could keep the tribute flowing without requiring further Tartar
intervention, largely stopped raiding Northern Rus directly for the next
forty years, a peace the chronicle credited entirely to Moscow's own new
standing. Vladimir's own name survived as the formal seat of the
Suzerain title for a while longer, a courtesy attached to whichever prince
of Moscow happened to hold it. The independent principality that
Andrei Bogolyubsky had built out of a lesser town with no assembly to
answer to, and that Vsevolod III had made the terror of every neighboring
prince, ended not in conquest or in a single battle, but in a rival branch
of its own family discovering that the Horde could be bought more
cheaply than it could be resisted.
