Vsevolod III took power in 1176 by defeating his own nephew, and over
the thirty-six years that followed he pushed the arrangement his brother
Andrei had improvised - a Suzerain title held from Vladimir rather than
Kiev - into something close to outright supremacy over the whole of
Rus. Like Andrei, he enforced recognition of his own seniority without
ever traveling south to occupy Kiev's throne in person, permitting the
princes who sat there to hold it only as his own subordinates, "underlings"
ruling on his sufferance rather than by independent right.

## Princes who answered to a ruler two hundred miles away

The reach of Vsevolod's authority astonished contemporaries. Rurik of
Smolensk, one of the underling Suzerains Vsevolod allowed to sit at Kiev,
told his own father-in-law plainly: "Thou thyself knowest that the will
of Vsevolod must be obeyed, and that without it we can do nothing." Far
to the south-west, in Galicia, a prince who had just taken his father's
throne with Polish help lost no time sending word to Vladimir pledging
his province and himself to Vsevolod "for ever." Even the princes of
Riazan, closer neighbors accustomed to a measure of independence, found
themselves supplying troops to Suzdal's campaigns whenever ordered to.

## A rebellious province burned to make the point stick

When Riazan's own princes were caught intriguing against him in 1207,
Vsevolod had them seized outright and brought to Vladimir, installing
his own officials in their towns and holding the captured princes and
their families until his death. When the town of Riazan itself later
defied him by banishing his own son, Vsevolod's response left no room
for further defiance: the entire population, together with their bishop,
was deported to other towns, and Riazan itself was burned to the ground.
The message was not subtle, and it did not need to be repeated.

## A name that traveled south on its own

Vsevolod's authority was felt as far as it was feared. The chronicle
records that his very name "made all lands to quake," and even a South
Russian poet writing in the same generation - the author of the Tale of
Igor's Campaign - invoked Vsevolod directly in a passage lamenting Rus's
military weakness elsewhere, addressing him as a power capable of
"dividing the waters of the Volga with thine oars." By the century's
turn, a principality that a hundred years earlier had been a remote,
half-colonized frontier had become the dominant power in the Russian
land - and the political center of gravity had shifted, in the space of two
reigns, from the banks of the Dnieper to the banks of the Klyazma.
