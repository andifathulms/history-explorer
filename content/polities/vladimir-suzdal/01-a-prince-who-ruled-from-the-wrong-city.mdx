---
title: A prince who ruled from the wrong city
drafted_from: kliuchevsky-1912

When Yuri Dolgoruki died in 1157, the two senior towns of his own
province, Rostov and Suzdal, had sworn to honor his wish that the land
pass to his younger sons after him. They broke that oath within days and
invited his eldest son, Andrei, to take power instead - and Andrei, for
all his father's memory, did not hesitate to accept an offer sworn against
his own younger brothers. He then drove those same brothers and their
sons out of the country as potential rivals, refusing to share the
province with kin the way custom expected.

## A capital chosen for what it lacked

What Andrei did next mattered more than how he had come to power.
Rostov and Suzdal each had its own vietche, the town assembly that had,
for generations, ruled alongside its prince rather than simply beneath
him. Andrei had no love for either town, or for the assembly tradition
both towns represented, and instead made his home at Vladimir on the
Klyazma - a lesser settlement with no assembly of its own to answer to.
There he built without needing anyone's consent: a magnificent Cathedral
of the Assumption, fortifications, and a town grand enough, in time, that
the chronicle records him taking real pride in the work. Rostov and
Suzdal's own citizens were not pleased. "Behold," they complained, "we
are the capital towns, whereas Vladimir is but a suburb."

## A title claimed without the city it belonged to

Andrei's ambitions reached further than his own province. He
proclaimed himself Suzerain Prince of all the Russian land - the title
that had, without exception, gone with the throne of Kiev - while
staying exactly where he was, in Vladimir. It was an arrangement nobody
had attempted before: a senior ruler content to remain, by choice, in
what custom treated as a junior posting. In doing so, Andrei split what
had been a single, unified Suzerain principality into two - a real one at
Vladimir, and Kiev's own throne reduced to a subordinate seat he allowed
lesser princes to occupy at his own pleasure.

## A ruler undone by the same will that built him

The same refusal to share power that let Andrei break with Kievan
precedent eventually cost him his life. He excluded his senior boyars
from his own court as thoroughly as he had excluded his brothers from
the province, and when he had a boyar named Kuchkovich executed, the
dead man's own brother joined a conspiracy that assassinated Andrei in
1174. "Though prudent in all things and valiant," the chronicle
concluded of him, "Prince Andrei did undo his own purposes with his own
intemperateness." He left behind a capital his own family had not chosen
and a title no longer tied to the city that had always defined it - the two
conditions that would let his successors turn Vladimir into something
considerably larger than the "suburb" Rostov and Suzdal once mocked it
as.
