title: A throne without a rota

The old system that had governed succession across the whole Rurikid
clan - a rota in which provinces passed between princes in strict order of
collective seniority, so that no single family line could treat any
territory as its own permanent property - broke down decisively under
Vsevolod III, and it broke down by his own hand. Shortly before his
death, Vsevolod fell out with his eldest son, Constantine, and simply
transferred Constantine's own right of succession to his second son,
Yuri - a personal act of designation that had no place in the old system
at all. Succession to Vladimir's own throne, from this point forward, was
something a reigning prince could grant or withhold at his own
discretion, not a birthright automatically due to whoever stood next in
line.

## A family, and then twelve families

The consequences compounded across two generations. On Vsevolod's
death, his own territory was divided among his five sons, carving four
new appanages - Rostov, Pereiaslavl, Yuriev, and Starodub - out of the
senior principality's original bulk. When Vsevolod's grandsons in turn
inherited their fathers' shares, the subdivision went further still: three
more appanages, Suzdal, Kostroma, and Moscow, were cut from Vladimir's
own portion; Rostov split into Yaroslavl and Uglich; Pereiaslavl split
into Tver and Dmitrov-Galich. A territory Vsevolod had ruled as five
undivided shares under his own sons had become twelve separate,
independently held fragments under his grandsons - among them the two
appanages, Moscow and Tver, whose own rivalry would eventually decide
who inherited the whole arrangement.

## A feud that outlived the ruler who started it

Vsevolod's own succession maneuver produced immediate conflict rather
than the stability he presumably intended. His disinherited eldest son,
Constantine, found an ally in Mstislav the Bold, and the two went to war
against Yuri over exactly the substitution their father had ordered. The
dispute that followed - waged between brothers over an inheritance their
own father had personally redirected - previewed, in miniature, the kind
of fratricidal contest that would recur across the fragmented Vsevolod
line for the whole of the following century, right up to the moment
Moscow finally ended the pattern by winning outright rather than
dividing further.
