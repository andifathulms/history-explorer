The fragmentation Vsevolod's own succession had set in motion was still
working itself out among his grandsons when a catastrophe arrived that
made the whole internal quarrel largely academic. A Mongol army under
Batu destroyed Vladimir in 1238, burning the capital Andrei Bogolyubsky
had built up from nothing eighty years earlier. The senior lines of
Vsevolod's own descendants did not merely lose ground in the invasion -
the sons of his two eldest sons, Constantine and Yuri, fell in battle
against the Mongols outright, wiping out the branches of the family that
would ordinarily have stood first in line for the throne.

## A title that outlasted the capital it named

With the senior lines gone, the Suzerain throne passed to the sons of
Vsevolod's third son, Yaroslav - first to the eldest, Alexander Nevsky,
whose own name would become the most celebrated to hold the title in
the century after Vladimir's destruction. Succession continued, in
remarkably orderly fashion given everything the principality had just
survived, through Yaroslav's remaining sons in turn: to a second son
banished by the Mongols before he could take his place, then to a third
son who held the appanage of Tver, and finally, by 1276, to the youngest,
ruling from Kostroma. That the old order of seniority survived
recognizably intact through four brothers, even after the capital itself
had been burned and the senior lines annihilated, says something about
how deeply the succession customs Vsevolod had once bent to his own
purposes were still, in their basic outline, respected by what remained of
his family.

## A throne without a capital worth ruling from

What the Mongol destruction did not do was restore Vladimir's own
practical importance. The throne's prestige survived 1238 more or less
intact - princes still competed for it, and holding it still meant something
- but the city itself never regained the standing Andrei and Vsevolod had
built for it. For the rest of the thirteenth century, the Suzerain title
functioned increasingly as a prize to be won rather than a seat to
actually govern from, and the appanage princes competing for it were, by
then, looking as much to the favor of a foreign khan as to any claim
rooted in Vladimir itself.
