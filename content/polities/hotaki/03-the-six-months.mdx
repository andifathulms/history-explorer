Mahmud Hotak did not storm Isfahan. He sat down around it in March 1722 and
waited until October, and what happened inside the walls in those six months was
recorded by a Jesuit who was in the city for all of it.

Judasz Krusiński was procurator of the Jesuit mission at Isfahan. He knew the
court, he spoke the language, and he was an eyewitness. His account is
partisan — he thought the Safavid government contemptible and said so — and on
the siege itself it is the fullest testimony there is.

What follows is difficult and it is not softened here, because softening it
would be the only real dishonesty available.

## The prices

Corn failed in September. A pound of bread sold for thirty shillings that month,
and for above fifty in October.

Isfahan had so many trees that Tavernier said it looked more like a forest than
a city. Part of them were felled during the famine, and the leaves and the bark
were sold by the pound. The roots of herbs were ground into meal. Boiled shoe
leather was, for a time, the common food.

## Afterwards

Then people ate the dead. Krusinski says the streets were full of bodies and
that some of them had their thighs cut off privately, because the practice was
not tolerated: men caught eating human flesh were bastinadoed for it. The fear
of punishment did not stop it. Children were stolen and eaten, half dead of
hunger already. He reports, and marks it as the worst thing in the account, that
there were mothers who killed and ate their own children.

The mortality was such that nobody buried anyone. The corpses were thrown out
into the streets in numbers that made it impossible to walk without stepping
over them, and people grew used to it. Eventually the citizens began throwing
them into the Zayandeh Rud where it ran nearest the city, in quantities that
corrupted the water so thoroughly that it was a year before anyone would drink
from the river or eat a fish out of it.

## One household

Krusinski gives one story in full, and it is the one to keep.

A Persian lord sold everything he owned piece by piece to feed his household.
When there was nothing left to sell and no food to be had, he decided to deliver
himself and his family from a famine he could see no remedy for. He ordered a
dinner rather more sumptuous than usual, and poisoned all of it privately, so
that he and his whole family down to the meanest servant found an end of their
miseries — none of them, except himself, knowing how it came.

## The surrender

On 23 October Shah Sultan Husayn rode out of the city and handed Mahmud his
crown in person.

That is the height of Hotaki power and it is worth being exact about what had
been won. Twenty thousand Afghans had taken the capital of an empire without
assaulting it, by sitting outside for six months while the people inside starved
to death. Isfahan had been one of the largest and richest cities in the world.
It never recovered its population, and Iran did not have a functioning central
government again for fourteen years.
