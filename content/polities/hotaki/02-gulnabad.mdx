Mahmud Hotak invaded Persia in 1722 with a force the sources put at something
like twenty thousand men. He was not attempting to conquer an empire; the first
campaign, two years earlier, had been a raid that reached Kerman and went home
again. This one reached Isfahan.

On 8 March 1722, at Gulnabad east of the capital, the Safavid army came out to
meet him — a much larger force, with artillery, commanded by court appointees
who disagreed with each other — and was broken in a few hours. Matthee's account
of the battle is unsparing about why: the Afghans were a tribal army that had
been fighting for a decade and the Safavid army had not fought a serious war in
a generation, was raised out of a system that had been decaying since Abbas I,
and was commanded by men chosen for their position at court.

There was nothing behind it. Mahmud did not have to storm Isfahan and does not
appear to have known how; he sat down around it instead. The siege lasted six
months, the city ate its animals and then worse, and the reported mortality is
enormous. On 23 October Shah Sultan Husayn rode out and handed Mahmud the crown
in person.

This site records the siege on the Safavid page as what it did to the Safavids.
It is recorded here as what it did to the Hotaks, and the two are different
claims about the same six months. A tribal confederation from one frontier
province had become, in an afternoon at Gulnabad, the government of an empire —
and it had no chancery, no revenue administration, no legitimacy in the country
and no plan.
