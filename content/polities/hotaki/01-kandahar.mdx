Kandahar was the Safavid empire's eastern frontier province, contested with the
Mughals for a century, and its population was Pashtun — Ghilzai in the country
around the city, Abdali to the west around Herat. Both were Sunni, which in a
state that had spent two centuries enforcing Twelver Shi'ism was a standing
problem, and the last decades of Safavid administration made it worse rather
than better.

<Figure id="old-kandahar" />

Isfahan sent Gurgin Khan, a Georgian convert and a capable soldier, to govern
the province firmly. He governed it very firmly. Mirwais Hotak, the leading man
of the Ghilzai, was arrested and sent to Isfahan as a hostage — where, having
been given the run of the capital, he formed a view of the empire that turned
out to be accurate: that it was rich, badly defended, and hated in its own
provinces.

He went on the pilgrimage from there, and is reported to have obtained a ruling
at Mecca that revolt against a Shi'i sovereign was lawful.

Whether that ruling existed in anything like the form the accounts give it
cannot be established, and the distinction matters. A revolt authorised by a
jurist is a different object from a revolt that simply succeeded; the first has
a document behind it that other people can be shown. Nobody has ever produced
this one. What the Hotaks ruled on, in the end, was the fact that they had
won.

He returned to Kandahar, was received as a loyal subject, and in 1709 had Gurgin
Khan killed at a banquet. The Ghilzai took the city. Isfahan sent four
expeditions over the following years and all four failed, which taught the
Ghilzai the thing that mattered most: that Safavid armies could be beaten by a
provincial tribal levy.

Mirwais died in 1715 having never claimed to be more than the leader of his
people. His brother succeeded him, argued for coming to terms with Isfahan, and
was murdered for it by Mirwais's son.
