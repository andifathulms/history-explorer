title: The career they created

Tahmasp II, the Safavid claimant in the north, acquired in 1726 a commander from
a Turkmen tribe in Khurasan named Nadir Quli. Within three years the Afghans
were gone.

Nadir beat Ashraf at Damghan in September 1729 and again at Murche-Khort in
November, took Isfahan, and drove the remnant east. Ashraf was killed in flight
in 1730, and the Hotak state contracted back to the province it had come from.
Husayn Hotak held Kandahar until 1738, when Nadir — by then shah himself —
besieged it for a year, took it, and destroyed the city.

The occupation's most durable consequence is the man who ended it, which is an
odd thing to say about a conquest and is nonetheless the case. Axworthy's
biography makes the point directly: Nadir's whole career was built on being the
person who reversed 1722, and the army he built to do it became the instrument
he used on Delhi nine years later. The Hotaks broke the Safavid state and
created the conditions for the next one.

For this corpus the page also fixes an empty field. The Safavid record's
`ended.by` had nothing in it because the Ghilzai Afghans were outside the
corpus; the edge now exists, and the sequence Safavid to Hotak to Afsharid is
the region's thread rather than a gap with a footnote.

A postscript, because it is the kind of thing this site should record rather
than tidy away. The Abdali of Herat, the other Pashtun confederation, served
Nadir; one of their commanders, Ahmad Khan Abdali, was with him when he was
murdered in 1747, took his treasury and his Afghan contingent, and founded the
Durrani state at Kandahar. Modern Afghanistan dates itself from that. Both
Afghan polities of the eighteenth century came out of this quarrel between a
Safavid province and its governor.
