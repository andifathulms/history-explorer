title: The red gospel

An occupying army of a few thousand men holding a country of several million has
one obvious problem and Mahmud solved it the obvious way, twice, and then went
mad.

The first was in 1723, after a revolt at Qazvin had shown him what the country
would do if it found the nerve. He calculated that he was in the middle of a
large and hostile population with a handful of soldiers, and that the way to be
master of one part of it was to kill the other; and that to avoid provoking the
rising he was afraid of, he should begin with the people whose rank or birth
would let them lead one. So on the day his detachment came back from Qazvin he
invited the ministers, lords and Persian chiefs to a public entertainment. Three
hundred came, without suspicion, and as soon as they were seated the guard fell
on them with sabres. Among them were the chancellor of the empire, the governor
of Isfahan and his brother, the khan of Gilan, and the grand master of the
artillery.

Hanway keeps one detail out of that room. A boy of twelve, a son of the prince
of Georgia, had been adopted by an Afghan; during the massacre he ran to the
officers of that nation and begged them in tears to protect him, and neither his
age nor their pleading saved him — he was taken from them and killed.

The bodies were laid out on the *maydan* in front of the palace. Then, so that
their children should not grow up to avenge them, two hundred young men of the
first nobility of Iran and Georgia were brought together from the academy where
they were being educated, taken to an open field, and told to run for their
lives; and the Afghans rode after them and hunted them down. Mahmud's public
explanation was that these young lords had been conspiring against him. Then he
assembled the three thousand men he had taken into his own pay out of Shah
Husayn's guard, on the pretext of giving them an extra allowance of *pilau*, and
had troops posted for the purpose cut them to pieces while they ate.

The second was in 1725, and it was the royal family. Several brothers, three
uncles and seven nephews of Shah Husayn were shut up with him; counting the shah's
own children the number came to more than a hundred, which Hanway pauses to
explain by the reign that produced them — 1701 was remembered in Iran as the year
of virgins, when Husayn had the provinces searched for beautiful girls, and there
was a month in which thirty cradles were carried into the seraglio. On 7 February
they were assembled in the palace yard with their hands tied behind them and
Mahmud began the killing himself with his sabre.

Two of Husayn's sons were still alive when their father heard the noise and came
running. The elder was five. They threw themselves into his arms; he wept over
them and put himself in the way of the stroke; and when Mahmud came forward to
kill them in their father's arms, Husayn put his arm out and took the blow
instead. Hanway's sentence for what happened next is the one worth keeping: at
the sight of blood running from the wound of a king, and a man of whom the tyrant
had been used to think with some reverence, heaven stopped his murdering hand.
The two children were left with their father.

After that Mahmud could not sleep. His mind, Hanway says, was perpetually
agitated with the most frightful ideas, his intellect — already weakened by a
long ascetic retreat — failed further, and he had an insupportable pain in his
bowels. The court physicians could do nothing, so they sent for a religious
remedy, and the one they sent for was Christian: the Armenians of New Julfa had
a practice of reading what they called the red gospel over the head of a patient
in such cases, and Armenians and Muslims in the country alike held that Persians
as mad as Mahmud had been cured by it.

In the first days of April the clergy of Julfa put on their vestments, lit wax
tapers, and walked in procession to the palace of the man who had conquered the
country. They were received with great respect. They passed through the
apartments between two rows of courtiers and guards whose modest bearing and
profound silence expressed, Hanway notes, a reverence for these priests that
could hardly have been expected from men of their religion. The ceremony was
performed, and the clergy walked back to Julfa in the same order, attended by a
great many of the lords of the court.

In a lucid interval Mahmud learned what the Armenians had tried to do for him,
and sent them two thousand tomans in coin and as much again in goods, with a
promise to return the rest of what he had extorted from them if he recovered. He
made the same promise to the Dutch and the Indian merchants. He did not recover.
His officers deposed him for his cousin Ashraf within weeks, and he died shortly
afterwards.
