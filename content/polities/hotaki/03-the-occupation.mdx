The Hotaks held Isfahan from 1722 to 1729 and never held Persia. Safavid
claimants were proclaimed elsewhere almost immediately — Tahmasp II got out of
the city before it fell and set up in the north — and the provinces mostly
ignored the new government. Russia took the Caspian coast. The Ottomans took
Georgia, Armenia and much of the west, and in 1724 the two powers agreed to
partition what they had between them.

What the occupiers did in the meantime is the reason this page's revenue field
is null with a comment rather than coded. They confiscated. There was no attempt
at a fiscal administration that any consulted source describes, because the men
who had taken the capital were a tribal levy without a secretarial class, in a
country whose own administrators had no reason to work for them and every reason
not to. An occupation that lives on what it can seize is not on a revenue basis;
it is spending a stock.

Mahmud's own behaviour deteriorated into something the sources describe as
madness and modern accounts have variously diagnosed. In 1725 he had most of the
surviving Safavid princes killed in the palace, reportedly with his own hand.
Shortly afterwards his cousin Ashraf had him killed and took the throne.

Ashraf was the abler of the two and inherited an impossible position: a shrinking
Afghan force in a hostile country, an Ottoman army in the west, a Russian one in
the north and a Safavid claimant in between. He beat the Ottomans once and made
terms, executed Sultan Husayn, and held on.

It is worth stating what this episode is not. It is not the fall of Iran to
Afghanistan, and treating it that way — as several nineteenth- and
twentieth-century national histories on both sides have — reads a modern border
back into a Safavid province in revolt. The Ghilzai of 1722 were subjects of the
empire they took, and the man who ended them was from Khurasan.
