The Sayfawa held the throne at Lake Chad, by the king list, from about 1075 to
1846. That is something close to eight hundred years under one dynasty.

For comparison, this corpus's longest-lived ruling houses are the Capetians in
France at three hundred and forty in the direct line, the house of Osman on the
Ottoman record at six hundred and twenty-three, and the Solomonic dynasty on the
Ethiopian record at seven hundred and four. The Sayfawa outlast all of them, and
almost nobody outside the field has heard of them.

The king list itself — the *girgam* — is the reason we can say so, and it is a
document of a kind this corpus rarely gets from a non-literate tradition: a
dynastic chronicle maintained over centuries, giving each mai's name, his
mother's name, and the length of his reign.

## Kanem, then Bornu

The state began east and north of the lake, in Kanem. Al-Muhallabi describes a
kingdom there in the tenth century whose ruler his people treated as a god and
who ate in private. Al-Bakri and later Ibn Sa'id add to it.

Around 1380, after generations of pressure from the Bulala, the court was driven
out of Kanem altogether and crossed to the west and south of the lake, into
Bornu, where it built a new capital at Ngazargamu and stayed.

This record carries both as one polity, and the span comment sets out why: the
dynasty, the chronicle, the trade road and the claim all crossed the lake
together. This corpus's Serbian record makes the same argument in the mirror
image — a change of dynasty inside a continuous country — and its two Bulgarian
records are the case where the reasoning does not apply, because there the state
stopped for a hundred and sixty-seven years.

Here nothing stopped. The country changed.

## The road

What the kingdom sat on was the shortest crossing of the Sahara.

The Fezzan road ran from Lake Chad north through Bilma and the Kawar oases to
Murzuq and on to Tripoli, and it is the least bad of the trans-Saharan routes —
shorter than the western ones this corpus's Ghana, Mali and Songhai records
describe, with reliable wells at intervals. Salt came south from Bilma. Natron
from the lake shore went north. Horses came south. And people went north, in
numbers that made this one of the two great suppliers of the trans-Saharan slave
trade for most of a millennium.

This record's `revenue_basis` reads `trade-toll, tribute`, and the first of those
is that road.

## A pedigree from Yemen

The Sayfawa took their name from Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan, a Himyarite noble of the
sixth century.

This corpus carries Himyar, and its sixth chapter has the man: in the Arabic
accounts he goes to Constantinople for help against the Ethiopians, is refused,
goes to Ctesiphon instead, and comes back with a Persian force that takes Yemen.
That record's `successor_claims` field was null until this one was written and
now counts him.

A dynasty at Lake Chad claiming descent from a prince in Yemen, five thousand
kilometres away and five centuries earlier, is not a genealogy anybody defends as
history. It is the same manoeuvre this corpus records three times over in Africa —
the Kilwa sultans claiming a prince of Shiraz, the Malian mansas claiming Bilal
the Prophet's muezzin, the Ethiopian emperors claiming Solomon — and coding rule
1 counts a claim without filtering on whether it is true, on the stated ground
that the count measures whose legitimacy was worth claiming.

What these four dynasties all wanted was to be legible to the world they traded
with. Three of them chose an Islamic pedigree and one chose a biblical one, and
all four are on this site now.
