The Sayfawa held the throne at Lake Chad, by the king list, from about 1075 to
1846. That is something close to eight hundred years under one dynasty.

For comparison: the Capetians held France for three hundred and forty years in
the direct line. The house of Osman held the Ottoman throne for six hundred and
twenty-three. The Solomonic dynasty of Ethiopia managed seven hundred and four.
The Sayfawa outlast all of them, and almost nobody outside the field has heard of
them.

The king list itself — the *girgam* — is the reason anybody can say so, and it is
a rare kind of document to have from a tradition that was not writing: a
dynastic chronicle maintained over centuries, giving each mai's name, his
mother's name, and the length of his reign.

## Kanem, then Bornu

The state began east and north of the lake, in Kanem. Al-Muhallabi describes a
kingdom there in the tenth century whose ruler his people treated as a god and
who ate in private. Al-Bakri and later Ibn Sa'id add to it.

Around 1380, after generations of pressure from the Bulala, the court was driven
out of Kanem altogether and crossed to the west and south of the lake, into
Bornu, where it built a new capital at Ngazargamu and stayed.

Kanem and Bornu are conventionally treated as two kingdoms and are better
understood as one. The dynasty crossed the lake, the chronicle crossed with it,
the trade road it lived on ran to both, and the claim its rulers made about
themselves did not change. Nothing stopped; the country moved.

## The road

What the kingdom sat on was the shortest crossing of the Sahara.

The Fezzan road ran from Lake Chad north through Bilma and the Kawar oases to
Murzuq and on to Tripoli, and it is the least bad of the trans-Saharan routes —
shorter than the western routes that Ghana, Mali and Songhai lived on, with
reliable wells at intervals. Salt came south from Bilma. Natron
from the lake shore went north. Horses came south. And people went north, in
numbers that made this one of the two great suppliers of the trans-Saharan slave
trade for most of a millennium.

Everything the kingdom collected came off that road or off the tributaries beside
it.

## A pedigree from Yemen

The Sayfawa took their name from Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan, a Himyarite noble of the
sixth century.

He is a real figure. In the Arabic accounts Sayf goes to Constantinople for help
against the Ethiopians occupying Yemen, is refused, goes to Ctesiphon instead,
and comes back with a Persian force that takes the country.

A dynasty at Lake Chad claiming descent from a prince in Yemen — five thousand
kilometres away and five centuries earlier — is not a genealogy anybody defends
as history. It is a manoeuvre, and it is one that African dynasties made
repeatedly: the sultans of Kilwa claimed a prince of Shiraz, the mansas of Mali
claimed Bilal the Prophet's muezzin, the emperors of Ethiopia claimed Solomon.

What all four wanted was the same thing, which was to be legible to the world
they traded with. Three chose an Islamic pedigree and one a biblical one, and in
every case the point was that a merchant or an envoy arriving from outside should
find a ruling house he already knew how to place.
