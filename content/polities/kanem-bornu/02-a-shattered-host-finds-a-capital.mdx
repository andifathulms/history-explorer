title: A shattered host finds a capital

The court that fled Kanem around 1380 did not arrive in Bornu as a
state. For the better part of a century afterward it was, in Barth's
own phrase drawn from the court chronicle he translated at Kukawa, "the
shattered host of a belligerent tribe driven from their home" - armed
encampments without a fixed capital, fighting the same Bulala dynasty
that had driven them out in the first place.

## Ceding the homeland to hold the rest

By 1396 the mai Umar had accepted what decades of pressure had already
made obvious: Kanem itself, the dynasty's own original territory, could
not be held. He formally ceded it to the Bulala rather than continue a
war that was draining what remained of the state's strength, and led
his people into what permanent exile in Bornu. For the next seventy
years the chronicle records little but survival - no fixed capital, no
settled succession, a kingdom that existed as a fighting force rather
than an administration.

## Ali Ghazi, and a capital built to last

The recovery began under Ali Dunamami, remembered by the surname his
own contemporaries gave him, el Ghazi - the warrior. Coming to power
around 1472, he broke the grip the kingdom's own aristocratic office-
holders had gained over the monarchy during the long crisis, reasserted
royal authority in fact rather than only in name, and did what none of
his predecessors since the flight from Kanem had managed: he built a
fixed capital. The new city, Ngazargamu, gave the reconstituted kingdom
something it had lacked since abandoning Njimi - a permanent seat of
government rather than a moving camp. It was during Ali Ghazi's reign,
by Barth's own reckoning, that a traveling Moroccan diplomat later
known as Leo Africanus passed through the region and recorded what he
saw of it, though the kingdom the Bulala still ruled in Kanem itself,
called Gaoga in Leo's own account, remained for the moment the more
visible power to outside visitors.

## Kanem retaken

That balance did not last. In 1505 Ali Ghazi's successor, Edris
Katarkamabi, led a Bornu army back into Njimi itself, ending the exile
Umar's own cession had begun a hundred and nine years earlier. Kanem
became a province of the Bornu-based kingdom rather than its own
independent seat - a reversal of the original relationship between the
two names that gave the state the paired identity, Kanem-Bornu, it
would carry for the rest of its existence. The kingdom Leo Africanus's
own account describes, and that the next mai's court would go on to
transform under Idris Alooma, was the direct product of this
generation and a half of recovery: a fixed capital, a reasserted
monarchy, and a homeland recovered rather than permanently lost.
