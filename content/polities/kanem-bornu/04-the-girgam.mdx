The *girgam* is the king list of Kanem-Bornu. It gives, for each *mai* in
sequence: his name, his father's name, **his mother's name**, and the length of
his reign.

It runs to something over sixty rulers. It was maintained in Arabic, added to as
reigns ended, and it is why anybody can say that one dynasty held the throne for
close to eight hundred years and can name the man at each end of it.

## What a king list is worth

Very little on its own and a great deal in combination.

A list of names and reign-lengths does not say how a state worked. It does not
state a succession rule, describe an institution, or date an event beyond a
reign. What it does is establish a sequence — and a sequence is the thing most
often missing.

It is worth appreciating how rare that is. The Greco-Bactrian and Indo-Greek
kings of the second century BC are known almost entirely from coins, and their
order has to be reconstructed from which types were overstruck on which, with the
result that several kings appear to have been issuing at once and nobody is sure
who followed whom. Kanem-Bornu has the opposite problem: a clean sequence of
sixty rulers over eight centuries, and very little else.

## The mother's name

The girgam's habit of recording each mai's mother is not decoration.

Royal women in Kanem-Bornu held formal office. The *magira* — usually the mai's
mother — and the *magara*, the senior royal sister, had their own establishments,
their own revenues and a recognised voice in the succession. Several are recorded
acting as regents. A king list that names the mother in every entry is a
document produced by a state that thought the maternal line was part of the
identification.

It is worth resisting the obvious inference. Prominent queen mothers do not by
themselves mean the throne descended through women: Ottoman valide sultans,
Ethiopian empresses and Asante queen mothers all held formal power under three
quite different succession systems, only one of which was matrilineal. What the
girgam demonstrates is that the maternal line was part of how a Sayfawa king was
identified. Whether it was part of who was eligible to be one, no source
says.

## What else was written here

More than the girgam, and less than survives.

Kanem sent students to Cairo from the thirteenth century and maintained a hostel
there for its own travellers — a madrasa endowment in a foreign capital, which is
the act of a state that expected a continuing traffic of its own scholars. It
corresponded with the Almohads in the twelfth century and with the Ottomans in
the sixteenth; some of those letters survive on the other side.

And under Idris Alooma it produced a narrative history of its own, written by the
mai's own imam, which is among the very few contemporary accounts of a
sub-Saharan state written from inside it.
