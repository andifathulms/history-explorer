The *girgam* is the king list of Kanem-Bornu. It gives, for each *mai* in
sequence: his name, his father's name, **his mother's name**, and the length of
his reign.

It runs to something over sixty rulers. It was maintained in Arabic, added to as
reigns ended, and it is why this record can say that one dynasty held the throne
for close to eight hundred years and can name the man at each end of that.

## What a king list is worth

Very little on its own and a great deal in combination, which is the position
this corpus keeps arriving at.

A list of names and reign-lengths does not say how the state worked. It does not
give a succession rule — this record's field is null and the second paragraph
below is about why. It does not describe an institution or date an event beyond
a reign.

What it does is make a sequence. This corpus has records where that is exactly
what is missing: the Greco-Bactrian and Indo-Greek pages are null on succession
because their dynastic order is reconstructed from coin overstrikes and shows
several kings issuing at once, and their comments say there is barely a sequence
to code a rule from. Kanem-Bornu has a sequence and not much else, and the two
conditions produce different kinds of thin page.

## The mother's name

The girgam's habit of recording each mai's mother is not decoration and it is the
reason this record's succession field had to be looked at twice.

Royal women in Kanem-Bornu held formal office. The *magira* — usually the mai's
mother — and the *magara*, the senior royal sister, had their own establishments,
their own revenues and a recognised voice in the succession. Several are recorded
acting as regents. A king list that names the mother in every entry is a
document produced by a state that thought the maternal line was part of the
identification.

This site added a `matrilineal` value to its succession vocabulary after five
records made the case, and this record does not carry it. The coding rules say
why in terms: the value is not to be coded from the prominence of royal women
alone, because queen mothers with formal office and matrilineal inheritance of
property are both compatible with a patrilineal throne, and nothing consulted for
this record states a rule of eligibility running through the royal women.

The field stays null. The girgam is why somebody had to check.

## What else was written here

More than the girgam, and less than survives.

Kanem sent students to Cairo from the thirteenth century and maintained a hostel
there for its own travellers — a madrasa endowment in a foreign capital, which is
the act of a state that expected a continuing traffic of its own scholars. It
corresponded with the Almohads in the twelfth century and with the Ottomans in
the sixteenth; some of those letters survive on the other side.

And under Idris Alooma it produced a narrative history of its own, which the
fourth chapter is about, and which is among the very few contemporary internal
accounts of a sub-Saharan state that this corpus can use.
