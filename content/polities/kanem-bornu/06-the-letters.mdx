In 1804 Usman dan Fodio declared jihad in Hausaland, west of Bornu, and within a
few years his followers had overthrown the Hausa states and founded the Sokoto
caliphate. In 1808 the movement took Ngazargamu, Bornu's capital, and the mai
fled.

The state was recovered by a scholar from the north — Muhammad al-Amin
al-Kanemi — who raised a force, drove the Fulani back, and then did something
that is the reason this chapter exists. He wrote to them.

## The argument

Al-Kanemi's objection was not military and not political. It was that the jihad
was illegitimate, because Bornu was a Muslim state and Muslims may not be
attacked as unbelievers.

The correspondence with Usman dan Fodio and later with his son Muhammad Bello
survives on both sides, and it is a genuine theological exchange conducted while
the two parties were at war. Al-Kanemi's case runs roughly: Bornu has been Muslim
for seven hundred years, its rulers pray and fast and endow scholarship, and the
practices you object to — surviving customary usages, laxity among the
uneducated, rulers behaving badly — are found in every Muslim country including
your own. If those make a country's people unbelievers, then no country is
Muslim. Show me the ruling that makes them so.

The Sokoto side replied that the practices in question amounted to unbelief, that
a ruler who tolerates them is complicit, and cited authorities.

Al-Kanemi answered the citations.

## What the exchange actually settles

Nothing, in the end, and that is the interesting part.

The question at issue — when is a Muslim state Muslim enough that another Muslim
state may not attack it — has no procedure for deciding it. There was no court
either side could appeal to, no authority whose ruling would have bound them, and
no possibility of a verdict. Two rulers argued it in writing, in good Arabic,
citing the same authorities, and then fought about it.

What the letters do is make visible something that is usually invisible in
religious wars, which is that both sides had a serious case and knew it. Sokoto's
position was that a ruler who tolerates unbelief among his subjects is complicit
in it. Al-Kanemi's was that unbelief has a definition, that his people met the
tests, and that calling a Muslim an unbeliever in order to make him lawful prey is
itself a grave offence in the law both sides accepted.

He was answering an accusation on which his country's existence depended, and he
answered it by argument, and he was writing to men who were entirely capable of
following the argument.

## Who governed afterwards

Al-Kanemi saved the state and then held it. The mai went on reigning at Kukawa
and al-Kanemi and then his son governed, for thirty-eight years, with the
formal ruler in place and the actual one beside him.

Ethiopia had gone through the same thing a generation earlier and had a name for
it — the Zemene Mesafint, the era of the princes, when the emperors at Gondar
reigned without ruling while regional lords fought over which of them would hold
the throne. Bornu's version had one lord instead of several and ended more
decisively: in 1846 al-Kanemi's son killed the last mai and took the throne
himself.

Eight hundred years of one dynasty ended in a room, and the man who ended it was
the grandson of the scholar who had saved the state from the Fulani.

## What 1846 was

Not a succession crisis, and not a conquest either.

It was a household officer's grandson deciding that the arrangement he had
inherited — a reigning king and a governing minister — had become pointless, and
removing the king. The Sayfawa had held the throne since about 1075. They lost it
to the family of a scholar who had come to their rescue two generations earlier
and had never gone home.

That is a remarkably common way for long dynasties to end and it almost never
gets described as such, because it makes a poor story. Nobody invaded. The state
carried on with the same borders, the same capital and the same administration.
What changed was whose name was said.
