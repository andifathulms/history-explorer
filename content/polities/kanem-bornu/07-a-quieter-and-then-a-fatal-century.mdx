Idris Alooma's own successors inherited a state at the height of its
reach, and for two centuries the court chronicle Barth translated names
them in unbroken succession - Haj Umar from around 1627, his son Ali
from 1645, and mais beyond them the chronicle itself no longer bothers
to narrate as more than names, because for a long stretch there was
nothing to narrate.

## A dynasty that kept its records and stopped making history

By Barth's own reading of the chronicle, the reigns that followed the
two Idris and the sixteenth century's other able rulers were, in his
own words, "a quieter period," when "pious and peaceful kings occupied
the throne." It is worth being exact about what that means: not that
the kingdom collapsed, or that the succession broke down, but that the
same institutions Idris Alooma had built - the firearm-equipped army,
the qadi courts, the administrative reach over Kanem and Bornu alike -
kept functioning well enough that generations of mais left no crisis
behind them worth a chronicler's attention. A girgam that names every
ruler in unbroken sequence recording nothing remarkable about most of
them is not a gap in the evidence. It is the evidence.

## A war against the Tuareg, and a king unequal to what followed

The quiet ended in the middle of the eighteenth century, when a mai
named Ali, distinguished from his predecessors by the same surname
Omarmi, took the field against the Tuareg - drawn, as Barth's own
account puts it, from the same steppe stock the Sefuwa dynasty itself
had originally sprung from, and which had by then become one of the
kingdom's most persistent enemies rather than a distant kinsman. His
campaigns were vigorous but did not settle the threat permanently, and
his own successor lacked whatever combination of energy and authority
had let Ali Omarmi hold the frontier at all. Barth names this successor
simply Ahmed, and describes him bluntly as indolent - the ruler under
whom the long-accumulated pressure on Bornu's own frontiers, Tuareg
raiding chief among them, went unanswered at exactly the moment a new
and more dangerous threat was gathering to the west.

## What the quiet decades left behind

That threat was the Fulani jihad Usman dan Fodio would launch in
Hausaland in 1804, a movement Ahmed's own weakened kingdom had none of
the reserves left to meet when it reached Bornu's own frontier four
years later. The two intervening centuries had not hollowed the
kingdom out through any single catastrophe - no lost war, no succession
collapse, nothing the chronicle troubles to name as a turning point in
its own right. What they left behind was a state that had simply
stopped needing to be exceptional to survive, governed by kings
competent enough to keep an inherited system running and not, when the
crisis finally came, equal to saving it.
