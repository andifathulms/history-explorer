On 17 February 1823 a British officer riding ahead of his party came out of the
trees a few miles short of Kuka and stopped, because there was a line of cavalry
in front of him extending right and left as far as he could see.

Dixon Denham had spent the approach genuinely unsure what he was going to find.
The reports had been contradictory — that the sheikh's soldiers were a few ragged
men with spears living off raids, or that his forces were numerous and to a
degree well trained — and he records the uncertainty in the sentence before he
finds out:

> we advanced towards the town of Kouka in a most interesting state of
> uncertainty, whether we should find its chief at the head of thousands, or be
> received by him under a tree, surrounded by a few naked slaves.

## What the reception was for

Several thousand horse, standing quite steady, without noise or confusion. Then
the welcome, which Denham understood perfectly well was a demonstration.

Three bodies detached from the centre and each flank and charged at the visitors,
stopping a few feet from their horses' heads without checking speed until the
moment of the halt, shaking spears and shouting *Barca! barca!* — blessing,
blessing, sons of your country — then wheeling back to do it again. Meanwhile the
wings closed in until the small Tripolitan party was entirely surrounded, packed
so close as to be nearly smothered and in some danger from the crowding of the
horses and the clashing of spears. Their chief was enraged and could do nothing;
he was answered only by shrieks of welcome and spears rattled over his head.

> I am quite sure this was premeditated.

The sheikh's own men — black chiefs and favourites, all raised to that rank by
some act of bravery — wore mail of iron chain from throat to knee, split behind
to hang down either side of the horse, some with iron skullcaps and chin-pieces,
and their horses' heads plated as well.

## The sultan behind the lattice

A fortnight later they were taken to Birnie to be presented to the actual king of
Bornu, and Denham's account of that audience is worth giving in full.

The courtiers rode up, dismounted, prostrated themselves, and sat down on the
ground in front of the ruler with their backs to him, as the custom required. The
sultan was seated in a sort of cage of cane or wood by the door of his garden, and
looked out at the assembly through the railing. Of him the visitors got only a
glimpse through the lattice-work: his turban was larger than anybody else's, and
his face from the nose downwards was completely covered.

The courtiers Denham describes at length and with open contempt. Large bellies
and large heads being indispensable at that court, those not naturally equipped
made up the deficiency with wadding, so that the belly appeared to hang over the
pommel of the saddle; eight, ten and twelve shirts of different colours worn one
over another helped; the head was wrapped in folds of muslin *so as to deform it
as much as possible*, the most studied turbans having the effect of setting the
head completely on one side. All of them hung about with charms in little red
leather parcels, and the horses too.

Then the judgement:

> Here was all the outward show of pomp and grandeur, without one particle of the
> staple commodity, power, to plead its excuse; he reigns and governs by the
> sufferance of the sheikh.

## What Denham was looking at

Two things, and he only understood one of them.

The man in the cage was the *mai* of Bornu, of a family that had been reigning
somewhere in the Chad basin since the eleventh century. The man who had put the
cavalry on the road was Muhammad al-Kanemi, a scholar from Kanem who had raised
the levies that saved the country from the Sokoto jihad fifteen years earlier,
and had been running it ever since without taking the title.

Denham read the arrangement as an absurdity: pomp without power, a figurehead
amused with ceremony so that the real ruler could govern more easily. That is
accurate as far as it goes. What it misses is that the ceremony was the thing
being preserved. A soldier who had saved the dynasty could not simply replace it
without becoming another usurper in a region full of them, and the cage of cane
was the price of legitimacy.

It held for twenty-three more years. In 1846 al-Kanemi's son had the last mai
killed and took the title himself, and a line that had been on the throne since
before the Norman conquest of England ended in a palace killing that nobody
outside the Chad basin noticed.
