Bornu's army was heavy cavalry. The horses were imported across the Sahara, the
riders wore quilted cotton armour and mail, and the horses were armoured too —
a description European visitors in the nineteenth century still found startling,
because it looked to them like the fourteenth century arriving on the far side
of a desert.

A horse cost slaves. Contemporary rates in the central Sudan put a good imported
horse at somewhere between ten and twenty people.

## The closed circuit

The arrangement is worth stating baldly, because it is the whole military and
fiscal system of the kingdom in one sentence.

Slaves fought — there were slave regiments, and the musketeers of the sixteenth
century were trained slaves. And slaves paid for the cavalry that captured them,
because the annual raiding south into the non-Muslim country produced the people
who were exchanged north for the horses that made the raiding possible.

That circuit ran for centuries and it is tighter here than anywhere else in West
Africa, because the commodity being bought is the thing that does the buying. A
kingdom in that position cannot stop raiding without losing its cavalry, and
cannot lose its cavalry without being raided itself.

## Idris Alooma

The reign that begins around 1564 is when the state acquires firearms and an
administration in the same decades.

Turkish musketeers came through Tripoli. A corps of slave gunmen was trained by
them. Brick fortifications were built. A standing camp replaced the seasonal
levy. Islamic law was applied through appointed qadis in place of customary
courts, and the mai made the pilgrimage.

And it was written up. Ahmad ibn Furtu, Idris Alooma's imam, produced narrative accounts of the Bornu and
Kanem campaigns, which are among the very few contemporary histories of a
sub-Saharan African state written from inside it.

The comparison that matters is two thousand kilometres west and almost exactly
contemporary. Songhai fell in 1591 to a Moroccan force that crossed the Sahara
with arquebuses; Bornu had been buying muskets through Tripoli since the 1570s.
Two Sudanic empires, the same decade, the same technology arriving from the same
direction — one of them bought it and one of them was destroyed by it.

## What nobody wrote down

How the throne passed.

The Sayfawa held it within one family for eight centuries, which is the longest
run any dynasty anywhere managed, and no source states the rule that made it
work. The girgam records each mai's mother's name beside his own, and the queen
mother and the senior royal sister held formal offices with revenues and a voice
in the succession — but a prominent queen mother is compatible with a patrilineal
throne, and several other African and Islamic states had both.

So the longest-reigning dynasty in the world is one whose succession rule cannot
be recovered. That is not an accident of survival so much as a consequence of
what the girgam was for: it was a list of who had reigned, kept by people who
knew perfectly well how the next one would be chosen and had no reason to write
it down.
