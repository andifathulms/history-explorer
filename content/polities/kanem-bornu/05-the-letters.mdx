In 1804 Usman dan Fodio declared jihad in Hausaland, west of Bornu, and within a
few years his followers had overthrown the Hausa states and founded the Sokoto
caliphate. In 1808 the movement took Ngazargamu, Bornu's capital, and the mai
fled.

The state was recovered by a scholar from the north — Muhammad al-Amin
al-Kanemi — who raised a force, drove the Fulani back, and then did something
that is the reason this chapter exists. He wrote to them.

## The argument

Al-Kanemi's objection was not military and not political. It was that the jihad
was illegitimate, because Bornu was a Muslim state and Muslims may not be
attacked as unbelievers.

The correspondence with Usman dan Fodio and later with his son Muhammad Bello
survives on both sides, and it is a genuine theological exchange conducted while
the two parties were at war. Al-Kanemi's case runs roughly: Bornu has been Muslim
for seven hundred years, its rulers pray and fast and endow scholarship, and the
practices you object to — surviving customary usages, laxity among the
uneducated, rulers behaving badly — are found in every Muslim country including
your own. If those make a country's people unbelievers, then no country is
Muslim. Show me the ruling that makes them so.

The Sokoto side replied that the practices in question amounted to unbelief, that
a ruler who tolerates them is complicit, and cited authorities.

Al-Kanemi answered the citations.

## Why it is on this page

Because it is the clearest surviving statement of a question this corpus keeps
walking past and has no field for: when is a Muslim polity Muslim enough that
another Muslim polity may not attack it.

The site codes `legitimation` and this record carries `descent, divine-sanction`.
It codes `religions_carried` and this record counts one. Neither of those
registers a ruler defending his state's standing in a religion he shares with his
attacker, in writing, on the merits, in the middle of a war.

This corpus has met the shape before from the other side. The Hotaki record has
Mirwais reported to have obtained a ruling at Mecca that revolt against a Shi'i
sovereign was lawful — and its field stays `conquest` because rule 8's test is
publicity and the ruling cannot be shown to have existed. Here the documents
exist and the argument is legible, and there is still nothing to code.

## Who governed afterwards

Al-Kanemi saved the state and then held it. The mai went on reigning at Kukawa
and al-Kanemi and then his son governed, for thirty-eight years, with the
formal ruler in place and the actual one beside him.

This corpus has a name for that and it is on the Ethiopian record: the Zemene
Mesafint, the era of the princes, when the emperors at Gondar reigned without
ruling while regional lords fought over which of them would hold the throne.
Bornu's version had one lord instead of several and ended more decisively — in
1846 al-Kanemi's son killed the last mai and took the throne himself.

Eight hundred years of one dynasty ended in a room, and the man who ended it was
the grandson of the scholar who had saved the state from the Fulani.

## The vocabulary, once

That 1846 killing is this record's fifth turning point and it is filed under
`succession-crisis`, which understates it. The build rejected the type that fits:
`dynastic replacement` is one of six ways a polity can *end* on this site and not
one of the nine ways an event inside its life can be classified.

The two closed lists overlap in subject and not in membership, which had not come
up before this record and has not come up since.
