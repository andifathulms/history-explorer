Bornu's army was heavy cavalry. The horses were imported across the Sahara, the
riders wore quilted cotton armour and mail, and the horses were armoured too —
a description European visitors in the nineteenth century still found startling,
because it looked to them like the fourteenth century arriving on the far side
of a desert.

A horse cost slaves. Contemporary rates in the central Sudan put a good imported
horse at somewhere between ten and twenty people.

## What the coded field is describing

This record's `military_basis` reads `slave-soldier, conscript`, and the first
value is doing double work that is worth spelling out.

Slaves fought — there were slave regiments, and Idris Alooma's musketeers were
trained slaves. And slaves paid for the cavalry that captured them, because the
raiding south into the non-Muslim country produced the people who were exchanged
north for the horses that made the raiding possible.

That is a closed circuit and it ran for centuries. It is the same structure this
corpus's Mali and Songhai records carry, and the same one Gomez's book — cited on
both of those — argues was formative for categories of caste and servility in
West Africa. Here the circuit is tighter, because the commodity being bought is
the thing that does the buying.

## Idris Alooma

The reign this record names as its peak, from about 1564, is when the state
acquires firearms and an administration in the same decades.

Turkish musketeers came through Tripoli. A corps of slave gunmen was trained by
them. Brick fortifications were built. A standing camp replaced the seasonal
levy. Islamic law was applied through appointed qadis in place of customary
courts, and the mai made the pilgrimage.

And it was written up. Ahmad ibn Furtu, Idris Alooma's imam, produced narrative
accounts of the Bornu and Kanem campaigns which are the closest thing this
corpus has to a contemporary internal history of a sub-Saharan African state
outside Ethiopia and the Timbuktu chronicles.

This corpus's Songhai record has an almost exactly contemporary case with the
opposite outcome: Askia Muhammad's state fell in 1591 to a Moroccan force with
firearms crossing the Sahara, and this record's own reign of firearms begins
seven years before that. Two Sudanic empires, the same decade, the same
technology arriving from the north — one bought it and one was destroyed by it.

## What it did not have

A reach figure, which the data comment explains as a refusal rather than an
absence, and a succession rule this site can code.

The `succession_rule` field is null. The Sayfawa passed the throne within one
family for eight centuries and the girgam records each mai's mother's name
alongside his own, which in this region is not decorative — the queen mother, the
*magira*, and the senior royal woman, the *magara*, held formal offices with
authority of their own.

Whether that adds up to a rule of eligibility running through the royal women is
not something the sources on this record settle. This corpus's Makuria and Ghana
records are null on exactly that question with the evidence to answer it, and its
Kush record is null with the evidence to argue about it. This one is null with
less than either, and the field stays empty rather than borrowing an answer from
its neighbours.
