The reign that begins around 1564 is this record's peak, and what makes it one is
that four separate things arrive together: firearms, a standing force, a legal
administration, and a historian.

## The guns

Turkish musketeers came south through Tripoli. Idris Alooma hired them, had them
train a corps of slave gunmen, and used both alongside the mail-clad heavy
cavalry the third chapter is about.

The timing is worth setting beside another record in this region. This corpus's
Songhai record ends in 1591 at Tondibi, where a Moroccan force of a few thousand
with firearms destroyed an army many times its size — the clearest case on this
site of gunpowder deciding an engagement outright.

Idris Alooma began buying the same technology through the same desert twenty
years before that battle. Two Sudanic empires, one generation, the same weapon
arriving from the north: one purchased it and one was destroyed by it.

That is not a moral about foresight. Bornu had a shorter road to the
Mediterranean and an existing diplomatic relationship with the Ottomans, and
Songhai had neither. But the two records together are the closest thing this
corpus has to a controlled comparison.

## The administration

Brick fortifications, which is unusual in the region and expensive. A standing
camp in place of a seasonal assembly. And qadis — appointed judges applying
Islamic law — in place of customary courts.

That last is the one with consequences. A ruler who replaces customary
adjudication with appointed jurists is taking authority away from the lineage
heads who held it and attaching it to men he pays. It is the same manoeuvre this
corpus's Songhai record has Askia Muhammad making seventy years earlier, and the
same one its Ethiopian and Ottoman records describe in different vocabularies.

## Ahmad ibn Furtu

The mai's imam wrote the campaigns up. Two books survive, on the Bornu wars and
the Kanem wars, and they are contemporary, internal, and specific.

That is rare enough in this corpus's African half to be worth naming precisely.
This site's Mali record is built on Arabic geographers who mostly had not been
there and on an epic written down in the twentieth century. Its Ghana record is
al-Bakri, at Córdoba, from merchants. Its Songhai record is two chronicles
composed at Timbuktu a generation after the state fell, by men whose families the
founder had persecuted. Its Great Zimbabwe record has no text at all.

Ibn Furtu was there, worked for the man he was describing, and wrote while it was
happening. That makes him partial in the ordinary way a court historian is
partial, and it makes this the one reign in this record where the inside of the
state is visible.

## And what it did not change

The succession, and the shape of the economy.

This record's `succession_rule` is null and its `military_basis` reads
`slave-soldier, conscript`, and neither of those moved. The circuit the third
chapter describes — raiding south for people, exchanging people north for horses,
using the horses to raid — went on running, and the musketeers were bought with
the same currency.

A state can acquire firearms, a judiciary and a historian in one reign and change
nothing about what it lives on. That is worth saying because the reign reads, in
summary, like a modernisation, and the coded fields on this page are the same
before it and after.
