The reign that begins around 1564 is the kingdom's high point, and what makes it
one is that four separate things arrive together: firearms, a standing force, a
legal administration, and a historian.

## The guns

Turkish musketeers came south through Tripoli. Idris Alooma hired them, had them
train a corps of slave gunmen, and used both alongside the mail-clad heavy
cavalry the third chapter is about.

The timing is worth setting beside what happened two thousand kilometres west.
Songhai ended in 1591 at Tondibi, where a Moroccan force of a few thousand with
firearms destroyed an army many times its size — about as clean an instance of
gunpowder deciding an engagement outright as the century produced.

Idris Alooma began buying the same technology through the same desert twenty
years before that battle. Two Sudanic empires, one generation, the same weapon
arriving from the north: one purchased it and one was destroyed by it.

That is not a moral about foresight. Bornu had a shorter road to the
Mediterranean and an existing diplomatic relationship with the Ottomans, and
Songhai had neither. But the two records together are the closest thing this
corpus has to a controlled comparison.

## The administration

Brick fortifications, which is unusual in the region and expensive. A standing
camp in place of a seasonal assembly. And qadis — appointed judges applying
Islamic law — in place of customary courts.

That last is the one with consequences. A ruler who replaces customary
adjudication with appointed jurists is taking authority away from the lineage
heads who held it and attaching it to men he pays. It is the same manoeuvre this
corpus's Songhai record has Askia Muhammad making seventy years earlier, and the
same one its Ethiopian and Ottoman records describe in different vocabularies.

## Ahmad ibn Furtu

The mai's imam wrote the campaigns up. Two books survive, on the Bornu wars and
the Kanem wars, and they are contemporary, internal, and specific.

That is rare enough in African history before the nineteenth century to be worth
naming precisely. Mali is known from Arabic geographers who mostly had not been
there, and from an epic written down in the twentieth century. Ghana is known
from al-Bakri, at Córdoba, from what merchants told him. Songhai is known from
two chronicles composed at Timbuktu a generation after the state fell, by men
whose families its founder had persecuted. Great Zimbabwe has no text at all.

Ibn Furtu was there, worked for the man he was describing, and wrote while it was
happening. That makes him partial in the ordinary way a court historian is
partial in the ordinary way, and it makes this the one reign in eight centuries
where the inside of the state is visible.

## And what it did not change

The succession, and the shape of the economy.

Neither moved. The circuit — raiding south for people, exchanging people north
for horses, using the horses to raid — went on running exactly as before, and the
musketeers were bought with the same currency as the horses had been.

A state can acquire firearms, a judiciary and a court historian in a single reign
and change nothing whatever about what it lives on. That is worth saying, because
the reign reads in summary like a modernisation and it was not one. It was a
better-equipped version of the same machine.
