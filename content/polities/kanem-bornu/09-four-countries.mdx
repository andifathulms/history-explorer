The kingdom's territory is now in four states and its lake is disappearing.

Rabih took Bornu in 1893 and was killed by a French column in 1900, and the
partition that followed drew the boundaries where the three European powers'
columns happened to meet. Kanem, where the dynasty began, is in Chad. Bornu,
where it ended, is in north-eastern Nigeria. The rest is in Niger and Cameroon.
Lake Chad, which the whole thing was organised around, has all four on its shore
and has lost most of its surface area since the 1960s.

## What the British did with it

They kept the dynasty, which is the standard colonial economy.

Indirect rule in northern Nigeria worked by finding an existing authority and
governing through it, and Bornu had one — the Shehu, of al-Kanemi's line, the
family that had displaced the Sayfawa in 1846. The British restored the office
after Rabih's defeat, installed a shehu, and ran the emirate through him. The
line is still there: the Shehu of Borno remains one of the senior traditional
rulers in Nigeria, with a palace at Maiduguri and a ceremonial standing that
long outlasted the colonial arrangement that preserved it.

So the succession runs: Sayfawa from about 1075 to 1846, al-Kanemi from 1846,
interrupted by Rabih for seven years, restored under the British, and continuing.
Somebody has been the ruler at Lake Chad, more or less continuously, for nine
hundred years.

## What the road was worth afterwards

Nothing, almost immediately.

The Fezzan road — Lake Chad to Bilma to Murzuq to Tripoli, the shortest crossing
of the Sahara and the thing the kingdom had existed to tax — stopped mattering
within a generation of the partition. The trans-Saharan caravan trade had already
been undercut by steamships on the West African coast, and the traffic that had
made a lake in the middle of the continent worth eight hundred years of dynastic
attention simply moved to the sea.

A colonial boundary then cut the road in two, which finished it.

## The girgam

What survives best is the list.

The *girgam* — sixty-odd rulers, each with his father's name, his mother's name
and the length of his reign — was still being kept when Europeans arrived to read
it, and it is the reason this kingdom can be discussed at all across the
centuries when nobody outside was writing about it.

It is a modest document. It states no rule, describes no institution and explains
nothing. What it does is establish that one family held a throne from the
eleventh century to the nineteenth — which is the single most remarkable fact
about the political history of Africa, and it is known because somebody kept
adding a line each time a king died.
