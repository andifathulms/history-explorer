Around 1510 Leo Africanus wrote down the exchange rate that ran the kingdom of
Bornu, and the arrangement it produced, and it is worth having in his words
because no modern summary improves on it.

The king, he says, has three thousand horsemen in continual readiness and a huge
number of footmen, and his subjects are so obedient that when he commands them
they arm and follow wherever he pleases. They pay him no tribute but the tithes
of their corn, and he has no revenues at all to maintain his estate *but onely
such spoiles as he getteth from his next enimies by often inuasions and
assaults.*

He was at war with a people beyond the desert who had once crossed it in force
and wasted a great part of his kingdom. So he sent for the merchants of Barbary
and told them to bring him horses.

> For in this countrey they vse to exchange horses for slaues, and to giue
> fifteene, and sometime twentie slaues for one horse.

## Waiting for payment

What Leo describes next is the part that turns a price into a system.

The merchants brought the horses. They could not be paid immediately, because the
king did not have the slaves yet — he would have them when he came back from
raiding, and he raided once a year, at one appointed season.

So the merchants waited. Often for three months, Leo says. Sometimes, when the
king did not bring home enough captives to satisfy everybody, for a whole year.
He maintained them at his own expense the entire time, which was cheaper than
losing his suppliers. And Leo says he met traders there who had given up: they had
extended credit for a full year and had stopped expecting to see it.

A state whose only revenue is what it takes by force, buying on credit the horses
it needs to take it, from merchants who have to camp in the capital until next
season's raid pays them.

## Why the horses

Because the country south of Lake Chad is open, and cavalry works there, and
almost nobody in it had any.

A mounted force operating against unmounted village populations does not need to
be large and does not need to fight battles. Bornu's raiding zone was the
non-Muslim country to the south and south-west, and the annual expedition against
it was not a war in any sense that had an objective beyond the captives
themselves.

The horses did not breed well at that latitude — the trypanosomiasis belt lies
just south — so the stock had to be replaced continually from north Africa, which
is what kept the circuit running instead of letting it close. Bornu could never
accumulate enough horses to stop buying them.

## What Leo got wrong

He should not be trusted on everything.

He describes the people of Bornu as embracing no religion at all — neither
Christians, Muslims nor Jews — living after a brutish manner, with wives and
children in common and no proper names, each one nicknamed for his height or his
fatness. He attributes this to a merchant who had lived among them.

Bornu had been ruled by a Muslim dynasty for four hundred years by 1510, had
endowed a hostel for its own students in Cairo, and would shortly be corresponding
with the Ottoman sultan. Leo was describing peoples on the kingdom's southern
raiding frontier and had been told they were the kingdom, or was repeating
something he half remembered.

Which is the standing warning about him. Where he is reporting prices, distances
and customs he watched, he is exact and nobody else is available. Where he is
passing on what he was told about places he did not enter, he is worth about as
much as the man who told him.
