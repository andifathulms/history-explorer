In 324 at Opis on the Tigris, Alexander told his Macedonians that he was
discharging the ones too old or maimed to fight, and sending them home rich
enough to be envied. He meant it as a kindness. The army took it as dismissal,
and the whole accumulated grievance of eleven years came out at once.

## What they were actually angry about

Arrian lists the causes, and none of them is pay or plunder.

They had been offended, he says, throughout the whole expedition: by Alexander's
adoption of Persian dress, which showed contempt for their opinion; by his
equipping the thirty thousand young Iranians called the Epigoni in Macedonian
style; and by his mixing foreign horsemen into the ranks of the Companions.

That last one is the sharpest. The Companion cavalry was the Macedonian
aristocracy — the body from which the king was, in practice, inseparable. Putting
Persians in it was not a courtesy to the conquered. It told the Macedonians that
their monopoly on being close to him had ended.

So when he offered to send the veterans home, they told him to discharge *all* of
them, and to go and prosecute the war with his father — a jibe at Ammon, whose
son he had let himself be called at Siwa.

## Thirteen men

Arrian's Alexander does not argue at this point.

He jumped down from the platform with his officers, and pointed out by hand the
most conspicuous of the men who had stirred up the crowd — thirteen of them — and
ordered them taken away and executed.

Then, when the rest had gone silent with terror, he went back up on the platform
and made a speech.

## The speech

It does not begin with himself. It begins with his father, and with what the
Macedonians had been before Philip:

> He found you vagabonds and destitute of means, most of you clad in hides,
> feeding a few sheep up the mountain sides, for the protection of which you had
> to fight with small success against Illyrians, Triballians, and the border
> Thracians.

Then the inventory of what Philip gave them: cloaks instead of hides; the plains
instead of the mountains; the capacity to beat their neighbours instead of hiding
in strongholds; cities with laws; rule over the very barbarians who used to
plunder them; most of Thrace annexed; the coastal ports taken, so that trade made
the country rich and the mines became safe to work; mastery over the Thessalians
they had once feared; the road into Greece opened by humbling the Phocians.

*From being slaves and subjects, he made you rulers.*

It is a remarkable thing for a king to say to a mutinous army. The argument is
not that they owe him — it is that they owe the institution, and that they were
nothing before it made them something. The claim about the Persians in the
Companions follows from it: the army is not a Macedonian possession, it is the
king's instrument, and it was always the king who decided what it was.

## What it tells you about the state

The empire had no constitution, no succession law, no capital, and no
administrative class of its own — Alexander was running the Achaemenid apparatus
with its satraps largely in place. What it had instead was the relationship
between one man and one army.

At Opis that relationship was renegotiated by execution and rhetoric, and it
held: the mutiny collapsed, the veterans went home under Craterus, and the
Persians stayed in the Companions.

But the terms had been made explicit, and they were not terms that could survive
the man. Twelve months later Alexander was dead at Babylon with no arrangement in
place, and the same army — which had just been told in public that it was
nothing without its king — had to decide what to do next.
