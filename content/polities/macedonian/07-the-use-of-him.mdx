He died at Babylon on the twenty-eighth of the month, in the evening. Plutarch
copies the last week out of the court diary almost day by day, and what it
records is administrative to the end. With the fever at its worst he had his
bed moved down beside the great bath and spent the day discussing with his
principal officers which men should be found to fill the vacant places in the
army. On the twenty-fourth, too ill to stand, he was carried out of bed to be
present at the sacrifices.

On the twenty-fifth he stopped speaking. The Macedonians outside decided he was
already dead, came to the gates shouting, and frightened his friends into
letting them in; and the whole army was let past the bed, unarmed, one man
after another. Two officers were sent to ask the god at the temple whether they
should carry the king there, and were told to leave him where he was.

## The body nobody was watching

Then the arguing started, and it went on for days.

Plutarch is interested in the poisoning story — Olympias killing people about
it six years later, the water so cold it could only be kept in an ass's hoof —
and dismisses it, and the reason he gives for dismissing it is the detail worth
keeping. Throughout the days of the commanders' quarrel, he says, the body
stayed clear and fresh, without any sign of taint, *though it lay neglected in
a close, sultry place*.

That is the first sentence of the afterlife. The man who had been the only
institution the empire possessed was lying unattended in a hot room in Babylon
while his generals worked out who was going to have what. Plutarch mentions the
neglect only in passing, as evidence in an argument about poison. It is the
whole situation in one clause.

Roxane, meanwhile, sent for Darius' daughter Statira with a forged letter in
Alexander's name, killed her and her sister, and had the bodies thrown down a
well and the well filled in — Perdiccas assisting, who was governing under
cover of the name of Arrhidaeus, Philip's half-witted son, whom he carried
about with him like a guard.

Everything the empire had was still there: the army, the treasuries, the
satrapies, the roads. What it did not have was a rule.

## What the Romans did with him

Three centuries later he was a career model.

Pompey's hair rose a little from his forehead, and there was something in the
set of his eyes that people said made his face resemble the statues of King
Alexander — more talked of, Plutarch adds drily, than really apparent. The name
was applied to him young, and he did not decline it; some used it in mockery. A
consular named Lucius Philippus, speaking for him in court, thought it a decent
joke that nobody should be surprised if Philip loved Alexander.

That is comedy. The next one is not.

Caesar, at leisure in Spain, was reading a history of Alexander, and sat a long
while afterwards without speaking, and then burst into tears. His friends asked
what was wrong. *Do you think I have not just cause to weep, when I consider
that Alexander at my age had conquered so many nations, and I have all this
time done nothing that is memorable?*

He was in his late thirties, governing a Roman province, and he measured
himself against a dead Macedonian and found himself a failure. He raised ten
new cohorts more or less immediately and went to war with the tribes on the
Atlantic.

And then the detail that says most about what Alexander had become. When Pompey
celebrated his third triumph, the people who liked to make the parallel with
Alexander exact could not quite get the ages to line up — so they said he was
not yet thirty-four. Plutarch notes, flatly, that he was in truth near forty.

A man's age was worth falsifying to preserve a resemblance to somebody who had
been dead for three hundred years. That is a better measure of the afterlife
than any of the cities named after him.

## What actually lasted

The kingdoms his officers cut out of the empire lasted between one and three
centuries and were taken, one after another, by Rome and by Parthia. The
Greek-speaking cities stayed. The language stayed longer than the states did.
And the figure himself detached from the history altogether and went into
circulation — as a model for generals, a warning about autocracy, an argument
about whether fortune or virtue explains success — a figure available to
anybody who needed one.

The empire lasted thirteen years. The example has not stopped yet.
