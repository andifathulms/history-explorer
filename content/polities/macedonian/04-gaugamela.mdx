---
title: The ground Darius chose
drafted_from: arrian-chinnock

Darius picked the battlefield, cleared it and levelled it so his scythed chariots
could run. Alexander's answer was to walk sideways off it.

That is the whole battle in a sentence, and Arrian's account is worth having in
detail because it shows a competent army being dismantled by a man who declined
to fight where it had prepared to win.

## Marching right

Alexander led his army towards the right, and the Persian line marched parallel
with him, already far overlapping his left. He kept going right, and *almost
entirely got beyond the ground which had been cleared and levelled by the
Persians.*

Darius then had to act, because chariots on broken ground are useless. He ordered
his left wing to ride round the Macedonian right and stop the sidestep.

What followed was a long cavalry fight on the flank that Alexander was steadily
losing. He fed in the Greek mercenary horse under Menidas, who were routed by the
more numerous Scythians and Bactrians. He fed in the Paeonians, who pushed the
Scythians back until the Bactrians rallied them. Arrian is candid that many of
Alexander's men fell, both from the numbers against them and because the
Scythians and their horses were far better armoured.

## The chariots

Then Darius launched the scythed chariots at the phalanx, and Arrian's verdict is
four words: *in this they were grievously deceived.*

The Agrianians and javelin-men posted in front of the Companions threw at the
teams. Others took the horses by the reins, pulled the drivers down and killed
the animals where they stood. And the ones that got through met a phalanx that
had been drilled to open its ranks and let them pass — so the chariots ran
harmlessly through the gaps and were dealt with behind the line by the grooms and
the royal guards.

The weapon the battlefield had been levelled for was neutralised by infantry
stepping aside on command.

## The gap

Everything so far was preparation for one moment, and it came from Darius's own
countermeasure.

When the Persian cavalry surged out to envelop the Macedonian right, they opened a
break in their own front line. Alexander wheeled into it, formed the Companions
and the nearest part of the phalanx into a wedge, and charged straight at Darius
with a battle-cry.

There was a short hand-to-hand fight. The Macedonian horse pressed in, *striking
their faces with their spears*; the phalanx came up bristling with pikes. And
then:

> all things at once appeared full of terror to Darius, who had already long been
> in a state of fear, so that he was the first to turn and flee.

*The first to turn and flee.* Arrian says it plainly, and he will remember it
later — when an Indian king with a spear wound in his shoulder keeps fighting,
Arrian will note that he did not do as Darius the Great King did, setting an
example of flight to his men.

## What it decided, and what it did not

Babylon, Susa and Persepolis followed with their treasuries. In 330 Darius was
killed by his own satrap Bessus while fleeing east.

That is where a war of conquest ends. Instead the hardest part began. Bessus took
the title of king in Bactria, and Alexander spent three years in the north-east —
Areia, Drangiana, Bactria, Sogdiana — fighting a countryside rather than an army,
garrisoning as he went and founding cities to hold ground he had already passed
through.

The Persian empire was beaten in three afternoons. Its eastern provinces took
thirty-six months, and they are where most of the army's dead are.
