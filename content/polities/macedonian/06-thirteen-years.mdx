Alexander crossed into Asia in 334 BC and was dead by 323, aged thirty-two,
having destroyed the largest empire in the world and reached the Punjab. The
empire he created did not survive him by a year in any organised form: his
generals divided it, fought each other for four decades, and produced the
Seleucid, Ptolemaic and Antigonid kingdoms.

It is the extreme case of an empire coming apart: one that ended not because it
was defeated or absorbed but because it had no institutions apart from one man
and no agreed way of replacing him. The
Achaemenid administrative apparatus he had captured largely intact continued to
function under new management, which is the strongest evidence for how good it
was.

The lasting consequence was linguistic and cultural rather than political. Greek
became the administrative and prestige language from the Aegean to Bactria for
centuries, and the Greek-speaking cities founded across Iran and Central Asia are
why there is a Buddhist art tradition in Gandhara with Corinthian columns in it,
and why the Kushan kings, three centuries later, minted coins with Greek
letters.
