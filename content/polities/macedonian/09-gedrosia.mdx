Having been stopped by his army at one river, Alexander went home by a route that
killed more of it than any battle had. Arrian says most of the historians thought
nothing else the army suffered in Asia compared with this, and then explains why
the route was chosen, which is the part worth sitting with.

## Why that way

He split the force. Part went back by an easier road. A fleet under Nearchus
followed the coast. He took the rest through the Gedrosian desert.

Nearchus, who was there, said Alexander chose it knowing it was terrible — because
he had heard that nobody had ever brought an army through and come out alive.
Semiramis had got out with twenty men, Cyrus son of Cambyses with seven. On
hearing this, Nearchus says, he was seized with the desire to beat Cyrus and
Semiramis.

That is the explanation from inside the expedition, given by one of its
commanders, and it is not flattering.

## The sand

The sand was piled in high ridges and was not packed; a foot went into it as into
mud or fresh snow. The baggage animals died first, of thirst and of the heat of
the sand itself.

Then the army began slaughtering the horses and mules and eating them, and
reporting that the animals had died of thirst. Every man was in it, so nobody
informed. Arrian's judgement on this is one of his best sentences about command:
Alexander knew perfectly well what was happening, and decided that pretending not
to know was the better cure.

With the animals gone and the waggons broken up, the sick and the exhausted were
left where they fell — because the marches were made at night and at speed and
there was nobody to guide them or nurse them. Some woke and followed the tracks;
few caught up. Most died in the sand, in Arrian's phrase, *like men shipwrecked
at sea*.

## The helmet

Two scenes survive from that march, and they are the ones Arrian chose to keep.

Some light-armed soldiers who had gone off looking for water found a little in a
shallow cleft — a small and mean spring. They collected it with difficulty and
hurried back to Alexander as if they were bringing him some great gift, and
poured it into a helmet.

He took it, commended them, and poured it out on the ground where everyone could
see.

Arrian says the effect on the army was as though the water Alexander threw away
had given every man a drink, and he singles the moment out: *this deed beyond all
others I commend as evidence of Alexander's power of endurance and self-control,
as well as of his skill in managing an army.*

## The guides

The other scene is the one that explains the deaths.

The guides eventually said they no longer remembered the way, because the wind had
blown sand over the tracks. In deep sand levelled flat there was nothing to
navigate by — no roadside trees, no solid hillock. And the army had never learned
to steer by the stars at night or the sun by day, as sailors do: the Phoenicians
by the Little Bear and other men by the Greater.

Alexander worked out that they had to bear left, took a few horsemen and rode
ahead to find the sea. Even those horses gave out in the heat.

It took sixty days to reach Pura. He was thirty-one, he had been marching for
eight years, and he had less than two to live.

An army that had not lost a battle in a decade was destroyed in pieces by a march
its own commander had chosen because two legendary figures had failed at it.
