This record's span begins in 336, when Alexander succeeded, and the thing that
made everything on it possible was finished before that date by his father.

Philip II inherited a Macedon that was a peripheral kingdom of no account,
raided by its neighbours and periodically partitioned by them. He spent
twenty-three years rebuilding it, and the military reform is the part that
matters here. He reorganised the infantry around the *sarissa*, a pike of four
to six metres held in both hands, in a formation deep enough that the points of
several ranks projected in front of the first — a wall of iron that no infantry
in the world could approach frontally. He paid the men year-round and drilled
them year-round, which is why they could execute manoeuvres that require
rehearsal. He built the Companion cavalry into a shock arm used to break a line
the phalanx had pinned. And he financed it out of the Pangaeum mines in Thrace,
which he took in 356 and which are the reason a Macedonian king could afford a
professional army at all.

This site's `military_basis` field on this record reads `standing-professional`
alone, and very little else in antiquity in this corpus carries it. It is the
value that explains the conquest, and it is the reason the empire on the previous
page — larger, richer, more populous, and by its own documentary record in good
working order — lost three battles.

Philip was murdered at his daughter's wedding in 336, in circumstances the
sources have argued about ever since and which cannot be resolved. Alexander was
twenty, had commanded the Companions at Chaeronea two years earlier, and
inherited an army built to invade Persia by a man who had already declared the
invasion and sent an advance force across.

What he added was command. This corpus should be careful about great-man
explanations and this is a case where the sources do not permit much else: the
instrument existed and had been used successfully, and what happened between 334
and 326 is not explicable from the instrument alone.
