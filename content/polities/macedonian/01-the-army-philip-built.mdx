title: The instrument
drafted_from: ab-bosworth-1988

Alexander succeeded in 336, and the thing that made everything after it possible
had been finished before that date by his father.

Philip II inherited a Macedon that was a peripheral kingdom of no account, raided
by its neighbours and periodically partitioned by them. He spent twenty-three
years rebuilding it, and the military reform is the part that matters here.

He reorganised the infantry around the *sarissa*, a pike of four to six metres
held in both hands, in a formation deep enough that the points of several ranks
projected in front of the first — a wall of iron that no infantry in the world
could approach frontally. He paid the men year-round and drilled them year-round,
which is why they could execute manoeuvres that require rehearsal. He built the
Companion cavalry into a shock arm, used to break a line the phalanx had pinned.
And he financed all of it out of the Pangaeum mines in Thrace, which he took in
356 and which are the reason a Macedonian king could afford a professional army
at all.

That last point is the one usually skipped. Nearly every army of the period was
raised from men who had to go home for the harvest. Philip's did not, and a force
that trains all year is not the same kind of object as one that assembles in
spring — which is most of the explanation for how a kingdom of no account beat an
empire that was larger, richer, more populous, and by its own documentary record
in good working order.

Philip was murdered at his daughter's wedding in 336, in circumstances the
sources have argued about ever since and cannot resolve. Alexander was twenty. He
had commanded the Companions at Chaeronea two years earlier, and he inherited an
army built to invade Persia by a man who had already declared the invasion and
sent an advance force across.

What he added was command. Great-man explanations deserve suspicion as a rule,
and this is one of the places where the sources do not leave much room for
anything else: the instrument existed and had already been used successfully, and
what happened between 334 and 326 is not explicable from the instrument alone.
