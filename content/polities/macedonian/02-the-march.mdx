Alexander crossed the Hellespont in 334 and reached the Hyphasis, the last
river in the eastern Punjab that his army would cross, in 326. What follows is
the shape of those eight years, from Arrian, who used Ptolemy and Aristobulus
and is the soberest of the five surviving accounts.

The first two years are field battles: the Granicus in 334, Issus in 333. Both
were won against Persian armies that outnumbered him, and neither ended
anything, because after Issus Darius escaped and the Persian fleet still held
the sea. So Alexander did not pursue him. He turned south and spent a year and
a half taking the coast, which is the least glamorous decision of the campaign
and the one that made the rest possible: an enemy navy with no ports is a fleet
looking for somewhere to tie up.

## Tyre

Tyre was an island city with high walls and its own ships, and behind a channel
three fathoms deep at the worst of it. Alexander decided to build a road to it.

The strait was shallow and muddy near the shore, and stakes could be driven
into the mud, which then held the stones. There was stone and timber in
quantity. The mole went out fast while the water was shallow and nobody was
shooting, and then it slowed, because the walls were high and built for
labourers to be shot from, and because the Tyrians still had the sea and could
row up to any point of the causeway and stop the work. The Macedonians answered
with two towers on the head of the mole, hung with hides against fire-arrows,
with engines on top of them.

Arrian records that Alexander lightened the work by walking about it — rousing
men by speaking to them and giving money to those who were working hardest for
the credit of it. The siege ran seven months on Diodorus' reckoning, and it was
decided in the end by ships rather than by the mole: the Phoenician and Cypriot
squadrons came over to him once their own cities had fallen, and Tyre lost the
water.

## Gaugamela and after

Egypt surrendered without a fight in 332. In 331 Darius assembled his last
army, on ground he had chosen and had levelled for chariots, and lost it at
Gaugamela. Babylon, Susa and Persepolis followed, with the treasuries in them,
and in 330 Darius was killed by his own satrap Bessus while fleeing east.

That is where a war of conquest would have ended, and instead the hardest part
began. Bessus took the title of king in Bactria, and Alexander spent three
years in the north-east — Areia, Drangiana, Bactria, Sogdiana — in a campaign
against a countryside rather than an army, garrisoning as he went and founding
cities to hold what he had passed through. The Persian empire had been beaten
in three afternoons. Its eastern provinces took thirty-six months.

## Into India, and the river that stopped him

He crossed the Indus in 326, beat Porus on the Hydaspes in the rain — the one
battle of the campaign fought against elephants — and went on east across the
Acesines and the Hydraotes to the Hyphasis. Beyond it, he was told, the country
was fertile and there were more nations and more rivers.

The army refused to cross.

Arrian gives Alexander a long speech to them, and the argument is a fair one on
its own terms: the labours are common to you and me, we share the dangers
equally, the land is yours and you are its governors, most of the money now
comes to you. When he finished there was a long silence, because nobody had the
nerve to contradict the king and nobody would agree.

He kept asking for someone to speak. Eventually Coenus did, and his speech is
the best evidence in Arrian for what eight years had cost:

> Thou thyself seest how many Macedonians and Greeks started with thee, and how
> few of us have been left.

Some had been settled in the new cities, he said, and not all of them
willingly. Some had died in the battles, some were crippled by wounds, some had
been left behind across Asia — and most of them, the majority, had died of
disease. Few were left out of many, and those few were no longer strong.

Alexander turned back.

## The desert

He did not go back the way he had come. He sent part of the force by an easier
road, put a fleet under Nearchus to follow the coast, and took the rest through
the Gedrosian desert.

Arrian says that most of the historians thought nothing else the army suffered
in Asia compared with this. Nearchus, who was there, said Alexander chose the
route knowing it was terrible, because he had heard that nobody had ever
brought an army through it and come out alive — Semiramis got out with twenty
men, Cyrus son of Cambyses with seven — and that on hearing this he was seized
with the desire to beat Cyrus and Semiramis.

The sand was piled in high ridges and was not packed; a foot went into it as
into mud or fresh snow. The baggage animals died first, of thirst and of the
heat of the sand itself. The soldiers began slaughtering the horses and mules
and eating them, and reporting that the animals had died of thirst, and every
man was in it, so nobody informed. Arrian says Alexander knew perfectly well
and judged that pretending not to know was the better cure.

With the animals gone and the waggons broken up, the sick and the exhausted
were left where they fell, because the marches were made at night and at speed
and there was nobody to guide them or nurse them. Some woke and followed the
tracks; few caught up. Most died in the sand, in Arrian's phrase, like men
shipwrecked at sea.

It took sixty days to reach Pura. He was thirty-one, he had been marching for
eight years, and he had less than two years to live.
