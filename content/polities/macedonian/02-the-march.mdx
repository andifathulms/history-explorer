Alexander crossed the Hellespont in 334 and reached the Hyphasis, the last
river in the eastern Punjab that his army would cross, in 326. What follows is
the shape of those eight years, from Arrian, who used Ptolemy and Aristobulus
and is the soberest of the five surviving accounts.

The first two years are field battles: the Granicus in 334, Issus in 333. Both
were won against Persian armies that outnumbered him, and neither ended
anything, because after Issus Darius escaped and the Persian fleet still held
the sea. So Alexander did not pursue him. He turned south and spent a year and
a half taking the coast, which is the least glamorous decision of the campaign
and the one that made the rest possible: an enemy navy with no ports is a fleet
looking for somewhere to tie up.

## Why the coast mattered

Darius's fleet was Phoenician and Cypriot and Egyptian — it did not belong to
Persia, it was hired from cities Persia ruled. A navy like that has no home port
of its own. Every squadron in it sails from a harbour that can be taken by land.

So Alexander went down the coast taking harbours, and the fleet dissolved behind
him as its crews' own cities changed hands. He never fought a naval battle. He
made the Persian navy irrelevant by capturing the places it came from, which took
eighteen months and won him the Mediterranean without a ship.

Three sieges mattered on that coast, and one of them took seven months and a
causeway.

The chapters that follow take the campaign in four pieces: the siege that decided
the sea, the battle that ended the Persian empire, the river the army refused to
cross, and the desert he chose to cross on the way back.
