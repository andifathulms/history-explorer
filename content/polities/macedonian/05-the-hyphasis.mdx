Alexander crossed the Indus in 326 and beat Porus on the Hydaspes in the rain —
the one battle of the campaign fought against elephants. Then he went east to the
Hyphasis and the army stopped.

The two things are connected, and Arrian puts them close enough together that you
can see it.

## Porus

Porus lost. Arrian is unusually interested in how.

When his cavalry had been cut up, his elephants were dead or straying without
keepers and most of his infantry had gone, he kept fighting as long as any body
of Indians held together — *he did not depart as Darius the Great King did,
setting an example of flight to his men.*

He was finally wounded in the right shoulder, the one part of him his armour did
not cover; Arrian notes that the mail was remarkable for its strength and the
close fitting of its joints, as those who saw it afterwards could observe. Then
he turned his elephant and began to withdraw.

Alexander, wanting him alive, sent Taxiles — an Indian king already on the
Macedonian side. Porus saw his old enemy approaching, wheeled round and got a
javelin ready, and would probably have killed him if Taxiles had not spurred out
of range.

Alexander was not angry. He kept sending men, and at last sent Meroës, an old
friend of Porus. Porus, by then overcome with thirst, stopped the elephant,
dismounted, drank some water, and told Meroës to take him to Alexander.

Then the exchange the whole episode exists for. Alexander rode out to meet him,
admired his height and the fact that he was not cowed but came on *as one brave
man would meet another brave man*, and spoke first, asking how he wished to be
treated.

> Treat me, O Alexander, in a kingly way.

He kept his kingdom and got more.

## The Hyphasis

Beyond the next river, Alexander was told, the country was fertile and there were
more nations and more rivers.

The army refused to cross.

Arrian gives Alexander a long speech, and its argument is fair on its own terms:
the labours are common to you and me, we share the dangers equally, the land is
yours and you are its governors, most of the money now comes to you.

When he finished there was a long silence — nobody had the nerve to contradict the
king, and nobody would agree with him.

He kept asking for someone to speak. Eventually Coenus did, and what he said is
the single best piece of evidence in Arrian for what eight years had cost:

> Thou thyself seest how many Macedonians and Greeks started with thee, and how
> few of us have been left.

Some had been settled in the new cities, he said, and not all of them willingly.
Some had died in the battles. Some were crippled by wounds. Some had been left
behind across Asia. And most of them — the majority — had died of disease. Few
were left out of many, and those few were no longer strong.

Alexander turned back.

## What the two scenes say together

A king who fought to the last and was beaten got his kingdom returned to him with
additions, because he had behaved as Alexander thought a king should behave. Then
an army that had won every battle it fought declined to take one more step, and
the king who had just rewarded courage in an enemy could not compel it in his own
men.

Both are the same fact seen from two sides. Alexander's authority was personal
and it ran on admiration, which is a real force and not an unlimited one. It could
give a defeated Indian his throne back. It could not get an exhausted army across a
river it had decided was the end.
