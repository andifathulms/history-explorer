title: Nothing written at the time

The Achaemenids were described almost entirely by their enemies. Alexander has
the opposite problem, and it is just as bad.

Not one contemporary account of Alexander survives. The people who were there
wrote: Ptolemy, who became king of Egypt and whose history was probably
self-serving about Ptolemy; Aristobulus, an engineer; Callisthenes, the official
historian, who was executed on campaign; Nearchus, who commanded the fleet;
Onesicritus, who was a fabulist. All of it is lost.

What exists is five accounts written between roughly three hundred and five
hundred years later, by people working from those lost sources and from each
other. Arrian, a Roman senator and governor writing in the second century AD,
used Ptolemy and Aristobulus and is the soberest. Plutarch wrote a biography
concerned with character. Quintus Curtius wrote in Latin, rhetorically, at an
uncertain date. Diodorus compiled. Justin epitomised somebody else's epitome.

So every statement about Alexander is at three removes minimum, filtered through
a tradition that had already decided what he was for. The Roman-era writers were
producing a figure for Roman purposes — a model for generals, a warning about
autocracy, an argument about fortune and virtue — and the underlying court
histories had been producing a figure for Macedonian purposes before that.

The shape recurs wherever a figure is famous enough to be useful. The
Saladin the modern world knows is substantially a nineteenth-century
construction; the Zenobia of the *Historia Augusta* is a fourth-century
invention; the Achaemenids are their enemies' account. Alexander is the extreme
version, because the volume of writing is enormous and the distance from the
events is total.

The personality — the drinking, the killing of Cleitus, the burning of
Persepolis, the divinity — cannot be adjudicated on evidence like that, and the
attempt has occupied two centuries of scholarship without settling anything.
What can be said is what an army did, where it went, what it captured, and what
happened when the man died. Those are the parts the tradition agrees on.
