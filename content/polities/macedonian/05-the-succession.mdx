Alexander died at Babylon in June 323 with no adult heir, no designated
successor, and by most accounts no clear instruction. His wife Roxane was
pregnant; his half-brother Arrhidaeus was held to be mentally incapable. The
army assembly and the senior officers settled on a joint kingship of the two —
an unborn child and an incompetent — with a regent to govern for them.

It lasted about as long as it deserved to. Within two years the regent was
murdered, within twenty both kings were dead, and the officers had stopped
pretending to rule on anyone's behalf and started calling themselves kings. The
wars of the successors ran for four decades and produced the Seleucid,
Ptolemaic and Antigonid states that shaped the eastern Mediterranean until Rome.

The interest for this site is structural. Every other polity here that
fragmented had something to fragment along: a patrimonial division among sons in
the Mongol and Seljuk cases, an administrative split in the Roman one, tribal
lines in the steppe khanates. Alexander's empire had a conquering army and a
captured Persian bureaucracy and nothing connecting the two except him. It is
the clearest demonstration in the corpus that conquest is not a state, and that
the difference is visible only when the conqueror dies.

The reach figure on this page — 5.2 million square kilometres — is therefore a
measurement of something that existed for about a decade. This site's longevity
axis says thirteen years, and the two numbers side by side are a more honest
portrait than either alone.
