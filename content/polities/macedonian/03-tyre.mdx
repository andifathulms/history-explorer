The longest operation of the campaign began over a religious formality and ended
with eight thousand people dead, and Arrian is careful enough to let you see both
ends of that.

## The request

Tyre did not defy him. Its ambassadors met him on the march — the king's own son
among them — and announced that the city had decided to do whatever he might
command. He commended both the city and its envoys.

Then he told them what he wanted: to enter Tyre and sacrifice to Heracles.

The temple there was, Arrian says, the most ancient mentioned in any history —
older than Cadmus leaving Phoenicia, older than the Argive Heracles the Greeks
knew. Alexander, who traced his own descent from Heracles, wanted to sacrifice in
it.

Tyre refused. It would obey him in everything and it would not let him inside the
walls, which for an island city with its own fleet was a reasonable position and
for Alexander was the only answer he could not accept. A king who is refused
entry to a temple he claims by descent has been told, in public, what his claim
is worth.

## The causeway

Tyre stood offshore behind a channel three fathoms deep at the worst of it, with
high walls and its own ships. Alexander decided to build a road out to it.

The strait was shallow and muddy near the shore, stakes could be driven into the
mud and the mud held the stones, and there was timber and rock in quantity. The
mole went out quickly while the water was shallow and nobody could reach the
workers — and then it slowed, because the walls were built so that labourers
could be shot from them, and because the Tyrians still had the sea and could row
to any point of the causeway and stop the work.

The Macedonians put two towers on the head of the mole, hung with hides against
fire-arrows, with engines on top.

Arrian notes what Alexander did about morale, and it is characteristic: he
lightened the work by walking about it, rousing men by speaking to them, and
giving money to the ones working hardest for the credit of it.

## What actually decided it

Not the mole. Ships.

The Phoenician and Cypriot squadrons that had been serving Darius came over to
Alexander once their own cities had fallen — which is the coastal strategy paying
off in the middle of the siege it was meant to support. Tyre lost the sea, and
with it the ability to interfere with the causeway or to be resupplied.

The end came from two directions at once. The Phoenicians burst the bar across
the Egypt-facing harbour and shattered the ships inside it; the Cypriots sailed
into the Sidon-facing harbour, which had no bar, and took the city on that side.
The Tyrians left the walls and rallied at the chapel of Agenor, and were destroyed
there.

## Why the assault went the way it did

Arrian gives the reason the killing was as thorough as it was, and it is not
generalship.

The Macedonians went in *full of rage* — angry at the length of the siege, and
angry because the Tyrians had taken some of their men captive sailing from Sidon,
carried them up to the top of the wall where the camp could see it, killed them
there, and thrown the bodies into the sea.

About eight thousand Tyrians were killed. Macedonian losses for the entire
seven-month siege were about four hundred.

Those who reached the temple of Heracles were given amnesty — most of the Tyrian
magistrates, the king Azemilcus, and a delegation of Carthaginians who had come to
their mother-city for the sacrifice, by ancient custom. The rest were sold.

The sanctuary Alexander had demanded entry to in the first place ended the siege
as the one place inside Tyre where a life was safe.
