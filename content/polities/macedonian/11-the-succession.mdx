Alexander died at Babylon in June 323 with no adult heir, no designated
successor, and by most accounts no clear instruction. His wife Roxane was
pregnant; his half-brother Arrhidaeus was held to be mentally incapable. The
army assembly and the senior officers settled on a joint kingship of the two —
an unborn child and an incompetent — with a regent to govern for them.

It lasted about as long as it deserved to. Within two years the regent was
murdered, within twenty both kings were dead, and the officers had stopped
pretending to rule on anyone's behalf and started calling themselves kings. The
wars of the successors ran for four decades and produced the Seleucid,
Ptolemaic and Antigonid states that shaped the eastern Mediterranean until Rome.

The failure is structural, and it is worth being exact about it. An empire that
fragments usually has something to fragment along: a patrimonial division among
sons, as with the Mongols and the Seljuks; an administrative split, as at Rome;
tribal lines, as in the steppe khanates. Those are all seams, and a seam implies
that something had been joined. Alexander's empire had a conquering army and a
captured Persian bureaucracy and nothing connecting the two except him. It came
apart not along lines but everywhere at once, because conquest is not a state,
and the difference between the two is invisible until the conqueror dies.

Five point two million square kilometres, held for roughly a decade. The extent
and the duration belong beside each other, and either figure alone is a
distortion of what the thing was.
