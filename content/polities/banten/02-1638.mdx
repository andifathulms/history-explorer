In 1638 an embassy came back from the Hijaz with the title of sultan for Abu'l
Mafakhir, granted by the Sharif of Mecca.

This corpus's Mataram record carries `investiture` for exactly the same grant,
obtained in 1641. Banten got there three years first.

Neither record is diminished by the order. What the two together show is that a
Javanese ruler in the 1630s who wanted a title that was not Javanese knew where
to send for one, that it took about three years, and that two rival courts on the
same island did it within a hundred and twenty months of each other — which makes
it a fashion rather than a stroke of individual genius, and fashions are more
interesting.

The value `investiture` exists on this site because `caliphal-investiture` named
a specific grantor and that was the anomaly. Its grantors now include a caliph, a
Ming emperor at Melaka, the Chinese court granting Dai Viet the title of King of
Annam, and — twice, on two Javanese records three years apart — the guardian of
the Hijaz. This corpus carries the Sharifate of Mecca as a record of its own, so
both ends of both transactions are on the site, which is unusual enough to be
worth a sentence.

## What Ageng did with it

Sultan Ageng Tirtayasa, who took the throne in 1651, ran Banten as the western
counterpart of what this corpus's Makassar record was in the east: a port open to
everybody, deliberately, against a Dutch company trying to close the archipelago.

English, Danish, French, Chinese and Indian traders were at Banten because they
could not be at Batavia, which was forty miles away. Ageng sent embassies to
England and to the Ottoman court, built a canal system, brought in Chinese
expertise for it, and spent thirty years refusing to sign the exclusive treaties
the company wanted.

Makassar was destroyed for that position in 1669. Banten survived it fifteen
years longer, and was ended by something the company could not have arranged
alone.
