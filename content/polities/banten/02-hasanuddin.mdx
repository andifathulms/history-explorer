The man who took Banten was not from Java. Nurullah came from Pasai in northern
Sumatra, a port that had been Muslim for more than two centuries; he had made
the pilgrimage to Mecca; and when he arrived at the court of Demak he was
received as what he was, a devout haji who had seen the world. He was given a
sister of the sultan in marriage, and it is reasonable to think that his
brother-in-law's adoption of the title of sultan and his conduct as protector
of the faith owed something to what the traveller had brought back with him.

In 1524 or 1525 Nurullah sailed from Demak to Banten. He is remembered as Sunan
Gunungjati.

Banten was already a town of some standing under the Sundanese kings, and there
was an older settlement upstream, Banten Girang, whose name appears in the Old
Sundanese *Carita Parahyangan*. With military help from Demak, Nurullah pushed
the Sundanese governor aside and took the port. Two years later came Sunda
Kelapa, and after that the other Sundanese harbours, one by one, until the
inland capital at Pakuan was cut off from the sea entirely. He never marched on
Pakuan. He did not need to.

## A cannon with a name

In 1528 or 1529, Sultan Trenggana sent his son-in-law a present: a large cannon
cast at Demak with the year on it.

It carried another name first and then was always called Ki Jimat, as though
the thing itself were the kingdom's amulet, and it was still standing in the
kampung of Karang Antu in the first half of the twentieth century. It was
probably cast by a Portuguese renegade from the Algarve, Khoja Zainul-Abidin,
which is the whole sixteenth century in one object: an Iberian gunfounder
working for a Javanese sultan, casting the weapon that would be pointed at
Iberian ships.

Guns kept turning up in the folklore of the western ports the way holy men did
in the folklore of the interior. Ki Amuk, mounted on the defence works at the
harbour, was expected to put a Portuguese carrack on the bottom with a single
well-aimed shot. Behind the stories is a sober calculation. Javanese ships
could not match Portuguese ships, as everyone who had watched Melaka knew;
heavy guns ashore were the answer to guns afloat.

## Lampung

Nurullah's elder son had been left at Cirebon as his deputy and died young,
about 1552, and the death took the father to Cirebon for good. The second son,
Hasanuddin, was already at Banten. He married a daughter of Trenggana in 1552,
and he ruled Banten and Jakarta as his father's authority receded.

Banten's chronicles, from the seventeenth century onward, make Hasanuddin the
first king of Banten and the founder of the line. He was not, and the mistake
is worth noticing: a dynasty that measures itself from him is a dynasty
measuring from the first ruler who was simply a Javanese Muslim sultan rather
than a travelling saint from Sumatra.

What he did was pick up the trade. He carried the authority of the Muslim kings
of Java across the strait into Lampung and the neighbouring districts of
southern Sumatra, where the common speech was Malay. Those districts turned out
to be a very large producer of pepper, and the pepper made Banten a port that
Chinese, Indian and European ships called at. The period in which Banten and
its Javanese officials shaped the government and the culture of Lampung runs
from the middle of the sixteenth century to the end of the eighteenth.

He also built. The port town was enlarged and given a new name in his time,
below the older Banten Girang upriver — probably an ambitious young ruler's way
of marking a marriage into the house of Demak.

Hasanuddin is thought to have died in 1570, the same year as his father. He was
buried at Saba Kingking, close to Banten, and his descendants came there as
pilgrims. But he was never given the religious honour his father had. There is
a difference, in Java, between the tomb of a saint and the tomb of a king, and
the Banten line began exactly where that difference opens.
