In 1527 a Demak expedition took Banten and then took Sunda Kelapa, the port at
the mouth of the Ciliwung that the Sundanese kingdom had promised to the
Portuguese five years earlier. It was renamed Jayakarta.

The Dutch company took it in 1619 and called it Batavia, and ran everything it
did in Asia from there for three hundred and twenty-two years. The name it has
now is the one given in 1527 by the force that arrived to stop a Hindu kingdom
selling the harbour to Europeans.

Banten is what that expedition turned into. Hasanuddin, whose father had led
the campaign, was ruling it as a sultanate of his own by 1552 — while Demak
itself was coming apart in the succession war that followed Trenggana's death.
The conquest outlived the conqueror by seceding from him.

## Pepper, again

Banten lived on exactly what Aceh lived on, three thousand kilometres away at
the other end of Sumatra: a toll at the port, and a crown monopoly on pepper.

Both lived on the same crop. Banten taxed it at the port and held a crown
monopoly over the growing districts of Lampung, across the Sunda strait in
southern Sumatra; Aceh did the same on the coasts it controlled at the northern
tip.

And both monopolies failed in the same way. A crown monopoly on a bulk crop
depends on being the only buyer, and a European company with ships and credit
could buy upcountry, from the growers, without calling at the capital at all.
Once that was possible the monopoly was a claim rather than a mechanism.

Two sultanates at opposite ends of Sumatra's pepper trade, with the same
arrangement, broken by the same manoeuvre, about eighty years apart.
