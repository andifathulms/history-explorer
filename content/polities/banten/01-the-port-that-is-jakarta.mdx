In 1527 a Demak expedition took Banten and then took Sunda Kelapa, the port at
the mouth of the Ciliwung that the Sundanese kingdom had promised to the
Portuguese five years earlier. It was renamed Jayakarta.

The Dutch company took it in 1619 and called it Batavia, and ran everything it
did in Asia from there for three hundred and twenty-two years. The name it has
now is the one given in 1527 by the force that arrived to stop a Hindu kingdom
selling the harbour to Europeans.

Banten is what that expedition turned into. Hasanuddin, whose father had led
the campaign, was ruling it as a sultanate of his own by 1552 — while Demak
itself was coming apart in the succession war that followed Trenggana's death.
The conquest outlived the conqueror by seceding from him.

## Pepper

Banten lived on a toll at the port and a crown monopoly on pepper grown in
Lampung, across the Sunda strait in southern Sumatra.

That is a workable arrangement for as long as the crown is the only buyer.
Everything grown upcountry has to come down to the harbour, and at the harbour
sits the sultan's price. It is also the arrangement most exposed to a European
company with ships and credit, because such a company can go to the growers
directly and buy where the pepper is, and once it can do that the monopoly is a
claim rather than a mechanism.

The whole political history that follows — the alliance with the Lampung
districts, the openness to Chinese, Indian, English, Danish and Portuguese
shipping, the long refusal to sign an exclusive contract with the Dutch, and
finally the war between a father and a son — is an argument about who gets to
stand between the pepper and the sea.
