Raffles has to be read as what he was — the Lieutenant-Governor who had just
taken Java, writing an account of the disorder his own government had come in
to settle. The judgements are his case. The detail is worth having, because it
is the fullest description of what happened to Banten in the century and a
quarter between the treaty and the abolition, and because at the end of it he
quotes his own terms.

By the time the British arrived, he writes, Banten — "once rich in its pepper
plantations and the emporium of the Archipelago" — had lost all its trade and
all its importance, from the restrictions of the Dutch company and the nearness
of Batavia. The succession to the throne was generally disposed of by the Dutch
government. The administration of the country and the collection of the port
duties were still left to the sultan, and European interference, resisted hard
at first, had long been acquiesced in.

## 1808

Then the Dutch government tried to get more out of the province.

Coffee was to be planted. New roads were to be opened. A new harbour was to be
built, first at Mew Bay and then at Merak Bay. All of it fell on the people as
forced labour, and so many deserted from the works that the Resident was
ordered to tell the sultan his first minister would be held answerable for the
task being done. The desertions continued. A more peremptory order followed:
the sultan was to hand the minister over. Going to enforce it, the Resident
exposed himself and was murdered.

A large military force was sent. The reigning sultan was deposed and banished
to Ambon, and a relative was put in his place under regulations dictated by the
Dutch — for whom, Raffles notes, "so fallen had the sovereigns of this once
flourishing and powerful kingdom now become" that no treaty was thought
necessary. The new sultan ceded the territory nearest Batavia and administered
the rest under Dutch superior authority.

The works went on and so did the risings. A lieutenant and eighteen dragoons
were surprised and killed. Princes and chiefs were murdered by their own
people. A second sultan was got rid of by being lured aboard a ship and carried
to Batavia, and a chief was installed as sultan of the highlands while the
Dutch took the lowlands into their own hands.

## Pangeran Ahmad

At that point the country produced a leader of the ordinary kind, and the
European authorities could not catch him.

Pangeran Ahmad gathered the discontented of every description. A permanent
garrison was kept in Banten and every attempt on him failed. He was offered
part of the country and would not take it. In the end the Dutch decided to
abandon the interior to him and wait for the inhabitants to grow tired enough
of him to come in for protection.

They had not grown tired when the British blockade arrived in 1811. Ahmad
supplied the cruisers offshore with provisions plundered from the people of the
country, and the officers took him for an unfortunate prince maintaining his
independence against the Dutch. When British troops landed, the sultan of
Banten was Ahmad's prisoner and the province was in Ahmad's hands.

## Ten thousand Spanish dollars

Raffles' summary of what the British inherited is that the fertile districts
were held by a lawless rebel, the inhabitants were in revolt, and for several
years there had been universal anarchy and distrust; that no revenue for
government could be raised at all; and that a general settlement under European
control was the most that could be hoped for.

His sentence about the end is this: "In the year 1813 the sultan voluntarily
resigned the administration of the country into the hands of the British
government, in consideration of an annual pension of ten thousand Spanish
dollars."

The adverb is doing a great deal of work. The sultan who resigned had recently
been a rebel's prisoner in a province where two of his predecessors had been
deposed and deported by a European government, and where the throne's occupant
had been chosen abroad for a generation. A land survey followed, cultivator by
cultivator, in the northern divisions.

The pepper port that refused the company's exclusive contracts for thirty years
under Ageng ended as a line in a revenue settlement, and its last sultan ended
as a pensioner priced in the currency of yet another empire.
