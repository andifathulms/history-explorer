In 1638 an embassy came back from the Hijaz with the title of sultan for Abu'l
Mafakhir, granted by the Sharif of Mecca.

Mataram, inland and hostile, obtained exactly the same grant three years later.
Banten got there first.

Neither court is diminished by the order, and the pair is more interesting than
either. What it shows is that a Javanese ruler in the 1630s who wanted a title
that was not Javanese knew precisely where to send for one, that the round trip
took about three years, and that two rival courts on the same island did it
within a decade of each other. That makes it a fashion rather than a stroke of
individual genius — and a fashion tells you more, because it means the idea was
in the air and both of them breathed it.

It is also worth noticing who was selling. The sharifs of Mecca had almost no
army and very little land, and lived largely on what sovereigns paid them for
recognition of exactly this kind. A Javanese sultan buying legitimacy from the
Hijaz and a sharif living off the proceeds are two halves of one transaction,
and the transaction ran the length of the Muslim world.

It is the high point of the sultanate's standing abroad, and it came in the
middle of a reign that had begun with an infant on the throne and a decade of
regents quarrelling over him. Abu'l Mafakhir had lost Jayakarta to the Dutch in
1619 and would not get it back. What he could still do, in 1638, was be
recognised in Mecca before the greatest kingdom on his own island was.
