The Chinese community at Banten was large, old, and the reason the pepper trade
worked.

They were the buyers, financing growers on advance and consolidating small lots
for export. They were the engineers and the labour for Ageng's canal. They
farmed revenues, ran the mint at various points, and provided the shipping that
took Banten's pepper north to China — which is where most of it went, not to
Europe.

The same is true of every major port in these seas. There were Chinese
communities at Melaka before 1511, at Makassar, at Ayutthaya, at Brunei since
before the sultanate, and at Batavia in very large numbers. Melaka ran four
*syahbandars* — harbourmasters, one for each of the main merchant communities —
and one of the four was for the Chinese.

## Neither subjects nor foreigners

What makes them hard to describe is that they fit none of the ordinary
categories.

They were not an arm of the state, like the Mongol *ortogh* merchant
partnerships financed by princes to farm taxes and run the long-distance trade.
They were not subjects in the way the Javanese cultivators of Lampung were
subjects. And they were not visitors: many were resident for generations,
married locally, and in several ports made up a quarter or more of the town.

A resident foreign merchant community that financed the crop, moved the export,
lent to the crown and had its own law in practice and its own market
connections abroad is a constitutional fact about a place. It is also invisible
in almost every summary of what a polity was, because summaries are built
around rulers, revenue and territory, and this is none of those.

## And what happened to them

Nothing good, mostly.

In 1740 the Dutch company killed something like ten thousand Chinese residents
of Batavia — its own capital, sixty miles east along the same coast, fifty-six
years after Banten's sultanate ended. The survivors fled inland and east, and
their arrival set off a war that pulled the company deeper into Javanese
politics than it had ever been.

So the community that had financed the pepper, moved the exports and lent to
the sultans enters the standard narrative of Java at the point where it is
massacred, and is remembered there for the consequences the massacre had for
somebody else.
