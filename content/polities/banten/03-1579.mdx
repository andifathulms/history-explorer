The Hindu kingdom of Pajajaran had been shut off from its own coast for half a
century when Banten finally went inland after it.

The reasons are not mysterious and not especially religious. A Muslim ruler at
Banten and Jakarta did not like having a Sundanese kingdom at his back; he had
to worry about whether the produce of the interior would keep coming down to
his harbour; and there was the prospect of plunder. Banten's own chronicle
gives the credit for the capture of Pakuan to Hasanuddin, which is almost
certainly wrong. The city seems to have fallen in 1579, when his son Yusup had
been ruling for nine years.

The *Sadjarah Banten* tells it without miracles, which is why it is believable.
An official of the Pajajaran king betrayed the place: he opened a gate to his
own brother, who commanded part of the Banten force. When the palace was taken
it was found that the king and his family had gone. The West Javanese
chronicles never mention them again.

Two details in that account matter more than the battle. Sundanese Muslims were
already fighting on Banten's side. And after the city fell, the Sundanese
nobility became Muslim and were confirmed in their ranks and titles — which is
how a conquest becomes an administration in one generation.

Yusup died about a year after his victory, still young, after a reign of ten
years.

## The kadi, 1580

His son was a small child, and the obvious claimant was elsewhere in the
family: Yusup's brother, who had succeeded their formidable aunt as ruler of
Jepara. He came to Banten by sea with an armed following. In the fighting
between the great men of Jepara and those of Banten his admiral was killed, and
having lost the servant he could least spare he turned round and sailed home.

What happened next was decided in the town. The *kali* — the chief Islamic
judge — acted, and the great men of the kingdom acknowledged the child Molana
Muhammad as king, with the judge and four others governing during the minority.

The refusal of the prince of Jepara, who was treated as an outsider, and the
elevation of a minor by his own officials, was read in West Java afterwards as
the moment the region stopped being an appendage of the courts of central Java.
Many of the local lords of Banten and Jakarta were Sundanese by then, or
married into the Sundanese families that had mattered under Pajajaran. Cirebon
became the frontier, in culture as much as in politics.

The other consequence lasted longer. Conversion in West Java had been carried
out by force at Banten, at Sunda Kelapa and at Pakuan, and the men who carried
it kept a corresponding weight in government. The chief Islamic judge at Banten
still held a position at court in the nineteenth century that the head penghulu
of Yogyakarta or Surakarta never had.

## Palembang, 1596

There was a guest at Banten in those years: the last claimant of the house of
Demak, driven out of central Java by Mataram, who had tried Portuguese Melaka
first and then come to his kinsmen here.

He was a little older than Molana Muhammad, and he had an idea. He still had,
he maintained, a claim on the kingdom of Palembang, and an expedition there
would also extend the territory of Islam. He talked his devout young cousin
into it.

Banten laid siege to Palembang, and Molana Muhammad was killed under its walls.
He was about twenty-five. The event is recorded in the account of the first
Dutch voyage to the Indies, whose ships were on the coast that year.

His heir, Abdul Kadir, was a few months old. For the rest of the century and
the first decade of the next, Banten was governed by older relatives acting as
regents for an infant, and the changes of regent and the quarrels between
princes did the kingdom no good at all.
