Sultan Ageng Tirtayasa took the throne in 1651 and ran Banten for thirty years
as the western counterpart of what Makassar was in the east: a port open to
everybody, deliberately, against a Dutch company trying to close the
archipelago.

English, Danish, French, Portuguese, Chinese, Gujarati and Persian traders were
at Banten because they could not be at Batavia, which is forty miles down the
coast. He sent embassies to England, to the Ottoman court and to Mecca. He
refused the exclusive treaties the company wanted, for three decades, and made
the refusal the basis of his revenue.

## The canal

The other thing he is remembered for is infrastructure. Ageng had a canal cut
inland from Banten toward Pontang, some thirty kilometres, with Chinese
engineers and Chinese labour, to open new rice land and move produce.

That is worth noting on a record coded `trade-toll` and `monopoly`. A ruler
whose income was pepper and customs spent heavily on agriculture, and the
reason is the one Makassar demonstrates at the other end of Java: a free port
needs to feed the people who come to it, and a polity that has to import its
food cannot afford to annoy the power that controls the sea.

Ageng had watched what happened to Makassar in 1669 — destroyed for exactly the
policy he was running — and the canal is what a man draws from that.

## Two open ports, fifteen years apart

Makassar fell in 1669. Banten fell in 1684.

The two records are the same argument and the same defeat, and between them
they close the archipelago. After 1684 there was nowhere in the islands to buy
pepper or spices outside the Dutch company's system, which had been the
company's object since it arrived.

What is worth holding onto is that neither was defeated on the merits of the
policy. Makassar was destroyed by a company fighting alongside a Bugis prince
whose people Makassar had conquered — the chapter argues it was a South
Sulawesi war the company joined. Banten was ended by the sultan's own son.

Two open ports, two closures, and in both cases the company's decisive asset
was a local quarrel it did not create.
