Mahmud of Ghazna wanted to be remembered as a patron of letters. He got his
wish, in the sense that he is remembered for nothing else — and for failing at
it, in one line, on the word of a poet he underpaid.

## What he was buying

Browne is blunt about the mechanism. The literary and scientific activity of
which Mahmud's court became for a while the focus was assembled, he says, by
virtue rather of compulsion than attraction. Scholars were sent for. Al-Biruni
came to Ghazna because Khwarazm was annexed and he came with it. The court was
famous because the sultan could afford it and could compel attendance, which is
not the same thing as being a centre of learning and produces, for a generation,
very similar results.

Mahmud himself was the son of Sebuktigin, who had been the slave of Alptigin,
who had been the slave of the Samanids — so, as Firdawsi observed, the son of
the slave of a slave. He is said to have been the first Muslim ruler to take the
title *sultan*, and he styled himself the Shadow of God on His Earth. At the
very end of his life, Ibn al-Athir records him crucifying Isma'ilis, exiling
Mu'tazilites, and burning books of philosophy and science.

He had spent thirty years assembling a reputation. It took one satire to spend
it.

## The satire

Firdawsi had worked on the *Shahnama* for thirty years and presented it at
Ghazna, and the reward was not what he had been led to expect. What he wrote
about it afterwards made full use of the pedigree — the grandson of a slave
sitting in judgement on the national epic — and it worked.

Browne's summary of the effect is the sharpest sentence in his chapter: the
satire turned, as it were, in a breath into infamy that reputation as a patron
of letters which the King so eagerly desired.

Five hundred years later Jami wrote the epitaph, and Browne translates it:

> Gone is the greatness of Mahmud, departed his glory,
> And shrunk to "He knew not the worth of Firdawsi" his story.

## Whether it is fair

Almost certainly not, in the details. The reward story exists in several
incompatible versions, the figures are literary, and the surviving satire may
not be entirely Firdawsi's. Browne, who spends a good deal of vol two showing
how later anthologists reassigned anecdotes to more famous men, is well aware of
it.

It is fair in substance, which is the part that mattered. A ruler who compels
scholars to attend him, pays them by the line, and expects a reputation in
return, has entered a transaction in which the other party sets the price
afterwards and has the last word by profession. Mahmud invaded India twelve
times, broke the Samanids, absorbed the Ziyarids and pushed back the Buyids, and
none of that is what his name carries.

## The other thing that outlived him

There is a second afterlife and it is in the same book.

Firdawsi finished the *Shahnama* at the court of the man who had just destroyed
the dynasty that commissioned the Persian revival, and the poem is about the
kings of Iran and their war against Turan. Mahmud was a Turk, the son of a
slave, ruling from an Afghan mountain town, paying for the definitive account of
Iranian kingship in Persian verse — in which people like him are the enemy.

He paid badly and the poem survived anyway, which is roughly what happened to
everything he touched.
