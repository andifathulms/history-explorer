The war Mas'ud lost in Khurasan was not lost in an afternoon. Barthold sets out
the asymmetry and then declines to narrate the campaigns, on the grounds that
they are all the same campaign: a Ghaznavid army much more numerous and much
better armed than the Seljuks, and encumbered in advance by its own baggage,
against light divisions of nomads for whom the steppe was "father and mother"
and who could leave their baggage a hundred and twenty miles behind the fighting
and not care.

The winter before was a fair sample. In January Mas'ud got as far as the pass of
Shuniyan and a despatch reached him saying the Seljuks were moving from Sarakhs
towards Guzgan, apparently meaning to reach Tirmidh, destroy the bridge and cut
him off from his own territory. The pass in front of him was held by a Qarakhanid
prince who knew the country and he did not. He turned round on 12 January and
took a fortnight to reach Tirmidh, pursued the whole way and losing part of his
baggage, his camels and his horses to a man whose reputation the retreat made.

In May 1040, at a fortress forty miles from Merv called Dandanqan, it ended. A
throne was put up on the battlefield and Tughril Beg sat on it and was acclaimed
amir of Khurasan, and letters went out announcing the victory to both khans of
Turkestan and to every Turkish prince who might be interested. Fugitives from the
Ghaznavid army were deliberately chased as far as the Oxus so that they would
cross it and be seen in Transoxiana, where their arrival would confirm what the
letters said.

Mas'ud's own reaction is the part that dates the collapse. He wrote to the
Qarakhanid ruler expressing confidence that Arslan Khan would not refuse him help
and might even join the campaign in person — Barthold notes that Bayhaqi drafted
the letter — while privately concluding that he would lose not merely Balkh and
its provinces but Ghazna itself. His vizier and his nobles told him there was no
cause for that. He decided to withdraw to India anyway, sent the Qarakhanid prince
diplomas for Balkh and Tukharistan in the hope of setting him against the Seljuks,
and gave the nobles he was leaving behind at Ghazna formal permission to take
service with the Seljuks when they arrived. He was deposed and killed in January
1041.

The despair turned out to be premature, which is the thing usually left out.
Mas'ud's son Mawdud took the throne in April 1041 and held Balkh and Tirmidh; the
Turkish ruler in Transoxiana submitted to him. The commander of Tirmidh, Amirak
Bayhaqi, held that town against the Seljuks for fifteen years and only gave it up
when there was no Ghaznavid hope left anywhere, and when the Seljuk offered him
the vizierate for it he refused and went home.

So Khurasan was gone and the dynasty was not. What Dandanqan settled was which
direction the Ghaznavids would face for the rest of their existence.
