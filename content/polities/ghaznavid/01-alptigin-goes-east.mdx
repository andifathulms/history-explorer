---
title: Alptigin goes east
drafted_from: bosworth-1963
phase: formation
---

Alptigin was Turkish, bought as a boy, raised in the Samanid slave establishment
at Bukhara, and promoted to commander of the guard and then governor of
Khurasan. That was a career, and by the standards of the system it was the top
of one.

In 961 the amir Abd al-Malik died and Alptigin backed a candidate who lost. The
new regime at Bukhara was not going to leave him in Khurasan. So in 962 he took
his personal troops east, out of the settled provinces, to Ghazna in the Afghan
highlands, and made himself its lord.

Nothing about this was a declaration. He remained, in his own presentation, a
Samanid officer holding a place; his successors at Ghazna continued to
acknowledge Bukhara, strike coin in the Samanid amir's name, and accept
appointments. The relationship on the ground was that the Samanids could not
remove him and did not try.

This is the same move the Samanids themselves had made against the Tahirids and
the Tahirids against the caliphate, performed one link further down the chain
and by a man who was, legally, property. The system that produced him was
designed to guarantee that he could never do it: a slave soldier has no tribe,
no land and no lineage, and therefore no base to defect with. What it did not
account for was that the troops were loyal to their commander rather than to
the institution, and that a commander with troops does not need a base. He can
go and take one.

Fourteen years later, Sebuktigin — Alptigin's own slave, and his son-in-law —
took Ghazna the same way. The dynasty is dated from him.
