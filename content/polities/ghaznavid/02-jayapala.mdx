Al-Utbi worked for the Ghaznavids and wrote about them accordingly: the Indian
kings are "the accursed", every Ghaznavid decision is generous, every victory
fulfils a verse. Read past the varnish and his account of Sebuktigin's Indian
wars is still the best one there is, partly because he records the arguments the
Ghaznavids had among themselves and the terms they actually agreed to.

Sebuktigin had been taking hill forts along the eastern approaches for some
years when Jayapala, king of the Punjab, worked out what was happening to his
frontier. Al-Utbi lets him reason it through: his border was shrinking a piece
at a time, his inherited kingdom would go to the winds if he did nothing, and
the world would judge him harshly for letting it. So he called in his
feudatories and allies and came up to the Muslim frontier with a great army,
hoping the sight of it would settle the matter.

The two armies met on the border and fought each other to a standstill, and
then the weather decided it. Al-Utbi reports a spring in that country which
was clear and sweet as long as nothing unclean went into it, but which, if
anything foul were thrown in, produced lightning, violent wind and bitter cold.
Sebuktigin ordered wine-flasks thrown into it. Darkness came over the sky, the
day went dim, and a freezing mist settled on the armies until, al-Utbi says, no
one could endure it and they were close to death. Jayapala asked for terms.

The terms he offered were everything: submission at discretion, annual tribute,
acknowledgement of Sebuktigin's authority over his states, and elephants. The
old amir was inclined to accept and to distribute the proceeds among his
commanders. His son Mahmud — al-Utbi, writing under him, calls him Sultan
throughout even at this date — refused outright and said the thing could only be
finished by storming the camp, that granting a truce to men in that condition
would be unworthy of the glory of Islam, and quoted scripture at his father.

What changed Sebuktigin's mind was Jayapala's reply, which is the one speech in
the book that is allowed to be dignified. You know, he said, how Indians behave
in extremity: they do not fear death, and in a matter of honour they will lay
themselves on the fire like roasting meat and on the dagger like sunlight. If
you are refusing us terms because you want our wealth, our elephants, our women
and our children, then when it comes to it we will put every one of them and
everything we own, living and dead, into the fire, and then kill ourselves, and
you will have dust and ashes. Sebuktigin thought they would do it, overruled his
son, and settled for a ransom: a thousand purses of a thousand dinars each, five
stables of elephants, some cities and fortresses, and the sons of Jayapala, his
courtiers and his senior officers as hostages until the cessions were made.

Jayapala got a few marches from the frontier, decided he was safe, and broke the
treaty, arresting the officers sent to take over the ceded forts in retaliation
for his own people held at Ghazna. Sebuktigin refused to believe the first
reports. When they were confirmed he went back and destroyed the country he had
spared: plundered, burnt the buildings, took the children and the cattle, left
Lamghan stripped bare, and put mosques where the temples had been.

Jayapala then wrote to every province in India for help, raised a hundred
thousand horse, and marched on Ghazna. Sebuktigin went forward to meet him and
rode into a thicket in disguise to count them, and saw an army like an ocean.
His answer was a tactic rather than a charge: five hundred men were to attack
and hold until they had nothing left, then five hundred more were to take their
place, and so on in succession. It worked. The Indian line was worn down, one
combined charge broke it, and the rest threw away their arms and went. The
frontier districts were annexed, and al-Utbi says that from then on a thousand
horse attended Sebuktigin's stirrup whenever he wanted them.

The last thing in this stretch of the book is the one that mattered most.
The Samanid amir Nuh, with an army out of the Turkish provinces taking his
inheritance away from him, sent to Ghazna asking for help. Sebuktigin went. The
reward for going was Khurasan, and the governorship of it for his son.
