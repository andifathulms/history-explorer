When Bukhara fell to the Karakhanids in 999, Mahmud was governing Khurasan in
Samanid name. He did not march north. He kept Khurasan, agreed the Oxus as a
frontier with the men who had destroyed his overlords, and turned south.

Then he did something the Samanids had never bothered with. He applied to
Baghdad, and the caliph al-Qadir sent him a diploma of investiture and the title
*yamin al-dawla*. Mahmud was the first ruler in this region to be styled
*sultan* in regular usage, and he had gone to considerable trouble to obtain a
piece of paper from an institution that had no soldiers within a thousand miles.

The trouble was worth it because of what he intended to do. Seventeen campaigns
into northern India, over three decades, presented as jihad and financed by the
proceeds of the previous one, need a frame — and a caliphal investiture is the
frame. The temple at Somnath in 1026 is the campaign everyone remembers, and it
was remembered by design; the loot paid for Ghazna, and Ghazna paid for a court.

That court is why the Ghaznavids are more than a raiding operation. Firdawsi
presented the *Shahnama* to Mahmud, badly rewarded and famously resentful about
it. Al-Biruni, taken from Khwarazm when Mahmud annexed it, wrote the *Kitab
al-Hind* — the most careful account of Indian science and religion produced by
any medieval Muslim scholar — under a patron whose Indian policy was the one
described above.

The administrative apparatus, the Persian chancery, the slave-soldier corps and
the court model were all inherited from Bukhara essentially unaltered, and
Mahmud made a public argument out of it. He did not merely run a copy of the
Samanid state; he presented himself as the man who had rightfully carried it
on.
