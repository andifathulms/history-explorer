---
title: Seventeen campaigns, and what was made of one of them
drafted_from: thapar-2004
phase: peak
---

Mahmud campaigned into northern India roughly seventeen times over thirty
years. The pattern was consistent and it was not conquest: an army went down
from Ghazna in the campaigning season, took what a rich temple town or a
defeated prince could be made to yield, and came back. Only the Punjab was
annexed. The rest was a revenue operation with a religious frame — presented as
jihad, financed by the previous year's proceeds, and paying for a court at
Ghazna that was the most expensive in the eastern Islamic world.

The raid on the temple of Somnath in Gujarat in 1026 is the one everybody
knows, and it is the reason this chapter is drafted from a book about memory
rather than from a chronicle.

What happened is not seriously in doubt in outline: a long march across the
desert, a rich coastal temple taken and stripped, heavy killing, and the return
to Ghazna with a quantity of gold. What Thapar's account establishes is how
little the event's later fame corresponds to its contemporary footprint. The
Sanskrit inscriptions of the region, the merchant and royal biographies, the
local chronicles, the court epics — the sources produced by the people it
happened to — say strikingly little about it. Somnath was rebuilt and endowed
afterwards, including with the participation of Muslim traders resident on that
coast, whose presence in the same ports continued through the whole period.
Trade across the Arabian Sea did not stop.

The event acquired its stature in stages: in the Persian court literature that
made Mahmud an idol-breaking hero for Ghaznavid and later audiences, in
Turco-Persian romance, and then — decisively — in nineteenth-century British
parliamentary rhetoric and colonial historiography, from which it entered
modern political argument as the founding injury of a civilisational quarrel.
Each of those layers had a use for it. None of them is the eleventh century.

It is worth being exact about what the raids did and did not accomplish, because
they are the famous part and they are not the consequential one. Raiding is not
conversion. Nothing Mahmud carried off from Somnath made a single Muslim. What
did establish Islam in northern India was the garrison and the court his
successors kept at Lahore for a century and a half after they had lost
everything west of the Indus, and that is the subject of a later chapter.
