In 1186 Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad of Ghur took Lahore. Khusrau Malik surrendered
under terms, was imprisoned, and was killed a few years later with his son. The
line ended there.

The Ghurids had been Ghaznavid vassals. Their conversion to Islam, their entry
into the wider political world, and their early standing all came through
Ghazna. They were the client house in the mountains that Mahmud's successors
used and did not think about much.

Set the two ends of this page against each other. The Ghaznavids begin with a
Samanid slave-general who took a frontier town and outlived his masters. They
end with a vassal house from the highlands that took their capital and
extinguished them. The dynasty is bracketed at both ends by the same mechanism,
running once in its favour and once against it.

Then it runs a third time. Mu'izz al-Din's Indian conquests were consolidated
by his own Turkish slave-generals, and when he died in 1206 one of them, Qutb
al-Din Aibak, kept the Indian provinces and founded the Delhi Sultanate.

Alptigin to Aibak is two and a half centuries and three repetitions of a single
move: the trusted instrument, having no legitimacy of its own, takes the
territory instead.
