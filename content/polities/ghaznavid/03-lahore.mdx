title: Pushed into the Punjab

In 1040 at Dandanaqan, near Merv, Mas'ud I lost Khurasan to the Seljuks in a
single engagement. The Ghaznavid army broke, and the western half of the empire
was gone within a year.

What is unusual is that the dynasty then lasted another century and a half.
Deprived of Khurasan, the Ghaznavids consolidated on what remained — eastern
Afghanistan and the Punjab — and the centre of the state drifted steadily
towards the Indian provinces that had originally been the raiding ground.

In 1151 Ala al-Din Husayn of Ghur, a vassal house from the mountains between
Ghazna and Herat, sacked Ghazna itself and earned the epithet *Jahansuz*, the
world-burner. The Ghaznavids retook the city, lost it again to Ghuzz Turkmen in
1163, and moved the capital permanently to Lahore.

The last Ghaznavid century is therefore an Indian one, and that is the durable
result. Lahore became a Persianate capital: a chancery working in Persian, a
court literature, an established Sunni institutional life in a region that had
had none of these. Everything the Delhi Sultanate would later run on had a
working model three hundred miles up the road, built by a dynasty that had
arrived intending to raid.
