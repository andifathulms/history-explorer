The Fatimid army that had just destroyed the Aghlabids at Raqqada came west and
took Tahert in 909, and the imamate ended in a sack.

It is worth noticing how quickly. The Kutama campaign of 909 finished two states
in the same year — the Aghlabid emirate at Kairouan and this one on the plateau —
and then went on across the Maghreb to Sijilmasa to collect the imprisoned man in
whose name the whole thing had been done. Three of the four polities of the
central and western Maghreb were removed by one army in one season.

## Why Tahert could not survive it

Because the thing that had kept it alive for a hundred and thirty years was
nobody wanting it enough.

Tahert had no gold, no great agricultural surplus and no strategic position worth
an expedition. It sat on a crossroads and taxed the traffic, and every power that
might have taken it had better uses for an army. What changed in 909 was that a
movement arrived whose objective was not any particular town but the whole
Maghreb, and to which the crossroads of the central plateau was simply on the
way.

There is also the doctrinal side, and it is not incidental. The Fatimids were
Isma'ili Shi'a claiming the imamate by descent from Ali and Fatima. The Rustamids
were Ibadi Kharijites holding that the imamate belongs to whichever qualified
Muslim the community elects, and that descent confers nothing at all. Those are
not two positions that can share a country.

## What was destroyed

The library, mostly, and this is the loss the Ibadi tradition itself dwells on.

Tahert had accumulated a century and a half of Ibadi scholarship — legal
opinions, correspondence with Basra, the records of the community's own disputes
about its own elections. Much of it went in 909. What survives of early Ibadi
law and history survives largely because copies had gone out to the Nafusa
mountains and to Jerba and to Oman, which is what a network of that kind is for
and is the reason the tradition did not end with the state.

## And then they left

The surviving leadership and a substantial part of the community did not submit
and did not disperse. They went south, out of the plateau and into the
pre-Saharan steppe, and started again in a worse place on purpose — which is the
subject of the next chapter and the most interesting decision anybody in this
history made.
