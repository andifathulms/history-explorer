Six hundred years after Tahert was sacked, a Granadan diplomat who had travelled
the Maghreb for the sultan of Fez wrote a description of Africa for an Italian
readership, and he passed through the country the Ibadis had withdrawn into.

Leo Africanus is describing the Sahara of the 1510s and 1520s from his own
journeys, and what makes his account useful here is not what he says but what he
does not. He was a Muslim of the Maliki west writing for Christians, and he had
no reason to notice a sect that had spent six centuries taking care not to be
noticed.

## What he found

Of the Mzab — he calls it Meszab — the entry is four lines long:

> This region being situate vpon the Numidian desert, 300. miles eastward from
> Tegorarin, and 300. miles also from the Mediterran sea, containeth sixe
> castles, and many villages, the inhabitants being rich, and vsing traffike to
> the land of Negros. Likewise the Negro-merchants, togither with them of Bugia
> and Ghir make resort vnto this region.

Six walled towns in a rock valley with no water worth the name, inhabited by rich
people, trading south to the Sudan, and drawing merchants from the coast and from
the desert to come to them. Tributary, he adds, to the Arabs.

Ouargla, where the refugees from Tahert had first stopped, he gives more room to:
an ancient city founded by the Numidians, walled, built very sumptuously,
abounding in dates, with castles and a great number of villages attached. The
inhabitants are rich because they are near the kingdom of Agadez; merchants of
Tunis and Constantine come there to carry the goods of Barbary into the land of
the Blacks; flesh and corn being scarce, they live on ostrich and camel. They
have a governor whom they reverence as if he were a king, with two thousand
horsemen always attending him.

Of the nearby town of Touggourt, where Leo stayed some days with the young prince
and liked him, he records the local custom that struck him hardest: they favour
strangers exceedingly and send them away without payment, they would rather marry
their daughters to strangers than to their own citizens, and their generosity is
such that they heap gifts on men they are sure never to see again.

## What is not in it

Not one word about religion.

Leo names no sect anywhere in these entries, records no doctrinal peculiarity,
and gives no sign of knowing that the towns he is describing were built by
people who had walked out of a destroyed imamate rather than accept a ruler they
held to be illegitimate. He notes what a traveller notes: the walls, the dates,
the caravans, the tribute, the manners.

That is exactly what the Ibadi doctrine of *kitman* — concealment, the condition
under which a community lives when no imam can be established — is designed to
produce. A community practising it does not advertise, does not dispute in
public with outsiders, and answers a passing geographer's questions about
commerce. Leo's silence is not ignorance so much as the successful operation of
a six-hundred-year-old policy.

## And what is in it

The other thing to take from these four lines is the continuity of the business.

Tahert had been a crossroads town on a plateau with no natural wealth, living on
the traffic between the Saharan oases and the Mediterranean and on being a place
where people of different confessions could trade without anybody asking. The
Mzab in Leo's day is six walled towns in a worse place, living on the traffic
between the Sudan and the coast, and rich.

They moved the site twice, lost the state, kept the trade and kept the doctrine,
and were still doing all three when a Granadan on a diplomatic circuit rode past
and wrote down that the inhabitants were wealthy and used traffic to the land of
the Blacks.
