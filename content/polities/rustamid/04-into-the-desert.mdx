The Fatimid advance destroyed Tahert in 909, in the same campaign and the same
year that removed the Aghlabids on the other side of this region. The Kutama
army the previous page describes took the city; the imamate ended.

What the Ibadis did next is the reason this page has an ending chapter rather
than a sentence.

They went south. The surviving leadership and a substantial part of the
community withdrew from the plateau into the pre-Saharan steppe and founded
Sedrata, near modern Ouargla, and when that failed them they moved again into
the Mzab — a rock valley in the northern Sahara with almost no water and no
reason for anyone to want it. There, from the eleventh century, they built five
walled towns on five hilltops, dug wells and a flood-irrigation system for the
palm groves, and organised themselves under a council of religious scholars
rather than a ruler.

That arrangement is the doctrine of the second chapter, applied to a community
with no state. Ibadi law provides for exactly this: where no imam can be
established, the community lives under *kitman*, concealment, governed by its
own learned men. The Mzabi towns did that for eight hundred years. They are
still there, still Ibadi, still speaking their own Berber language, and the
scholarly tradition has been continuous.

Set that against the record on this page. The reach axis is null, the
population axis is null, and all three influence counts are null. The polity
lasted a hundred and thirty-three years and ended in a sack. The thing it was
carrying — a legal tradition, a way of constituting authority, and a community
that could operate without a state — is on Jerba, in the Nafusa mountains of
Libya, in the Mzab, and in Oman, where this site's other Ibadi imamate has been
reconstituted repeatedly for twelve hundred years.

This corpus measures polities. On the two axes it can compute for the
Rustamids, they barely register. On the question of what survived them, they
are among the more successful things in it.
