title: Doctrine along the caravan routes

Tahert sat where the routes from the Sudan met the routes to Ifriqiya and
al-Andalus, and the Ibadi communities that ran its commerce carried their
doctrine with their goods.

That is the mechanism this site's religion-carried axis is designed to catch, and
the Rustamid page nonetheless counts nothing. The reason is procedural rather
than historical: the works consulted for this polity describe the commerce and
the communities, and no source read here connects Rustamid state action to the
propagation. Under the rules a count needs a cited source describing the
propagation, and merchants sharing a ruler's confession is not the state carrying
it. The value is null — nobody looked in the right place — rather than 0.

What is not in doubt is the outcome. Ibadi communities exist today in the Mzab
valley of Algeria, on Jerba, in the Nafusa mountains of Libya and in Oman, and
the West African connections established in this period are why Ibadi jurists
appear in the trans-Saharan record for centuries afterwards.

Tahert itself was destroyed by the Fatimid advance in 909, and the surviving
Ibadi leadership withdrew south into the desert at Sedrata and then to the Mzab,
where they built five walled towns and a scholarly tradition that outlasted every
polity that had tried to end them.
