Aflah ibn Abd al-Wahhab succeeded his father as imam already prepared for the
job. He had played a real part in struggles the chronicler does not spell
out in detail, and was, in the chronicler's own words, practiced in the
handling of affairs before his father even died. What
followed was the longest reign any Rustamid held: fifty years by one count,
sixty by another, the contemporary chronicle itself unwilling to pick a
single number.

## What the length actually bought

A short reign at Tahert meant an imam who had barely finished being elected
before the next succession dispute began. Aflah's reign meant something
different - decades in which the same authority did not have to be
re-litigated, re-argued and re-won every few years.

The chronicler is direct about what that authority looked like in practice.
Aflah governed with real power, not an Ibadi first-among-equals fiction, and
under his own direction the town underwent what the source calls
extraordinary development. His own skill lay less in doctrine than in
politics: he succeeded in rallying the region's tribes around himself and
making them wary of moving against him, while still keeping the peace among
a city built out of communities - Ibadi, Sunni, Christian, Jewish, Persian,
Berber - that had nothing else in common.

## A peak with no obvious heir

Even fifty or sixty years of stable rule could not settle what came next.
When Aflah died, the succession did not pass smoothly to the son closest at
hand: his own son Abu Bakr only took power because his brother Abu'l-Yaqzan
happened to be absent at the moment their father died. The same elective
theory that had let Aflah govern without constant challenge for half a
century reasserted itself the instant he was gone - a reminder that what
made his own reign exceptional was never the office itself, but one man's
particular hold on it.
