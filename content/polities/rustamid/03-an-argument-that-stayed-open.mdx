---
title: An argument that stayed open
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987
phase: peak
---

The Rustamid imamate was elective in principle and dynastic in practice, and the
Ibadi scholars of Tahert argued about the contradiction openly. That argument is
the most unusual thing this small state produced, and none of the ordinary
measures of a polity — its territory, its revenue, its army — registers it at
all.

Ibadi doctrine holds that the imam is the most qualified Muslim, chosen by the
community's learned men, and removable by them if he fails. It is not a
formality. The Ibadi tradition distinguishes conditions under which an imamate
may exist at all — open rule, defence, concealment, self-sacrifice — and it
holds that a community with no qualified candidate should have no imam rather
than an unqualified one. The same doctrine produced the same
institution at the other end of the Islamic world: the Ibadi imamate of Oman,
three thousand miles east, elected its imams on the same principles for the same
reasons and went on doing so into the twentieth century.

What happened at Tahert is what happens when a doctrine of that kind meets a
successful family. Abd al-Rahman ibn Rustam was elected. His son Abd al-Wahhab
was elected too, in the sense that the community accepted him — and a faction
refused, on the grounds that the choice had been made by too narrow a circle
and that hereditary succession was precisely what the doctrine existed to
prevent. Those who broke away are called the Nukkar, the deniers, and the split
is permanent: Ibadism has been divided along that line ever since.

That is a schism about a procedure rather than about a person or a doctrine, and
it is worth registering how rare that is. The Sunni-Shi'i division is about who
should have succeeded the Prophet. The Isma'ili splits are about which designated
son was the true imam. The Nukkar dispute is about whether an election was
properly conducted, which is the kind of thing that divides a parliament rather
than a religion.

The Rustamids went on being elected and went on being Rustamids for a hundred
and thirty years. Tahert accordingly was a town where the legitimacy of the
government was a subject of open scholarly disagreement conducted in public by
people the government could not silence — which attracted Ibadi jurists,
Mu'tazilites, and, the sources say, Christians, and which is the reason a small
Berber city on the central Maghrebi plateau produced a legal literature that is
still in use.
