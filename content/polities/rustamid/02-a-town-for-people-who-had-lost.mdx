Tahert was founded in 761 by people who had just been beaten, and it filled up
with other people who had just been beaten.

The Kharijite risings of the 740s had taken most of the Maghreb out of Umayyad
and then Abbasid control, and had then been put down, piece by piece, by armies
sent from the east. What survived was scattered: Ibadi communities in Tripolitania
and the Nafusa mountains, Sufris in the far west, and a great many people with a
doctrine and nowhere to hold it.

Abd al-Rahman ibn Rustam had been the Ibadi governor of Kairouan and had lost it.
He withdrew west onto the high plateau, to a site with water and a defensible
position and nothing else recommending it, and was elected imam there.

## Who came

Everybody with the same problem.

Ibadis from Tripolitania and the Jebel Nafusa. Persians — Ibn Rustam's own
family were of Persian descent, and the connection to the Ibadi scholars of Basra
who had sent him west in the first place stayed live for generations. Berbers of
several confederations. And, in numbers the sources notice, refugees from
Kairouan and Ifriqiya who were not Ibadi at all: Christians, Jews and Sunnis who
found a town on the plateau where nobody was going to ask.

That last is the thing worth stopping on. A sectarian state founded by a
persecuted minority turned out to be the most religiously mixed town in the
central Maghreb, and the reason is not tolerance in the modern sense. It is that
a community which has just been hunted knows exactly what hunting costs, and that
a town on a plateau with no natural wealth needs people.

## What it lived on

The road, and the fact that it was neutral.

Tahert sat where the routes from the Saharan oases to the Mediterranean crossed
the routes running east and west along the plateau, and it had no interest in
anybody's war. It could not have won one. What it could do was be the place where
Ibadi traders from Tripolitania, Sunni merchants from Ifriqiya and caravans from
the desert all did business without the local authority caring which of them was
which.

By the ninth century it was known well enough that Arabic writers call it the
Iraq of the Maghreb — a compliment about its learning and its markets, from
people who did not share its confession.

## And the argument that came with them

The other thing the refugees brought was the habit of arguing, which the next
chapter is about.

An Ibadi community is constituted by an election, and an election can be
disputed. A town made of people who had left everywhere else rather than accept
an illegitimate authority was never going to be quiet about the legitimacy of its
own.
