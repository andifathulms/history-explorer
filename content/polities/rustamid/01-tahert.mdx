---
title: An elected imamate
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987

The Rustamids were Ibadis — a surviving moderate branch of the Kharijite
movement, which held that the leader of the community should be the most
qualified Muslim regardless of descent, and could be deposed if he failed. It is
the doctrine that had put the Kharijites in permanent opposition to both the
Umayyads and the Alids, and at Tahert it produced a state.

Abd al-Rahman ibn Rustam, a Persian, was elected imam around 776. The imamate
that followed lasted a century and a third, elective in principle and in practice
dynastic within his family — a contradiction the Ibadi scholars of the town
argued about openly, which is itself the point. Tahert was a place where the
legitimacy of the ruler was a live question rather than a settled one, and it
attracted Ibadis, Mu'tazilites and Christians accordingly.

Its wealth came from the trans-Saharan trade, running gold north from the Sudan
and sending Ibadi merchants south along routes that carried the doctrine with
them. That commerce is why Ibadi communities exist today in the Mzab, on Jerba
and in Oman.

The Fatimids destroyed Tahert in 909 on their way to Ifriqiya.
