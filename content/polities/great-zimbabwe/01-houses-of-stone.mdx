On a granite plateau in southern Zimbabwe there is a wall. It is about two
hundred and fifty metres round, up to eleven metres high and five metres thick at
the base, built of shaped granite blocks laid in courses without mortar, with a
chevron pattern running along the top of one stretch. Inside it there is a solid
conical tower, ten metres high, that is not a granary and is not a tomb and has
no known function.

Around and above it are further enclosures on the hill, and a valley of smaller
ones. The whole complex covers something like seven hundred hectares and is the
largest stone structure in Africa south of the pyramids.

Nobody who built it wrote anything down.

## What this record is made of

Every field on this page comes from the ground.

The span is radiocarbon and imported ceramics. The `revenue_basis` is gold
workings and the direction the imports came from. The three `rulers` fields are
null — not a name, not a title, not a regnal length, because there are no
sources for one. And the `turning_points` list is empty.

That last is worth dwelling on. Coding rule 4.3 says a polity with no turning
points is complete and warns against padding to avoid an empty section, and
twenty records on this site have none. This is the only one that *could not*
have any. A turning point is dated to a year, and this record has no years —
only radiocarbon ranges, a ceramic sequence and a construction phasing.

## The gold, and the record two entries away

The one institution field this page can fill is filled from other people's
rubbish.

Excavation at the site has produced Chinese celadon and stoneware, Persian and
Syrian glazed wares, glass beads by the tens of thousands, and a coin struck for
a thirteenth-century sultan of Kilwa.

This corpus added Kilwa two passes ago, and that record's first chapter is about
the monsoon: a ship from Arabia could reach Kilwa and return within one year's
winds and could not reach Sofala and return, so the last sixteen hundred
kilometres to the gold belonged to Kilwa. The gold in that sentence came from
this plateau.

So two records on this site, three thousand kilometres apart, describe two ends
of one trade — and neither has an edge to the other, because nothing passed
between them but metal and pots. The Kilwa coin found here is the physical proof
of a relationship the site's data model has no way to record.

## Why it stopped

Probably not one thing. Pikirayi's synthesis, which this record is drafted from,
sets out the candidates: soil exhaustion and firewood depletion around a
population concentrated for three centuries, a shift in the gold trade northward
towards the Zambezi and the Mutapa state, and possibly climate.

The site was not destroyed. It was left, over some decades in the fifteenth
century, and people went on living in the area and building smaller versions of
the same architecture elsewhere — Khami, Danangombe, and a hundred and fifty or
so other zimbabwes across the plateau.

This record's `ended.type` is `gradual absorption` and `by` is empty. Nine of
this region's twelve records have an empty `by`, and this one means something
different from the other eight: not a power the site cannot name, but no power at
all.
