On a granite plateau in southern Zimbabwe there is a wall. It is about two
hundred and fifty metres round, up to eleven metres high and five metres thick at
the base, built of shaped granite blocks laid in courses without mortar, with a
chevron pattern running along the top of one stretch. Inside it there is a solid
conical tower, ten metres high, that is not a granary and is not a tomb and has
no known function.

<Figure id="gz-enclosure" />

Around and above it are further enclosures on the hill, and a valley of smaller
ones. The whole complex covers something like seven hundred hectares and is the
largest stone structure in Africa south of the pyramids.

<Figure id="gz-hill" />

Nobody who built it wrote anything down.

## Everything here comes from the ground

There is no chronicle, no inscription, no king list and no dated event. The
occupation is known from radiocarbon and from imported ceramics; the economy
from gold workings and from the direction the imports came from; the phasing
from how well the walls are coursed. No ruler of this state is known — not a
name, not a title, not a length of reign — because there is nobody who wrote
one down.

That is worth pausing on, because it is a different kind of silence from the
usual one. Most states that seem undocumented are documented by their
neighbours, or by their successors, or by an archive that partly burned. This
one had no literate neighbour within three thousand kilometres and no successor
who wrote. What is left is the stone and the rubbish.

## The gold, and the pots

The one thing that can be said with confidence about how this state paid for
itself is said by other people's imports.

Excavation has produced Chinese celadon and stoneware, Persian and Syrian
glazed wares, glass beads by the tens of thousands, and a coin struck for a
thirteenth-century sultan of Kilwa.

Kilwa is three thousand kilometres up the coast, on an island off what is now
Tanzania, and its whole position rested on the monsoon: a ship out of Arabia
could reach Kilwa and return inside one year's winds and could not reach Sofala
and return, so the last sixteen hundred kilometres to the gold belonged to
Kilwan boats. The gold in that sentence came off this plateau.

A copper coin in a museum case is the physical proof of a relationship between
two places that never exchanged an ambassador, a treaty or a word of writing —
only metal, cloth and pots.

## Why it stopped

Probably not one thing, and the fifth chapter is about it: land exhausted after
three centuries of concentration, a gold trade whose centre moved north towards
the Zambezi, and possibly climate.

The site was not destroyed. It was left, over some decades in the fifteenth
century, and people went on living in the area and building smaller versions of
the same architecture elsewhere — Khami, Danangombe, and something over a
hundred and fifty other zimbabwes across the plateau.

Nobody conquered it. There was nobody to.
