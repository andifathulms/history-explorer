The first written description of a zimbabwe was printed at Lisbon in 1552, by a
man who never went to Africa, from reports sent back by Portuguese officers at
Sofala who had not seen it either. It is one of the more remarkable passages in
sixteenth-century travel writing, and everything in it is second-hand.

## What Barros was told

In the mines of Toroa, otherwise called the kingdom of Butua, ruled by a prince
who was a vassal of Benomotapa — the most ancient workings known in that
country, all of them in the open plain —

> in the midst of which there is a square fortress, of masonry within and
> without, built of stones of marvellous size, and there appears to be no
> mortar joining them. The wall is more than twenty-five spans in width, and
> the height is not so great considering the width.

Then, above the door, an inscription which some learned Muslim merchants who
went there could not read, and could not even say what the character was. The
building is nearly ringed by hills, and on the hills are others made the same
way, without mortar, "and one of them is a tower more than twelve fathoms
high."

Anyone who has stood inside the Great Enclosure will recognise every element:
the massive battered wall, wider than it is high in proportion, the hill
complex above, and the conical tower. The "inscription above the door" is
almost certainly the chevron frieze, described by men who assumed a decorated
band on a monumental wall must be writing.

## The word

The most valuable sentence is the one about the name.

> The natives of the country call all these edifices Symbaoe, which according
> to their language signifies court, for every place where Benomotapa may be is
> so called; and they say that being royal property all the king's other
> dwellings have this name.

So *zimbabwe* was not the name of a ruin. It was a live administrative term for
a royal residence, in use across the plateau a century after the great one was
abandoned, and the Portuguese heard it applied to buildings still being lived
in. Barbosa, writing about 1518, says there was a great town called Zimbaoche
fifteen or twenty days inland where the king of Benametapa often stayed, with
many houses of wood and straw.

Barros goes further: each of these places was kept by a nobleman with charge of
it in the manner of a chief warden, called the *Symbacayo*, keeper of the
Symbaoe, and some of the king's wives were always resident there in his care.

That is an institution, not a relic. A hilltop stone court with a resident
custodian and a household of royal women was the ordinary furniture of
government on this plateau, and Great Zimbabwe was the largest example of a
type that outlived it by centuries.

## Two admissions in one paragraph

Barros then reports two things his own informants told him, and puts them next
to each other without appearing to notice.

The first: nobody in the country knew when or by whom the buildings had been
raised, since the people had no writing, "but they say they are the work of the
devil, for in comparison with their power and knowledge it does not seem
possible to them that they should be the work of man."

The second: Vicente Pegado, captain of Sofala, showed some Muslim visitors the
Portuguese fortress there — its windows, its arches — so that they could
compare it with the stonework of the inland edifice. They told him the two
could not be compared, for smoothness and perfection.

The Portuguese fort lost. That is a Portuguese source, printed under royal
privilege, recording that its own new fortification was judged inferior
workmanship to a wall built without mortar by people the same book calls
heathens.

Three and a half centuries later a man would stand in front of the same wall
and conclude that Africans could not have built it.

## What the plateau did next

The court kept moving and kept working. North on the Zambezi escarpment,
Mutapa, which the Portuguese reached, traded with, tried to convert and
eventually undermined — the first state here that outsiders wrote about while it
was alive. West, Khami, and after it the Torwa and the Rozvi at Danangombe and
Naletale, building the same walls better.

The gold went on going to Sofala and being paid for in advance with Gujarati
cloth, exactly as before, until the Portuguese took Sofala and began buying at
the source.

And the word survived all of it. In 1980 a country took its name from a
building whose builders left no name of their own, and whose name in the
sixteenth century simply meant the place where the king is.
