Great Zimbabwe did not begin around 1100 and did not stop when its capital was
abandoned. The state on this plateau has a predecessor two hundred kilometres to
the south, at the confluence of the Limpopo and the Shashe, and successors to the
north and west.

## Mapungubwe

From about 1075 to 1220 there was a capital on a sandstone hill above the
Limpopo. It has terracing, a royal residence on the summit and commoner
settlement below, imported glass beads and Chinese celadon, and gold.

The graves on the hilltop were excavated in 1932. One held a burial with a
sceptre, a bowl, and a rhinoceros of gold foil tacked over a wooden core, about
fifteen centimetres long. The rhinoceros is now one of South Africa's national
symbols. For decades it was kept largely out of public view by governments whose
account of the country's past it contradicted — the same treatment, in a
different country at the same time, that was being given to the ruins north of
the Limpopo.

Mapungubwe declines as Great Zimbabwe rises, and the conventional reading is a
shift of the gold trade's centre of gravity northward. Whether the second grew
out of the first, replaced it, or simply outlasted a climate shift is argued.

## Mutapa and Khami

When the capital was abandoned in the fifteenth century, two things followed it.

North, on the Zambezi escarpment, the state the Portuguese called Monomotapa —
Mutapa — which they reached in the sixteenth century, traded with, tried to
convert, and eventually undermined. It is the first polity on this plateau that
outsiders wrote about while it existed.

West, Khami, and after it Danangombe and the Torwa and Rozvi states: the same
stone-building tradition continuing at new capitals, with better masonry than
Great Zimbabwe's and less bulk.

So the plateau has a continuous political tradition running from roughly 1075 to
the nineteenth century, and Great Zimbabwe is three hundred and fifty years of
its middle. A reader who arrives at the ruins and takes them for the whole story
has the shape wrong in a particular way: nothing stopped here. A capital was
left, and the tradition moved.

## What the gold did downstream

The gold from these workings went east to Sofala, and from Sofala north to Kilwa
— a town whose whole position rested on being where that gold had to stop, and
which was able to enforce the arrangement because the monsoon gave it the timing.

A Kilwan coin was excavated at Great Zimbabwe.

Two places three thousand kilometres apart, one known from stonework and
potsherds and the other from Arabic chronicles and a chronicle of its own, and
the physical proof of the relationship between them is a copper coin in a museum
case.
