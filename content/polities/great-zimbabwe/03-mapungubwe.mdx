This record's span opens around 1100 and it did not begin there. The state on
this plateau has a predecessor two hundred kilometres to the south, at the
confluence of the Limpopo and the Shashe, and a successor to the north on the
Zambezi.

## Mapungubwe

From about 1075 to 1220 there was a capital on a sandstone hill above the
Limpopo. It has terracing, a royal residence on the summit and commoner
settlement below, imported glass beads and Chinese celadon, and gold.

The graves on the hilltop were excavated in 1932. One held a burial with a
sceptre, a bowl and a rhinoceros of gold foil tacked over a wooden core, about
fifteen centimetres long. The rhinoceros is now one of South Africa's national
symbols and was for decades kept largely out of public view by governments whose
account of the country's past it contradicted — which is the same story this
record's fourth chapter tells about its own site, in a different country, at the
same time.

Mapungubwe declines and Great Zimbabwe rises, and the conventional reading is a
shift of the gold trade's centre of gravity northward. Whether the second grew
out of the first, replaced it, or simply outlasted a climate shift is argued.

## Mutapa and Khami

When this site was abandoned in the fifteenth century, two things followed it.

North, on the Zambezi escarpment, the state the Portuguese called Monomotapa —
Mutapa — which they reached in the sixteenth century, traded with, tried to
convert, and eventually undermined. It is the first polity on this plateau that
outsiders wrote about while it existed, and this corpus does not carry it.

West, Khami, and after it Danangombe and the Torwa and Rozvi states: the same
stone-building tradition continuing at new capitals with better masonry than
Great Zimbabwe's and less bulk.

## The sequence, and the record

So the plateau has a continuous political tradition running from roughly 1075 to
the nineteenth century, and this site carries three hundred and fifty years of
its middle.

This chapter is tagged `aside` because of that. It is about what stands either
side of this record rather than about a stage inside it, and hard rule 10 keeps
the arc running one way — a chapter covering both a predecessor and a successor
is not a phase of anything.

That is worth being explicit about because a reader arriving at a single record
called Great Zimbabwe will reasonably take it for the whole story, and its own
`ended.type` of `gradual absorption` with an empty `by` reinforces the
impression that something stopped. Nothing stopped. A capital was left and the
tradition moved.

This corpus has a field for two records that are one object seen twice — the two
Babylons, the two Assyrias, the two Elams, the two Egypts and the two Bulgarias
carry `resumes` — and it does not apply here either, because there is no
interruption to resume across. What is missing is not a field. It is Mapungubwe
and Mutapa, which have no pages.

## What the gold did downstream

One thing this record can say that its neighbours cannot say for themselves.

The gold from these workings went east to Sofala and from Sofala north to Kilwa,
and this corpus's Kilwa record was written before this one and describes the far
end of the same trade — a town whose whole position rested on being where that
gold had to stop, and whose second chapter is about how the monsoon made the
arrangement enforceable.

A Kilwan coin was excavated at this site. It is one of the imports that let this
record's single institution field be coded at all.

Two records, three thousand kilometres apart, one written from stonework and
potsherds and the other from Arabic chronicles and a chronicle of its own, and
the physical proof of the relationship between them is a copper coin in a
museum case.
