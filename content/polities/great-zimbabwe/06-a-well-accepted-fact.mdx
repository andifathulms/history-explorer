The theory that Great Zimbabwe was built by foreigners was not folklore. It was
assembled by a competent excavator who published his reasoning, and the book is
still in print because it shows the whole operation being carried out in the
open.

Theodore Bent dug at Zimbabwe in 1891, the year after the British South Africa
Company occupied the country, under the Company's auspices. He surveyed the site
accurately with R. M. W. Swan, produced plans that are still consulted, and
catalogued the finds. Then he explained what they meant.

## The argument

It runs through astronomy. Swan's measurements of the curves and orientation of
the enclosures established, Bent says, that the builders were well versed in
geometry and studied the heavens carefully. He proposes that the great elliptical
enclosure is a Sabaean temple of the god Almaqah, the points of comparison being
very strong; Professor Sayce contributes a parallel with the prehistoric temples
of Malta. A Roman coin of Antoninus Pius, found seventy feet down an old mine
shaft near Umtali, is offered as *a valuable link in the chain of evidence*.

Then the conclusion, in the plainest form he gives it:

> It is, however, very valuable confirmatory evidence when taken with the other
> points, that the builders were of a Semitic race and of Arabian origin, and
> quite excludes the possibility of any negroid race having had more to do with
> their construction than as the slaves of a race of higher cultivation; for it
> is a well-accepted fact that the negroid brain never could be capable of taking
> the initiative in work of such intricate nature.

The last clause is not an inference from anything he dug up. It is the premise,
stated as a fact everybody accepts, doing the work the evidence could not do.

## The cemetery

The most instructive passage in the book is about a negative result.

Bent's party searched for the builders' graves and found none. What they did find
was African remains in abundance, and a small cemetery of about twenty bodies
under piled stone ten miles off, also African. From which:

> Consequently we came to the conclusion that the ancient inhabitants, who formed
> but a garrison in this country, were in the habit of removing their dead to
> some safer place.

He supports it with a parallel from the Bahrain mounds and the Persian custom of
carrying the dead to Kerbela and Mashhad.

Read forwards, that is a reasonable piece of comparative argument. Read for what
it is doing, it is the conclusion consuming the evidence: the only human remains
on the site belong to the local population, and the response is not to wonder
whether the local population built it but to invent a funerary practice that
explains the absence of anybody else.

## What he would not claim

Bent is not a crank, which is exactly why the book mattered.

On Ophir he refuses to commit. Mashonaland may have been the land of Ophir or it
may not; it may have been Punt or it may not; the two may be identical or not;
*there is not enough evidence, as far as I can see, to build up any theory on
these points which will satisfy the more critical investigation to which subjects
of this kind are submitted in the present day.* All that can be established, he
says, is that the ancient Arabians got a great deal of gold from this country.

A man who declines the biblical identification, publishes his plans, and says
plainly where the evidence runs out is a much more persuasive witness than an
enthusiast. The restraint is what carried the rest of it.

## What it was for

The timing is not incidental and does not need to be alleged: Bent says who sent
him. A settler company had taken a country the previous year, and its expedition
reported that the ruins in it had been built by Arabians and that the people
living among them could not have done it except as slaves.

Everything that followed — the Company-funded clearance of 1902, the official
line held by the Rhodesian state for most of a century, the pressure on
archaeologists not to publish what they had found — was built on a foundation
Bent laid in one season with a theodolite and a spade.

And the foundation is visible in the book, because he was honest enough to show
his working. He declined the biblical identification, said where the evidence ran
out, published his plans, and then wrote down that the local population could not
have built it because the negroid brain was incapable of initiative, as a fact
everybody accepted. The careful parts and the assumption are on the same pages,
by the same hand, and the assumption is the only thing holding the conclusion
up.
