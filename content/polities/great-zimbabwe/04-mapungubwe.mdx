Great Zimbabwe did not begin from nothing around 1100. Two hundred kilometres
to the south, at the confluence of the Limpopo and the Shashe, there was a
capital doing the same things a century earlier, and what happened to its
excavated grave goods in the twentieth century is a smaller version of what
happened to the ruins north of the river.

## Mapungubwe

From about 1075 to 1220 there was a capital on a sandstone hill above the
Limpopo. It has terracing, a royal residence on the summit and commoner
settlement below, imported glass beads and Chinese celadon, and gold.

The graves on the hilltop were excavated in 1932. One held a burial with a
sceptre, a bowl, and a rhinoceros of gold foil tacked over a wooden core, about
fifteen centimetres long. The rhinoceros is now one of South Africa's national
symbols. For decades it was kept largely out of public view by governments whose
account of the country's past it contradicted — the same treatment, in a
different country at the same time, that was being given to the ruins north of
the Limpopo.

Mapungubwe declines as Great Zimbabwe rises, and the conventional reading is a
shift of the gold trade's centre of gravity northward. Whether the second grew
out of the first, replaced it, or simply outlasted a climate shift is argued.

## Why it matters that it came first

If the plateau's political tradition begins at Mapungubwe around 1075, then
Great Zimbabwe is not a beginning and not a peak. It is the middle three
hundred and fifty years of something that ran from the eleventh century into
the nineteenth, moving its centre several times — south of the Limpopo, then
the granite plateau, then the Zambezi escarpment and the western hills.

A reader who arrives at the ruins and takes them for the whole story has the
shape wrong in a specific way. Nothing stopped here. A court was left, and the
tradition moved, and it had already moved once before to get here.
