This chapter is about the record rather than the polity, which on this site
usually means the sources are thin. Here it means something worse: for most of a
century the question of who built Great Zimbabwe was answered by people who had
decided the answer in advance.

## The sequence

Karl Mauch reached the site in 1871 and concluded it was built by Phoenicians —
the Queen of Sheba's palace, on the reasoning that a beam of cedar he took to be
Lebanese could not be local. It was local sandalwood.

Cecil Rhodes's British South Africa Company funded excavation by Richard Hall in
1902, who cleared the deposits down to bedrock across large areas of the site in
search of the ancient foreign layer, destroying the stratigraphy that would have
dated it. He published his conclusion that it was Phoenician or Sabaean.

David Randall-MacIver excavated in 1905 and said it was African and medieval.
Gertrude Caton-Thompson excavated in 1929, with a stratigraphic method Hall had
not used and could no longer be used in the areas he had cleared, and said the
same: African, medieval, and built by the ancestors of the people living there.

She was right, and the professional argument was substantially over from that
point. The political one was not.

## What was done with it afterwards

The Rhodesian government of the 1960s and 1970s required that official guides,
museum displays and publications present the site's origins as unproven or
foreign. Archaeologists working in the country were placed under pressure not to
publish the African attribution. Peter Garlake, the country's senior inspector of
monuments, left the post and the country over it.

The state that did this took its name — Rhodesia — from Rhodes, whose company had
funded the excavation that destroyed the evidence. The state that replaced it in
1980 took its name from the site.

## Why it is in this corpus

Because this site's job is to record what a polity was, and for this one the
historiography is not a footnote to that — it is the largest single fact about
how the polity has been known.

This corpus has other records where an account was assembled by people with a
stake. The Songhai record's fifth chapter is about two chronicles written by the
scholarly families the empire's founder persecuted. The First Bulgarian record
says almost everything about its first two centuries was written by Byzantines.
The Ghana record's last chapter dismantles an Almoravid conquest that probably
did not happen and says the tidiness of the story is a good part of why it
lasted.

This is the same problem with a different motive. In the other cases the
interested party was a rival or a successor. Here it was a colonial state that
needed the ruins on its territory not to have been built by the people it was
governing, and it had the archaeology and the museum labels to say so with.

## And what it costs the fields

Everything except one.

Hall's clearance removed the stratified deposits from large parts of the site, so
the dating is coarser and the sequence thinner than it would have been. Whatever
was in those layers about how the place was organised, fed and governed is gone.

This record's institution block reads null, `mining, trade-toll`, null, null. The
first, third and fourth are empty because nobody wrote anything down. Some part of
them might not have been, if the first serious excavation had been looking for
what was there rather than for who else it could have been.
