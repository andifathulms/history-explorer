The granite of the Zimbabwe plateau exfoliates. Heated by the sun and cooled at
night over long periods, it splits along planes parallel to the surface and
sheds slabs, and a slab can be broken into blocks of roughly even thickness with
a hammerstone.

That is the whole technical basis of the architecture. The builders did not
quarry in the sense of cutting into a face; they collected what the rock was
already producing, dressed it, and laid it in courses.

There is no mortar anywhere in the complex. Nothing holds the walls up but the
weight of the stone and the accuracy of the coursing.

## What that constrains

Everything about the architecture follows from a wall that cannot be tied
together.

The walls are massive because they have to be — up to five metres thick at the
base and battered inwards as they rise. There are no arches and no spanned
openings of any width: entrances are narrow, and the ones that survive are
either lintelled with short stone slabs or stepped. There are no upper floors of
stone. The buildings people actually lived in were *dhaka* — puddled clay and
gravel, thatched — set inside the stone enclosures rather than built of them.

So the walls are not buildings and were never roofs. They are enclosures: they
mark, screen and separate, and what they contain is a set of clay houses and the
space between them.

That is a different thing from the European castle a Victorian visitor saw, and
correcting the misreading is part of what the fourth chapter is about.

## The courses, and the chevron

The quality of the coursing varies systematically, and the archaeology uses it to
phase the site.

The earliest work is rough, uncoursed and irregular. The best — on the outer wall
of the Great Enclosure — is regular, level, and laid with a consistency that
takes practice to achieve and a labour force to sustain. On one long stretch of
that wall, near the top, there is a band of chevron decoration a dozen courses
deep, made by laying blocks at alternating angles.

Nobody knows what the chevron means. It appears elsewhere in the Zimbabwe
culture, and the readings offered — a serpent, water, fertility, a boundary
marker — are guesses of the kind that are made when a symbol survives and its
users do not.

## The conical tower

Inside the Great Enclosure there is a solid stone cone, about ten metres high and
five across at the base, tapering upwards.

It is not hollow. It has no entrance, no chamber, no inscription and no
associated deposit. It is not a granary — the granaries on the site are clay and
elsewhere. It is not a tomb; nothing has been found in it and it is solid.

The suggestions are that it represents a grain bin as a symbol of royal
provision, or a phallic symbol, or a marker of authority with no specific
referent. Pikirayi is candid that these are inferences from ethnography rather
than readings of evidence.

A solid ten-metre tower that nobody can explain is a fair emblem for the whole
place: unmistakably deliberate, unmistakably important, and mute.

## And a hundred and fifty more

Great Zimbabwe is the largest of the stone enclosures on this plateau and it is
not the only one. There are something over a hundred and fifty *zimbabwes* across
Zimbabwe, Mozambique and Botswana — Khami, Danangombe, Naletale, Manyikeni and
the rest — built in the same tradition, some before this one, most after it.

That is what archaeologists mean by the Zimbabwe *culture*, and it is the right
unit. A tradition of six hundred years with many centres is what the evidence
actually describes; one capital, however large, is a slice through it. The
Swahili coast four hundred miles east has exactly the same shape and causes
exactly the same trouble: a civilisation of thirty towns, and a habit of
telling its story through whichever one was richest.
