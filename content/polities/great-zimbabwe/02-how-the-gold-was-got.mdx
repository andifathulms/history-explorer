The plateau's gold is the reason there was anything here to build with, and
nobody described the working of it until the sixteenth century, by which time
the capital had been empty for a hundred years. João de Barros, printing in
1552 from reports sent home by men at Sofala, is the first written account of
how it was actually done — and he says of the fields he is describing that
they are the most ancient known in the country.

## Water, and two hundred men

There is no deep mining in it. All the gold in the Manica field, fifty leagues
west of Sofala, is dust, and the difficulty is that the land is dry: the earth
has to be carried to water before it can be washed, so they dig hollows that
fill in the winter, generally six or seven spans deep, because at twenty spans
you hit rock.

Further inland, a hundred to two hundred leagues from the coast, the specks are
larger — some in veins in the rock, some cleaned out by the winter torrents.
There, Barros says, they dive in the still pools the rivers leave in summer and
wash the mud they bring up. Where there are ponds, two hundred men gather,
drain away half the water, and go through what is left.

That is placer work at scale: no shafts, no timbering, no pumps, and a labour
force organised in hundreds for a season. It explains a great deal about the
kind of state that grew on top of it. What such a polity has to be able to do
is assemble people at the right time of year and take a share of what they
bring up. It does not need an army, and there is no sign this one had one.

## The credit trap

The most revealing paragraph in Barros is about how the coast got the metal out
of the interior, and it is not by force.

He says the country was rich enough that a great quantity could be had if the
people were covetous, but that they wanted gold so little that a man had to be
very hungry before he would dig for it. So the Muslim traders who worked the
interior used artifice to raise their appetite. They dressed the men and their
wives in cloth, beads and trinkets until they were delighted with them; then
gave them the whole lot on credit, and told them to go and dig, and if they came
back within a certain time they could pay for it.

*And by thus trusting them they oblige them to dig, and they are so trustworthy
that they keep their word.*

Read that from the plateau's side and the trade looks different from the way it
usually does. The imports found in the excavations — the celadon, the glazed
Persian wares, the beads by the tens of thousands — are not the proceeds of a
gold trade. They are the advance against it. Someone handed them over first and
came back for the metal.

## What the state took

Barros does not say what the ruler's share was, and no source does. What the
ground shows is where it accumulated: at one place, on one granite hill, for
about three centuries.

A trade of this shape — seasonal, dispersed, run on credit extended by
outsiders, in a commodity the diggers did not particularly want — puts the
power with whoever can guarantee the outsiders access and guarantee the diggers
their side of the bargain. That is a job for a court, not a garrison. The
enclosures on this hill are the physical form of it: not walls to hold an army,
but walls to say where the authority is.

## And where it went out

East to Sofala, and from Sofala north on the monsoon. Barbosa, writing a
generation later, says that before the Portuguese came the Muslim towns of
Sofala, Cuama, Angoya and Mozambique were all subject to the king of Kilwa, who
was the mightiest of them, and that there was great plenty of gold in that town
because no ship went towards Sofala without first coming to that island.

That sentence is the far end of this one. The gold in it came off this plateau,
was washed out of ponds by men in gangs of two hundred, and was paid for in
advance with cloth.
