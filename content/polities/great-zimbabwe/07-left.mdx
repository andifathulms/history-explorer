Nobody sacked Great Zimbabwe. There is no destruction layer, no burning, no
weapons in the deposits, no defensive rebuilding of the walls and no siege
works outside them. Some time in the fifteenth century the people who lived
there went somewhere else, and the walls stayed up because nobody had any
reason to pull them down.

That is an unusual way for a capital to end and it is worth being precise about
what is known.

## What is known

The date is a range and not a year. Radiocarbon and the ceramic sequence put
the abandonment somewhere in the fifteenth century, over some decades rather
than in a season, and the excavation that would have tightened it was destroyed
in 1902 by a man digging for Phoenicians.

Pikirayi's synthesis sets out three candidates and does not choose between
them, which is the correct posture.

**The land.** A concentration of people and cattle in one district for three
hundred years exhausts what is under it. Soil fertility falls, the firewood
within carrying distance is gone, grazing degrades, and the point arrives at
which the settlement costs more to feed than the site is worth. This is the
ordinary fate of pre-industrial capitals that do not sit on a river delta, and
this one sits on granite.

**The trade.** The gold trade's centre of gravity moved north, towards the
Zambezi, and the state that grew there — Mutapa — took the traffic with it. A
capital whose whole revenue came from being where the trade was routed stops
being viable when the routing changes, and nothing about the routing was
guaranteed.

**The climate.** Possibly. The evidence is thinner and the argument is made
with more hedging.

None of the three excludes the others, and the honest reading is that a place
that had been marginal in three ways at once stopped being worth the trouble at
roughly the same time in all three.

## What did not happen

The people did not disappear, and the tradition did not stop.

They went on living across the plateau and went on building the same
architecture at other places — Khami to the west, Danangombe after it, and
something over a hundred and fifty smaller stone enclosures across what is now
Zimbabwe, Mozambique and Botswana. Some of the later masonry is better than
anything at the capital, with decorative panelling the older walls do not have,
and on a smaller scale.

So the fifteenth century did not end a civilisation. It relocated a court.

## Why that is hard to see

Because the capital is enormous and the successors are not, and because a
visitor standing inside the Great Enclosure is looking at the biggest thing in
the tradition and naturally reads it as the tradition's whole life.

The scale is also what made the ruins available for a different story
altogether. A large abandoned stone complex with no inscription, no chronicle
and no living court to contradict anybody was an invitation to whoever came
along next with a theory — and the people who came along next had one.
