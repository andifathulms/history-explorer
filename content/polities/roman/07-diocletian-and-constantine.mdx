Diocletian ruled twenty-one years, from 284, and then retired to grow cabbages
in a fortified palace at Split, which nobody in the office had done before or did
afterwards. Between those two events he rebuilt the state.

The office was divided: two senior emperors called Augusti and two junior called
Caesars, each with a territory and an army, the Caesars expected to succeed. The
provinces were roughly doubled in number and made smaller, grouped into dioceses
under vicars, with the civil and military commands separated so that no man held
both in one place. The tax system was rebuilt on a standard unit of land and
labour with a periodic reassessment and collection largely in kind. Prices were
fixed by edict across the empire, which did not work. The army was enlarged and
split into frontier troops and a mobile field army.

And the imperial person was removed from view: purple and jewels, prostration
before the emperor, an elaborate court ceremonial, and a title — *dominus* —
that the arrangement in this record's first chapter had spent three centuries
avoiding.

## What the tetrarchy was for

It was an attempt to solve the problem of the fifth and sixth chapters by making
the succession administrative. If there are four emperors, a frontier army that
acclaims its commander has not created a civil war, because its commander is
already an emperor; and if the junior emperors are designated in advance, the
question of who comes next has an answer before it is asked.

It worked once, at Diocletian's own abdication in 305, and then failed
comprehensively. Within a year Constantine had been acclaimed by his father's
troops in Britain, which is precisely the thing the system existed to prevent,
and eighteen years of civil war followed.

## Constantine

He won them, and made three decisions this record carries.

He attributed his victory at the Milvian Bridge in 312 to the Christian God, and
what followed is the turning point on this record: not the conversion of the
empire, which took a century and was never complete, but the conversion of
imperial patronage. Bishops were given civil jurisdiction, churches were endowed
on a scale nothing else could match, and the emperor summoned and paid for the
council at Nicaea in 325 to settle a doctrinal question by vote. This record's
`religions_carried` count exists because of what was done here.

He struck a new gold coin, the solidus, at a weight that held for seven hundred
years and became the currency of this corpus's Byzantine record and, as the
*bezant*, of Mediterranean trade generally.

And in 330 he founded a second capital at Byzantium and called it after himself.

## The city

Constantinople is why the two records on this site divide where they do.

It was not intended as a division. It was a second imperial residence in a
strategically superb position — controlling the straits, close to both the
Danube and the eastern frontier, defensible on three sides — and emperors had
been governing from wherever the war was for a century already. What it became
was a permanent eastern capital with a senate, an annona from Egypt, and an
administration that outlasted the western one by a thousand years.

The eighth chapter is about the year this site cuts the record, and about how
arbitrary that is.
