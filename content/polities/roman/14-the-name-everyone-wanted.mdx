The most durable thing the Roman empire produced was its own name, and the
history of the next fifteen centuries is partly a history of people claiming
it.

Charlemagne's coronation in 800 was an attempt to have the western half again.
Mehmed II took Constantinople in 1453 and the title Kayser-i Rûm with it. Those
two claims span six hundred and fifty years and two religions, and neither
claimant had any connection to the Roman state beyond standing on ground it had
once held.

<Figure id="hre-crown" />

The Byzantines are not on that list, and it is worth saying why. They did not
claim to be the successors of Rome; they claimed, correctly, to *be* Rome — the
eastern half continuing without a break in institutions, law or
self-description, calling itself *Basileia Rhomaion* until the walls came down.
A state does not claim itself.

The empire itself is worth stating plainly. At Trajan's death it held about five
million square kilometres and perhaps a fifth of the world's population, ran on
a professional standing army and a body of civil law that is the ancestor of most
legal systems in use today, and maintained a Mediterranean-wide economy that did
not recover its scale in the west for a thousand years after it stopped.

Dating its end to 395 is a choice about where to cut a continuous thing. The
west was deposed in 476; the east continued to 1453; and neither of those is the
end of the same object.
