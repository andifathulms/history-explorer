In 260 Shapur I of Persia captured the emperor Valerian alive at Edessa. This
corpus's Sasanian record carries the same day as its first turning point, and
the Persian version of it is carved on a cliff face at Naqsh-e Rostam: the king
of kings on horseback, a Roman emperor kneeling, another standing with his wrists
held. Nothing like it had happened before and nothing like it happened again.

Within a decade the empire was in three pieces. Postumus held Gaul, Britain and
Spain as a separate Roman state with its own senate and consuls. Palmyra — this
corpus carries it as a record in the Eastern Mediterranean region — took Syria,
Egypt and most of Anatolia, and its queen struck coins with her son as Augustus.
The central government held Italy, the Balkans and Africa.

## The count

Between 235 and 284 there were more than twenty men that later lists count as
emperors and a good many more who claimed it. Almost all were raised by the
troops they commanded and almost all were killed by soldiers. The average reign
is under three years.

At the same time: the Rhine and Danube frontiers were crossed repeatedly and
deeply, with raids reaching Spain, northern Italy and Athens; the silver coinage
became copper with a wash; a plague ran through the empire in the 250s and 260s;
and the cities of the west began building walls, in many cases out of their own
demolished monuments, for the first time since the republic.

## What did not happen

It did not end, which is the fact worth putting in front of the list.

Aurelian, in a reign of five years from 270, retook Palmyra and then Gaul and
reunited the empire; he also built a wall around the city of Rome, which had not
needed one for six hundred years and which says most of what there is to say
about the period. Diocletian, from 284, then did the reorganising, and the seventh
chapter is about it.

The recovery is the reason this record's ending is 395 and a fragmentation rather
than 284 and a collapse. Whatever the third century was, it was survivable, and
the thing that survived it was recognisably the same state with a different
constitution.

## What the fifth chapter says

This record's fifth chapter, which was written before this one, sets out the
mechanism: the empire had no way of transferring the office that the army could
not override, and once the army had learned that an emperor could be made outside
Rome it kept making them.

Two things are worth adding to it here.

The first is that the frequency was not madness but arithmetic. The empire had
several frontier commands, each with an army that could plausibly march on the
centre, each facing a real emergency in this period, and each with soldiers whose
pay was losing value. Any commander who did not take the purple when his troops
offered it was likely to be killed by them; any who did was likely to be killed by
somebody else's troops later. There was no stable choice available to an
individual.

The second is that the coded field on this record reads `nomination,
acclamation` and not `factional`, and this chapter is the case against that
coding as much as for it. For fifty years the practice was closer to the Mamluk
sultanate's than to anything else on this site. What the field records is that
the rule existed and kept being performed — every usurper had himself acclaimed
and sought a senatorial ratification he did not need — and coding rule 7 says the
field takes the rule. The outcome is here, in prose, where the argument can be
seen.
