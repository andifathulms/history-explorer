The claim made for this empire more often than any other is that its law is
the ancestor of most legal systems still in use, and the claim is worth
examining rather than simply repeating, because it rests on two very
different moments three and a half centuries apart.

## The Twelve Tables

Roman law's own origin story is a republican one, not an imperial one. Around
450 BC, after a plebeian demand that the law stop being an unwritten
privilege of the patrician priesthood who alone knew and interpreted it, a
commission produced a code of ten tables, later extended to twelve, and had
it posted in the Forum. Livy calls it, writing centuries later, the source of
all public and private law - and whatever the truth of the tradition around
its drafting, the underlying grievance it answered is a familiar one: a
community that could not be governed by a law nobody but its rulers were
permitted to read.

Almost nothing of the Twelve Tables survives except as quotations in much
later writers, and what does survive is procedural and specific rather than
philosophical - rules about summoning a defendant to court, about debt
bondage, about the burial of the dead outside the city walls. It was not a
constitution and did not claim to be one. It was a fixed, public starting
point that every later jurist could cite, amend by interpretation, and argue
from, for the better part of a thousand years.

## The jurists

The law that later ages actually inherited was not the Twelve Tables
themselves but the enormous body of professional commentary built on top of
them, above all in the second and early third centuries AD, when a small
number of jurists - Gaius, Papinian, Ulpian, Paulus - produced systematic
treatises addressing nearly every question private law could raise:
property, inheritance, contract, the status of slaves and freedmen, the
obligations owed between family members. Gaius's *Institutes*, written as a
teaching text, organised the whole of private law into a scheme - persons,
things, actions - that later codifications, including modern civil codes
written in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, still substantially
follow.

Alongside the jurists' own writing sat a second source of law already
mentioned in connection with how an emperor actually spent his days: the
rescripts, the written answers
to petitions that accumulated by the thousand across three centuries and were
treated, cited and argued from exactly as a jurist's opinion or an old
statute was. A legal system built this way - case answered by case, opinion
layered on opinion, with no single moment of comprehensive drafting - is
difficult to summarise and was, by the fourth century, difficult even for
trained lawyers to navigate in full.

## What came after

The effort to make it navigable again came three and a half centuries after
this empire's own conventional end, when the eastern emperor Justinian had
the whole inherited mass of statute, juristic opinion and imperial rescript
collected, edited down, and reissued as a single authoritative compilation in
the 530s. That codification is why the classical jurists' work survives at
all in anything like its original form - most of it would otherwise have
been lost the way the Twelve Tables themselves mostly were - and it is the
version of Roman law that medieval and early modern Europe actually
rediscovered and taught, centuries after the western half of the empire that
had produced the material was gone.

Two moments, then, sit behind the claim: a plebeian demand in the fifth
century BC that law be public and written, and a scholarly and administrative
achievement of the second and third centuries AD that gave later ages
something organised enough to rediscover. Neither survives complete on its
own terms. What was transmitted was the second moment's own systematic
reading of the first.
