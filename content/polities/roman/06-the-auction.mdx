In March 193 the Praetorian Guard killed the emperor and sold the office to the
highest bidder, and a serving senator who was in the city that day wrote down how
the bidding went.

Cassius Dio owed his praetorship to Pertinax, the emperor who had just been
murdered, and he says so in the middle of the account, which is one reason to
trust the rest of it. When the news broke, he says, some people ran home and some
ran to the soldiers' houses and everybody looked to his own safety. Sulpicianus,
the city prefect, happened to be inside the Praetorian camp already — Pertinax
had sent him there to settle things — and began working on his own appointment.
And Didius Julianus, a senator Dio describes as an insatiable money-getter and a
reckless spender, always wanting a change of government, heard the news, went
straight to the camp and stood outside the gates making offers.

Then the sentence. *Just as is done in some market and auction-room, both the
city and her whole empire were bid off. The sellers were the people who had
killed their emperor, and the would-be buyers were Sulpicianus and Julianus, who
vied to outbid each other, one from within, the other from without.*

The guardsmen ran between them. *Sulpicianus is willing to give so much; now what
will you add?* And back the other way: *Julianus offers so much; how much more do
you make it?* They reached five thousand denarii a man. Sulpicianus, who was
inside the walls and was prefect of the city and had said five thousand first,
would have won it — except that Julianus stopped raising by small amounts and
jumped twelve hundred and fifty denarii in one bid, shouting the figure and
holding it up on his fingers. The guard took the higher offer, partly for the
difference and partly because Julianus had put it into their heads that
Sulpicianus might avenge Pertinax. They opened the gate and proclaimed him.

Towards evening he went down to the Forum and the senate house escorted by a
great many Praetorians with standards, as if going into action, in order — Dio
says plainly — to frighten the senate and the people into acquiescence. The
soldiers shouted *Commodus* at him, which was a compliment.

And here is the part a modern account of the third-century crisis cannot supply.
Dio describes what it was like to be a senator receiving this news at home. *We
were possessed with fear of Julianus and the soldiers*, he writes — himself
especially, since he owed his career to the dead emperor and had repeatedly
proved Julianus in the wrong when appearing against him in court. And then the
senate went in and did what was required of it.

Julianus lasted sixty-six days. Three provincial armies proclaimed their own
commanders within weeks, and Septimius Severus reached Rome first.

Everything structural about the later empire is legible in that afternoon. The
office was detachable from the man; the price was payable in cash to the troops
who held the capital; and the body that had once conferred it stood in the Forum
being shown standards. What Dio adds to the analysis is that everyone present
understood all of this at the time, and that the shame of it was the thing he
remembered — *a most disgraceful affair, and one unworthy of Rome.*
