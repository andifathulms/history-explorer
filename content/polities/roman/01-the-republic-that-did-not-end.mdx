In 27 BC Octavian stood up in the senate and gave the state back. He resigned
his extraordinary powers, was voted a set of ordinary ones in return, received
the name Augustus, and spent the next forty-one years governing an empire while
holding no office that had not existed under the republic.

That is the constitutional arrangement this record begins with and it is worth
taking seriously rather than treating as a fraud. Augustus held tribunician
power, which gave him the tribune's veto and personal inviolability without the
office; and a proconsular command over the provinces that contained the armies,
which put every legion under a man with no formal superiority over any other
magistrate. The senate met, the consuls were elected, the courts sat. There was
no coronation, no regalia, no title meaning king, and no law that said the
republic had ended.

## The bronze in the Capitol

The best evidence that the fiction was maintained rather than merely asserted is
a document from a century later.

In 69, after the year this record's first turning point is about, the senate
passed a law conferring powers on Vespasian, and a bronze copy of part of it
survives. It grants him, clause by clause, the right to make treaties, to
convene the senate, to extend the boundary of the city, to do whatever he judges
to be in the interests of the state — and it cites precedent for each: as it was
lawful for the deified Augustus, for Tiberius, for Claudius.

A general who had just won a civil war stopped to have his powers voted to him,
itemised, with references. That is why this record's `legitimation` field carries
`election` alongside `descent` and `divine-sanction`, and why the value comes
first. In this site's vocabulary `election` means a right to rule conferred by a
body, and for three centuries the Roman answer to *by what right* was: the senate
and people conferred these specific powers, and here is the law.

## What an emperor did

Millar's book, from which this field is coded, arrives at the same conclusion
from the opposite direction. Rather than asking what the constitution said the
emperor was, it asks what emperors demonstrably spent their time doing, and the
answer is: replying.

Hearing embassies from cities. Judging appeals. Answering petitions from
individuals, in writing, at length, on questions of inheritance, taxation,
citizenship and local privilege — the *rescripts*, thousands of which survive in
the legal codes. Confirming or refusing what somebody had asked for. The
emperor's power was enormous and it was overwhelmingly reactive, exercised on
cases brought to him by subjects who expected an answer and usually got one.

An empire of fifty to sixty million people was governed by a few hundred senior
officials and a man who answered letters, and the reason that was possible is
the subject of the third chapter.

## What it was not

It is worth saying plainly what this record does not carry, because the reader
arrives with an image.

There is no Roman bureaucracy in the sense the Chinese or Byzantine records have
one. There is no standing police. Below the provincial governor and his small
staff, the empire was administered by the cities — councils of local landowners
who collected the taxes, maintained the roads, ran the courts of first instance
and paid for the games out of their own pockets, in exchange for status and
Roman citizenship. Rome ruled by co-opting the people who were already in charge
locally and making it worth their while.

That arrangement worked for two and a half centuries. What broke it is the
subject of the sixth chapter, and it is closely connected to the edict in the
fourth.
