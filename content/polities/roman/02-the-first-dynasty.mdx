Augustus had built a monarchy without naming it, and for the next fifty-four
years the empire's central question was whether one family could keep holding
an office that officially did not exist.

Tiberius, his stepson and adopted heir, succeeded in AD 14 with the senate's
powers voted to him in the same form Augustus had received them, and spent the
back half of his twenty-three-year reign governing from the island of Capri,
communicating with Rome by letter and leaving the city to a prefect, Sejanus,
who used the distance to make himself the second man in the empire until
Tiberius had him arrested and killed. It was the first demonstration that the
office could be run at a remove, by correspondence, which is exactly how
Millar's later emperors mostly governed anyway.

Caligula, Tiberius's great-nephew, succeeded in 37 to an enthusiastic welcome
and reigned not quite four years before his own Praetorian officers murdered
him in a palace corridor, the first emperor killed by the men meant to guard
him rather than by a rival army. The ancient accounts make him erratic to the
point of madness; what can be said with more confidence is that he demanded
worship as a living god in a way Augustus and Tiberius had both been careful
to avoid, and that the demand did nothing to protect him.

## An accession nobody planned

Claudius, found by the Praetorians hiding behind a curtain in the palace after
his nephew's murder and reportedly made emperor because he was the last adult
male of the family left standing rather than because anyone had chosen him,
turned out to be the most capable administrator of the four. He completed the
conquest of Britain in 43, extended citizenship into Gaul, and built the
harbour at Ostia that fed Rome for centuries afterward. An emperor discovered
behind a curtain governed longer, and more competently, than the two young men
who had inherited the position by right.

## Nero

Nero came to the throne at sixteen in 54, murdered his own mother five years
into his reign after at least one failed attempt, and spent much of what
followed on music, poetry and the stage rather than administration, while the
empire around him was actually run by his advisers for as long as they kept
his confidence. The great fire of Rome in 64 burned two-thirds of the city;
Nero rebuilt it on a more regular plan and built himself an enormous palace on
the cleared ground, and the rumour that he had started the fire himself, to
clear space for it, could not be disproved and has never gone away.

He killed himself in 68, the last man with a blood claim to Augustus, having
alienated the senate, the Praetorians and finally the provincial armies whose
commanders began declaring against him one after another. Tacitus's judgment,
that a secret of empire had been revealed - that an emperor could be made
somewhere other than Rome - describes what happened within the year, but the
condition that made it possible was created here: a dynasty that had spent
four reigns treating the office as a family possession left no institutional
answer for what happened when the family ran out.

## What legitimacy had rested on

Augustus's own cult sat carefully on one side of a line: temples to *Roma et
Augustus* were built and encouraged in the provinces, especially in the Greek
east where ruler cult was an old and familiar idiom, but Augustus himself was
titled a god only after his death, by senatorial vote, and no living emperor
in Rome itself claimed outright divinity. Tiberius declined honours he thought
excessive. Caligula did not, and asked to be worshipped as one while he still
breathed - the clearest sign the ancient sources give that he had stopped
reading the room his predecessors had read carefully. The pattern set under
Augustus, of divine honours granted rather than demanded and mostly reserved
for the dead, is what every later emperor except Caligula actually followed,
Christian ones included: an emperor's authority was backed by claims about
divine favour, not by a living claim to be a god outright.
