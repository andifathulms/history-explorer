Theodosius I died at Milan in January 395 and the empire passed to his two sons,
Arcadius in the east and Honorius in the west, aged seventeen and ten. That year
is the conventional end of the Roman empire and the beginning of the
Byzantine.

It is a choice about where to cut a continuous thing, and the honest position is
that no date works.

## The candidates

**395** is the date used here. Nothing constitutional happened: the empire had
been governed by two or more emperors for most of the previous century, the
division was administrative, the law was still one law, and everyone involved
expected the halves to be reunited as they had been before. What makes it usable
is that they never were. It is the last division that stayed.

**476** is the traditional answer and it is the worst of them. Odoacer deposed a
twelve-year-old western emperor whose authority did not extend much beyond
Italy, sent the imperial regalia to Constantinople with a message that the west
did not need an emperor of its own, and ruled Italy as a patrician nominally
under the eastern emperor. Contemporaries did not treat it as the end of
anything. The date's prominence is a later western construction.

**1453** is what the eastern half's own inhabitants would have said, since they
called their state the empire of the Romans until the walls were breached.

**Or no date at all**, which is the position that the Roman state ended
gradually and in different places at different times: in Britain around 410, in
Gaul in stages across the fifth century, in Africa in 439, in Italy over the
sixth century's wars, and in the east not until an Ottoman cannon.

## What kind of relation this is

Whatever happened in 395, it was not a conquest, not a collapse and not a claim
by one state on another's legitimacy. It was a partition.

And it is a partition unlike the other cases where a state disappears and
reappears. Babylon, Assyria and Elam each have two periods separated by
centuries of somebody else's rule, which is genuinely one thing seen twice
across a dark age. Rome and Byzantium have no gap at all. The eastern empire
begins in the year the division is dated to, with the same law, the same army,
the same taxes, the same capital that Constantine had built, and the same
emperor's brother on the throne.

Which is why every attempt to say precisely what ended in 395 comes back to the
same answer: nothing did, and the division became permanent afterwards for
reasons nobody in 395 could have known.

## What actually ended

The western administration, over the following eighty years, and with it the
integrated Mediterranean economy. The shipwreck count
falls. The African grain stops reaching Italy after 439. Britain drops out of the
coin economy entirely within a decade of 410. The city of Rome, at over half a
million people when Constantine founded his rival, is under fifty thousand by the
sixth century.

The eastern administration did not end. It ran for another ten and a half
centuries, which makes the Roman state, counted honestly from Augustus to the
last emperor, the longest-lived polity in European history by a very wide
margin.
