The rates were low. A tax on land, assessed from periodic censuses, and a tax on
heads, at levels that varied by province and were generally a small fraction of
production; customs dues of two to five per cent at the internal frontiers of the
empire and rather more at its external ones; and, until Diocletian, no direct
taxation of Italy at all.

The yield was enormous, because the base was fifty to sixty million people and
because the collection cost the state almost nothing.

## Government by co-option

This is the part that is hard to hold in the head, and it is why this record's
institutions are coded from Garnsey and Saller rather than from a narrative.

An empire of that size was administered by something on the order of a hundred
and fifty senior officials — governors, procurators, legionary commanders — plus
their small staffs. There was no imperial revenue service. The taxes were
assessed and collected by the cities: councils of local landowners, several
thousand of them across the empire, who were personally liable for the sum their
community owed and who competed for the honour of being liable.

What they got for it was status, citizenship, priesthoods, the right to wear
particular clothes, statues with inscriptions, and access to the emperor. What
Rome got was an empire it did not have to garrison internally, run by the people
who would otherwise have led the resistance.

The classic statement of the deal is that Rome ruled the Mediterranean by making
the local elite of every city into Romans. This record's fourth chapter is about
what happened when it finished doing that.

## The grain

One flow of goods was not a market and it is the clearest single illustration of
what the empire was for.

The city of Rome was fed by a state grain supply — the *annona* — bringing
something in the order of two hundred thousand tonnes a year from Egypt and North
Africa, shipped in a fleet the state contracted, stored in warehouses the state
built, and distributed free to a registered list of a few hundred thousand adult
male citizens. Egypt was acquired by Augustus as an imperial possession governed
by an equestrian prefect that senators were forbidden to enter without
permission, and the grain is the reason.

When the eastern half went its own way, the grain went with it. This corpus's
Byzantine record inherits Egypt and Constantinople gets an annona of its own;
Rome does not, and the city's population falls by something like ninety per cent
over the following two centuries.

## What the coins say

The empire ran on a silver denarius that Augustus struck at about ninety-eight
per cent fine. By the reign of Septimius Severus it was around fifty per cent. By
the 260s the coin of the same name was a copper piece with a silver wash that
wore off.

Debasement is the state paying its army with money it does not have, and the
army in the third century is being paid a great deal more often because there are
a great many more emperors. The sequence runs: no more conquest, so no new
revenue; more frontier pressure, so a larger army; more usurpations, so more
donatives; less silver per coin, so prices rise; taxes collected in coin lose
value, so taxes are collected in kind instead; and by Diocletian the state is
requisitioning grain, uniforms and transport directly and assessing them in
units of land and labour.

The sixth chapter is what that looks like from the top. The point of putting it
here is that the fiscal machinery described above — light, cheap, delegated,
dependent on a stable coin and a co-operative local elite — is what the third
century destroyed, and the state that came out the other side taxed harder,
counted more and trusted nobody.
