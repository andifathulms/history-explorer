Rome never solved the succession. There was no law of inheritance for the
principate, because the principate officially did not exist — Augustus had
constructed a monarchy out of republican offices and never named it, which left
the question of who came next without a rule to answer it.

For two centuries the gap was papered over by adoption, dynastic luck, and the
Senate's willingness to ratify whatever had already happened. In 193 the
Praetorian Guard auctioned the office. In the fifty years after 235 there were
more than twenty emperors, almost all of them raised by their own troops and
killed by other troops, while the currency debased and the frontiers gave way in
several places at once.

This is the same failure mode that runs through the whole of this site. The
Abbasids bought Turkish slave soldiers to escape provincial armies and were
chosen and destroyed by them at Samarra within thirty years. The Ayyubids' Bahri
regiment took the sultanate in 1250. The Safavids broke the Qizilbash with
Georgian slave troops who became a court faction in turn. An army that can make
a ruler will eventually make one it prefers, and no polity here found a durable
answer.

Diocletian's answer was to divide the office among four holders and to make the
division administrative rather than personal, which stabilised the empire and
began the split this site records as its ending in 395.
