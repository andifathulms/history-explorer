Trajan reached Ctesiphon in 116, the deepest a Roman army ever marched into
Mesopotamia, and died the following year with the empire at its widest
extent: something over five million square kilometres, from Britain to the
Persian Gulf. Hadrian, his successor, gave nearly all of it back within
months - the new provinces of Mesopotamia and Assyria were abandoned almost
as soon as they had been declared, on the judgment that they could not be
held at a cost worth paying. The empire's territorial peak and the decision
to stop expanding for good happened in the same eighteen months.

What made that succession possible without a war is worth setting out on its
own, because for most of the second century the empire solved its oldest
problem by means that did not survive it.

## Adoption instead of birth

Nerva, an elderly senator raised to the throne after the Praetorians murdered
Domitian in 96, had no son. He adopted Trajan, a general commanding on the
Rhine with no blood connection to him at all, and the empire passed to a
capable outsider rather than to a disputed heir. Trajan, dying without a
son of his own, adopted Hadrian - the ancient sources disagree about whether
the adoption happened cleanly or was arranged after the fact by Trajan's wife
once he was already dead, which is itself a measure of how much rode on the
formality being observed. Hadrian in turn adopted Antoninus Pius, on the
explicit condition that Antoninus adopt two further heirs of Hadrian's own
choosing: Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus.

Four consecutive successions, over eighty-four years, without a disputed
succession or a usurping general. Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian and Antoninus Pius
each chose an adult of demonstrated competence rather than passing the office
to a son, because none of the first three had a surviving son to pass it to.
It looked, for the better part of a century, like a solution: not a rule of
descent but a habit of picking the ablest available man and calling him son
by adoption instead of by birth.

## What the habit was not

It was not a rule. Nothing compelled an emperor with an actual son to adopt
someone else instead, and the test came the moment one did.

Marcus Aurelius, who reigned from 161, spent much of his eighteen years on
the Danube fighting the Marcomanni and other confederations pressing across
the frontier, while a plague - brought back, by the ancient accounts, from
the Parthian campaigns of his co-emperor Lucius Verus - killed a significant
fraction of the empire's population and, with it, a share of the tax base
and the army's own strength. It is the point at which the integrated,
lightly-taxed empire of the second century begins visibly straining, two
decades before the third century made the strain a crisis.

Marcus had a son, Commodus, and made him co-emperor and then sole heir rather
than adopting a Nerva or a Trajan of his own generation's choosing. Commodus
proved unfit for the office by nearly every account that survives him,
governed erratically for twelve years, and was strangled by a wrestling
partner in 192 in a plot his own household organised. The four adoptive
successions had not been a new constitutional settlement. They had been four
decisions by four childless men, and the fifth emperor in the sequence, the
first one who did not need to choose an heir from outside his own house, took
the alternative the moment it was available to him. What his successor
Pertinax got for it, and what the Praetorians did the moment Pertinax was
dead, is a story of an auction, told in full a little further on.
