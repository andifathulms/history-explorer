In AD 9 three legions under Varus were destroyed in the Teutoburg forest by a
Germanic coalition led by a Roman-trained auxiliary officer, Arminius, whom
Varus trusted. Suetonius has Augustus beating his head against a door and calling
for Varus to give him back his legions. The legion numbers XVII, XVIII and XIX
were never used again.

Rome did not stop expanding because of it — Britain was invaded thirty-four years
later and Dacia a century after that — but the Rhine became a frontier rather
than a stage on the way to the Elbe, and it stayed one for four hundred years.
That is why the Teutoburg disaster belongs in a chapter rather than among the
hinges: it changed where a line was drawn and not how the state worked.

## An empire that stops

The structural fact underneath the whole record is that a conquest state has a
fiscal problem when it stops conquering.

The republic's expansion had been paid for by the expansion: plunder, indemnities,
slaves, and the provinces that each war produced. By the middle of the second
century the profitable directions had run out. North of the Rhine and Danube
there was nothing worth the cost of taking. East of the Euphrates there was
Parthia, which could not be conquered — Trajan
took Ctesiphon in 116 and Hadrian gave it back the following year, and every
subsequent Roman attempt did the same thing with the same result.

So the empire lived on taxes — land, heads and trade — and not on plunder. For
the republic, plunder plainly had been structural income: the conquests paid for
themselves and for everything else. For the empire it was not, and the
difference between those two states of affairs is the reason Hadrian's Wall
exists.

## What a frontier cost

The army was between three hundred and fifty and four hundred thousand men and
it consumed the great majority of the state's revenue. It was stationed almost
entirely on the frontiers, in Britain, on the Rhine, on the Danube, in Syria and
in Egypt, and Italy — the notional centre — had no legions in it at all.

This produced the arrangement the fifth chapter is about. The soldiers were paid,
long-service professionals whose loyalty was to their commander and their unit,
concentrated in a few provinces far from Rome, and the man who commanded a
frontier army was three weeks' march from an undefended capital. Almost
everything in the empire's political history follows from where the troops
were.

## The peace

It is also worth stating what the arrangement bought, because it is not the kind
of thing that shows up in a list of provinces.

For roughly two centuries the Mediterranean basin was a single tax area with no
internal customs barriers worth the name, one currency in three metals, a road
network measured in the tens of thousands of kilometres, and no piracy. Wine from
Italy and Spain, oil from Baetica, grain from Egypt and Africa and marble from
Asia Minor moved in quantities the western Mediterranean did not see again for
more than a thousand years, and the archaeological evidence for it is the
shipwrecks: the count of dated Mediterranean wrecks peaks in this period and
falls to a fraction of it afterwards.

The empire covered about five million square kilometres, which does not put it
in the first rank by area. The Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates were larger. So
were the Yuan, the Ming, the Han, the Xiongnu confederation and even the
Rashidun conquests. The Mongol empire was seven times the size.

That is worth stating rather than hiding, because Rome is generally imagined as
the largest empire in history and it was not close. What was unusual about it was
never the extent. It was the integration: one tax area, one currency, one law, a
road network, and a sea with no piracy on it, held together for two centuries.
Nobody else did that at all, at any size.
