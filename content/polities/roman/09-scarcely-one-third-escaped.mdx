title: Scarcely one-third escaped
drafted_from: ammianus-yonge

Ammianus Marcellinus had been a staff officer in the eastern armies and was
writing within a few years of the battle, and his account of 9 August 378 is the
last thing in the record of the undivided empire that reads like testimony rather
than administration.

The Romans came up to the Gothic position in high summer, after a long march, and
found the plain in front of them on fire: the Goths had set the standing crops
alight with burning faggots on purpose, so that the army arrived parched and
exhausted, with men and animals both short of food.

Then the embassy. Fritigern, who Ammianus says was good at guessing how things
would go and did not want a doubtful battle, sent a herald asking for a few
nobles as hostages for his own safety, after which he would come over with
military help and supplies. The proposal was well received. The tribune Equitius,
a relative of the emperor and steward of the palace, was chosen by general
agreement to go — and refused, because he had been a prisoner of the Goths once
already and had escaped, and was afraid of what they would do to him. Richomeres
volunteered instead, thinking it a bold thing to do and becoming in a brave man,
and set off carrying documents proving his rank.

He never arrived. The archers and Scutarii escorting him, under two officers
Ammianus names, gave way to what he calls an indiscreet impetuosity, attacked
without orders as they came near the Goths, and then ran — disgracing, he says,
the beginning of the campaign. And while that was happening the Gothic cavalry
came back from foraging with the Alans, and descended from the hills, in the
phrase every account of this battle has borrowed since, like a thunderbolt.

The casualty list Ammianus gives is a roll of the eastern command: the two senior
generals, thirty-five tribunes holding no particular command, many battalion
commanders, the master of the horse and the steward of the palace, and a young
tribune whose father had commanded the armies and whom everybody had liked.
*Scarcely one-third of the whole army escaped.* And then the comparison, which is
the sentence the empire remembered: except for Cannae, no such destructive
slaughter is recorded in our annals.

The emperor died somewhere in it and no body was found. Ammianus then writes
Valens's obituary in the manner of his age — a faithful friend, a severe punisher
of ambition, a man who insisted that everything be decided according to law and
would not allow any decision against his own wishes, who listened to every
informer without troubling about whether the charge was true. And then, without a
transition, the body: dilatory and sluggish, swarthy, a cast in one eye not
visible at a distance, well-set limbs, neither tall nor short, knock-kneed and
rather pot-bellied.

He also notes that Valens had been given an oracle in three lines, the last of
which mentioned a plain called Mimas, and that being unlettered he had laughed at
it and then, as things got worse, became terrified of the very name of Asia. A
pillar with that name on it was found near where he was last seen.

The empire did not end in 378 and Ammianus does not say it did. What the chapter
records is the last time a Roman field army of that size was destroyed on Roman
soil with an emperor inside it, described by a man who had served in it, who knew
the officers by name, and who thought the disgrace had begun before the fighting
did.
