In 212 Caracalla issued an edict granting Roman citizenship to very nearly every
free inhabitant of the empire. A papyrus from Egypt preserves a damaged Greek
copy of it. Between twenty and thirty million people became Romans in a
sentence.

Citizenship had been the empire's central instrument for two centuries. It was
what an auxiliary earned by twenty-five years' service, what a provincial city
negotiated for over generations, what a freedman's son could acquire and a
freedman could not, and what separated a man who could not be flogged without
trial from a man who could. The system described earlier — government by
co-opting local elites — worked because citizenship was scarce and desirable.

Caracalla gave it to everybody, and the near-contemporary explanation is that he
did it to widen the base of the inheritance tax, which only citizens paid. Later
writers add a religious motive: more citizens, more people obliged to sacrifice
for the empire's welfare. The edict itself, in the surviving text, says something
about giving thanks to the gods.

## What it did

It removed the distinction the empire had been built on, and the distinction that
replaced it was wealth.

Within a generation the legal sources stop dividing people into citizens and
non-citizens and start dividing them into *honestiores* and *humiliores* — the
more honourable and the more lowly — with different penalties for the same
offence. A humilior could be flogged, tortured, sent to the mines or thrown to
the beasts; an honestior was fined, exiled or, at worst, beheaded. The old
protections attached to citizenship survived for the people who were already
powerful and were withdrawn from everybody else at exactly the moment everybody
else acquired them.

That is not an accident of drafting. A society that has abolished a legal
distinction it depended on will produce another one, and the one it produced was
franker about what it was measuring.

## What it altered

The empire's tax system was administered by cities whose elites paid for the
privilege of administering it. The privilege was citizenship. After 212 a city
councillor's status came from the office rather than from the citizenship, the
office became compulsory and hereditary during the third century because nobody
wanted the liability any more, and by the fourth the *curiales* were a legally
bound class forbidden to leave their cities.

An empire that had ruled by making people want to be Roman finished making them
Roman and then had to start compelling the work that the wanting had paid for.
