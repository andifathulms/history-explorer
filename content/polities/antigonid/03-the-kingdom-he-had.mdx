Philip V came to the throne at seventeen and was, in the phrase Polybius uses of
his early years, the darling of Greece — a young king of an old kingdom,
energetic, personally brave, and better liked than a Macedonian had been for a
long time.

<Figure id="philip-v-coin" />

Polybius watched him closely and disliked what he became, and the value of the
account is that it is written by a Greek from a rival league who nonetheless
records what the kingdom could actually do.

## What that was

It could put a fleet to sea and did, repeatedly, in the Aegean and off Asia. It
could campaign in Illyria, in Thessaly, in the Peloponnese and against the
Aetolians in the same decade. It could take cities: Polybius describes Philip
delighted at capturing one as though the whole war had been settled by it, which
is a criticism of the man and a statement of the fact.

<Figure id="trireme-relief" />

And it could operate at range. When Rome and Carthage were fighting in Italy,
Philip made a treaty with Hannibal — a Macedonian king negotiating a partition
of the western Mediterranean with a Carthaginian general — and later he was
anxious above all things to anticipate the Romans in getting to a position
first, which is Polybius's way of saying that by then the calculation ran the
other way.

## The limitation, stated early

Set against all of that is the thing Polybius keeps returning to whenever he
describes a Macedonian army in the field: the phalanx.

Nothing in the world could stand against it in a straight line on level ground.
Polybius says so as a professional soldier who had seen both systems, and his
comparison of the phalanx and the legion is the most quoted passage he ever
wrote. He also says the other half, which people quote less: the formation is
useless in broken country, and armies do not get to choose the ground.

That is not hindsight applied after Pydna. Polybius sets it out as a general
principle about the two military systems, and it explains why a kingdom that
could beat any Greek opponent, hold Thessaly, garrison the Peloponnese and treat
with Hannibal was nonetheless going to lose to Rome the first time the two met
somewhere hilly.

## What the peak actually looked like

A kingdom of perhaps a few hundred thousand people, fielding an army of its own
farmers, holding the passes into Greece and the three fortresses the Greeks
called the fetters of Hellas, financed by its own silver mines, with a fleet and
a foreign policy that reached from the Adriatic to the coast of Asia.

<Figure id="demetrias" />

It is small by the standards of the other Successor kingdoms and it lasted longer
than either of them in Alexander's own country. What it could not do was lose an
army, because the men in it could not be replaced — and it was about to meet a
state that could lose several.
