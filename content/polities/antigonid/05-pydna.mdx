Aemilius Paullus destroyed the Macedonian army at Pydna in 168, and what Rome
did afterwards is better documented than the battle, because Rome made a
spectacle of it.

<Figure id="paullus-inscription" />

Perseus surrendered. He was brought to Italy and kept for the triumph, which
took three days, and Plutarch describes the third.

## The procession

First the plate: gold coin divided into seventy-seven vessels of three talents
each; a consecrated bowl of ten talents set with precious stones; the cups of
Antigonus and of Seleucus, and the Thericlean ware, and all the gold service
from Perseus' own table.

<Figure id="phiale" />

Then the king's chariot, with his armour laid in it and his diadem on top of the
armour.

Then, after a pause, the children — two sons and a daughter — led as captives
with their attendants, their tutors and their teachers, all weeping and holding
out their hands to the spectators and making the children beg too. They were too
young to understand what was happening to them, which Plutarch says made it
worse to watch. Many Romans wept. Perseus himself walked behind them and was
scarcely looked at.

He came last of the family, dressed in black, wearing the boots of his own
country, and looking, in the account, like a man stunned out of his reason.

## The request

Before the triumph Perseus had sent to Aemilius asking to be left out of it.

The reply is the coldest sentence in the chapter. Aemilius answered, deriding
his cowardice and his fondness for life, that as for that, it had been before
and was still in his own power — meaning that a man who did not wish to be
displayed could avoid it by dying.

Perseus did not have the spirit for it, and, in Plutarch's phrase, *allowed
himself to appear as a part of his own spoils*.

<Figure id="vernet-triumph" />

## What the money did

Behind the captives came four hundred gold crowns sent by Greek cities to
congratulate the victor, and then Aemilius in a purple robe worked with gold,
holding a laurel branch, with the whole army following and singing.

<Figure id="triumph-coin" />

The consequence of all that treasure is stated by Plutarch as a plain fact and it
is the most far-reaching thing in the chapter. Aemilius brought so much money
into the public treasury from Macedonia that the Roman people paid no direct tax
again until the consulship of Hirtius and Pansa — which is to say, for the next
hundred and twenty years, down to the first war between Antony and Caesar.

The Macedonian monarchy did not merely fall. It funded the republic that
abolished it, through the whole of that republic's greatest period, until the
republic itself came apart.
