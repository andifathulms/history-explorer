Perseus was moved out of the common prison at Rome, the Carcer, into somewhere
cleaner and more humane, because Aemilius Paullus pitied him and could obtain
nothing better. He died there.

<Figure id="carcer" />

Plutarch gives two accounts of how and declines to choose. In one, he starved
himself. In the other, the soldiers guarding him conceived a spite against him,
and finding no other way to torment a prisoner they were forbidden to harm,
they kept him from sleeping — disturbing him whenever he lay down, inventing
contrivances to keep him permanently awake — until he was worn out and died.

Two of his children died soon after him.

## The third

The third was called Alexander, and Plutarch records what became of him in a
single sentence that says more about the end of Macedon than the battle does.

He became an accomplished craftsman in turning and engraving small figures; he
learned to speak and write Latin so perfectly that he was made clerk to the
Roman magistrates; and he performed the work with great skill and good conduct.

<Figure id="intaglio" />

The great-great-grandson of Antigonus Gonatas, and the last male of the house
that had governed Alexander's homeland for a hundred and ten years, spent his
adult life making ornaments and keeping records for the officials of the city
that had taken his father's kingdom, and by the account was good at it.

<Figure id="wax-tablet" />

## What Rome did to the country

The kingdom itself was not transferred to anybody. It was dismantled.

Macedonia was divided into four republics forbidden to trade or intermarry
across the new internal borders. The royal land was taken and the mines shut.
The leading men of the Greek states were deported to Italy in their thousands —
a group that included Polybius, which is the only reason a detailed account of
any of this survives.

Rome had removed kings before and had always put someone in the place: a
brother, a son, a client, a settlement. Here it abolished the office. Twenty
years earlier that had not been in the repertoire; twenty years later it was
what Rome did to Carthage and Corinth in the same year.

## And the money

The last consequence is the longest-running. The treasure Aemilius brought back
from Macedonia was so large that the Roman people paid no direct tax again for
something like a hundred and twenty years.

So the practical afterlife of the Antigonid kingdom is this: its silver relieved
Roman citizens of taxation for four generations, its aristocracy's deportation
produced the greatest historian of the Roman rise, its country was legislated
into four pieces that could not trade with each other, and its last prince
engraved small figures and kept the minutes.
