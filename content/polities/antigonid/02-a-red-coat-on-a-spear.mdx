In 222 BC an Antigonid king destroyed a Spartan army in the field and entered
Sparta, which no enemy had done in its history. Plutarch tells the battle twice,
in two different lives, and the two accounts together are the best picture there
is of what Macedonian hegemony in Greece actually consisted of.

<Figure id="taygetus" />

Cleomenes had seized the passes and was posted on the hills at Sellasia.
Antigonus Doson came up and drew out opposite him, meaning to force the
position. On the Macedonian side the Achaean contingent and the Illyrian foot
were held in reserve with orders not to engage until they saw a red coat raised
on a spear point from the wing where the king was fighting in person.

The Achaeans obeyed. The Illyrians did not — their own officers led them
forward — and Cleomenes' brother Euclidas, seeing the foot separated from the
horse, sent his best light troops round to take them in the rear. At that point a
young Achaean officer named Philopoemen went to the king's staff to tell them
what the moment needed, and was ignored as a hare-brained fellow, having as yet
no reputation that would make anyone listen. So he charged with his own citizens
instead, broke the light troops, and then got off his horse to fight on foot in
horseman's armour over broken ground, and had both thighs pinned by a thonged
javelin thrown hard enough to come out the other side. He stood there a while, as
if shackled, unable to move.

<Figure id="philopoemen" />

From the other side, Plutarch tells it as a disaster with a specific cause.
Cleomenes, watching from high ground, could not see the Illyrians and Acarnanians
and guessed they had been sent round; he called for the officer whose job was
exactly that and told him to watch his rear. The officer — bribed by Antigonus,
according to one source Plutarch names — told him all was well and to mind the
enemy in front. So Cleomenes charged, pushed the Macedonian phalanx back half a
mile, then looked across, saw his brother's wing surrounded, and cried out that
he was lost. Six thousand Spartans were killed. Two hundred survived.

<Figure id="helmet" />

Then the detail that makes the whole campaign an accident. Letters recalling
Antigonus to Macedonia — a barbarian invasion at home — had been on the road and
almost reached him before the battle; if they had, he would have marched away and
left the Achaeans to manage alone. The messengers arrived immediately after
Cleomenes had lost his army and his city. Had Cleomenes retreated two days
longer, Plutarch writes, he need not have fought at all and could have had what
terms he liked. He fought because he had run out of money to pay his mercenaries,
with twenty thousand men against thirty.

<Figure id="sellasia-plan" />

Cleomenes rode back into Sparta and advised the citizens he met to receive
Antigonus, and then left for Egypt.

That is the Antigonid kingdom at its greatest reach, and everything about how it
got there is in the account: a king who ruled Macedonia and was also the elected
hegemon of a Greek league, fighting a Greek war on Greek ground with Illyrian and
Acarnanian mercenaries, whose signal system failed, whose victory turned on an
Achaean officer nobody had heard of, and who won because his opponent could not
afford another fortnight. Ten days later he was needed a long way north, which is
where Macedonian kings were usually needed, and which is the reason the hegemony
of Greece was never a thing the dynasty could hold with both hands.
