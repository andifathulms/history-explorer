---
title: No unworthy rival

With the Welsh frontier settled, Offa turned to a goal every strong
Mercian king before him had failed to secure outright: real authority
over the other English kingdoms. His method was calculated rather
than martial. He avoided outright conquest almost entirely, preferring
influence he could deny holding to a supremacy he would have had to
defend by arms. A campaign against Hastings, and a victory at Otford,
reasserted Mercian dominance over a rebellious Kent; East Anglia
seems to have been annexed outright. But with Northumbria and Wessex
he worked through marriage rather than war, placing a client of his
own on the West Saxon throne and wedding him to his daughter
Eadburh, then marrying a second daughter to the Northumbrian king.
Both neighboring thrones, threatened by rival claimants of their own,
came to depend on Offa's own support to survive - dependence he never
had to name as subjection to keep.

## A rival's court fills with his enemies

That quiet supremacy might have hardened into outright sovereignty
had a foreign power not intervened. Charlemagne's own court, drawn
into English affairs through missionary contacts, became a refuge for
everyone Offa's rise had displaced: Eardwulf, driven from Northumbria
by the husband of one of Offa's daughters, and Ecgberht, driven from
Wessex by the husband of another. When Kent revolted against Mercia
again, appealing to Charlemagne for protection, Offa answered the
Frankish king's threats with open defiance, reconquered Kent by force,
and uncovered a plot by the Archbishop of Canterbury to bring Frankish
troops ashore - a plot he crushed by driving the archbishop into
exile and setting up a rival archbishopric at Lichfield to punish his
see. The most powerful ruler in Western Europe had, without quite
meaning to, become the patron of every man Offa had reason to fear.
