Aethelbald had ruled Mercia for forty-one years when his own
bodyguard murdered him by night at Seckington, near Tamworth, in
757. No source explains the motive with any certainty, though a
contemporary writer described him bluntly as a royal tyrant, and
one surviving charter records land granted to a Mercian abbess
"because he had stabbed her kinsman" - language that suggests
something closer to a private killing than an act of war. A king of
that temperament could easily generate the kind of grudge that ends
in assassination by the men sworn to protect him.

## A rival with the same claim

The murder left no clear heir, and a civil war followed between two
men with roughly equal claims to the throne: a certain Beornred,
whose connection to the Mercian royal house is otherwise unrecorded,
and Offa, son of Thingfrith, who like Aethelbald traced his descent
from a brother of the great seventh-century king Penda. Offa won.
Before 757 was over he had driven Beornred into exile and taken the
kingdom for himself.

Victory in Mercia was not, at first, victory over much else. The
wider confederacy of dependent kingdoms that Aethelbald had spent
his own long reign assembling fell apart the moment he died, and for
the first seven years of Offa's rule he held little authority beyond
Mercia's own midland heartland and its immediate dependencies. No
Mercian chronicle survives from this period to say how he began
rebuilding it; what evidence exists comes from charters alone, and
what they suggest is that Kent, not the midlands, was the first
established kingdom to fall under his growing influence.
