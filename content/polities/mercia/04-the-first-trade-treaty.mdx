The breach with the Franks that had been building for years came to a
head over a marriage. In or shortly before 789, Charlemagne proposed
that his own son marry one of Offa's daughters. Offa's answer was to
insist on an equal exchange: Charlemagne's daughter Bertha would have
to marry Offa's own son Ecgfrith in return. It was not an unreasonable
demand from a king whose own family line ran older than Charlemagne's,
but Charlemagne took it as an insult. He broke off correspondence
with Offa outright and closed the ports of his own kingdom to English
traders.

The closure lasted for years. Alcuin, writing from the Frankish court
at the end of 790, still described the ports as shut and himself as
expecting to be sent to England to help make peace. What the dispute
revealed, almost as a side effect, was how much ordinary commerce had
been passing between the two kingdoms before the quarrel interrupted
it - trade substantial enough that its suspension registered, on both
sides, as a genuine act of hostility rather than a minor diplomatic
snub.

## Equals, on paper

Peace, when it came, came through Alcuin and Gervold of Saint-Wandrille,
working the two courts over years of patient negotiation. In 796 -
the last year of Offa's own reign - the two kings concluded the first
commercial treaty in the whole of English history: traders crossing
from England to Gaul, or Gaul to England, would have the protection of
the public authorities wherever they landed. It was a modest document
for the quarrel that had preceded it, but the fact of its existence
said what neither king had been willing to say outright during the
dispute itself - that Offa of Mercia was a ruler Charlemagne dealt
with as an equal, not as a neighbor to be corrected.
