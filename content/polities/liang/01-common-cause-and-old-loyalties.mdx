Xiao Yan, a cadet of the fallen Qi ruling family who had led the
revolt against it, founded the Liang dynasty in 502 under the
personal style Wuti. He overcame his rivals with little real
difficulty, and Boulger credits his own moderation with disarming
much of the hostility that might otherwise have gathered against a
new ruler who had, after all, just helped destroy the family he
himself belonged to. One early episode captures the mood exactly: a
minister of the old regime chose to starve himself rather than eat
the new emperor's bread, and Wuti, more philosophical than offended,
remarked, "Is it not from Heaven, and not from court notables, that I
have received the crown? What reason then induced this miserable
personage to commit suicide?" Boulger reads the incident as evidence
of how thoroughly loyalty to a fallen house could outlast the house
itself, even among men the new ruler had done nothing personally to
wrong.

## An idealist's early stumble

Wuti's own instincts ran toward reform rather than conquest, and his
very first substantial act as emperor - abolishing capital punishment
outright - showed both the appeal and the danger of that instinct. The
policy produced, in short order, a marked rise in crime, and Wuti was
forced to suspend what Boulger calls a well-meant but very
injudicious experiment. It was an inauspicious start for a reign that
would go on to last forty-seven years, longer than any other ruler
across the troubled span between the fall of the Han and the rise of
the Tang - and it set the tone for a monarch who would spend
his own long rule more concerned with governing well than with
expanding what he governed.
