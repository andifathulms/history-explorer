For most of Wuti's long reign the Liang held their own against
Northern Wei without ever quite defeating it. The wars that opened in
503 produced real heroism on both sides and, in most seasons, more
credit to Wei than to the Liang. In one encounter alone more than
fifty thousand imperial soldiers were reckoned slain; Wuti's generals
lost heart afterward and would not come out of their entrenchments,
even when the Wei army rode round their camp displaying a dead man's
head dressed in a widow's cap and chanting a doggerel challenge naming
them cowards by name. They escaped only under cover of a night's heavy
rain.

## Mengqi at Chouyang

The one clear exception came at Chouyang, a fortress entrusted to one
of Wei's most capable lieutenants, Ginching. During his temporary
absence with part of the garrison, Liang generals learned the town was
undermanned and appeared before its walls in force, carrying the outer
defences without a blow. At that point, with the place almost in their
grasp, Ginching's wife Mengqi came out onto the ramparts and restored
the garrison's sinking courage herself - arming the townspeople,
strengthening the gates, and holding the defence together by the
inspiration of her own presence until the besiegers gave up and
withdrew. It was the single Liang success of an otherwise one-sided
season; Ginching himself won a battle in the field at the same time,
but had to abandon his own siege of the fortress of Chongli when the
Hoaiho River flooded his camp.

## Chongli, and the tiger of Hofei

In 507 Wuti put two hundred thousand men in the field under Weijoui,
the one general who had reliably won him battles, and the war returned
to Chongli. The Wei general Yuenyng laid siege to it with three hundred
thousand men and, confident in the bridges he had thrown across the
Hoaiho to keep his divided army in communication, held his ground even
as Weijoui's relief force approached. It was a fatal confidence.
Weijoui saw the weakness at a glance, filled vessels with combustibles,
and sent them upriver by night against the bridges on which Yuenyng's
whole position depended. By morning the Wei army was split in two with
no way to reinforce itself, and the Liang assault that followed put the
southern division to the sword or the river. Weijoui and his lieutenant
then turned on the northern division and broke it in a second, harder
battle. More than two hundred thousand Wei soldiers died, and their
standards and baggage became the victors' spoil - a defeat severe
enough that a captured challenge song had already named Weijoui, half
in fear, "the tiger of Hofei."

It was the single most complete victory either side won in the whole
war, and Wuti did nothing with it. Implored by Weijoui to press the
advantage, he chose instead to rest on the triumph and return to his
own religious devotions, calling his army home and letting Wei recover
from a blow that could otherwise have broken it for good. Several years
of peace followed Chongli, and Wuti spent them on the education and
internal government of his own subjects rather than on the war his own
best general had just as good as won for him.
