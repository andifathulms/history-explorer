Age did not improve Wuti's own judgment. Northern Wei broke apart into
two rival, warring halves not long after Chongli, and it was in the
aftermath of that collapse that Wuti dreamt a dream promising him the
long-contested province of Honan - and was weak enough to treat it as
a genuine sign. He ordered his troops to seize what he believed had
been supernaturally decreed his; the army was defeated in the field and
driven back across the frontier in confusion, and with it went whatever
was left of the scheme of conquest Wuti had once had Weijoui's own
unused victory to build on. Boulger's verdict is blunt: "an aged
prince, broken in power as in health, hastening to the fall which was
inevitable."

## Hou Jing

That fall came from within his own ranks. In 549 Hou Jing, one of
Wuti's vassals, renounced his allegiance, published an indictment of
the emperor, and marched on the capital. Wuti's palace guard offered
only token resistance before the eighty-year-old emperor found himself
a captive of the very soldiery he had once commanded.

"It was I who raised my family and it is I who have destroyed it," Wuti
is recorded as saying. "I have no reason to complain." He did not long
survive the humiliation - Boulger records that the shock told on his
shattered constitution and he found relief within days, though another
account, Du Halde's, has him dying after his own attendants refused him
a request for honey to ease a pain in his throat. Either way, a reign
of forty-seven years, the longest and, in Boulger's own judgment, the
most praiseworthy of the whole period between the Han and the Tang,
ended in captivity in his own palace at the hands of a man he had once
trusted.
