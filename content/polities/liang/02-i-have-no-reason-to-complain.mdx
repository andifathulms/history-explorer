For most of Wuti's long reign the Liang held their own against
Northern Wei without ever quite defeating it. The wars that opened in
503 produced real heroism on both sides - at Chouyang, the wife of
the Wei commandant, Mengqi, rallied a garrison stripped of most of
its defenders and held the walls until her own husband's army could
return, while imperial forces suffered losses Boulger records in the
tens of thousands in a single season. By 507 Wuti had two hundred
thousand men in the field under Weijoui, the one general who had
reliably won him battles, in a renewed contest for the fortress of
Chongli - fighting that dragged on for decades without either side
gaining a lasting advantage, even as internal troubles began
splitting Wei itself into rival factions Wuti's own generals could
never quite exploit.

## An aged emperor's dream

Age did not improve Wuti's own judgment. Late in his reign he took a
dream promising him the long-contested province of Honan as a
genuine omen, and ordered his troops to seize what he believed had
been supernaturally decreed his - an invasion that ended in defeat and
retreat, and left Boulger's own verdict blunt: "an aged prince, broken
in power as in health, hastening to the fall which was inevitable."
That fall came from within his own ranks. In 549 Hou Jing, one of
Wuti's vassals, renounced his allegiance, published an indictment of
the emperor, and marched on the capital; Wuti's palace guard offered
only token resistance before the eighty-year-old emperor found
himself a captive of the very soldiery he had once commanded.
"It was I who raised my family and it is I who have destroyed it,"
Wuti is recorded as saying. "I have no reason to complain." He did
not long survive the humiliation, dying within days - a reign of
forty-seven years, the longest and, in Boulger's own judgment, the
most praiseworthy of the whole period between the Han and the Tang,
ending in captivity in his own palace.

## Three emperors, eight years, one library burned

What followed Wuti's death unraveled fast. His third son Wenti,
installed by Hou Jing as a puppet, was murdered by his own minister
within three years; Wenti's brother Yuandi overthrew Hou Jing in turn
and had his body publicly displayed, only to be besieged himself
within three more years by the general Chen Baxian. Yuandi's own
surrender came with a gesture that outlasted the man who made it: he
broke his sword and burned a library of a hundred and forty thousand
volumes, declaring "all is over! All my skill in war and letters
henceforth becomes useless to me" - and was killed shortly afterward,
his capital handed over to plunder. The last Liang ruler, Jingdi,
Yuandi's own ninth son, held the throne two more years before Chen
Baxian murdered him in 557 and founded his own dynasty in the Liang's
place - closing a fifty-six-year span in which one exceptional
forty-seven-year reign was followed, within a single decade, by the
same cycle of murder and usurpation that had ended every dynasty
before it.
