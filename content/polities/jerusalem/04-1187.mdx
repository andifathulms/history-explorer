On 3 and 4 July 1187 the kingdom marched its entire field army — every knight
that could be raised, garrisons stripped from the castles, the treasure sent
from England spent on hiring more — across a waterless plateau in high summer
towards Tiberias, and Saladin destroyed it at the Horns of Hattin.

The disaster is not that a battle was lost. It is that there was nothing behind
it. The army at Hattin was the kingdom, in the literal sense that the men
holding every fortified place in the country were standing in it, and when it
was gone the castles had nobody in them. Acre surrendered in four days. Jaffa,
Sidon, Beirut, Ascalon, Nablus, Ramla followed. Jerusalem, defended by a
population swollen with refugees and almost no soldiers, surrendered on 2
October after twelve days. By the end of the year the kingdom consisted of
Tyre, which held only because Conrad of Montferrat happened to arrive by sea in
the week it would otherwise have fallen.

Eighty-eight years of state-building ended inside five months. The kingdom did
not.

What reassembled itself on the coast after the Third Crusade kept the name,
kept the Assizes and the Haute Cour, kept the dynasty through Isabella and her
successive husbands, and was recognised by everyone as the same kingdom in law.
Historians commonly distinguish the first kingdom from the second, and the
distinction is real: different capital, different territory, different economy,
and a different balance of power between the crown, the military orders and the
Italian communes. It is a distinction between two periods of one state, not
between two states, and it is worth insisting on, because the kingdom of
Jerusalem spent longer not holding Jerusalem than holding it.

Saladin's problem after 1187 was the mirror image. Saladin's problem after 1187
was that he had won the country and could not finish the Franks on the coast,
because the sea was not his, and every crusade after his death arrived on it.
