For a century after 1191 the kingdom of Jerusalem was a coastal strip governed
from Acre, and it did not much resemble the thing it was named after.

Its money was trade. The Italian communes — Venetian, Genoese, Pisan — held
quarters in the ports with their own courts, consuls, weights and fleets, and
the customs of Acre were worth more to the crown than the agricultural revenue
of what territory remained. It also meant the kingdom contained three sovereign
commercial powers whose wars with each other it could not stop: the War of
Saint Sabas in the 1250s was fought inside Acre between Venice and Genoa, with
the military orders taking sides, while the Mamluks were consolidating Egypt.

Its kings were increasingly absent. The crown passed to the Hohenstaufen and
then, by the Haute Cour's own judgement, to the Lusignans of Cyprus, and by the
last decades the king of Jerusalem was normally to be found on Cyprus. And the
rule for who inherited is genuinely hard to state. The crown was hereditary; it
passed four times through women to their husbands; and every one of those
transfers had to be accepted by an assembly of barons who argued the law of it
at length and in public. Jerusalem produced the most elaborate constitutional
literature of any Frankish state precisely because its succession was never
settled enough to stop being argued about.

Jerusalem itself was held again for those fifteen years by the treaty
described earlier. The court never moved back.

The end came in 1291. Al-Ashraf Khalil brought siege artillery from Damascus
and Baalbek and took Acre in six weeks; Tyre, Sidon, Beirut and Tortosa were
abandoned within weeks, most without being attacked. Nothing succeeded the
kingdom and nothing had preceded it: it was founded by an expedition from
outside the region altogether, inherited by nobody, and ended by the state next
door.

The title outlived the state by six hundred years, carried by the Lusignan
kings of Cyprus and afterwards claimed by the houses of Savoy, Naples, Lorraine
and Habsburg — down to the twentieth century, long after anyone believed it
meant a country. What those claims were worth is a question about heraldry
rather than about states, and the answer changes depending on which century is
asking.
