The king had already left. He had got away to Cyprus in the early days of the
siege of Acre, and the grand master of the Hospital and a crowd of other
fugitives followed him.

Sixty thousand Christians were still in the city when it fell, and their
choices were slavery or the sword. The Templars and the people who had taken
refuge with them made the best end: they held out in their fortress until it
was undermined, and went down in the ruins along with a large number of the men
storming it.

## The demolition

What followed was not a conquest of the coast but its dismantling.

Acre was stormed, sacked and burnt, its towers thrown down and its
fortifications levelled. A town rose on the site later; the Acre of the
crusading century was gone. Tyre, Sidon, Beirut and the rest were taken or
surrendered within weeks, mostly without a fight, and all of them except Beirut
were demolished and their inhabitants killed or enslaved.

The reasoning was strategic and entirely deliberate. A fortified port on that
coast was a place a European fleet could land at and hold, and the Mamluk state
had decided there would not be any. It cost them the Levantine harbours as
well, which is a large price paid on purpose.

Lane-Poole's phrase for what ended is worth keeping: the debate of the world
was closed.

## The victor's procession

Khalil entered Damascus with a crowd of captives and a display of Christian
skulls on his spearheads.

Then he did something that says more about the period than the skulls do. He
had the old Abbasid caliph brought out of his quiet retreat in the citadel of
Cairo and made him preach the holy war in the mosque three times, and held a
service at his own father's tomb. He announced that he would conquer Asia and
the land of the Romans until his rule ran from sunrise to sunset, and went
home.

The caliph in that sentence is the powerless one of Cairo, kept for exactly
this: a man with no soldiers, produced in public to say that what had happened
was lawful.

## What survived

The title survived, and nothing else did.

The kings of Jerusalem went on being kings of Jerusalem from Cyprus, and after
the Lusignans the claim was picked up by the houses of Savoy, Naples, Lorraine
and Habsburg, and was still being included in royal styles in the twentieth
century, when it had been six hundred years since anyone holding it had
governed so much as a village in Palestine.

The kingdom itself had existed for a hundred and ninety-two years, held its
capital for eighty-eight of them, and spent the remaining hundred and four
being named after a city it did not have.
