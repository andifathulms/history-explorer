On 3 and 4 July 1187 the kingdom marched its entire field army — every knight
that could be raised, garrisons stripped from the castles, the treasure sent
from England spent on hiring more — across a waterless plateau in high summer
towards Tiberias, and Saladin destroyed it at the Horns of Hattin.

The disaster is not that a battle was lost. It is that there was nothing behind
it. The army at Hattin was the kingdom, in the literal sense that the men
holding every fortified place in the country were standing in it, and when it
was gone the castles had nobody in them. Acre surrendered in four days. Jaffa,
Sidon, Beirut, Ascalon, Nablus, Ramla followed. Jerusalem, defended by a
population swollen with refugees and almost no soldiers, surrendered on 2
October after twelve days. By the end of the year the kingdom consisted of Tyre,
which held only because Conrad of Montferrat happened to arrive by sea in the
week it would otherwise have fallen.

Eighty-eight years of state-building ended inside five months, and this record's
span does not stop.

That is the decision this page has to defend. The kingdom that reassembled
itself on the coast after the Third Crusade kept the name, kept the Assizes and
the Haute Cour, kept the dynasty through Isabella and her successive husbands,
and was recognised by everyone as the same polity in law. Historians commonly
distinguish the first kingdom from the second, and the distinction is real —
different capital, different territory, different economy, different balance of
power between crown, orders and communes. But it is a distinction between
periods of one state and not between two states, and the corpus has no edge type
for a polity succeeding itself.

So there is no edge from this page to the Ayyubid record, although 1187 is the
year an Ayyubid army took this kingdom's capital and nine-tenths of its ground.
`conquered by` asserts that the earlier polity was ended by the later one, and
that is not what happened here: the state survived, badly, for another hundred
and four years. The claim belongs in a turning point, where it can be dated and
sourced and say what it actually altered, and that is where this record puts it.

The Ayyubid page tells the other side. Saladin's problem after 1187 was that he
had won the country and could not finish the Franks on the coast, because the
sea was not his, and every crusade after his death arrived on it.
