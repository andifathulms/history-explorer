Jerusalem fell to Saladin in 1187 and the kingdom did not end. It moved to the
coast, held Acre for another century, and got the city back once — in 1229, by
treaty, without a battle, under an emperor the pope had excommunicated.

## A crusade with no army worth the name

Frederick II's crusade was unique in all its circumstances, Lane-Poole writes,
and the list of what was wrong with it is long.

His forces were too weak to risk an engagement with the Saracen armies. His
contemptuous treatment of John of Brienne had alienated the settled Christians of
Syria. His quarrel with Rome cost him the churchmen. The Templars and Hospitallers
sullenly refused to follow a leader under the curse of the Holy See. And, in a
sentence worth pausing on, nobody in Syria seemed to care very much about
recovering Jerusalem.

What he had instead was a diplomatic position, and it had been offered to him.

## Why Cairo made the offer

Al-Kamil, sultan of Egypt, had a problem inside his own family: his brother
al-Mu'azzam in Damascus, suspected of using the exhaustion after Damietta and his
own war record to shake off Egyptian authority over Saladin's empire.

Alarmed at this, al-Kamil sent an embassy to Frederick — approaching him
specifically as the sovereign of the Muslims of Sicily — offering him the kingdom
of Jerusalem in exchange for his support. The bishop of Palermo went to Cairo in
return and costly presents were exchanged.

Frederick was on unusually good terms with Muslims, and it was noticed. The pope
called him a follower of Muhammad. His published correspondence with the
philosopher Ibn Sab'in, and the metaphysical arguments he liked to draw
al-Kamil's envoys into after he arrived, point to what Lane-Poole calls
emancipated views — the kind of views that took less distinguished men to the
stake. An Arab historian's verdict is quoted directly: *the emperor was the most
excellent among the kings of the Franks, devoted to science, philosophy and
medicine, and well-disposed towards Muslims.* Twenty years later Joinville found
that being Frederick's kinsman was the best passport a Frank could carry among
the Mamluks.

Then al-Mu'azzam died in the winter of 1227 and the danger that had prompted the
offer largely went away. That Frederick still got the treaty is the measure of
the diplomacy.

## The terms

Signed 11 February 1229 and sworn a week later. Lane-Poole calls it the most
remarkable agreement ever signed between a Christian and a Muslim power before
the European treaties with the Ottomans.

Egypt surrendered Jerusalem — **not to be fortified** — with Bethlehem, Nazareth
and the pilgrim road to Jaffa and on to Acre, into the emperor's absolute
possession, reserving the *haram* with the Dome of the Rock for the exclusive use
of unarmed Muslims. All Christian prisoners were released, including survivors of
the Children's Crusade.

In return Frederick undertook to defend the sultan against all enemies, *even
Christians* — and guaranteed that Antioch, Tripoli and the other northern Syrian
principalities would receive no assistance from any outside power.

The Latin states of the north were signed away, by a Christian emperor, as
consideration in a contract with Cairo. Ten and a half years.

## Everybody's objection

Both sets of zealots were appalled, and the papal party's complaint was precise:
Frederick had bargained with the infidels instead of killing them. The prince of
Antioch and the military orders holding castles in the north bitterly resented
the clause cutting them off from Europe, and read it — plausibly — as revenge for
having refused to follow him. Muslims regarded the whole transaction as a
shameful betrayal of Islam.

Frederick entered Jerusalem within the month, enthroned himself in the Church of
the Holy Sepulchre, and put the crown on his own head, because no churchman would.

The next day the archbishop of Caesarea placed the Holy City under interdict.
Pilgrims who had walked across the world to reach it stood in the streets saying
that the place where Jesus suffered and was buried had been banned by a pope, and
then hurried after the emperor to Acre.

## What it says about the kingdom

The recovery held for fifteen years and Jerusalem was lost again in 1244, this
time for good.

But the manner of the recovery is the real information. By the thirteenth century
the kingdom was not a conquest state and could not act like one. It was a coastal
polity with a legal claim, embedded in a Levantine system where the decisive
resources were treaties, dynastic quarrels in Cairo and Damascus, and the ability
to guarantee somebody else's frontier.

Frederick understood that and got the city. It cost him the support of every
constituency he had, and it cost the northern principalities their access to
Europe. The kingdom of Jerusalem spent its last century being defended by exactly
these means, and finally lost because there was nobody left in Cairo who needed
anything from it.
