Usama ibn Munqidh was a Syrian nobleman of Shayzar who spent a long life among
the Franks — fighting them, hunting with them, negotiating with them, visiting
their cities — and wrote his memoirs in old age. He is the closest thing there
is to an ordinary Muslim account of what the kingdom of Jerusalem was like to
be in.

His general rule is worth stating first, because everything else follows from
it: anyone freshly arrived from the Frankish lands is ruder in character than
those who have become acclimatised and have had long association with the
Muslims.

## In the Aqsa

Whenever he was in Jerusalem, Usama went to pray at the Aqsa mosque. Next to it
stood a smaller mosque the Franks had converted into a church. The Aqsa itself
was occupied by the Templars, who were, he says, his friends, and who would
clear the little adjoining mosque so that he could pray in it.

One day he went in, said the opening *Allahu akbar*, and stood to pray. A Frank
rushed at him, seized him and turned his face east: *this is the way thou
shouldst pray*. A group of Templars hurried over, took hold of the man and
pulled him off. Usama started again. The same man, while the Templars were busy
with something else, rushed at him a second time and turned his face east again
with the same words. The Templars came back and threw him out, and then
apologised: this is a stranger, newly arrived from the land of the Franks, who
has never seen anyone pray except towards the east.

Usama's response is the most human sentence in his book. *Thereupon I said to
myself: I have had enough prayer.* And he went out, and remained astonished
ever afterwards at the man's face and his trembling and his fury at the sight
of somebody praying towards Mecca.

## What that scene contains

Read it slowly, because a great deal of the kingdom is in it.

An enemy nobleman walks into the third holiest site in Islam, then a Templar
headquarters, expecting to pray there. The knights of a military order founded
to kill men like him keep a mosque cleared for his use, and physically restrain
a fellow Christian for interfering. They apologise, and they explain, and their
explanation is that the offender has not been in the country long enough to
know better.

None of that is tolerance in the modern sense and none of it is friendship
between religions. It is what happens when two societies occupy the same ground
for two generations: the people at the front stop being able to afford the
purity of the people who have just arrived, and the newcomers become the
problem. That is the mechanism the kingdom actually ran on, and it is invisible
in the chronicles, which are written by and for the people who have just
arrived.

## The doctors

Usama's other famous story cuts the other way, and he tells it as comedy.

His uncle sent a Christian physician named Thabit to treat some sick people for
a Frankish lord. Thabit was back in ten days. He had put a poultice on a
knight's abscessed leg until it opened and healed, and put a woman with a
nervous complaint on a diet to restore her humours. Then a Frankish physician
arrived, said the man knew nothing, and asked the knight whether he would
rather live with one leg or die with two. A strong knight was fetched with a
sharp axe, the leg was laid on a block, and it took two blows; the marrow ran
out and the patient died on the spot. The physician then diagnosed a devil in
the woman's head, had it shaved, made a cruciform incision down to the skull
and rubbed the bone with salt. She died instantly. Thabit asked whether his
services were still required, was told they were not, and went home having
learned of their medicine what he had not known before.

Then Usama does the thing that makes him a source and not a joke book. *I have,
however, witnessed a case of their medicine which was quite different from
that* — and he goes on to describe one that worked.

He is not writing propaganda. He is writing down what happened, including the
parts that spoil the story, which is why he can be believed about the Templars.
