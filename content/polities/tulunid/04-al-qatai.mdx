Fustat was too small for the retinue and the army Ibn Tulun had assembled, and
he had no intention of living in a governor's house in any case. In 870 he took
a site on the hill of Yashkur between Fustat and the Muqattam, levelled the
graves of the Christian cemetery there, and laid out a new town.

It was called al-Qata'i, the Wards, because each class and each nationality —
household servants, Greeks, Sudanis — was assigned a quarter of its own. It
covered a square mile. There was a palace with a great garden, an enclosed
*maydan* for horses with mews and a menagerie beside it, a separate palace for
the household, baths, markets, and a private passage from the residence to the
emir's oratory.

## The building that outlived the town

The great mosque was begun in 876 and took two years.

Two things about it are firsts. It uses brick piers instead of stone columns
robbed from older buildings, which is what everyone else had been doing. And it
is the earliest dated building carried entirely on pointed arches — at least
two hundred years before anything of the kind in England.

<Figure id="tl-arches" />

Its architect was a Copt. He was given a hundred thousand dinars to build it,
ten thousand for himself, and a handsome allowance for the rest of his life.
The same man built the aqueduct that brought water to the palace from a spring
in the southern desert. Ibn Tulun also dredged the Alexandria canal, repaired
the Nilometer on Roda and put a fort there.

## What the aqueduct cost a scholar

Somebody objected to the water. Ibn Tulun sent for the jurist Muhammad ibn Abd
al-Hakam, and the scholar's own account of the evening is preserved.

A slave came to his house at night and said the emir wanted him. He mounted in
a panic and was led off the road. Where are you taking me? To the desert; the
emir is there. Certain his last hour had come — an old and feeble man, he says
— he asked whether the slave knew what this was about. The slave took pity on
him: beware of speaking disrespectfully of the aqueduct.

Then torches in the desert, and Ibn Tulun on horseback at the aqueduct door
with great wax candles burning in front of him. The jurist dismounted and gave
the greeting and got nothing back. So he said he was exhausted and thirsty and
asked leave to drink. Pages offered him water; he refused it and drew for
himself, under the emir's eye, and drank until he thought he would burst. Then:
God quench your thirst at the rivers of Paradise, for I have drunk my fill, and
I do not know which to praise more, the excellence of this cool sweet clear
water or the delicious smell of the aqueduct.

*Let him retire*, said Ibn Tulun. And the slave whispered: you have hit the mark.

## The sums that will not close

Egypt's revenue under Ibn Tulun is given as 4,300,000 dinars a year.

Against that: 750,000 sent to the caliph as tribute in 870 and 2,200,000 over
four years; buildings at al-Qata'i estimated near half a million; at least a
thousand dinars a month to the poor over and above the obligatory alms; a
thousand a day on his table; open house; generosity to scholars; a large army
and household; and frontier forts to keep up.

Lane-Poole's conclusion is that it cannot be made to add up, and that this is
why the legend grew that Ibn Tulun paid for his mosque with buried treasure he
had dug up. The likelier answer, on the Christian sources, is that the Coptic
patriarch was fined heavily from time to time — though the Coptic population at
large was taxed no worse than anyone else and was, unusually for the period,
left alone.
