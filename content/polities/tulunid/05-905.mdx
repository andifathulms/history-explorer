title: What was left of the quarter

In 904 an Abbasid army under Muhammad ibn Sulayman moved into Syria, took it
without much fighting, and came down into Egypt. The Tulunid fleet went over.
The Tulunid regiments were bought or beaten. In January 905 the last Tulunid
was sent to Baghdad, and Egypt was a caliphal province again for the first time
in thirty-seven years.

Then the army sacked al-Qata'i and burned it.

That is the detail this page ends on, because it is unusually explicit about
what the reconquest was for. The quarter Ibn Tulun had built north of Fustat —
the palaces, the barracks, the hippodrome, the administrative offices, the
whole apparatus of a capital that had been paid for out of revenue that should
have gone to Iraq — was systematically destroyed. What was deliberately left
standing was the mosque, which is the subject of the previous chapter and which
is still there.

The message is not subtle and it was not wrong. What Baghdad objected to was
never the mosque, or the Friday prayer, in which Ibn Tulun had always named the
caliph. It was a provincial capital, an independent army and a treasury that
did not remit. Those were flattened; the religious building, which conceded the
caliph's authority, was spared.

And it did not work. Egypt was governed from Baghdad for thirty years, and then
the Ikhshidids on this site's next page did the same thing again, and then the
Fatimids came and did it permanently. The Tulunid dynasty lasted thirty-seven
years and the demonstration it made was irreversible: this corpus records six
Egyptian regimes after it, and every one of them kept Egypt's revenue in Egypt
and used it to buy an army.

The ruined quarter was not rebuilt. Its ground was used as a rubbish tip and a
cemetery for a century until the Fatimids built Cairo a little to the north, on
the same instinct and for the same reason.
