The sack that ended al-Qata'i worked, in the sense that there is almost
nothing to see of a square mile built over thirty-five years.

## The list

When Lane-Poole set out the evidence available for this period, the monuments
he could name came to three things: the mosque of Ibn Tulun, the aqueduct south
of Cairo, and the second Nilometer on the island of Roda. To those he could add
inscriptions from the mosque and the Nilometer, some gravestones, and two title
deeds to shops, written on wood.

<Figure id="roda-nilometer" />

That is the surviving physical record of a state that ran Egypt and Syria for
thirty-seven years: one mosque, one water channel, one river gauge, two shop
deeds.

The coins are better, and they say something the buildings cannot. The mints
worked at Fustat, Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, Antioch, Harran, Raqqa and Ramla —
which is the map of what the dynasty actually held, issued in metal, by a
governor who never stopped naming the caliph on it.

## The lake

There is one more trace, and it is the one worth finishing on.

Years afterwards, when the ground where Khumarawayh's palace had stood was dug
over, they found the quicksilver.

The garden planted in the shapes of sentences, the aviary, the painted golden
house, the silver columns and the silken cords had all gone the way of
everything else in the Wards. What was left in the soil was the mercury,
because mercury does not burn and nobody could carry it away, and because the
thing a demolition leaves behind is whichever part of the extravagance was
least useful to anyone else.
