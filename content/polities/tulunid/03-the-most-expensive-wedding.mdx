---
title: The most expensive wedding of the ninth century
drafted_from: petry-1998
phase: contraction
---

Khumarawayh succeeded his father in 884 at about twenty and held Egypt and
Syria for twelve years, and the way he held them is the reason the dynasty
lasted twenty-one years after him rather than a hundred.

He settled the quarrel by paying for it. An agreement with Baghdad recognised
Tulunid rule over Egypt and Syria as hereditary for thirty years in exchange
for a large annual tribute — the arrangement this site's Aghlabid page records
a century earlier at the other end of the Mediterranean, bought rather than
granted. Then he cemented it with a marriage: his daughter Qatr al-Nada was
sent to Baghdad to marry the caliph al-Mu'tadid, with a trousseau and a
progress that the sources describe at length and in figures they clearly enjoy
repeating. It is remembered as the most lavish wedding of the age and it cost a
substantial fraction of a year's revenue.

The rest of the spending was on himself. Al-Qata'i under Khumarawayh had
gardens with trees whose trunks were sheathed in gilded copper, a pool of
quicksilver he is said to have floated on to sleep, and a menagerie including a
blue-eyed lion that kept his door. Some of this is chronicle embroidery of the
kind that attaches to a prince the next regime wants remembered as decadent,
and the site does not vouch for the quicksilver. What is not in doubt is the
direction of travel: a revenue that his father had turned into an army and an
aqueduct, his son turned into a court.

He was murdered by his own servants in Damascus in 896. What followed is a
familiar sequence — young successors, a treasury emptied, and the Turkish and
Sudanese regiments his grandfather had bought discovering that they could
choose between them. This corpus has recorded that at Samarra, at Bukhara, in
Cairo under the Ayyubids and in Isfahan under the Buyids.

The tribute stopped being payable, and Baghdad had spent a decade rebuilding
its capacity to collect.
