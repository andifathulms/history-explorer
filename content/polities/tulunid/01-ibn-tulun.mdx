---
title: The governor who stopped remitting
drafted_from: petry-1998
phase: formation
---

Ahmad ibn Tulun was the son of a Turkish slave soldier sent from Samarra to
govern Egypt in 868, at exactly the moment the caliphate's own guard was
destroying caliphs at will. He arrived as a deputy of a deputy and within a
decade was the effective ruler of Egypt and southern Syria.

The method was fiscal. Egypt's grain and tax surplus had gone to Iraq since the
conquest; Ibn Tulun took control of the province's revenue administration and
began spending it in Egypt — on his own army of Turkish and Sudanese troops, and
on a new administrative quarter north of Fustat with the congregational mosque
that still carries his name. He never renounced the caliph, and the Abbasid name
stayed on the coinage.

This is the first time since 641 that Egypt's wealth stayed in Egypt, and it set
the pattern that the Ikhshidids, Fatimids, Ayyubids and Mamluks all followed. The
Abbasids retook the province in 905 and held it for thirty years, which turned
out to be the interruption rather than the rule.
