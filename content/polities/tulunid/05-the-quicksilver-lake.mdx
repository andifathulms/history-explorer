Khumarawayh had his father's appetite for building and none of his judgement
about what it was for.

He enlarged the palace and turned the horse-ground into a garden:
sweet-smelling flowers planted in the shapes of sentences and other designs,
rare trees, date palms set about with gilded tanks of water, an aviary of
beautiful birds. His "golden house" was decorated with painted images of
himself, his wives and his singers, which Muslim opinion of the period did not
approve of.

And because he slept badly, he had an air-bed floated on a lake of quicksilver
said to be nearly a hundred feet square, moored by silken cords to silver
columns, so that it rocked. A tame lion out of the menagerie lay guard while he
slept.

The kitchen alone cost twenty-three thousand dinars a month. The caliph in
Baghdad, watching a dangerous vassal spend himself into weakness, was well
pleased.

## The verse at the graveside

Neither the lion nor his bodyguard of Hawf Arabs was any use against his own
household. Early in 896, on a visit to Damascus, some domestic intrigue ended
with his slaves murdering him. They were crucified. His body was brought home
and buried beside his father's under the Muqattam, near the palace.

Seven Qur'an readers were reciting at Ibn Tulun's tomb. As the bearers lowered
the son into the grave, the verse they had reached was *seize him and hurl him
into the fire of hell*.

## There are your emirs for you

What followed is the ordinary machinery of a slave-soldier state without an
adult at the top of it.

The heir, Jaysh, was fourteen, incapable of taking his position seriously, and
absorbed in the amusements of his age. Syria and the northern frontier stopped
obeying him, the army and the administration were neglected, and the treasury
was empty. He murdered three of his uncles. Then his own troops mutinied and
killed him after a few months.

His last public act was to throw two of the murdered uncles' heads to the
mutineers with the words: there are your emirs for you.

His younger brother was set up next, with the major-domo as regent. The prince
was as careless as his brother and the regent was no statesman; the Turkish
officers did as they liked; an uncle brought a rebel army to Fustat and was
beaten; and Syria and Tarsus were under nobody's control at all.

Eleven years separate the quicksilver lake from the Abbasid army that came to
take Egypt back.
