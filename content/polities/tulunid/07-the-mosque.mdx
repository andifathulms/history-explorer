Ibn Tulun built a new administrative quarter, al-Qata'i, north of Fustat, and
in it a congregational mosque that still stands — one of the oldest in Egypt in
substantially its original form, with a spiral minaret that copies Samarra, the
city he had come from.

<Figure id="ibn-tulun-mosque" />

That copy is the whole political statement. A governor who has stopped
remitting Egypt's revenue to Iraq builds, in Egypt, an imitation of the
caliphal capital's architecture, while continuing to name the caliph in the
Friday prayer. It asserts equivalence rather than independence, and it is the
same posture the Samanids adopted in the east at the same time and for the same
reason: a formal subordination nobody intended to test.

The mosque was paid for out of a surplus that had previously left the country.
This is what autonomy meant in practice — not a change of flag, but a
redirection of the tax base into local troops, local building, and a local
court. Ibn Tulun also built a hospital and an aqueduct, and expanded the
Egyptian army with Turkish and Sudanese soldiers bought for the purpose.

The dynasty lasted thirty-seven years and the Abbasids retook Egypt in 905. But
the demonstration held: Egypt could pay for itself and for an army, and every
subsequent regime in Cairo — Ikhshidid, Fatimid, Ayyubid, Mamluk — was built on
that discovery.
