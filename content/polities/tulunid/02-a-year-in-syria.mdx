In April 878 the governor of Egypt marched into Syria without permission and
took it. He was back within a year, and he came home with a province, a
frontier, and — the detail that says most — his own name on the coinage.

## The opening

Ibn Tulun had wanted Syria for some time. He had once been on the point of
occupying it at the caliph's request, and although the appointment then went to
somebody else, he took the view that he retained the prior claim.

The obstacle was Amajur, governor of Syria, whom Lane-Poole calls a formidable
and jealous obstacle to his advance. When Amajur died the post passed to his
son. Ibn Tulun set the son's title aside, dropped what remained of the pretence
of obedience to Baghdad, and marched.

Damascus gave him immediate homage — officials and inhabitants together, with no
fighting. From there he made a progress through Syria taking the allegiance of
the chief towns, as far as Tarsus, where he had studied as a young man.

Only Antioch held out, under a commander called Sima the Long. It was bombarded
with mangonels, betrayed from inside, stormed and sacked in September. Massisa
and Adana were occupied next. Tarsus, for the moment, defied him.

## What he had at the end of it

His territory now ran from the Euphrates and the Byzantine frontier to Barqa on
the Mediterranean and Aswan at the first cataract — Egypt, Syria and the
Cilician marches, held from Fustat.

He left strong detachments at Raqqa, Harran and Damascus to hold it, extracted
six hundred thousand dinars from Ibn al-Mudabbir, his old enemy and the treasurer
of Syria, and went home.

The money is worth pausing on because it explains the whole enterprise. The
Egyptian land tax under Ibn al-Mudabbir's administration had produced eight
hundred thousand a year; under Ibn Tulun's, one authority puts it at four million
three hundred thousand. Whatever the exact figures, a governor who could raise
that much and was no longer forwarding it to Iraq could pay for a campaign out of
his province and keep the proceeds.

## The coins

The declaration is not in any inscription. It is in the mint.

Until this campaign, coins struck in Egypt carried only the reigning caliph's
name. From 879-880 the dinars of Misr carry Ahmad ibn Tulun's name alongside it.
He went on issuing at Misr every year until he died, and later at Raqqa and at
Damascus.

Two things about that are deliberate. He never left the caliph's name off — this
is not a claim to be caliph or to be independent of the office. And he never
added the name of al-Muwaffaq, the caliph's brother, who was actually running the
empire and whose name other provincial governors were putting on their coinage as
a matter of course.

So the coins say precisely what the campaign said: I acknowledge the caliph, I do
not acknowledge the man who governs for him, and I am now a party rather than a
functionary.

## What it cost at home

While he was away his eldest son al-Abbas, left as deputy, threw off his
father's authority.

On Ibn Tulun's approach the young man lost his nerve, took what treasure and
material he could carry, and retreated to Barqa with eight hundred horse and ten
thousand of his father's black infantry. Ibn Tulun sent the qadi Bakkar to reason
with him; he refused every offer and set about imagining a kingdom of his own in
North Africa.

A state built in twelve years by one man's competence had produced, in its first
year of empire, a succession problem — and the son's rebellion used the same
troops, the same treasury and the same logic his father had used against Baghdad.
