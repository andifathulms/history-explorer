title: The army, and the quarrel

The first chapter says the method was fiscal. What the money bought is the
subject of this one, because it is what made the autonomy real and what the
sources are most detailed about.

Ibn Tulun built an army for Egypt. It had not had one of its own since the Arab
conquest: the province had been garrisoned by troops the caliphate posted and
paid, and its surplus had gone to Iraq to pay for troops elsewhere. He reversed
that. Turkish slave soldiers of the kind he had come out of himself, black
African infantry from the Sudan, and Greek and Slav mercenaries, bought and paid
from Egyptian revenue, quartered in a purpose-built quarter — plus a fleet, and
the arsenal to maintain it.

That gave Egypt something it would keep for six centuries. This site's
Ikhshidid, Fatimid, Ayyubid and Mamluk pages all describe states funded by
Egyptian agriculture and defended by purchased foreign soldiers, and every one
of them is running the arrangement Ibn Tulun assembled in the 870s.

He then used it. In 878 he took southern Syria and the frontier districts, on
the argument that Egypt could not be defended from Egypt — which is also true
and which every Egyptian regime in this corpus subsequently acted on. Syria is
what the Fatimids fought the Seljuks for, what Saladin unified, and what the
Mamluks held against the Mongols.

The quarrel is what makes the constitutional position clear. Al-Muwaffaq, the
brother of the caliph and the actual government in Baghdad, wanted Egypt's
money and could not get it. He denounced Ibn Tulun; Ibn Tulun assembled jurists
at Damascus in 882 and had them declare al-Muwaffaq deposed from the succession
for failing in his duties, and offered the caliph refuge in Egypt from his own
brother. Neither side could reach the other. Ibn Tulun died in 884 with the
dispute unresolved.

What that exchange demonstrates is the thing this site's Iranian Intermezzo
pages describe from the other end: by the 880s a provincial governor with an
army and a tax base could argue with the caliphate as an equal, in the
caliphate's own legal vocabulary, and neither party could enforce anything.
