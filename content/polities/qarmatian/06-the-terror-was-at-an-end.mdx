For most of the tenth century the Qarmatians were not a foreign enemy of
Baghdad. They were a party in its politics.

They kept a permanent representative in the capital — Ibn Shahuya — who lived
there, in the words of a Baghdad chronicler with a government career, like a
vizier. He obtained audiences of the sovereigns, who fell in with his ideas. The
grandees were afraid of him, put up with his arrogance and obeyed his commands,
and the chronicler is explicit that there was no reason for any of this except
his relations with those people.

Successive Buyid rulers had assigned the Qarmatians fiefs in Iraq itself, at
Wasit and on the Euphrates canal. Their requests were regularly granted and
their requirements carried out. This was the settled arrangement with a power
whose formidable character, as he puts it, had caused many a monarch to make
friends of them.

<Figure id="adud-dinar" />

It is a remarkable position for a state of eastern Arabia that had sacked Mecca
and denied the caliphate of both claimants. It was also, by then, mostly credit
— and credit can be spent.

## 985

Two Qarmatian leaders, Ishaq and Ja'far, seized Kufa.

<Figure id="kufa-mosque" />

The vizier at Baghdad handled it the way such things had always been handled. He
wrote them a courteous letter with a mild remonstrance, invited them to peace
and friendship, and offered them what they were trying to get; and he released
their representative from confinement and made it up to him with gifts and
honours so that he could mediate.

The reply was evasive. They complained about the arrest. And then they went on
pushing their men into more districts and collecting the revenue on their own
account, until, in the chronicler's phrase, patience was exhausted.

What followed was routine. A chamberlain went out from Baghdad with Daylamites,
Turks and Arabs. An Uqaylid chief was brought in with his tribe. They bridged
the Euphrates under fire, crossed, charged together, and routed the Qarmatians;
the Qarmatian commander was captured and immediately beheaded by the Uqaylid
over a private feud. The survivors fell back on Kufa, and Baghdad celebrated.

Stung — the chronicler notes that pride runs strong in these people — they
equipped a second force under another trusted officer. A second chamberlain went
out, crossed the water, and destroyed it: the commander killed, many officers
taken, the camp and baggage plundered. The two leaders retreated and were chased
as far as Qadisiyya.

Then the sentence that ends them:

> The disturbance was now at an end, and therewith the reputation of the
> Qarmatians, and the terror which they had inspired in men's breasts.

## What it took to finish them

Not much, and that is the point.

The Qarmatians had held Mecca's stone for twenty-two years, wrecked pilgrim
caravans, taken tribute from two caliphates and made governments in Baghdad and
Cairo negotiate with a permanent embassy. What ended their standing was two
ordinary field engagements against a force assembled in the normal way from the
troops available.

Nothing had changed in their doctrine or their organisation. What had run out
was the belief that fighting them was different from fighting anybody else, and
once that was gone the thing it had been protecting turned out to be a modest
regional power with fiefs in Iraq and a diplomat in the capital.

The chronicler draws the moral himself, and it is his own and not borrowed:
every community has a term of prosperity up to which it advances, and then
retrogression begins — except, he adds, in the case of religious prosperity,
which goes on rising, and when it parts from the perishing world passes into the
world that endures.

He was writing about a movement whose entire claim was religious, whose members
had been taught that the age of the law was over and the awaited one was
returning. The exception he carves out is precisely the one they had believed
themselves to be.

Al-Hasa held out for another ninety years under its council, losing the island
of Bahrain and then the mainland. Nobody wrote about it much.
