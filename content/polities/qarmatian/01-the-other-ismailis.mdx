This site's Fatimid page describes a clandestine mission that spent decades
preparing, produced a caliphate in Ifriqiya in 909, and directed the rest of
its network from Cairo afterwards. That is the branch that won. It is not the
whole of what the mission produced.

Abu Sa'id al-Jannabi, a Qarmati missionary, took Hajar and then al-Hasa in
eastern Arabia in 899 and founded a state there ten years before Abd Allah
declared himself mahdi in the Maghrib. When the declaration came, the eastern
communities did not accept it. The doctrine they had been taught was that the
seventh imam, Muhammad ibn Isma'il, had gone into concealment and would return
as the mahdi; a living man in Ifriqiya claiming to be the mahdi himself was not
the fulfilment of that expectation but a substitution for it.

So the Isma'ili movement split at the moment of its first success, and the
split is the reason this polity exists as a separate thing. The Qarmatians of
Bahrayn kept waiting. They took tribute from the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad and
from the Fatimid one in Cairo, recognised neither, and for most of the tenth
century were the strongest power on the Gulf.

The internal arrangement is the part travellers found remarkable, and the fullest
account comes from Nasir-i Khusraw, who passed through in the middle of the
eleventh century. He describes a state governed by a council rather than a
ruler; residents of al-Hasa paying no taxes; state-owned estates, mills and
granaries worked by slaves; and interest-free loans available to a man whose
affairs had gone wrong. Whether that is description or admiration is hard to
separate at this distance — he was an Isma'ili himself — but it is a coherent
picture of a polity organised on a principle rather than on a dynasty, and the
`succession_rule` field on this page is null because there was no succession to
code.
