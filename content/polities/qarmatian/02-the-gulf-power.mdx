---
title: Taking tribute from both caliphs
drafted_from: daftary-2007

For most of the tenth century the Qarmatian state of Bahrayn was the strongest
military power on the Gulf and one of the more alarming facts of Abbasid
political life, and the way it operated is worth setting out because it is
unlike anything else on this site.

It was small. Eastern Arabia — al-Hasa, Qatif, the Bahrain islands, the coast —
supports a limited population, and the Qarmatian army is not described in the
sources as large. What it had was mobility, a fortified base four hundred
kilometres from anywhere the Abbasids could easily reach, and the willingness to
do things nobody else would.

Abu Tahir al-Jannabi raided Basra in 923 and sacked it. He raided the pilgrim
caravans repeatedly, and in 924 took the entire Baghdad caravan, including a
substantial part of the court. In 927 he beat an Abbasid army in Iraq and moved
towards Baghdad itself; the caliph's government evacuated. The pilgrimage was
suspended for years at a time because the road could not be protected.

What that bought was tribute. The Abbasids paid to have the pilgrim route left
alone. So, at various points, did the Fatimids, whose own caravans and whose
Syrian ambitions ran through the same country. This site codes the polity's
revenue basis as `tribute` — the vocabulary's term for revenue received from
another polity — and it is one of the very few entries in the corpus where that
value stands alone, because al-Hasa's residents are described as untaxed and the
state's other income was its own estates.

A state financed by two caliphates it refused to recognise, in order not to
attack them, is a specific and unusual thing. It is not banditry, exactly: the
Qarmatians were not extracting money for its own sake but running a polity on
proceeds their doctrine let them regard as legitimate, from powers their
doctrine held to be illegitimate. And it is not a protection racket either,
quite, because they went on believing the thing that made them dangerous.

The arrangement had one weakness, which is that it depended on being feared. In
930 Abu Tahir did something that made him feared as much as anyone in Islamic
history has been, and it began the destruction of his cause.
