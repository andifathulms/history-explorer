The state in eastern Arabia was gone by the 1070s. The word it had given the
language was not, and the word did more damage than the state ever had.

*Qarmati* — Carmathian — stopped meaning a member of the polity at al-Hasa and
became the standard hostile term for Isma'ilis of every kind, applied by writers
who had never been near the Gulf, for centuries afterwards. It carried the
Black Stone with it, and the massacre of pilgrims, and the eighty days of the
false mahdi, and it attached all of that to anybody the user wanted to
discredit.

The clearest demonstration is a book written in Yemen more than three hundred
years after the raid on Mecca.

## A chapter called "The Carmathians"

Al-Janadi was a fourteenth-century Yemeni scholar whose life's work was a
biographical dictionary of the jurists of Yemen, arranged town by town — an
entirely conventional Sunni undertaking, in which princes and dynasties appear
only briefly because, as he says himself, they matter far less.

Into it he inserted a history of the Carmathians of Yemen.

What that chapter actually describes is the Isma'ili mission: Ibn Hawshab, the
missionary sent to the Yemeni highlands in the 880s, and the network he built,
and the man he despatched from Yemen to North Africa who converted the Kutama
Berbers and summoned Ubayd Allah to come and be caliph. In other words, it is
the account of how the Fatimid caliphate was founded — filed by a Yemeni
biographer of Sunni lawyers under the name of a state in eastern Arabia that had
rejected the Fatimid imam outright and been at odds with Cairo for a century.

The chapter is not stupid; it is detailed and it preserves a first-person
narrative of how a mission was actually run. A missionary joins a party of men
from a mountainous district, travels with them entertaining them with prophetic
traditions, urges them to pray, settles in their town, frequents its mosques,
worships until he is loved — and only then tells them what he came for, takes
their oaths, begins collecting their alms, and explains that the money will need
a fortress to keep it in.

That is careful reporting. The heading on top of it is a slur, and a slur that
the Qarmatians of al-Hasa had earned on their own account and that everybody
else in the tradition then inherited from them.

## Who paid for it

The Fatimids, mostly.

They had refused nothing to the eastern communities and had been refused by
them: the whole reason a separate state existed at al-Hasa was that its people
would not accept a living mahdi in place of a returning one. Cairo and al-Hasa
fought each other in Syria. None of that mattered to the polemic. A caliphate
built on the same doctrinal family as the men who carried off the Black Stone
spent two hundred years being told it was the same thing, by people for whom
*Qarmati* was simply the word.

<Figure id="al-azhar" />

This is an unusual kind of legacy and worth naming exactly. The state left no
successor, no institution, no building anyone visits and no community that
claims it. What it left was a name that its enemies found useful, and the enemies
were not its own.
