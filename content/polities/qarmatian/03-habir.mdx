Miskawayh worked inside the Abbasid administration and describes the Qarmatian
problem the way a government describes a problem it cannot solve. The clearest
instance is what happened to the returning pilgrims of the year 311.

Abu Tahir came up to Habir, on the Kufa road, to meet them. Word of his arrival
reached the caravan at Faid, and Abu'l-Hayja ibn Hamdan — whose office was
guardian of the Kufa and Mecca roads and protector of the pilgrims, which is to
say that this was exactly the situation he existed for — advised them to let him
take them by a side road to Wadi al-Qura so that they would not pass Habir at
all. The pilgrims made an outcry and refused. They stayed at Faid until they had
eaten the town's stores and it could not hold them any longer, and then they
marched, and their protector went with them of necessity.

At Habir they were met and beaten. A great number were killed. Abu'l-Hayja was
taken prisoner, and so were the queen mother's uncle and a number of men and
women of the caliph's household. Abu Tahir took the camels of every caravan and
as many men, women and children as he wanted, and carried them off to Hajar. The
rest of the pilgrims he left where they stood, without food and without camels,
and most of them died of thirst and sore feet and walking.

Then Miskawayh adds six words that change the size of the event: Abu Tahir's age
was at the time seventeen.

The rest of the chapter is Baghdad. On Saturday 15 May 924 the city came apart.
Women came out barefoot with their hair down, beating their faces black and
shrieking in the streets, and were joined by the women of the officials whom the
vizier Ibn al-Furat had ruined; Miskawayh, who did not like the vizier and does
not pretend to, calls the spectacle one of unexampled hideousness. The chief of
police was sent to the great mosques on both banks with cavalry, infantry and
naphtha-throwers, and quieted the mob. The first survivors came in and explained
what had happened. And the vizier rode to the caliph to tell him, feeling, says
Miskawayh, his courage fail him on the way — at which the chamberlain, given the
opening, attacked him in the caliph's presence.

That is the mechanism by which a small state on the Gulf coast dominated the
politics of an empire for half a century without ever holding an inch of its
territory. The Qarmatians did not besiege Baghdad. They stood on the pilgrimage
road, which the caliphate could not close, could not defend along its length, and
could not stop being responsible for — and every year the caliph had to decide
whether the hajj would go, and every failure was legible to everyone in the
empire at once.

It was worked as a business as much as a war. Caravans were taken and ransomed;
protection was sold; the road was periodically declared safe on terms. The
caliphate paid, in the end, for the pilgrimage to be allowed through, which is a
tax collected by one state on the central religious obligation of another.

Six years after Habir the same man walked into Mecca during the pilgrimage
itself.
