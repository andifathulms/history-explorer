Nasir-i Khusraw was a Persian poet, philosopher and Isma'ili missionary who left
Khurasan in 1046 and travelled for seven years — Egypt, Arabia, Palestine,
Mecca four times — and wrote it down. His *Safarnama* is one of the best travel
accounts of the medieval Islamic world, and he passed through al-Hasa in the
1050s, near the end of the Qarmatian state's life.

<Figure id="persian-anthology" />

What he describes is worth having in his own words rather than at second hand.
The town was walled in four concentric rings. The residents paid
no tax of any kind. A man whose affairs had gone wrong could obtain a loan on
which no interest was charged, repayable when he could. The state owned grain
mills and ground the townspeople's grain without charge. The government was a
council of six with six deputies, not a ruler. There was a mosque, and nobody
prayed the Friday prayer in it, and no sermon was preached.

<Figure id="jawatha-interior" />

He also records, without any apparent discomfort, that the state owned something
like thirty thousand slaves, who worked the fields and gardens.

Two cautions belong with it. Nasir-i Khusraw was himself an Isma'ili — a
Fatimid missionary, in fact, of the branch the Qarmatians had refused — and he
was describing a society he had reason to find interesting. And he arrived a
century and a half after the state was founded and a few years before it lost
Bahrain, so what he saw was a late arrangement rather than a founding one.

With those caveats it is still the fullest description anyone has of a polity
organised on a principle instead of a dynasty, written by somebody who was
standing in it. A council of six is not an election, not a nomination and not
any kind of inheritance; there was no succession, because after Abu Tahir there
was no single office to succeed to.

The last sentence of the account is the one that stays with a reader. There was
a mosque, and nobody prayed in it, because the age of the law had ended and had
not yet been replaced by anything.
