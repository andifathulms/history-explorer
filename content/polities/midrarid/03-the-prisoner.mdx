title: The prisoner in the oasis
drafted_from: halm-1996

Around 905 a man arrived in Sijilmasa with his young son and settled quietly as
a merchant. He was Abd Allah, the head of the Isma'ili mission — the hidden
imam whose agents were at that moment converting the Kutama Berbers of the
Kabylie for him, as this site's Fatimid and Aghlabid pages describe. He had come
west from Syria ahead of Abbasid agents and chosen the far end of the world to
wait in.

The Midrarid ruler eventually worked out who his guest was and imprisoned him.
The reasons the sources give are the ordinary ones: pressure from Baghdad, and
the ruler's own reluctance to be holding a man claiming to be the mahdi.

In 909 the Kutama army finished the Aghlabids at Raqqada and then marched west
across the Maghreb — a very long way, for one prisoner — took Sijilmasa, and
released him. Abd Allah was proclaimed caliph and mahdi, and the Fatimid
caliphate on this site's caliphates page dates from that moment. The dynasty
that would rule Egypt for two centuries and found Cairo collected its founder
from a cell in this oasis.

The town was left with a Fatimid governor and then, repeatedly, without one.
Sijilmasa was too far from Ifriqiya to hold and too valuable to abandon, so the
tenth century runs through a sequence of Midrarid restorations, Fatimid
reconquests, a ruler who converted to Sunnism and declared for the Umayyads of
Cordoba, and the proxy war between Cairo and Cordoba that this site's Idrisid
page describes consuming the far Maghreb. The dynasty was finally removed in
976, and the town passed between Zanata clients until the Almoravids took it in
1055 — the event the Almoravid page records as one of the two that gave a desert
movement a bullion supply before it had a country.

The record leaves the last ruler null, because the sequence of restorations
means the works consulted here do not agree on which Midrarid was the last.

What is worth keeping from this page is the disproportion. A walled oasis town
of no military weight, with no cited extent and no cited population and three
empty influence counts, sat on the pipe through which West African gold reached
the Mediterranean for two hundred years, minted it, and happened to be holding
the founder of the Fatimid caliphate when his army came to get him.
