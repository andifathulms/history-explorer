Sijilmasa is a field of mounds. The town declined after the fifteenth century,
was fought over, and was finally abandoned; what stands in the Tafilalt now is
decayed mud-brick under windblown sand along the east bank of the Ziz, spread
over something like a square mile and a half.

A Moroccan-American excavation worked the site through the late 1980s and 1990s,
and almost everything specific anybody can say about this state comes out of it
rather than out of any text.

Three findings matter here.

The site is not one town. The stratigraphy shows successive settlements on
overlapping ground rather than a single walled city continuously occupied — a
place that was rebuilt, moved and re-walled as its fortunes changed, which is
what an oasis dependent on one trade route would do.

The excavators found the foundations of a congregational mosque of over two and
a half thousand square metres, with its earliest phases dated to the eighth
century. That makes it, on the excavators' reading, the oldest mosque
identified in Morocco — earlier than the Qarawiyyin at Fez, founded in 859. The first substantial Islamic building in the far
Maghreb was in a Kharijite caravan town on the desert edge, not in either of
the cities the written tradition treats as the country's origins.

And the ceramics — some twenty-two thousand pieces catalogued — show the trade
directly: material from Ifriqiya, from al-Andalus, from further east, in an
oasis a fortnight from anywhere.

Nobody knows how large this state was or how many people lived in it, and its
written record is a handful of paragraphs in chroniclers who were mostly
interested in somebody else. What is actually known about it comes from pottery
counts and wall lines — which is a different kind of evidence entirely, and the
reason a caravan town of no military weight can be described at all.
