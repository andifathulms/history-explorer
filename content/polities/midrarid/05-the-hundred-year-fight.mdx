The Midrarid state did not decline. It was captured, repeatedly, by people who
wanted the mint.

After 909 the town spent most of a century changing hands: Fatimid governors,
Midrarid restorations, a Midrarid ruler who converted from Kharijism to Sunnism
and declared for the Umayyads of Córdoba, Fatimid reconquests, and Zanata clients
of whichever caliphate was currently paying.

That is not the ordinary shape of a small state's ending. It is what happens to
an asset in a war between two much larger powers.

## The proxy war

Cairo and Córdoba spent the tenth century fighting over the Maghreb without
either of them being able to occupy it.

The Fatimids had taken Ifriqiya and then Egypt, and claimed the caliphate. The
Umayyads of Córdoba responded by claiming it themselves in 929 — the first time
in nearly three hundred years that three men simultaneously called themselves
caliph — and by funding anybody in the Maghreb who would fight the Fatimids.
Neither could reach the far west in force. Both could pay.

So the western Maghreb spent decades as a board on which two caliphates moved
Berber clients, and Sijilmasa was the most valuable square on it. A Midrarid
prince changing his madhhab from Kharijism to Sunnism in order to take Umayyad
money is exactly what that environment produces, and it is also, doctrinally, the
end of the thing the dynasty had been founded to be.

## What was lost before the state was

The sect.

The Midrarids had been Sufri Kharijites — a community holding that the caliphate
was illegitimate and that leadership belonged to whichever Muslim was qualified,
which had been the whole reason a merchant republic on the desert edge could
exist beyond the reach of Baghdad. By the middle of the tenth century their ruler
was a Sunni client of a caliph in Spain.

The dynasty was formally removed in 976 and the town went on. That order matters:
Sijilmasa lost its doctrine, then its dynasty, and kept its trade for another
five hundred years, because the trade never depended on either.
