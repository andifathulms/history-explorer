Ibn Battuta came to Sijilmasa in the winter of 1351, intending to cross the
Sahara, and describes it in two sentences that say most of what the town was
for.

> Sigilmasa, which is a very handsome city. It produces many very good dates,
> and in the abundance of these it may be compared with Basra, except only that
> those of this place are the best.

He lodged with a scholar named Muhammad al-Bushri — whose brother, he notes, he
had met in the city of Qanjanfu, in China.

That is worth stopping on. A family of religious scholars in an oasis on the
edge of the Sahara had one brother at home and another in southern China, and a
Moroccan traveller happened to meet both. It is the single best illustration of
what the fourteenth-century Islamic world actually was: not a region but a
network, along which a literate man could travel from Morocco to the Pacific
and expect to find someone to lodge with.

## What he went out into

He left in February 1352 with a large company of merchants, and after
twenty-five days reached Taghaza — the salt mine at the far end of nothing.

> a village in which there is nothing good, for its houses and mosque are built
> with stones of salt, and covered with the hides of camels. There is no tree in
> the place; it has nothing but sand for its soil; and in this are mines of
> salt.

They dug in the earth and found thick tables of salt lying as though they had
been cut and stacked underground. Nobody lived there except the merchants'
servants, who did the digging and lived on dates and other provisions brought up
from Sijilmasa.

## Which is the whole system

Set the two descriptions side by side and the trade explains itself.

Sijilmasa: a handsome walled city with the best dates in the world, colleges,
water-wheels, a mint, and scholars whose brothers were in China. Taghaza:
twenty-five days south, no tree, no soil, houses built out of salt because there
was nothing else to build with, inhabited only by other men's slaves.

The first place existed because of the second, and the second existed only
because the first could feed it. Neither could support a population on its own.
Between them they moved the salt that West Africa had no source of and brought
back the gold that the Mediterranean had no source of, and the arrangement ran
for six hundred years.

Ibn Battuta's caravan went on from Taghaza with water for ten days and reached
Walata, and from there he went to Mali. He was, in effect, travelling down the
pipe this town had been built to control — four hundred years after the
Midrarids had lost control of it, and with the trade still running exactly as
they had organised it.
