Sijilmasa struck gold coin, and the coin is why a caravan town has a place in
the monetary history of the medieval world.

Gold arrived from the south as dust and nuggets. It left as dinars. The mint at
Sijilmasa is the point at which West African metal became Mediterranean money,
and for two centuries a substantial share of the gold circulating in North
Africa, Egypt, al-Andalus and — through them — Christian Europe had passed
through this one customs house and been struck with this one town's name on it.

Sijilmasa dinars turn up in hoards across the Islamic west and beyond. They were
known for their fineness, which is what one would expect of a mint sitting at the
source with no incentive to debase: a town whose whole business is being trusted
does not clip its own coin.

## What that made the town worth

Everybody wanted it, and nobody could hold it.

The Fatimids took it in 909 and lost it. The Umayyads of Córdoba backed a
Midrarid restoration against them and lost it too. Zanata clients held it in
between. The Almoravids took it in 1055 as one of their first acts, before they
had a country, because a movement that intends to conquer needs a bullion supply
more than it needs territory.

That sequence is not a story about Sijilmasa's politics. It is a story about what
the mint was worth: a state that held it could pay armies in good gold while its
rivals could not, and every serious power in the western Mediterranean understood
that for three hundred years.

## The other thing that came north

It should not be left out.

The same caravans that carried the gold carried people. Slaves moved north from
the sahel markets alongside the metal, in numbers nobody can quantify and over
the same six centuries, and Sijilmasa taxed both. The salt going south was cut by
slaves at Taghaza; the gold coming north was accompanied by slaves walking; and
the mint that made the town famous was fed by a route on which human beings were
one of the standard cargoes.

A dinar struck at Sijilmasa is a beautiful object and a very fine piece of
metalwork, and the system that produced it is the one described in this
paragraph.
