By the time Leo Africanus reached the Tafilalt, around 1510, Sijilmasa was gone.

He describes what it had been — a town on a plain by the river Ziz, *enuironed
with most stately and high wals, euen as in many places it is to be seene at
this present*; gallantly built; rich inhabitants with a great traffic to the land
of the Blacks; stately temples and colleges; water lifted out of the river by
wheels; and an air he calls temperate and wholesome except for the winter damp.

And then: *But now since the towne was destroied, the inhabitants began to plant
themselues in the next castles and villages.*

Leo lived in the region for about seven months, in one of those castles.

## What replaced a city

Three hundred and fifty of them.

The oasis along twenty miles of the Ziz, he says, contained about three hundred
and fifty castles besides villages and hamlets — fortified settlements, each
walled, each with its own irrigation off the river. Three were principal:
Tenegent with a thousand families and a great many craftsmen, Tebuhasan eight
miles south and busier with merchants than anywhere else in the region, and
Mamun, which Jewish and Muslim traders both used.

The three had three separate governors, and Leo's sentence about them is the
whole political condition of the place:

> These three castles haue three seuerall gouernours, who are at great dissension
> among themselues. They will oftentimes destroy one anothers chanels, whereby
> their fieldes are watered, which cannot without great cost be repaired againe.
> They will stow the palme-trees also to the very stocks.

Cutting a rival's irrigation channel and pruning his date palms to the stump is
warfare adapted precisely to an oasis: it destroys the only two things anybody
there has, and both take years to restore.

## The wall they built and then lost

The most telling detail is what they did immediately after the city fell.

The inhabitants walled the entire territory — an eighty-mile circuit, Leo says,
done cheaply — so that horsemen could not raid into it. It worked while they were
at peace with each other. Then they fell to quarrelling, the wall was pulled
down, and each faction invited in the Arabs it thought would help against the
others.

A community that could organise an eighty-mile wall could not keep it, because
keeping it required the three governors to agree about anything.

## And the road, still there

They were still striking coin, in silver and in gold, and still rich: some of
their principal men, Leo says, had great traffic to the land of the Blacks,
carrying the wares of Barbary and exchanging them for gold and slaves. The
system the Midrarids had built was running six hundred years later, in the
absence of the town, the dynasty and the sect that built it.

The road north was still being taxed too. Between the Tafilalt and Fez the way
ran through country held by people who exacted heavy tribute from the caravans,
and Leo describes one of the three castles on it — set on a rock so high it
*seemeth to touch the cloudes*, with a guardhouse below where soldiers stood
permanently and took a quarter of a ducat for every camel-load that passed.

Which is what became of an empire whose entire purpose had been to charge for
crossing the desert: three hundred and fifty walled villages cutting each other's
water, and a man on a rock taking a quarter-ducat a camel.
