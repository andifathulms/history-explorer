title: Salt for gold

The trade this town lived on had a shape, and almost everything else in the
medieval Maghreb depends on it.

Two things moved. Gold came north from the Bambuk and Bure fields beyond the upper Senegal and
Niger, worked by people no empire on either end of the route ever governed. Salt came south from the Saharan pans — Taghaza above all, where the mines were
worked by slaves and the blocks were cut and loaded straight onto camels. The exchange was between a region with gold and no salt and a region
with salt and no gold, and both parties were prepared to trade them at rates
that look extraordinary from anywhere else.

The caravans ran on an annual cycle dictated by the desert: assembled in the
autumn at Sijilmasa, south by the wells, weeks in the sand, then the sahel
markets — Awdaghust first, later Timbuktu and Walata — and back before the
heat. Nobody crossed the Sahara casually. A caravan was a capitalised venture
with investors, guides, guarantors and a return date, and a town at either end
that could store, finance and tax it.

That is what Sijilmasa was. Not a market in the ordinary sense but the northern
terminal: warehouses, moneychangers, a mint striking the gold as it arrived,
and a customs house. Ibn Hawqal's bill of exchange for forty-two thousand
dinars is a credit instrument — which tells you
the trade was sophisticated enough to move value on paper across the desert
rather than carrying it.

One story about it should be handled carefully because it is repeated
everywhere. Herodotus describes a silent trade on the African coast — one party
leaves goods, withdraws, the other leaves gold, and neither sees the other —
and Arabic writers later attach the same account to the gold of the western
Sudan. It is a very old literary motif, told about the edge of the known world
in several traditions, and there is no good evidence that the Saharan trade
worked that way. The actual mechanism, as far as the sources show it, is
ordinary: brokers, agents, credit, and Muslim merchant communities settled at
both ends who spoke to each other constantly.

That last point is why this town's Kharijism matters commercially as well as
doctrinally. Trust across a fortnight of desert requires a network, and a
sectarian community that is a minority everywhere is a network by construction:
its members can identify each other, have obligations to each other, and can be
disciplined by exclusion. The Ibadis of Tahert carried their doctrine south along
these same routes for the same reason, and the doctrine travelled because the
credit did.
