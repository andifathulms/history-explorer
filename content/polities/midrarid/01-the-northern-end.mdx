---
title: The northern end of the road
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987
phase: formation

This site has now described the trans-Saharan gold trade from four directions.
The Mali page has the goldfields of Bambuk and Bure at the southern end. The
Almoravid page has a Saharan movement that took both termini before it took
Morocco. The Zayyanid page has the funduk at Tlemcen where the metal met
European bills of exchange. The Rustamid page has Ibadi merchants carrying
doctrine along the same routes.

Sijilmasa is the place they were all pointing at.

It was founded in the late 750s in the Tafilalt, an oasis on the northern edge
of the Sahara where the desert crossing ends and the roads to Fez, Tlemcen and
the Mediterranean begin. The founders were Sufri Kharijites of the Miknasa —
part of the same Kharijite world as the Ibadis of Tahert on this site's
Rustamid page, holding that the leader of the community is whichever Muslim is
qualified, and in permanent doctrinal opposition to the caliphate.

The town became rich immediately and stayed rich for two centuries. Ibn Hawqal,
who was there in the tenth century, records seeing a bill of exchange for
forty-two thousand dinars and says he had never encountered such a sum anywhere
in his travels — which, from a man who had been across the Islamic world, is
the single most useful sentence anyone wrote about this polity. Al-Mas'udi
records that gold from the Sudan was minted here.

That is the whole state, and this site's `revenue_basis` coding for it says so:
trade-toll and mining, and nothing else. A town with a wall, a mint, a customs
house and a fortnight of desert between it and the next town, taxing the only
route by which the gold of West Africa reached the Mediterranean economy — and
therefore, indirectly, taxing the coinage of half the polities on this site.

Kharijite doctrine and enormous commercial wealth are not an obvious
combination and it is worth naming the fit. A community holding that the
caliphate is illegitimate needs to be somewhere the caliphate cannot easily
reach, and a fortnight of desert is such a place. The Rustamids made the same
calculation at Tahert. Both were merchant states run by sectarians in country
nobody could occupy cheaply, and both ended in the same decade at the hands of
the same movement.
