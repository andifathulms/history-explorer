title: Four ways to explain one city

No native chronicle explains how Tiwanaku actually governed itself, and
archaeologists studying the site have reached no consensus either.
Scholarship on the question has produced several competing models,
each built on a different body of evidence and each carrying different
implications for how centralized, or how loosely organized, Tiwanaku
society really was.

## An urban revolution, or a trading network

The earliest influential model, developed from excavations in
Tiwanaku's own ceremonial core, borrowed the idea of an "urban
revolution" from studies of early Near Eastern cities: a sudden
transformation in which surplus production and the growth of the city
itself set off a chain reaction - a state apparatus, social
stratification, craft specialization - with military conquest carrying
Tiwanaku to imperial status by the eighth century. A rival model
proposed instead that Tiwanaku's own rise was fundamentally economic
rather than political: highland communities turning to long-distance
trade and llama-caravan transport as a substitute for direct control of
distant farmland, with Tiwanaku growing into an industrial and
commercial hub whose political reach simply followed wherever demand
for its goods extended.

## A managing bureaucracy, or self-governing villages

A third model, built on evidence of Tiwanaku's own vast raised-field
agricultural systems, argues that constructing and maintaining
farmland on this scale required a centralized authority capable of
organizing labour on the scale of the Inca mit'a system centuries
later - land reclamation directed by the state itself to feed a
growing urban and administrative population. Against this stands a
fourth model, drawn from research in agricultural communities distant
from the capital, which finds those same raised-field systems built
and worked by local, self-organizing communities - ayllus - with no
clear evidence of outside direction at all.

## A question the evidence has not settled

None of these four models fully accounts for the evidence on its own,
and each was developed from a different part of the archaeological
record - the monumental core, exchange networks, one valley's raised
fields, another's. What they share is a basic disagreement about
whether Tiwanaku was ever the kind of centrally directed state its
monuments might suggest at first glance, or whether those same
monuments coexisted with a much more loosely integrated society than
their scale implies.
