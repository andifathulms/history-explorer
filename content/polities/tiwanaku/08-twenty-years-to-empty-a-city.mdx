For decades, the standard explanation for Tiwanaku's collapse pointed
to a prolonged regional drought recorded in lake-sediment and ice-core
records from the surrounding altiplano, drying up the lake margins and
the raised-field systems that had fed the city for centuries. The
Bayesian radiocarbon chronology built from a hundred and two dates
complicates that story rather than confirming it: permanent habitation
at Tiwanaku ended within a span of roughly twenty years, centred
around 1010, a pace and a starting point that together place the
collapse before the driest phase of the drought sequence the older
theory relied on.

## A faster ending than the old story allowed

Twenty years is a strikingly narrow window for a city of Tiwanaku's
own size to lose its population entirely - not the slow, generations-long
decline a purely climatic explanation would predict, but something
closer to a rapid unravelling. A shorter interval followed the end of
permanent residence, in which visitors continued returning to the site
and using pottery in styles consistent with Tiwanaku's own established
traditions, before even this residual, intermittent use of the site
ceased by around 1050.

## An older rival explanation, and an unresolved question

An earlier generation of scholarship instead attributed Tiwanaku's
end to conquest or displacement by Aymara-speaking populations
expanding across the altiplano, a claim built mainly on linguistic
reconstruction of where Aymara and its relatives were spoken by the
time of the Spanish conquest rather than on direct archaeological
evidence of invasion at Tiwanaku itself. Between a drought sequence the
new chronology now shows arrived too late to be the sole trigger and an
invasion the evidence has never directly demonstrated, what actually
ended permanent life at Tiwanaku after eight centuries remains an open
question - one the same precise dating that ruled out the simplest
answer has not yet been able to replace with a settled one.

## What outlasted the city

Tiwanaku's abandonment did not erase its presence from the altiplano.
Its monuments remained standing landmarks for the societies that
followed in the Titicaca basin, and its name and imagery were later
invoked by the Inca state, centuries afterward, as a link to a
deep and prestigious Andean past.
