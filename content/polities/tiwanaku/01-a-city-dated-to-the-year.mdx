For most of the twentieth century, dating Tiwanaku's own founding meant
working from ceramic seriation and comparative stratigraphy - useful
tools, but ones that left the site's early history fixed only in
approximate ranges spanning whole centuries. A recent Bayesian analysis
combining a hundred and two radiocarbon dates, forty-five of them
previously unpublished, has narrowed that uncertainty dramatically: the
settlement at Tiwanaku, on the southern shore of Lake Titicaca, was
founded around the year 180.

## A modest beginning by a lake that would outlast it

Nothing about Tiwanaku's own first years marks it out as the future
capital of one of the Andes' great civilizations. It began as one
settlement among the communities scattered around the Titicaca basin,
its own advantages - access to the lake, to the surrounding altiplano's
pasture and wetland, to routes running toward both the Pacific coast and
the eastern valleys - no more pronounced at first than those enjoyed by
its neighbours.

## Four centuries before the city that made it famous

What later distinguished Tiwanaku from those neighbours took time to
develop. The precisely dated chronology now available places the site's
own transformation from a modest lakeside settlement into a genuine
urban centre roughly four centuries after its founding - a slow build
whose final, rapid phase would come to define everything most people now
associate with the name.
