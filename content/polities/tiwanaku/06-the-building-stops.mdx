Around the year 720, by the median estimate of the same Bayesian
radiocarbon chronology that dates Tiwanaku's founding so precisely,
construction of new monuments at the city's core came to a stop. No
destruction layer, burning, or sign of violence marks the change - the
Akapana, the Kalasasaya and the Pumapunku simply stopped growing,
three centuries before the city itself would empty out entirely.

## A gap the evidence does not yet explain

What ended the building programme is not established. The pause could
mark a shift in priorities away from monumental construction and
toward some other form of investment the archaeological record
preserves less visibly, a loss of the labour mobilization that had
raised the core in the first place, or a change in the city's own
religious or political life that made further construction seem less
urgent than it once had. None of these readings is yet demonstrated
against the alternatives, and the chronology's own authors are careful
to note the date as a genuine stopping point rather than as evidence
for any single cause.

## Three centuries of a finished city

Whatever caused it, the halt did not end Tiwanaku itself. The
population that had built the monumental core went on living
alongside it for three more centuries, using and maintaining a
ceremonial complex that had, in effect, reached its final built form
long before the wider collapse that would eventually empty the city
out altogether.
