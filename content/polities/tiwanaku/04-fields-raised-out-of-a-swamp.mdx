The altiplano around Lake Titicaca is not obviously good farmland - a
high, cold, often waterlogged landscape where frost could destroy a
harvest overnight. Tiwanaku's own hinterland turned this liability into
one of the most productive agricultural systems the pre-Columbian Andes
ever built: raised fields, called suka kollus, formed by digging
parallel canals and piling the excavated soil into long planting
platforms between them. The standing water in the canals moderated
night-time temperatures near the crops and could be drained or
channelled as conditions required, letting farmers work land that
would otherwise have been too wet or too frost-prone to plant reliably.

## Less labour than it looks

The scale of these field systems, stretching across thousands of
hectares in the valleys around the capital, long suggested to
archaeologists that only a powerful centralized authority could have
organized their construction. Fieldwork using the traditional
Andean footplow, the chakitaqlla, complicated that assumption: a single
worker using the tool could raise roughly a cubic metre of field
platform in an hour, a pace that made the fields considerably less
labour-intensive to build than earlier estimates had assumed, and well
within reach of ordinary households and small kin-based work groups
without state direction.

## A system older than the city that came to rely on it

Raised-field cultivation in the wider Titicaca basin predates Tiwanaku's
own rise by centuries in some locations, with construction phases
recorded as early as 600 to 800 BCE at sites well outside Tiwanaku's own
core territory. What the city's later growth changed was not the basic
technology but the scale at which it was deployed and, in the valleys
closest to the capital, quite possibly who was deciding where and when
new fields were built - the very question the competing models of
Tiwanaku's own political organization were built to answer.
