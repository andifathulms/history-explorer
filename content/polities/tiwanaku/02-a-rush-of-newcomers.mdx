title: A rush of newcomers
drafted_from: marsh-2023

Around the year 600, Tiwanaku's own character changed decisively.
Radiocarbon evidence points to a sudden surge of migration into the
settlement in the years just before this, families arriving from across
the wider Titicaca region until the city became the principal
destination for anyone in the area looking to relocate. The newcomers
did not simply move into an existing town; their arrival coincided with
the first residential construction on a genuinely urban scale, and with
the first tombs and reused stone blocks appearing at what would become
two of the city's own greatest monuments, the Pumapunku and the
Akapana.

## A style of pottery invented by the people who came

Only after this wave of migration had already reshaped the settlement
did its own most recognizable material signature appear: a distinctive
decorated redware pottery, first showing up in the archaeological
record around 620. The sequence matters - families seem to have
migrated to Tiwanaku first, and only afterward developed the pottery
style that would go on to define Tiwanaku identity across the wider
region for the following four centuries, carried outward from the city
by trade and by the same networks that had first brought settlers in.

## A city built by the people it attracted

What this chronology suggests is a settlement whose own growth was
driven less by any single founding decision than by its own accumulating
pull - a place people converged on, in numbers large enough to found
new neighbourhoods and begin monumental construction almost
immediately, and whose culture was shaped as much by what those
migrants brought and built together as by whatever institutions
already existed there to receive them.
