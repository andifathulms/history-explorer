---
title: Bodies in the refuse pits
drafted_from: marsh-2023

---

Around the year 910, the way Tiwanaku's own dead were treated changed
abruptly. For centuries the city had interred its people in built
tombs, cist graves and other formal burial architecture. At almost the
same radiocarbon-dated moment that this practice ceased, a different
kind of human remains begins appearing in the record instead: bodies
showing clear signs of violent death - blunt-force trauma, wounds to
the skull, evidence of binding - deposited in refuse pits and other
informal contexts with no burial architecture around them at all.

## A pattern too abrupt to be gradual change

The Bayesian chronology places both the end of formal burial and the
appearance of these violently killed bodies close enough together in
time that the researchers who identified the pattern describe it as a
single, previously unrecognized turning point in Tiwanaku's own
history, rather than two separate and coincidental shifts. What
provoked it is not established: the dating rules out earlier
assumptions that blamed such violence squarely on regional drought,
since the shift by this reckoning arrives well before any climatic
decline the region is known to have suffered, but nothing yet excavated
identifies who these victims were, why they were being killed, or what
social breakdown - internal conflict, a change in ritual practice,
loss of control over the city's own population - the killings reflect.

## A century before the population itself vanished

The instability the burial evidence points to did not immediately end
Tiwanaku's occupation. Roughly a century still separates the onset of
this ritualized violence from the rapid depopulation that would
eventually empty the city out - long enough that whatever was
happening around 910 should be read as an early symptom of Tiwanaku's
troubles rather than as their final act.
