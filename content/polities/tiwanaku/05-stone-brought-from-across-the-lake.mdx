By the seventh century, the monumental core that still draws visitors
to Tiwanaku today had largely taken the form it would keep. The Akapana
rose as a stepped earthen platform faced in cut stone, its own summit
carrying a sunken court that filled with water during the rains and
drained through an internal system of stone channels built into the
mound itself. Nearby, the Kalasasaya's own raised rectangular
enclosure, walled in alternating standing pillars and coursed masonry,
framed a gateway carved from a single slab of andesite - the Gateway of
the Sun, its lintel cut with a central staring figure surrounded by
rows of winged attendants.

## Stone moved without wheels or draft animals

None of this construction used a wheel, a pulley, or an animal capable
of hauling comparable loads; the sandstone and andesite blocks, some
weighing many tons, were quarried at sites on the far side of Lake
Titicaca and at the Kimsachata range to the south, then moved to the
building site by human labour alone, very likely rafted partway across
the lake itself before being dragged the rest of the distance. The
Pumapunku, the last and most technically ambitious of the core's great
structures, took this masonry tradition furthest: precisely fitted
andesite blocks cut with interlocking joints and drill holes for metal
cramps, worked to tolerances that still draw comment from anyone who
examines them closely.

## A capital built to be seen from within

Unlike a fortified citadel, Tiwanaku's monumental core was not built
for defense; its sunken courts and raised platforms instead shaped how
people moved through the city and where they gathered, channelling
processions and rituals through a sequence of thresholds - courtyard,
gateway, platform summit - that framed the experience of arriving at
the city's own ceremonial heart rather than keeping anyone out of it.
