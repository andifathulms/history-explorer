Tidore outlasted Ternate by a hundred and twenty-two years, and the reason has
a name.

Nuku had been fighting the Dutch from the sea since 1780, with a fleet drawn
from the Papuan coasts and the Raja Ampat islands — Tidore's own dependencies,
the eastern end of its half of the dualism — and with British help when it
suited both of them. In 1797 he took Tidore itself. He held the sultanate as an
independent power until he died in 1805.

Seventeen years of insurgency, then eight years of a state.

## A different kind of win

Throwing a European power out and keeping your capital happened rarely enough
to count on one hand — the Ya'ariba took Muscat from the Portuguese in 1650,
Ethiopia destroyed an Italian army at Adwa in 1896, and Ternate's five-year
siege put the Portuguese garrison off the island in 1575.

Nuku's is a different shape from any of those. Tidore had already been
absorbed: company treaties, a resident, a sultan installed and deposed at the
company's convenience. There was nothing left to expel. What Nuku did was take
the sultanate back from the inside, with a fleet raised from the Papuan coasts
and the Raja Ampat — the eastern half of the network the previous chapter
describes, which was the one asset the company had never bothered to take.

Not an expulsion, then, but a recovery, by a polity that on any ordinary
measure had stopped being one.

What he recovered did not survive him. He died in 1805 and the sultanate went
back under Dutch authority, which is why the story ends there rather than at
1797. But those eight years are the reason Tidore's history runs a century past
Ternate's, and they were paid for by seventeen years at sea.
