Spain pulled its garrisons out of Maluku in 1663 to concentrate on defending
Manila, and Tidore was left facing the Dutch company without the ally it had
held for a hundred and forty-two years.

It should have been the end. It was the best thing that happened to the
sultanate in the seventeenth century.

## Why the company gave better terms

By 1663 Ternate had been a company dependency in all but name for a generation;
its formal end came with a treaty in 1683, and the substance had gone long
before the document. A company that had reduced one of the two Malukan centres
did not want the other reduced too. It wanted a counterweight: a second court
with enough standing to keep Ternate's ambitions in check and enough dependence
to be manageable.

So Tidore negotiated, and got recognition of its eastern dependencies, a
lighter version of the extirpation obligation, and a standing the company took
care not to erode. Saifuddin reigned thirty years on that footing, and it is
the strongest position the sultanate ever held.

## What it proves about the dualism

The opposition between the two islands is usually explained as something
Europeans made or at least sharpened — two spice powers set against each other
by Portuguese and Spanish backing.

The best evidence against that is negative, and it is here. By 1663 both
Europeans were out of the arrangement: the Portuguese expelled from Ternate in
1575, the Spanish withdrawn from Tidore in 1663. The dualism kept working
anyway — and a Dutch company with no interest whatever in preserving it found
that it had to.

A structure that outlives the outsiders it recruited, and then obliges a third
outsider to maintain it, was not produced by outsiders.
