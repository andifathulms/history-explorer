Ternate's half of Maluku ran west and south: Halmahera, the Sula islands, the
eastern arm of Sulawesi. Tidore's ran east, and it ran a very long way.

The Raja Ampat — four island groups off the western tip of New Guinea, whose
rulers held titles from Tidore. The Onin and Kowiai coasts of what is now
Papua. Seram Laut and the islands used as exchange points for the trade beyond
them.

What came back along that network was not cloves. It was massoi bark, tortoise
shell, bird of paradise plumes, ambergris, sago, and slaves — and the plumes in
particular went from western New Guinea through Tidore into a trade that
reached China and, eventually, European hat-makers.

## The hongi

The instrument was the *hongi*: a fleet of *kora-kora*, the long oared war
craft of the northern Moluccas, sailing east in season to collect tribute,
enforce the titles, and take what was owed from anybody who had not sent it.

Both sultanates ran them. The Dutch company later turned the institution to its
own purpose — the clove extirpation voyages, in which Malukan fleets destroyed
the crop on Malukan islands to the company's schedule — but the fleets and the
obligation long predate the company, and this is what they had been for.

## Two islands, two Islams

The obvious assumption is that Islam reached the north Moluccas once and spread
from there, and that Ternate and Tidore are two names for the same event. They
are not.

Islam went west and south with Ternate's fleets, into Halmahera and the Sula
islands and the eastern arm of Sulawesi. It went east with Tidore's, into the
Raja Ampat and along the coasts of western New Guinea, carried by the same
titles and the same tribute voyages described above.

Those coasts are the eastern limit of Islam's spread in the archipelago before
the modern period, and it got there because a small sultanate on a volcanic
island had a network running six hundred miles in the one direction its rival
did not.
