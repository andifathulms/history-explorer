Nuku's death in 1805 ended the independence he had spent seventeen years
fighting to recover, but not the sultanate itself. Dutch authority resumed
over Tidore much as it had before him, and when the bankrupt VOC handed its
Asian possessions to the Dutch state in 1799, the arrangement simply
continued under a government rather than a company - a sultan kept in his
palace, his eastern dependencies in the Raja Ampat and along the Papuan
coast still nominally his, and the actual power to grant or withhold land
increasingly somebody else's.

## The declaration that took the land without taking the title

That process reached its formal conclusion in 1910, when a "short
declaration" imposed on Tidore alongside Ternate and Bacan stripped all
three sultans of their personal customary land rights outright. What had
been the sultan's own to grant, tax and inherit became land the colonial
government administered directly - the last practical difference between
a sultan who ruled and a sultan who merely presided disappearing in a
single document.

## What the title still carries

The sultanate survived that stripping the way it had survived every
earlier reduction: as an office without the substance that had once come
with it. Tidore's ceremonial line continues in Indonesia today, and the
eastern network of tribute and titulature that Nuku had once mobilised
into an army - the Raja Ampat islands, the coastal chiefdoms of western
New Guinea - passed first to the colonial state and then, at independence,
to Indonesia itself. It is the documentary basis Jakarta has since pointed
to for its own claim over West Papua: a sovereignty question in the
twentieth and twenty-first centuries still tracing its paper trail back to
a network of island tribute relationships an early modern sultanate built
to keep itself solvent.
