*Extirpatie* — the Dutch company's programme of cutting and burning clove trees
everywhere except the islands it chose — was carried out by Malukan fleets
against Malukan dependencies, on the company's orders. Ternate, whose entire
revenue was that one crop, was made to destroy it wherever the company could not
watch it grow.

The same was done to Tidore. The difference is what Tidore had besides.

## Two revenues, one destroyed

The clove monopoly was the value that could be taken away, and it was. What
could not be taken as easily was the tribute — the massoi bark, the plumes, the
shell and the sago coming west along a chain of dependencies running six
hundred miles into New Guinea, through waters the company had no interest in
patrolling and no capacity to.

That is a large part of why Tidore outlasted Ternate by a hundred and
twenty-two years. A polity reduced to one commodity the company controlled had
nothing left when the commodity went. A polity with a second network in
territory nobody else wanted had somewhere to be.

It is also where Nuku's fleet came from, seventeen years of it, which is the
subject of the last chapter.

## Three ways to break a monopoly

Aceh and Banten both held crown monopolies on pepper, and both were broken the
same way: a company that could buy upcountry from the growers, without calling
at the capital, turned the monopoly from a mechanism into a claim.

Ternate's clove monopoly was broken the opposite way — not by competition but
by destruction, because cloves grew in a small enough area that eliminating the
supply was cheaper than controlling the trade.

Tidore had the same monopoly destroyed and did not stop, because a second
revenue existed in a direction nobody was looking. That is not resilience in
any planned sense. It is an accident of where the islands are.
