This site's Seleucid record has a chapter called losing the east without
noticing, and this page is the thing that was lost.

Around 256 BC Diodotus, the Seleucid satrap of Bactria, stopped acknowledging
the king and began striking coins with his own portrait. There is no account of
why or how. The classical sources give the whole of Greco-Bactrian history a few
paragraphs — Strabo, Justin's epitome of a lost work, some notices in Polybius —
and they were written by people who had never been there, from sources that are
gone.

What exists instead is coins, in very large numbers and of remarkable quality;
one excavated city; a handful of inscriptions; some administrative documents on
parchment and leather; and archaeology across northern Afghanistan, Uzbekistan
and Tajikistan that is difficult to conduct for reasons that have nothing to do
with the ancient world.

Mairs' book is written from that material rather than around it, and this page
follows her because the alternative is to write four hundred words of dynastic
narrative that the evidence does not support. The dynastic sequence itself is a
reconstruction: kings are known because coins exist, their order is inferred
from overstrikes and hoards, and several of them were issuing at the same time
in different mints. This record's `succession_rule` field is null for that
reason and the comment says so — there is no rule to code because there is
barely a sequence.

What can be said is that Bactria was worth having. It is irrigated country, the
most agriculturally productive in Central Asia, with a dense settled population
that long predates any Greek and a position on the routes between India, Iran
and the steppe. Alexander had fought hardest here and had settled the most
colonists here. When the chain of Seleucid cities running back to the
Mediterranean stopped functioning, the far end did not collapse. It became a
kingdom.
