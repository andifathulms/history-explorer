Greek kingship in Central Asia ended without a battle and without a chronicle,
and the most complete surviving portrait of any of these kings was written by a
Buddhist, in Pali, in a book of religious argument, some generations after he
was dead.

The king is Menander, who ruled south of the Hindu Kush in the later second
century BC — probably from about 140 to somewhere between 115 and 110, on the
evidence of his coins, which show him young on some issues and very old on
others. He is the one Greek king of this region whom Indian tradition
remembered, and, as it happens, the one the classical writers say most about,
which is still almost nothing.

What they say is worth having in full, because it is four sentences.

Strabo names him as one of the two Bactrian kings who pushed Greek rule
furthest east, across the Hypanis and as far as the Isamos — the Sutlej and
probably the Jumna. The lost forty-first book of Justin is titled with his name
and that of Apollodotus, calling them Indian kings. And Plutarch preserves a
tradition that he was noted for justice, that he died in camp on campaign, and
that after his death several cities contended for his ashes, the quarrel being
settled by dividing the relics among them so that each could raise a monument.

Rhys Davids points out the obvious thing about that last item and then declines
to build on it: it is exactly what the tradition says happened to the remains of
the Buddha, and the coincidence will not carry an argument.

## What the coins were doing

Twenty-two different coin types survive with his name on them, eight of them
carrying his portrait, and they have been found from Kabul in the west to
Mathura in the east and as far north as Kashmir. The author of the *Periplus of
the Erythraean Sea* notes that Menander's coins, and Apollodotus', were still
circulating at Barygaza — Broach, on the Gujarat coast — many years after his
death.

The legends on them are the interesting part. Menander is one of at least six
Greek kings of the region whose coins call the king righteous, in a formula
that answers to the Pali *dhammika dhamma-raja*, the king who rules by the
Dhamma. Rhys Davids reads that carefully: it is firm evidence that these kings
were surrounded by Buddhist subjects whose views they wanted to meet, and it is
no evidence at all that any of them became Buddhists.

## The dialogue

Then there is the book. The *Milinda-pañha*, the Questions of King Milinda, is
a long dialogue between a Greek king and a Buddhist elder named Nagasena, on
the self, on rebirth without a soul, on merit, on what it is that continues
when a person does. It is a work of didactic ethics and religious controversy
cast in the form of a historical romance, and it became a classic — quoted by
Buddhaghosa, translated across Buddhist Asia, still read.

Its Milinda is a formidable interlocutor. He is quick, well-read, sceptical,
and unwilling to be satisfied; the book's whole method depends on the king
being difficult, which is a strange compliment to pay a foreign monarch who had
died generations before you were born.

At the end of it, as the text now stands, the king is converted, hands his
kingdom to his son, enters the Order, and becomes an arahat. Rhys Davids does
not believe it. The passage stands where a lost final leaf might have been, the
Sinhalese manuscripts flag it as coming from a Siamese copy, and none of
Menander's coins shows any sign of a conversion. His judgement is that a writer
composing in north-western India, at a distance from the events, converted the
king because it was the fitting close to the argument he was making — and would
have been astonished at later critics who took his history literally while
treating his ethics as optional.

## What that amounts to

Set the two records beside each other.

From the Greek and Roman side: a name, a river crossed, a death in camp and a
disputed funeral. From the Buddhist side: a fully realised character with a
speaking voice, a philosophical position, and an argument he loses over several
hundred pages.

The kingdom that produced the finest coin portraits in the ancient world left
no history of itself in Greek, and the one thing that preserved a Greek king as
a person rather than as a profile was a religion his subjects held and he
probably did not.
