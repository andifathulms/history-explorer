At the confluence of the Oxus and the Kokcha, in what is now Takhar province in
northern Afghanistan, a French mission excavated between 1964 and 1978 the only
extensively dug Hellenistic city in Central Asia. Its ancient name is not
certainly known; it is called Ai-Khanoum, which is Uzbek for lady moon.

What came out of it is the reason the kingdom is not a footnote. A gymnasium
of
considerable size. A theatre seating several thousand. A palace with a
courtyard of over a hundred columns. Corinthian capitals, a mosaic, a
sundial calibrated for a latitude that is not Bactria's. A treasury with
inscribed jars. Fragments of a philosophical text on papyrus and of a Greek
literary papyrus, surviving only as impressions in the soil where the papyrus
itself had rotted.

<Figure id="gb-cybele" />

And in a sanctuary, a stone inscribed by a man called Klearchos, who recorded
that he had copied the Delphic maxims — know thyself, nothing in excess — at
Delphi and brought them here to set up. It is about four thousand kilometres.

<Figure id="gb-kineas" />

Mairs' argument is that the obvious reading of this material is the wrong
one. Ai-Khanoum is routinely described as a piece of Greece transplanted, a
homesick colony reproducing the metropolis at the ends of the earth. What the
site actually shows is a place where a Greek civic vocabulary coexists with
Bactrian temple architecture, Achaemenid administrative practice, local
building techniques and a population that was overwhelmingly not Greek. The
sanctuary Klearchos put his stone in is not a Greek temple; it is a stepped
platform of Mesopotamian and Iranian type.

The people who lived there were not Greeks abroad. They were, after three or
four generations, Bactrians who used Greek — which is what the coins show too,
and what the site's later material shows when the Greek names give way to local
ones in the same offices.

The city was abandoned around 145 BC. There is destruction, there is looting of
the treasury, and there is a squatter occupation afterwards. What ended it is
the subject of the last chapter, and the honest answer is that nobody knows
precisely.
