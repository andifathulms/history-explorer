The end of Greek Bactria is a chain of displacements that begins two thousand
kilometres east and is recorded, unusually, from the Chinese side.

The Xiongnu — the steppe confederation that Han China spent two centuries
managing — defeated the Yuezhi on the Gansu frontier
in the 170s BC and drove them west. The Yuezhi displaced the Sakas, the Sakas
moved south and west into Sogdiana and Bactria and eventually into Iran and
India, and the Yuezhi followed them. Zhang Qian, the Han envoy who opened the
first Chinese contact with Iran, was sent to find the Yuezhi and propose an
alliance against the Xiongnu; he reached them in Bactria around 128 BC and
reported that they had settled there and were no longer interested in revenge.

<Figure id="gb-mogao" />

His report is the best contemporary description of Bactria anybody wrote, and it
describes the country as prosperous, walled, full of markets, and militarily
feeble.

By then the Greek kingdom north of the Hindu Kush was finished or finishing.
Ai-Khanoum was abandoned around 145; the northern mints stopped; the last
Bactrian Greek issues are of the 130s. There is no battle in any source and no
year that can be given as the year the kingdom ended — only a decade in which
the coins stop and nobody appears to have taken the country from anybody.

The Yuezhi settled, divided into five principalities, and a century and a half
later one of them — the Kushanas — absorbed the others and produced an empire
that would rule from the Oxus to the Ganges. So Bactria's Greek period ends and
its
Kushan period begins, in the same valleys, with a good deal carried across: the
Kushans struck coins on Greek models with Greek legends for generations, used
the Greek alphabet to write their own Iranian language, and out of their
territory came the Gandharan sculpture that put Greek drapery on the Buddha.

<Figure id="gb-kujula" />

The other half of the dynasty was south of the mountains, in a different
country, and outlasted the northern kingdom by more than a century.
