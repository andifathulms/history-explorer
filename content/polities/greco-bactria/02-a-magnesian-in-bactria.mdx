In about 206 BC the Seleucid king came east with an army to take Bactria back,
besieged its king in Bactra, and went home having recognised him. The argument
that produced that result is preserved by Polybius and it is the best evidence
anywhere for what this kingdom thought it was.

## Not a rebel

Euthydemus was, Polybius notes, *himself a Magnesian* — a Greek from the Ionian
coast of Asia Minor, ruling in Central Asia, two thousand miles from where his
family came from.

His case to Antiochus was a lawyer's, and it turned on a distinction:

> He was not himself a revolted subject, but had destroyed the descendants of
> some who had been such, and so had obtained the kingdom of Bactria.

Somebody had certainly seceded from the Seleucids — Diodotus, two generations
back. Euthydemus had killed that man's heirs and taken the throne from them.
Whatever crime founded the kingdom, he had punished it, and therefore held
Bactria not against the Seleucids but in succession to people he had removed on
their behalf.

It is an argument entirely about legitimacy rather than about strength, made by a
man under siege. He is asking to be treated as a king, and the whole claim rests
on how he came by the throne rather than on his ability to keep it.

## The warning

The second half of the argument is the reason it worked, and it is the sentence
the whole kingdom turns on.

Euthydemus urged the envoy to press Antiochus not to begrudge him the royal title
— because if he refused:

> neither of them would be safe: seeing that great hordes of Nomads were close at
> hand, who were a danger to both; and that if they admitted them into the
> country, it would certainly be utterly barbarised.

The king of Bactria told the king of Asia that their war was the opening the
steppe was waiting for. Two Greek armies grinding each other down on the Oxus
would use up exactly the force that kept the frontier shut.

Antiochus had been looking for a way out of the siege anyway, and took it.

## Demetrius

The settlement was sealed by sending Euthydemus's son.

Antiochus received Demetrius, and Polybius records the grounds on which he
decided: from the young man's appearance, his conversation and the dignity of his
manners, he judged him *worthy of royal power*. He promised him one of his own
daughters, and conceded the royal title to his father.

So the dynasty got what it had asked for — recognition, a marriage into the
Seleucid house, and a king's name — and paid for it in elephants and supplies for
an army that then marched away over the Hindu Kush.

Demetrius would later take Greek arms into India and give this kingdom its widest
reach, and the Indo-Greek states that followed him lasted long after Bactria
itself was gone.

## The warning came true

Euthydemus was right about the nomads and wrong only about the sequence.

The two Greek kingdoms did not destroy each other. They made a treaty, exchanged a
prince, and parted on good terms. And roughly sixty years later the Yuezhi came
over the Oxus anyway and ended the kingdom of Bactria — not because two Greek
armies had exhausted each other, but because the frontier needed holding by
somebody, permanently, and a Greek dynasty two thousand miles from Magnesia
eventually could not.

He had identified the thing that would kill his kingdom and used it as a
bargaining chip, and both of those were correct.
