Bactria the kingdom lasted about a hundred and twenty years. Bactra the city was
there before it and was still the greatest city in Khurasan a thousand years
afterwards, under the name Balkh. The Arabic geographers call it the Mother of
Cities.

The geographers describe a place of a size that is hard to reconcile with the
ruin standing there now. In the ninth century Ya'qubi calls it the greatest city
in all Khurasan, with forty congregational mosques in it. It had had three
concentric walls and thirteen gates in the old days; by the tenth century it had
seven, and the list of their names is a census of who lived there — the Gate of
the Square, the Iron Gate, the Gate of the Sixty Dykes, the Jews' Gate, the Gate
of the Hindus.

The houses ran three miles square. A stream called the Dahas, meaning ten mills,
turned them as it passed the Naw Bahar gate and went on to irrigate the farms
towards Termez. Around the city were gardens growing oranges, sugar cane and
the water lily, and the produce was exported in quantity. Muqaddasi runs through
the beauty, the many streams, the cheapness of food, the broad streets and the
well-built palaces, in the tone of a man who had enjoyed himself there.

## The New Monastery

Outside the walls stood the suburb the Arabic writers call Naw Bahar, and their
account of it is the most interesting thing in the chapter, partly for what it
describes and partly for what they thought they were describing.

They report it as a fire temple: one of the chief sanctuaries of the Zoroastrians
in Sasanian times, whose hereditary chief priest was a man called Barmak. It was
built, they say, in imitation of and in rivalry with the Ka'ba. Its walls were
set with precious stones and hung with brocade curtains, and were anointed with
perfumes, particularly in spring — Naw Bahar means early spring, and spring was
when the pilgrimage was made. The central building carried a dome more than a
hundred ells high, and around it stood three hundred and sixty chambers housing
three hundred and sixty priests, one for each day of the year. A silk banner on
the summit streamed out in the wind for an implausible distance. Inside were
figures, and before one of them the pilgrims from Kabul, from India and from
China prostrated themselves, afterwards kissing the hand of Barmak. The
sanctuary owned the land for seven leagues around and drew a great revenue from
it.

Read that again with the geography in mind. Pilgrims from India and China,
prostrating before an image, in a domed building with three hundred and sixty
cells for monks, in Bactria.

*Naw Bahar* is not Persian for anything. It is Sanskrit — *nava vihara*, the new
monastery. What the Muslim writers are describing, several centuries later and
through a Zoroastrian vocabulary because that was the vocabulary they had for
Iranian religion, is one of the great Buddhist foundations of Central Asia. The
priest is an abbot; the hand-kissing is what pilgrims did; the three hundred and
sixty chambers are cells.

The sanctuary was destroyed by Ahnaf ibn Qays in the conquest of Khurasan under
the caliph Uthman. Barmak's family converted, and his descendants became the
Barmakids — the viziers who effectively ran the Abbasid caliphate under Harun
al-Rashid until he destroyed them in a night.

## What finally did it

Balkh survived the loss of its sanctuary by five hundred years.

The Ghuzz Turks laid it in ruins in 1155; the population came back and rebuilt
it a little way off, and it had substantially recovered by the time Yaqut
described it in the early thirteenth century. Then the Mongols came in 1220. Ibn
Battuta, passing through more than a century later, found Balkh still completely
ruined and uninhabited — Chinggis Khan had pulled down a third of the Great
Mosque looking for treasure that was not there — with a scatter of tombs and
shrines outside the walls that pilgrims still visited. Timur rebuilt part of it
at the end of the fourteenth century.

So the sequence in this one place runs: a Greek kingdom minting the best coins
in the ancient world, a Buddhist monastery drawing pilgrims from China, a
Zoroastrian priesthood whose heirs governed Baghdad, forty Friday mosques and
sugar cane, and then two devastations eighty years apart from which it never
fully returned.

The Greek kingdom is the shortest item on that list by a wide margin. It is
also, in Europe, the only one of them anybody has heard of.
