Resistance at Riau-Lingga's own court, in its final decade, took a
form that looked almost trivial on its surface: a dispute over which
flag flew where. Dutch colonial administration insisted that its own
flag be flown above, or alongside, whatever banner the Sultan of
Riau-Lingga used to mark his own status, on land and at sea alike. In
1904, Dutch officials at Penyengat discovered that Sultan Abdul
Rahman had not raised the Dutch flag in front of his own court
house at all - a lapse the Resident of Riau reported directly to the
Governor-General in Batavia as evidence the sultan was "acting as a
free king."

## A contract written to settle a flag

The Dutch response was not a punishment but a formal contract,
signed in May 1905, that spelled out flag protocol in exacting detail:
the Dutch flag had to stand taller than any Riau-Lingga banner on
land, and where only one flagpole existed aboard ship, the Dutch flag
alone had the right to fly from it. That a colonial power felt the
need to write such specifications into a formal political agreement
says something about how seriously symbolic sovereignty was being
contested at Penyengat, even after the sultanate's own practical
independence had been reduced to very little.

## Advisors who counted the cost of every concession

Behind the sultan stood a circle of court scholars and officials,
led by Raja Ali Kelana, who spent years pressing Sultan Abdul Rahman
to recognize how much authority and dignity his own court had already
surrendered to the Dutch. When the Resident of Riau proposed a new,
even more restrictive political contract in 1910, it was this same
circle's persistent argument - framed around what Riau-Lingga's court
had once been, set against what it had become - that persuaded the
sultan to refuse it outright.

## A refusal answered by warships

That refusal did not go unanswered. Dutch steamships gathered in
Riau's own waters, and a formal decree from the Governor-General,
dated 10 February 1911, terminated Sultan Abdul Rahman's own
recognition as ruler. A Dutch-approved successor, styled merely a
representative rather than a sultan in his own right, was installed
in his place - the sultanate's own last independent act having been,
in the end, a refusal to sign rather than any attempt at open
rebellion.
