---
title: A throne behind a throne
drafted_from: andaya-1975

Riau-Lingga's own arrangement of power did not begin with its own
founding in 1824. It was inherited whole from the confederation that
preceded it, in which a Malay sultan sat on the throne while Bugis
newcomers - adventurers who had settled throughout the strait as
soldiers and traders after Makassar's own defeat a century and a half
earlier - held the actual reins of the state, through the office of
viceroy at Riau. The sultan reigned; the Bugis governed. Neither side
had any interest in dissolving an arrangement that suited both of them
well enough.

## A line drawn by outsiders

What changed in 1824 was not the internal balance between the Malay
court and its Bugis viceroys, but the map around them. An Anglo-Dutch
treaty settled a colonial rivalry that had nothing to do with any
quarrel between the two halves of the Malay-Bugis partnership itself,
assigning the peninsula's own southern tip to a British sphere and the
Riau-Lingga archipelago to a Dutch one. A single confederation that
had once claimed both sides of the strait as one connected world
became, on paper, two separate countries' worth of territory
overnight.

## An old arrangement in a smaller frame

Riau-Lingga inherited the Dutch-side half of that division intact - the
Malay sultan, the Bugis viceroy actually running the state's affairs,
the same basic constitutional logic that had governed the wider
confederation for over a century. What had shrunk was not the
structure of power but its territorial scope, now confined to the
archipelago rather than spanning both sides of the strait.
