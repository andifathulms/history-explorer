Riau-Lingga's own existence, once the 1824 partition had settled the
country's borders, was defined by a relationship it could not
renegotiate: a Dutch colonial administration that tolerated the
sultanate's own internal arrangement of power as long as it served
Dutch interests, and that held every real lever of authority beyond
the archipelago's own shores. The Malay sultan and his Bugis viceroy
went on governing the same internal balance the older confederation
had run on, but the wider political weather no longer answered to
either of them.

## An arrangement that outlived its own usefulness

What had once been a workable division of labor between a ceremonial
Malay throne and an effective Bugis administration eventually stopped
being useful to the power that actually mattered most: the colonial
government whose sphere the sultanate occupied. Direct Dutch rule,
extending steadily across the archipelago through the later nineteenth
century, had less and less reason to preserve a local court whose own
practical authority had already been hollowed out by decades of
colonial oversight.

## An ending without a battle

The sultanate's own formal end, in 1911, came not as a conquest in the
usual sense but as an abolition - the Dutch colonial government simply
withdrawing recognition from an arrangement it no longer needed to
maintain. A confederation that had once claimed both sides of the
strait as a single world, split once by treaty in 1824, was
extinguished the second time not by a rival power's army but by the
same colonial administration that had tolerated its existence for
nearly a century.
