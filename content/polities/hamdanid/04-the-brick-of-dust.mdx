The court at Aleppo is described by al-Tha'alibi, who saw the tail end of it,
in terms that are worth quoting because they are what a tenth-century writer
thought a court was for. The sons of Hamdan were princes whose faces were
formed for beauty, whose tongues were formed for eloquence, whose hands were
formed for liberality and whose minds were formed for pre-eminence; and Sayf
al-Dawla was the middle pearl of the necklace.

His court, the same writer says, was where visitors were drawn, where
beneficence rose like the sun, the *qibla* towards which the hopes of the needy
were turned, the place where caravans put down their travellers, the meeting
ground of men of letters and the lists in which poets contended. Never, except
at the doors of the caliphs, had so many masters of the poetic art been
assembled at one prince's gate.

Then he adds the sentence that stops it being flattery: *but sovereignty is the
market to which such wares are brought, because that is where they fetch the
best price.*

## What the price actually was

Ibn Khallikan collected the transactions, and they are specific.

A secretary compiled an anthology of ten thousand verses selected from the
panegyrics composed on Sayf al-Dawla. A desert Arab in shabby clothes stepped
forward at an audience, recited four lines ending with the observation that
fortune was the prince's slave and had wronged the petitioner, and was given
two hundred gold pieces. A judge from Nishapur pulled an empty purse and a
rolled paper from his sleeve, recited a poem whose first line said his master's
generosity was undiminished and his servant needed somebody to mend his silver;
the prince laughed and had a thousand gold pieces put into the purse he had
brought with him.

Sayf al-Dawla once set his companions to cap a line of his own, remarking that
only his nephew Abu Firas could do it. He recited: *you are mistress of my body
and have made it languish; how can you lawfully shed my blood?* Abu Firas
answered on the spot: *she replied — if sovereign power be mine, my authority
extends over everything.* For that couplet he was given an estate near Manbaj
worth two thousand gold pieces a year.

## The prince as poet

He wrote himself, and what survives is not the work of a soldier dabbling.

There is a description of a rainbow — the wind spreading dark mantles over the
sky with their trains sweeping the ground, embroidered yellow on red and green
overlaid with white, like girls approaching in gowns of different colours each
shorter than the last.

And there is the poem about the Greek slave girl. She was the daughter of a
Greek prince and his favourite, and his other women were sufficiently jealous
to be planning to poison her. He was told, and he sent her away to a castle
where she would be safe, and wrote:

> *I therefore wished thee far away, our mutual love still subsisting. Thus
> absence is sometimes caused through fear of absence, and separation through
> dread of separation.*

## The brick

He died at Aleppo on a Friday in February 967, of a retention of urine, and his
body was carried to Mayyafariqin and buried in the tomb he had built over his
mother's grave inside the city walls.

He had left instructions. Through the years of campaigning against the
Byzantines, the dust that settled on his clothes had been shaken off and
collected on his orders. It was made into a brick about the size of a hand, and
by his dying command it was put under his head in the tomb.

A man who paid a thousand dinars for a joke about a purse, and kept the dirt of
his own campaigns for a pillow, is not two people. He was performing both, for
an audience he had assembled and paid, and the performance is the reason
anything about him survives at all.
