---
title: The poet and the frontier
drafted_from: kennedy-2004

Al-Mutanabbi spent nine years at Sayf al-Dawla's court at Aleppo, and the poems
he wrote there are the reason a minor Arab dynasty of the Jazira is remembered
at all outside specialist history.

They are panegyrics, written for payment, about campaigns that were largely
unsuccessful against a Byzantine empire in the middle of its strongest recovery
in three centuries. They are also, by common consent, the summit of classical
Arabic verse. Both things are true at once, and the combination is the most
useful thing about the whole dynasty.

What Sayf al-Dawla bought was not a record of victories. It was a permanent
literary statement that someone was still fighting the frontier war, at a
moment when the caliph in Baghdad was a Buyid pensioner and the institution
that had justified that war for three centuries could no longer prosecute it.
The Hamdanids supplied the symbol, and the symbol outlasted the emirate by a
thousand years.

Measured by territory, population or the things it exported, the emirate of
Aleppo is a small item. Measured by what came out of that court and is still
being read, it is one of the more consequential places of its century. The two
statements do not contradict each other, and any account that keeps only the
first has lost the reason anyone remembers Sayf al-Dawla at all.
