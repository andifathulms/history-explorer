The Hamdanids were an Arab dynasty from the Jazira, unusual in a century when
almost every effective military force in the Islamic world was Turkish, Daylami
or Berber. One branch held Mosul. The other, under Sayf al-Dawla, took Aleppo in
944 and spent twenty-three years fighting the Byzantine empire on the Anatolian
frontier.

He mostly lost. The Byzantine recovery under Nicephorus Phocas and John Tzimiskes
was the strongest the empire had mounted in three centuries, and by 969 Antioch
was Byzantine again and Aleppo was tributary. What Sayf al-Dawla achieved was not
strategic but cultural: the frontier war gave the Islamic world a symbol of jihad
against Byzantium at precisely the moment the caliphate had stopped providing
one, and his court at Aleppo held al-Mutanabbi, the most celebrated Arabic poet
of the age, along with the philosopher al-Farabi.

The Hamdanid court is the standing reminder that the tenth century — politically
the caliphate's worst, with the caliph a Buyid pensioner and the provinces gone —
was among the best centuries Arabic letters and Islamic philosophy ever had. The
site's rating axes do not capture that, and this is one of several polities where
the empty bars are the more honest half of the page.
