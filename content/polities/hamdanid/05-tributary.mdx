title: Paying both sides

Sayf al-Dawla died in 967, and what he had been holding off arrived
immediately.

Nikephoros Phokas and then John Tzimiskes ran the strongest Byzantine offensive
in three centuries down through Cilicia and northern Syria. Tarsus and Antioch
went in 969. Aleppo was taken and then, rather than annexed, made a tributary
under the treaty of Safar the same year — the emirate kept its ruler and its
religion and paid, and Byzantine forces raided as far as Palestine in the
following decade.

That left the Hamdanids of Aleppo in the position this corpus has now recorded
for the Nasrids, the Trapezuntines and the Zayyanids: a small state whose
survival depended on paying one large neighbour and being useful to another.
They paid Constantinople and looked to Cairo, where the Fatimids on this site's
caliphates page had taken Egypt in 969 and wanted Syria. Then they looked back
to Constantinople when Cairo pressed too hard. It worked for a generation and
then it did not: the Fatimids deposed the last Hamdanid of Aleppo in 1004 and
the emirate passed to one of its own officers.

The Mosul branch had gone fourteen years earlier, to the Uqaylids.

Two things to keep from a page whose rating panel is entirely empty.

The Hamdanids are the last Arab tribal dynasty to matter in the central Islamic
lands. After them the region belongs to Turks, Kurds, Daylamis and Berbers for
eight hundred years — the Seljuks, the Zangids and Ayyubids on this site's own
central-lands pages, the Mamluks, the Ottomans. An Arab dynasty holding Arab
provinces with Arab tribal manpower stops being a thing that happens.

And the frontier they fought on stopped existing. This site's Great Seljuk page
has Manzikert a hundred and two years after Sayf al-Dawla's death, after which
the Byzantine frontier is in Anatolia rather than Cilicia and the war the
Hamdanids made their name on is somebody else's war in a different country.
What survived them is the poems.
