---
title: Two branches, one family
drafted_from: kennedy-2004

The Hamdanids were Arabs of the Taghlib, a tribe of the Jazira that had been in
that country since before Islam and had been Christian for a good part of it.
That matters for the same reason their Shi'ism does: in the tenth century,
almost every effective military force in the Islamic world was Turkish, Daylami
or Berber, and this was an Arab tribal dynasty holding provinces on its own
manpower.

The family rose the way most of the Iranian Intermezzo dynasties on this site
rose, by being useful to a caliphate that was running out of options. Hamdan
ibn Hamdun and his sons fought for and against Baghdad in the Jazira through the
late ninth century; one of them was made governor of Mosul around 905; and by
the 930s the family held the upper Tigris and the upper Euphrates.

Then it split, and the two halves had opposite fortunes.

The **Mosul branch** under Nasir al-Dawla went south. In the 940s, in the chaos
this site's Abbasid page describes between the collapse of caliphal government
and the arrival of the Buyids, the Hamdanids of Mosul took Baghdad itself and
held the caliph — briefly, twice. They were the last Arab dynasty to have the
caliphate in their hands. The Buyids took it off them in 945 and kept it for a
hundred and ten years, and Mosul spent the following half-century as a
tributary paying to be left alone, under a ruler the sources treat as capable
and rapacious in about equal measure. The Uqaylids, another Arab tribal
dynasty, removed the branch in 990.

The **Aleppo branch** under Sayf al-Dawla went west, and the next chapter is
about what happened to it there.

Both branches were Twelver Shi'i, ruling populations that were largely not, in
a period when this site's Buyid page describes the same combination at Baghdad
and its Fatimid page describes a Shi'i caliphate assembling in Ifriqiya. The
tenth century is the one in which Shi'i dynasties held most of the central
Islamic world without any of them converting the people they governed, and the
Hamdanids are the Arab instance of it.
