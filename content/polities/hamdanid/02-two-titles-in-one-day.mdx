---
title: Two titles in one day
drafted_from: ibn-khallikan

---

On the first of Sha'ban 330 — April 942 — the caliph al-Muttaqi granted two
honorific titles to two brothers of the Banu Hamdan. The elder became Nasir
al-Dawla, champion of the empire. The younger became Sayf al-Dawla, sword of
the empire.

Ibn Khallikan records the date and then adds one sentence: *their influence then
became very great.*

## What the family had already spent

The titles were not a gift. They were a settlement of accounts.

The brothers' father, Abu'l-Hayja Abdallah ibn Hamdan, had been made governor of
Mosul and its dependencies in 904-5 and entered the city at the beginning of the
following year. He was a Taghlibi Arab of an old tribal lineage, and Ibn
Khallikan gives the genealogy back through fifteen generations, which is what one
did for a family whose claim was descent rather than office.

He died at Baghdad in March 929, killed while protecting the caliph al-Qahir.

That is the transaction underneath the two titles. A provincial governor died
defending a caliph in his own capital, and thirteen years later that caliph's
successor formally recognised the dead man's sons as the champion and the sword
of the empire — which is to say, acknowledged that the empire now depended on
them and could not pay them in anything but words.

## The division

What the brothers did with it was split the north between them and stay split.

Nasir al-Dawla took Mosul and the Jazira. Sayf al-Dawla took the Syrian side and
made Aleppo his capital, facing Byzantium. Two capitals, one family, and a
frontier of their own choosing between them.

Arrangements like that normally end in a war between cousins within a
generation. This one did not, and Ibn Khallikan preserves why in the form the
period preserved everything — a poem.

There had been a coolness between them, and Sayf al-Dawla wrote to his elder
brother:

> Though suffering from your harshness, I shall not act unkindly; neither shall I
> neglect my duty, in whatever state I may be. You are to me as a parent, and a
> parent's severity is best repaid with patience and resignation.

And on another occasion, more precisely:

> I concede to you exalted rank, although I am worthy of it... I have no aversion
> for rank, it is true; but I forego my rights, so that you may enjoy the
> plenitude of yours. I must surely hold the second place, if I consent to yield
> you up the first.

The younger, more famous, more capable brother put in writing that he was
entitled to precedence and was declining to take it. Ibn Khallikan notes
elsewhere that Nasir al-Dawla was the elder and stood higher with the caliphs,
and that the two had done most of their studies together.

A dynasty that divided its territory in two and did not fight over it for a
generation is rare enough to want an explanation. This is the only one the
sources offer, and it is a family arrangement rather than a constitutional one.

## What it could not survive

The arrangement was personal, and personal arrangements end with people.

When Sayf al-Dawla died, Ibn Khallikan writes, a great change came over his
brother, who had loved him dearly: his intellectual faculties became so weak that
his own children and servants no longer had any respect for him. In May 967 he
was arrested at Mosul by his son Abu Taghlib — with the approval of the other
brothers — and sent to a fortress, where he stayed until he died about eighteen
months later. His body was carried back to Mosul and buried on a hill east of the
city.

He had been emir over those provinces for thirty-two years.

The house that had been given two titles on one day, for a father who died
defending a caliph, ended its unity with a son imprisoning a father, and it never
had a mechanism for the succession that did not depend on brothers liking each
other.
