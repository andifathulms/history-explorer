Sayf al-Dawla died in 967 and the emirate of Aleppo lasted another thirty-seven
years without him, paying Constantinople and looking to Cairo. What ended at
once was the court.

Ibn Khallikan follows one of its members out. Al-Babbagha, "the parrot", had
been in Sayf al-Dawla's service and was one of the better poets there; on the
prince's death he travelled from one country to another, and went on doing so
for forty years, and died in 1008. That is the ordinary fate of a man whose
profession depended on a particular patron in a particular room.

The room does not reassemble. There was never another market in Aleppo where a
judge could arrive with an empty purse and leave with a thousand dinars in it.

## What did survive

The verse did, and it survived better than the dynasty by a very long way.

By Ibn Khallikan's own century, two hundred and fifty years later, the *Diwan*
of al-Mutanabbi was an industry: scholars wrote commentaries on it, other
scholars wrote commentaries on the commentaries, men are described in his
dictionary as having spent careers on the text, and poets had written elegies
on the death of a man they had never met. Al-Mutanabbi's reputation as the
greatest of the Arab poets was made at Aleppo, in a nine-year quarrel with a
patron he could not flatter honestly and would not leave quietly.

The anthology of ten thousand verses in praise of Sayf al-Dawla went on being
read too. So did the prince's own poems — with one complication that says
everything about what a literary afterlife is worth.

## Whose lines are these

Ibn Khallikan gives Sayf al-Dawla's poem about the Greek slave girl, the one
ending with the observation that absence is sometimes caused by the fear of
absence. Then he adds, with the flatness of a man who has checked: *I have seen
these identical verses in the collected poetical works of Abu al-Muhsin
al-Suri, and am unable to decide which of the two was the author of them.*

The same doubt hangs over the description of the rainbow, which some attribute
to a different poet, and which al-Tha'alibi insists is the prince's.

So the most durable thing the Hamdanids produced is a body of poetry, and some
of the pieces most confidently attributed to the greatest of them may not be
his. The court that was assembled to make a name permanent succeeded, and the
name that became permanent is his poet's.

## The frontier, and after

The emirate was finished by the Fatimids and their allies in 1004; the family's
Mosul branch had gone a generation earlier.

The war they had made their reputation on outlasted them by less than a
century. After Manzikert in 1071 the Byzantine frontier was in Anatolia and not
in Cilicia, and the annual raiding across the Taurus that had given Sayf
al-Dawla his title and his poets their subject simply stopped being a thing
that happened.

What is left is a brick of campaign dust under a dead man's head in
Mayyafariqin, and several thousand lines that are still set for examination.
