Sayf al-Dawla's court is famous for its poet. It also held, in the last years of
his life, the most important philosopher in the Islamic world.

Al-Farabi came to Aleppo around 942 and died in Damascus in 950, an old man,
after a career spent mostly in Baghdad. What he had done there is the reason
Arabic philosophy exists as a tradition rather than as a set of translations.
This site's Abbasid page describes two centuries of Greek science and philosophy
being put into Arabic on caliphal money; al-Farabi is the generation that
started arguing with it.

He wrote commentaries on Aristotle so thorough that the tradition afterwards
called him *al-Mu'allim al-Thani*, the Second Teacher — Aristotle being the
first. He wrote on logic, on music with a treatise still cited by
ethnomusicologists, and on political philosophy: *The Principles of the Views
of the Inhabitants of the Virtuous City*, which takes Plato's question about
the best-ordered state and works it through in the vocabulary of a religion
Plato had never heard of, arguing that the ideal ruler is one whose intellect
has been perfected and who therefore governs by knowledge rather than by force.

He was writing that at the court of a man losing a war.

Ibn Sina, on this site's Ma'munid and Kakuyid pages, said he had read
Aristotle's *Metaphysics* forty times without understanding it until he came
across al-Farabi's commentary in a Bukhara bookstall. Ibn Rushd, on the Almohad
page, worked from him. The line runs from Baghdad through Aleppo to Bukhara and
Isfahan and then to Cordoba and out into Latin Europe, and this page is one of
its stations.

The chapter before this one argues that a minor dynasty bought a permanent
literary statement. This one is the same observation about philosophy and it
sharpens the point the site's rating panel cannot make: for a few years in the
940s, in a small emirate on a losing frontier, the best poet and the best
philosopher writing in Arabic were being paid by the same man.
