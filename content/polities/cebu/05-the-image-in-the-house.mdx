Forty-four years passed before another Spanish fleet returned to Cebu.
Miguel Lopez de Legazpi, sent from New Spain to find a westward return
route and settle the islands in earnest, anchored there in 1565 and was
at first, Morga records, "well and peacefully received by the natives and
by their chief Tupas" - a ruler the sources never connect by name to
Humabon, whether as heir, rival, or stranger to that older alliance.
Peace did not last: the Spanish took provisions from the islanders
without, it seems, the islanders' consent, and Tupas took up arms. It
went no better for Cebu the second time than the first had gone for
Lapulapu's rivals: the Spanish prevailed, and neighboring islands, seeing
the outcome, came in peacefully rather than test it themselves.

## A miracle already forty years old

In one of the captured houses, Legazpi's men found a carved image of the
Christ child that the islanders had kept and revered, crediting it with
miraculous help in their own troubles. Both sides came to believe, Morga
writes, that it was the very image Magellan's fleet had left there in
1521 - the same small figure the queen once called Johanna had asked to
keep in place of her own idols, still standing in a native house after
four and a half decades of a history the object itself could not tell.
The Spanish placed it in a newly built monastery of St. Augustine and
founded, on that same port, the first permanent Spanish settlement in the
islands: the City of the Most Holy Name of Jesus, named for the image
found within it.

## An alliance history did not get to keep

What Cebu's own first raja had sealed in blood, and lost within two weeks
to a chief who refused it and a slave who avenged himself through it, a
second Spanish fleet under a different commander finally made permanent
by force - not restoring an old friendship so much as replacing the
memory of one with a colonial town built, quite literally, on top of it.
