In 961 Ashot III moved the capital to Ani, a triangular promontory in a bend of
the Akhurian gorge with cliffs on two sides, and for the following eighty years
his family built on it.

<Figure id="ani-general" />

The double walls of Smbat II still stand. So do parts of the cathedral, finished
around 1001 by an architect named Trdat — who is one of the few named architects
of the period anywhere, and who had shortly before been called to Constantinople
to rebuild the dome of Hagia Sophia after the earthquake of 989. The church of
St Gregory built by Gagik I was a rotunda modelled on the seventh-century
cathedral at Zvartnots; the church of the Holy Redeemer held a fragment of the
True Cross; the church of St Gregory of Tigran Honents has fresco cycles that
are among the best surviving Armenian painting.

<Figure id="ani-cathedral" />

<Figure id="honents-fresco" />

The medieval Armenian sources call it the city of a thousand and one churches,
which is a formula and not a count. What is not a formula is the scale: Ani in
the early eleventh century was one of the larger cities in the world, comparable
to Constantinople's rivals rather than to a regional capital, sitting on the
trade between the caliphate, Byzantium, the Caucasus and the steppe, with a
population that included Armenians, Greeks, Jews, Muslims and Kurds.

It is also, and this belongs on the page, a ruin standing on a closed border.
The Akhurian is the frontier between Turkey and Armenia, and it has been shut
since 1993; the city can be walked through from the Turkish side and only
looked at from the other one.

The building is what the kingdom spent its hundred and sixty years on, and it is
the answer to what the kingdom was for. Political weight and cultural output
come apart all the time; Ani is the version where the output is architecture and
the weight was never there at all. The kingdom that built this could not raise
an army capable of defending it, and had been divided among cousins for most of
the period the cathedral was going up.
