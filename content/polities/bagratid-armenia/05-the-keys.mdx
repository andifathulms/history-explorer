---
title: The keys
drafted_from: aristakes-bedrosian
phase: contraction
---

Ani was taken in 1045, and the Armenian who wrote the account had been alive for
it. His first sentence about it is the verdict: the city was taken not through
warfare but through treachery.

The Byzantine approach was an oath. The emperor's people persuaded the young king
Gagik, swearing on the Cross, that the emperor needed only to see him — after
which the kingdom would be returned and a document written giving him his land
and his city in perpetual inheritance. Aristakes cannot decide why Gagik believed
it, and offers three possibilities without choosing: the oaths and the Cross, or
immaturity, or a timid nature. What he supplies instead are two proverbs, which
is what a man reaches for when he cannot explain something. The wise man swears
and the fool believes. The words of liars are as succulent as cheese, and fools
gulp them down.

Before going, Gagik gave the keys of the city to the catholicos Petros, and
entrusted him with the affairs of the land under great conditions and oaths. He
did not listen to Vahram and the nobles who had put him on the throne. He
listened to the party advising him to go, and went — in Aristakes' phrase, like a
fish caught on the line, or a bird in a trap.

The emperor, on seeing him, forgot the oath and the Cross, kept him, and proposed
a trade: give me Ani and take Melitene and the districts round it. Gagik refused,
and went on refusing.

What broke was everything underneath him. Grigor, son of Vasak — a man Aristakes
plainly admired, sagacious and without equal in theology — worked out that Gagik
would never be let home, went to the emperor himself, and handed over the keys of
Bjni and the whole of his patrimonial inheritance; he was made a magister and
given cities and villages on the Mesopotamian frontier, in writing, under a
golden seal, to hold from generation to generation until the end of time. The
leading citizens of Ani, seeing the king detained, began considering which
neighbouring ruler to hand the city to — a prince of Dvin, or the king of
Abkhazia. And the catholicos, understanding that the city was going to be given
to somebody, wrote to the Byzantine official at Samosata with a question: find
out what the emperor will give us in return if I surrender the city and the other
strongholds of this land.

The emperor, Aristakes records without comment and with complete precision,
satisfied him with treasures and authority. And thus did they rule Ani, and the
entire land.

He then sets the two settlements side by side, which is the cruellest thing in
the chapter. The emperor, out of compassion, gave Gagik the place he asked for,
which was greatly inferior in value to Ani or to the lands he had lost. The
catholicos, from whom they had taken the city, they recompensed with thanks and
rewards.

The administration that followed did what such administrations do. The first
Byzantine lieutenant honoured the patriarch extravagantly, subdued the country,
and marched on Dvin, where a great many men were killed at the gate — including
Vahram, the great prince of Armenia, and his son, which Aristakes says caused the
Armenians great mourning. His successor did not honour the patriarch at all,
wrote accusing letters about him to the emperor, and got him out of the city with
a fabricated order to go and live at Karin.

Nineteen years later the Seljuks came and took Ani by storm, and Aristakes wrote
that chapter too. But the kingdom was not there to lose by then. It had been
handed over, in pieces, by the men holding the keys, each of whom had asked first
what he would be given.
