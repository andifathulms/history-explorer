---
title: A crown from Baghdad
drafted_from: hovannisian-1997
phase: formation
---

The Armenian kingdom of the Arsacids ended in 428, partitioned between Rome and
Persia. What followed was four centuries in which Armenia was a province of
somebody: Sasanian, then Byzantine, then, from the 640s, of the caliphate, which
governed it through a resident governor at Dvin and through the Armenian noble
houses it found already in place.

Those houses are the continuity. The *nakharars* were hereditary lords holding
their own districts with their own fortresses and their own cavalry, and they
had been there under the Sasanians and were still there under the Abbasids. One
of them, the Bagratuni, made themselves the caliphate's principal intermediary —
collecting the tribute, holding the title of prince of Armenia, and being
useful.

In 884, with Abbasid authority collapsing in every province at once, the caliph
al-Mu'tamid sent Ashot Bagratuni a crown. Constantinople, not wanting to be
outbid, sent another.

That is the founding act, and it is worth pausing on how ordinary it was at the
time and how odd it looks now. An Armenian Apostolic Christian king, whose
church had rejected
Chalcedon and was therefore heretical to Constantinople, took his crown from a
Muslim caliph in Baghdad, who was content to have a reliable Christian tributary
on the Byzantine frontier rather than a province he could no longer garrison.

The kingdom that resulted was less a state than a settlement: the Bagratuni as
first among the noble houses, with a title from outside that none of the others
could match.
