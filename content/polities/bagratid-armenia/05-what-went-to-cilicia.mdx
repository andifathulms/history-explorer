title: What went to Cilicia

Alp Arslan took Ani in 1064 and the city was sacked. Descriptions of it are in
the Armenian and Arabic sources and are not gentle. The Seljuks held it, then
granted it to a Kurdish dynasty this site now carries on the Shaddadid record,
and Ani spent the rest of its life as a provincial town changing hands until an
earthquake and the Timurid period finished it.

What moved was people. Armenian populations had been moving west into Cappadocia
since Basil's annexations, in organised transfers that the emperors intended and
paid for; after 1064 the movement was flight rather than policy. The Armenian
nobility that had held the highland districts dispersed, and a substantial part
of it ended up in the Taurus mountains and on the Cilician plain, in country
Byzantium held loosely and would shortly hold not at all.

That is where the Rubenids appear — an Armenian noble family connected to the
last Bagratid court, established in the Taurus in the 1080s, whose descendants
built the kingdom this site carries in its Anatolia region as Cilician Armenia
and which lasted until 1375. This record therefore has an edge into that one:
the polity absorbed the remnants of this one, in people and in claim.

It is worth registering what that means for the shape of Armenian history as
this corpus can show it. The Bagratid kingdom ends in 1045 and the Cilician
kingdom begins in 1080, and they are in different regions on this site because
they were in different countries — one on the Armenian highland, one on the
Mediterranean coast three hundred miles away, with a Seljuk sultanate between
them. An Armenian state existed continuously from 884 to 1375, and it did so by
moving.

The highland did not get another one. Ani passed among Shaddadids, Georgians,
Mongols and Turkmen; the Armenian church kept its seat there and then moved that
too. The next Armenian state on the highland is in 1918.
