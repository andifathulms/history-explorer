Alp Arslan took Ani in 1064. Three centuries later, when Hamdallah Mustawfi
compiled the standard Persian geography of the Ilkhanid world, he had occasion
to mention it, and what he wrote is this: Ani, the older capital of Christian
Armenia, is a town in the mountains where much fruit is grown.

That is the whole entry.

## What is not in the entry

The double walls of Smbat II. The cathedral finished around 1001 by the
architect Trdat, who had been called to Constantinople to rebuild the dome of
Hagia Sophia after the earthquake of 989. The rotunda of St Gregory modelled on
Zvartnots. The frescoes of Tigran Honents. A city that the Armenian sources
called the city of a thousand and one churches, on the trade between the
caliphate, Byzantium, the Caucasus and the steppe, with Armenians, Greeks, Jews,
Muslims and Kurds living in it.

None of that is wrong in Mustawfi's account. It is simply not what the place was
any more, and he was writing a gazetteer rather than an elegy. Ani had passed
from the Seljuks to the Shaddadids to the Georgians to the Mongols; an
earthquake did the rest; and by the fourteenth century it was a place in the
hills with orchards, worth one line in a list.

## Where the rest of Armenia went, in the same geography

Read on in the same book and the country is all there, under other names, doing
other things.

Dvin — Dabil to the Arabs, the seat of the caliphate's governor and the older
capital — is a walled town with three gates, larger than Ardabil, where a Friday
mosque and a church stand side by side. Muqaddasi says the population was Kurdish
and that the Christians had the upper hand. Its woollens, dyed red with the
kermes insect, were famous.

Ararat stands over it across the Araxes, and the Muslims called it Jabal
al-Harith, the mountain of the ploughman, because they located Noah's ark
elsewhere, on Jabal Judi in upper Mesopotamia. Istakhri says both summits were
always under snow and neither could be climbed. The people of Dvin cut firewood
on the slopes and hunted there, and Muqaddasi reckoned a thousand hamlets lay
among the spurs of it.

On Lake Van they caught a herring called *tirrikh* in enormous numbers, salted
it, and sold it as far as Mesopotamia and Khurasan — Yaqut says he bought some
of this Armenian salt fish in Balkh, two thousand kilometres away.

## The shape of the afterlife

Put those together and the finding is not a melancholy one, or not only.

The kingdom lasted a hundred and sixty-one years and its capital lasted rather
less. The country went on for a thousand: farming, dyeing wool, salting fish,
cutting wood on Ararat, worshipping in the same church, under Byzantine and
Seljuk and Mongol and Turkmen and Ottoman and Persian government in turn, with
the Christians having the upper hand in Dvin under a Muslim governor because
that is who lived there.

Ani is the ruin and the country is the fruit trees. Both entries are accurate.
