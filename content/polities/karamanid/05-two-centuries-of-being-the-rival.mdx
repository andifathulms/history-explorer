---
title: Two centuries of being the rival
drafted_from: finkel-2005
phase: contraction
---

From about 1330 to 1487 the Karamanids were the other power in Anatolia, and
the record of it is a list of the enemies of the Ottomans.

They allied with the Mamluks, whose sultans regarded Anatolia as their northern
frontier and the Ottomans as an upstart. They allied with Venice, which wanted
anything that slowed the Ottoman advance and could supply money and ships at
the Mediterranean ports the beylik held. They welcomed Timur, and after Ankara
in 1402 got their territory back from him and took and burned Bursa, the
Ottoman capital, while the Ottoman princes fought each other. They supported
the Crusade of Varna in the 1440s by attacking Ankara and Kütahya while the
Ottoman army was in the Balkans. When Mehmed II was besieging Constantinople,
the possibility that Karaman would move behind him was one of the things he had
to price.

None of it worked, and the reason is instructive. The Karamanids could never
convert an opportunity into a permanent gain, because their strength was
defensive — the Taurus, from which they could not be dislodged, and onto the
plateau, where they could not stay. The Ottomans by contrast had Rumelia. Balkan revenue and Balkan manpower on the
far side of a strait meant that losing Anatolia cost them a province; losing
Anatolia cost Karaman everything, because Anatolia was all there was.

None of that changed between 1330 and 1487, and the beylik kept making the same
bet anyway — that if enough outside powers pressed the Ottomans at once, the
plateau would come loose. Timur came closest to proving it right, and even
Timur only bought them twenty years.
