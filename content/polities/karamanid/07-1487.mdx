Mehmed II annexed the beylik in 1468 and it did not stay annexed.

Rival Karamanids reappeared with Akkoyunlu backing and then with Mamluk
backing, held parts of the mountains, and had to be dealt with again. Bayezid II
finished it in 1487 — not by winning a decisive battle but because by then
Akkoyunlu power had collapsed, Mamluk power was in decline, and there was nobody
left willing to supply a Karamanid claimant.

That is how these things actually end. A dynasty in mountains is not destroyed;
it is starved of patrons.

## What was done with them

The family was resettled across the Ottoman provinces, which was the standard
treatment and was not a punishment so much as a filing system. A displaced ruling
house given estates four hundred miles from its own following stops being a
political problem inside a generation.

The territory became the province of Karaman, which is the beylik's name attached
to Ottoman administrative geography, and it kept that name for the rest of the
empire's existence.

## Nineteen years

Between the fall of Karaman in 1487 and the Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517
there is a shift worth marking.

Taking Constantinople in 1453 had been holy war and could be presented as such
anywhere. Taking Karaman was not. It was the annexation of the leading Muslim
Turkish principality in Anatolia by another Muslim Turkish principality, against
a dynasty with a better claim to the Seljuk inheritance and a longer pedigree on
the plateau, and it needed six campaigns and two annexations across a hundred and
fifty years because it was politically difficult in a way that fighting
Christians never was.

The Ottomans solved the problem of legitimacy afterwards rather than before, and
they solved it by going elsewhere for it: to Cairo for the caliphal succession,
and to Constantinople for the Roman one. Neither answer had anything to do with
Anatolia, which is where the argument had actually been.
