In 1432 Philip the Good of Burgundy sent his first esquire-carver east in the
guise of a pilgrim, with instructions to come home overland and report whether a
crusading army could be got through. Bertrandon de la Broquière rode from
Antioch across Anatolia to Bursa and on to the Danube, and the notes he brought
back are a quartermaster's: which lord holds what, how many men he can raise,
who hates whom, and whether that hatred could be used.

He spent sixteen days crossing Karaman, and what he wrote about it is the
closest thing to a survey that any outsider made of the place.

## The prince

The ruler was Ibrahim II — Broquière renders the name *Imbreymbas* and explains
that he is called Karaman only because he is lord of the country. He saw him
ride through the town on a Friday on his way to prayers, with about fifty
horsemen, most of them his slaves, and thirty foot around him, a sword in his
belt and a drum at his saddle-bow. He was about thirty-two, handsome, well
obeyed, and married to a sister of Murad II.

Broquière's Christian informants in the country told him the rest. That the
prince was cruel, and few days passed without noses, feet or hands being cut
off; that a rich man could be condemned to death so his fortune could be taken,
and most of the nobility had gone that way; that eight days before Broquière
arrived one had been torn to pieces by dogs, and two days after that the prince
had had one of his own wives killed — the mother of his eldest son, who did not
yet know. This is hostile testimony collected from a man who had been forced to
renounce his religion to save his life, and it should be read as what the
country's foreign residents said about their ruler, which is a real fact of a
different kind.

Of the country itself: its territories began a day's journey short of Tarsus and
ran sixteen days' ride to the frontier of the Ottoman lands, reaching north-east
as far as the borders of Persia; it held the coast from Tarsus toward Cyprus,
which produced the most expert sailors known, though they had revolted against
him. And the inhabitants, Broquière concludes with the traveller's usual
generosity, were thieves, cheats and great assassins who killed one another
without anyone being arrested for it.

## The finding

The point of the mission was the last part, and it is precise.

Although Ibrahim was allied to the Ottoman sultan by marriage, he detested him
for having taken a portion of Karaman from him. He was afraid to make war,
because the sultan was stronger. But Broquière was persuaded that if he saw the
sultan successfully attacked by Europeans, he would not leave him in peace.

Broquière had also been told how Ibrahim had taken Ramedan and then given it up.
He had a brother whom he had banished, and who had found asylum at a neighbouring
court. That court did not dare declare war on him, but let it be understood that
unless Ramedan was handed over, the brother would be sent back with troops to
take it. Ibrahim handed it over without hesitation. His informant's verdict —
that the prince was weak and cowardly although his people were the bravest in
all Turkey — is a courtier's spite, but the mechanism it describes is not. A
banished brother in a neighbour's keeping was a standing instrument that could
be used against the man on the throne without a shot being fired, and everyone
in Anatolia knew it.

That is the whole Karamanid position in one page of a spy's notebook: the
largest principality left, a frontier sixteen days across, a marriage into the
dynasty it hated, a brother in somebody else's hands, and a ruler waiting for
someone else to strike the first blow. The crusade Broquière was scouting for
never came. Fifty-five years later the Ottomans finished the job themselves.
