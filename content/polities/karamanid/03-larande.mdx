In about 1332 Ibn Battuta came into Konya and found it large and handsome,
abounding in water and gardens, and belonging to Badr al-Din ibn Karaman —
though the king of Iraq, he adds, has occasionally had the rule of it, on
account of the nearness of some of his states in those parts.

That single qualification is the beylik's whole position in one clause. The
Karamanids held the old Seljuk capital, and everybody understood that they held
it at the sufferance of somebody stronger who could take it back when it suited
him, and had.

## The capital that was not Konya

Ibn Battuta then went on to Larande, and records something the chronicles tend
to skip:

> I next proceeded to the city of Larande, the Sultan of which is El Malik Badr
> al-Din ibn Karaman, who makes this place his capital. I met him, and was
> entertained with the greatest kindness as his guest.

Konya was the prize and Larande was where the ruler actually lived. It sits at
the foot of the Taurus, with the mountains at its back and the plateau in front
of it, which is precisely the position the dynasty occupied for two centuries: a
seat you cannot be forced out of, within a day's ride of the seat that confers
legitimacy.

Every Karamanid campaign is a variation on moving between those two places.

## What he found in the city

At Konya he lodged with the qadi, a member of the brotherhood of youths, in a
cell he calls most beautiful, with a great many disciples who traced the
authority for their legal opinions back to Ali and who dressed as Sufis did, in
the patched cloak and close trousers.

And he went to the tomb of the Shaykh Jalal al-Din, *better known by the title
of Mawlana*, who was, he says, very highly esteemed.

That is the Karamanid claim from the outside, seen by a visitor with no stake in
it. A learned judge running a house of law and hospitality; a shrine that people
came a long way to visit; a prince who kept an open table forty miles off in the
hills. It is a small state doing exactly what a large one does, and doing it in
the room the Seljuks had built.
