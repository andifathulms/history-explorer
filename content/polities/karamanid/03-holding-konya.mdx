---
title: What holding Konya was worth
drafted_from: peacock-yildiz-2013
phase: aside
---

The Karamanids took Konya in 1277 and lost it within weeks, took it again in
the early fourteenth century, were driven out by Ilkhanid governors, and took
it again — and from the 1320s held it, on and off, for most of the following
century and a half. This chapter is about why they kept coming back for a city
that was hard to hold and sat on open ground they could not defend.

Part of the answer is the obvious one. Konya had been the Seljuk capital, and a
dynasty claiming the inheritance of Rum needed to be sitting in the place the
inheritance was administered from. A claim asserted from Larende in the
mountains is a claim by a hill lord; the same claim made from the Seljuk palace
is something else.

The other part is the tomb. Jalal al-Din Rumi died at Konya in 1273 and was
buried there, and this site's Sultanate of Rum page describes what he was: the
most widely read Persian poet who ever lived, working in a Turkish sultanate in
a Greek-speaking country. Within a generation of his death the order that
formed around his memory, the Mevlevis, was an institution with a lodge, an
endowment and a hereditary leadership descended from him — and the Karamanids
became its patrons. They endowed the lodge, built at the shrine, and married
into the family.

That is a specific kind of authority and it is not the kind the site's
influence counts measure. A Turkmen dynasty with a thin genealogy and a
contested claim acquired standing by attaching itself to the burial place of a
saint whose following was already international — Persians, Turks, Greeks and
Armenians came to Konya — and by being the house that maintained it.

The Ottomans understood exactly this. When they finally took Karaman they took
the shrine with it, continued the endowments, and the Mevlevi order became one
of the institutional fixtures of the Ottoman empire for four hundred years,
with sultans girded with a sword at a Mevlevi ceremony on accession.

So the object the Karamanids fought for two centuries to hold turned out to
transfer intact to the people who beat them, which is the ordinary fate of
things in this corpus and is the reason the beylik's own successor-claims count
is a gap. Nobody claimed the Karamanids. They claimed Rum, held its capital and
its saint, and the Ottomans took the lot and claimed Rome instead.
