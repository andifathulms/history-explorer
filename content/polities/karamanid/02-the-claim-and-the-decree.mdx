---
title: The claim, and the decree that may not have happened
drafted_from: peacock-yildiz-2013
phase: peak
---

In 1277 Mehmed Bey of Karaman marched onto the plateau and took Konya.

The moment was chosen. The Sultanate of Rum had been an Ilkhanid tributary
since Kösedağ, its sultans installed and removed by Mongol governors, and a
Mamluk army under Baybars had just come north and beaten the Mongols at Elbistan
— which this site's Mamluk page describes as the sultanate's expansive decade.
Mehmed Bey took the Seljuk capital and put on the throne a man presented as a
son of the Seljuk house, remembered in the sources by the nickname Cimri, the
miser.

It lasted weeks. Mongol and Seljuk forces retook Konya, Cimri was killed and
displayed, and Mehmed Bey was executed in 1278.

The claim outlived him by two hundred years. The Karamanids held that they, not
anybody else, were the heirs of Rum — they had taken its capital, they held its
heartland, and they were the senior Turkmen house on the plateau. That is the
argument the Ottomans spent the fifteenth century having to answer, and it is
why the Ottoman conquest of Karaman was politically harder than the conquest of
Constantinople: taking a Christian empire was holy war, and taking the leading
Muslim Turkish principality in Anatolia was not.

The other famous thing about that year is probably not true, and it is worth
the paragraph.

It is widely stated that in 1277 Mehmed Bey decreed that Turkish alone would be
used in the divan, the court and the chancery — the first official promotion of
Turkish over Persian, and a foundation stone of modern Turkish national
history. The evidence is a passage in Ibn Bibi, a Persian-writing historian of
the Seljuk court with reason to present the Karamanids as barbarians, and there
is no independent corroboration that the Karamanids ever conducted official
business in Turkish. Their own surviving inscriptions and documents are largely
in Persian and Arabic.

This site records the taking of Konya as a turning point and does not record
the decree, because the decree is a claim resting on one hostile witness. It is
the same standard the corpus applies to the Nizari legend and to the Somnath
tradition on the Ghaznavid page: a story that is repeated everywhere is not
thereby attested.
