In 1890 W. M. Ramsay, working through the ancient place-names of Anatolia
against the modern ones, came to the town at the foot of the Taurus and wrote
down one sentence:

> Laranda is still called Laranda by the Christian population, as well as
> Karaman, which is the official and usual name.

Four hundred years after the dynasty was extinguished, the town it had made its
capital was officially named after it — and the Greek-speaking Christians of the
place were still using the name it had carried under Rome, Byzantium and the
Seljuks.

## Two afterlives, running at once

That sentence contains both of them.

The **official** one is that the name won. A Turkmen family that was beaten,
annexed twice and scattered across the Ottoman provinces left its name on a
province, a town and a mountain district, and it is still there: the modern
Turkish city is Karaman and the province is Karaman. Nothing else about the
beylik survived — no dynasty, no institution, no claim anybody pressed — and the
name outlasted the empire that erased the state.

The **local** one is that the older name did not go away. Ramsay does not say
much about the people who kept it — only that they were the Christians of the
place, and that Laranda was what they called it. They were the descendants of the
population the Karamanids had governed, and they had gone on using the town's
pre-Turkish name for six hundred years, alongside the official one, without
anybody recording that they were doing it until a Scottish geographer walked
through and noted it in passing.

Thirty-three years after he wrote that sentence, the population exchange of 1923
moved the Orthodox Christians of Anatolia to Greece. The second name stopped
being said in the place it belonged to, and Ramsay's aside is one of the last
times anybody wrote it down as a living usage.

## The shrine

The other inheritance transferred whole and is still working.

The Karamanids had made themselves patrons of the Mevlevi lodge at Konya,
endowed it, built at it and married into the family that ran it — which was the
best available substitute for a pedigree. When the Ottomans took Karaman they
took the shrine, continued the endowments, and made the order one of the
institutional fixtures of the empire for four hundred years, to the point where
sultans were girded with a sword at a Mevlevi ceremony on their accession.

So the object the beylik had fought two centuries to hold went on doing exactly
what it had been held for, in the hands of the people who took it.

There is a lesson in that about what a small state's inheritance actually is. The
Karamanids' claim to be the heirs of Rum died with them and convinced nobody. The
tomb they had endowed, the town they had named and the mountains they had held
are all still there, and two of the three are still called after them.
