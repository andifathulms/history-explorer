Home from Futuna, Kau-ulu-fonua called his brothers together and drew
the lesson from his own father's death. "I am the chief," he told
them, "but this people have dared to slay the Tui Tonga. What will
they not dare? And how shall the land stand fast if the chief be
slain? Now therefore it is my mind to set a chief over the people to
govern them, and I will be supreme lord of the soil only, and of the
offerings." He made his brother Mounga-motua lord over the people in
his place, sent him to govern from the peninsula, and gave him the
title Tui Ha'atakalaua.

It held. From that day forward, whatever wars broke out between
rival chiefs in Tonga, the Tui Tonga himself passed through them
unharmed - lord of the soil and of the tribute, but no longer within
reach of the grievances a civil ruler collects. The killing that had
opened Kau-ulu-fonua's own reign never repeated itself against a
sacred Tui Tonga again.

## A dignity built on the same idea twice over

The system this created governed Tonga at the height of its reach.
The Tui Tonga held divine honours by virtue of an origin no one
living could fully explain, hedged by tabu so strict that his own
wife was taken from him after bearing him two children, lest there be
more than one person alive of such transcendent rank at a time. His
son inherited the title; his daughter outranked him - she was the Tui
Tonga fefine, the female Tui Tonga, to whom even her own father owed
the moemoe, the gesture of touching his forehead to her foot. Her own
daughter in turn became the Tamaha, the highest earthly dignity of
all, before whom neither mother nor grandfather could so much as eat.

The Tui Ha'atakalaua held none of that inherited sanctity, but he
held everything the sanctity could no longer safely carry: the
ordering of tribute, the burden of civil government, and, in time,
the executive authority that the Tui Tonga's own tabu made it
dangerous for him to exercise himself. Two chiefs now stood where one
had stood before - one who could not be touched by the politics of
the islands he nominally owned, and one who governed those islands in
his name and, increasingly, in his own.
