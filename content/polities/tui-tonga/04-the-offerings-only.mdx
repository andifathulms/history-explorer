The arrangement Kau-ulu-fonua built to protect one Tui Tonga
eventually finished dividing the power that had been his. Generations
after Mounga-motua took the title Tui Ha'atakalaua, one of his own
successors, Moungatonga, married a Samoan chief woman named Tohu'ia
and had by her a son, Ngata. Moungatonga saw, as his own ancestor once
had, that the people no longer honoured the chief they obeyed so much
as the chief to whom they gave their offerings - and he judged that a
second delegation would settle the imbalance a second time. He made
Ngata lord over the people in his own place, gave him the title Tui
Kanokubolu, and kept for himself only the offerings, exactly as
Kau-ulu-fonua had once kept only the offerings for the Tui Tonga.

Working back from a king-list that runs unbroken to the reign of
Tabuoji, the Tui Tonga who entertained Tasman's ships in 1643, the
tradition places Ngata's assumption of the new title at about 1610.
By then Tonga was governed, at least in principle, by three chiefs at
once: a Tui Tonga who held the land, the gods and the tribute; a Tui
Ha'atakalaua who had inherited the temporal authority Kau-ulu-fonua
first delegated away; and now a Tui Kanokubolu who held the temporal
authority the Tui Ha'atakalaua's own line had, in turn, found it
safer to hand off. Every later ruling chief of Tonga - down to the
line that produced King George Tupou, whom this same tradition still
remembered generations afterward - descended from Ngata rather than
from either of the two older titles.

## What the sacred office kept

None of this touched the Tui Tonga's own dignity. The office continued
to be filled, generation after generation, by men whose birth alone
qualified them, and continued to receive tribute and homage long after
Ngata's line had become the effective government of the islands.
Captain Cook, arriving in 1777, at first mistook a forceful island
chief for the king of Tonga, and was corrected only when he watched
that same chief perform the moemoe before a frail, elderly Tui Tonga
named Pau. The gods, the soil and the offerings were still the Tui
Tonga's alone. Everything else - the men, the wars, the government of
a network of islands his ancestor Kau-ulu-fonua had once run down to
its last hiding place in Futuna - belonged, by 1610, to lines the
sacred office itself had chosen to create and could no longer call
back.
