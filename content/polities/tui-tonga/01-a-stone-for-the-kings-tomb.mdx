For generations before this story can be dated with any confidence,
one title stood over the whole of Tonga: the Tui Tonga, lord of the
soil, the men and the first-fruits, tribute-taker from Ha'apai,
Vava'u, the Niuas, 'Uvea, Futuna and even distant Samoa. No man could
say where the power had come from, unless the Tui Tonga were truly
descended from the gods themselves - and no other chief in the islands
held so much as a fraction of it unchallenged.

Power of that size, unchecked by any rival, wore the land down.
Takalaua, a Tui Tonga remembered as strong and harsh, kept his
fishermen working the bay out of Mu'a whatever the weather, and when
they came home empty-handed he had the chief among them bound and
readied for the knife before a promise of a beautiful woman bought
back his life. Worse followed. Wanting a tomb greater than any of his
fathers', Takalaua ordered the people of Belehake to drag a huge stone
from the reef in the heart of the planting season, and when they
begged to be allowed to sow their yams first and haul the stone after,
his answer was that they were idle and had dishonoured him. They
hauled anyway, through a day so long and so thirsty that by evening
they were licking mud from a dry waterhole and cursing their king
where he could not hear them.

## Killed on the path to his bath

He did not need to hear them. That night two old men of Belehake,
Tamajia and Malofafa, decided the matter for the rest. In the morning
they met Takalaua on the path as he went down to bathe, killed him
there, and cut the body into pieces so that none would know it, hiding
the limbs in a tree in the grove above Mu'a. For the first time within
living memory of the tradition, a Tui Tonga had died at his own
people's hands rather than the gods'.

Word reached Takalaua's sons where they were racing toy canoes at
Niutao. Of his several children, raised apart from their mother and
from each other by a father's decree that no child of his should know
his face, only the youngest, Lo-tawai, had ever been allowed near him
- and it was Lo-tawai alone who could say, when the body was found
hanging in its basket in the sacred grove, "This is our father: I know
the face for his face." The eldest, Kau-ulu-fonua, broke the mast of a
canoe into pieces and handed one to each of his brothers and his
sister as a summons to war, and led them out to bury their father and
call the people to arms against his killers.
