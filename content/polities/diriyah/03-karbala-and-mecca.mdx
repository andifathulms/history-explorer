---
title: Karbala and Mecca

By the 1780s the emirate held most of the Najd. Riyadh fell in 1773. Al-Hasa,
the eastern oasis province on the Gulf — Shi'i in a substantial part of its
population, and this site's Qarmatian record two entries back — was taken in
1795. Then the emirate moved outwards in both directions at once.

In April 1802 a force from the Najd reached Karbala in Ottoman Iraq, took the
city, killed a large number of its people and demolished the tomb of Husayn ibn
Ali — the grandson of the Prophet, killed there in 680, and the central shrine
of Shi'i Islam. The treasury of the shrine was carried off.

The consequences of that single day are still running. It made the doctrine a
matter the Ottoman government could not ignore, since a state that claims to
protect the Muslim world cannot allow one of its shrine cities to be sacked by
its own nominal subjects. And it established a hostility between this movement
and Shi'i Islam that two centuries have done nothing to soften; every subsequent
episode in that quarrel, down to the present, is argued about with 1802 in it.

The following year they took Mecca, and in 1805 Medina. The tombs in the Baqi
cemetery at Medina were levelled; the pilgrimage caravans from Egypt and Syria
were refused entry on the grounds that those who came with them were not
Muslims; the sharif was displaced, which this site's Sharifate of Mecca record
carries from the other side as the first time in eight centuries that the holy
cities had been held by a power which regarded the whole sharifian arrangement
as illegitimate.

That is the moment the emirate's trajectory becomes fatal rather than
impressive. The Ottoman sultan's most valuable title was servant of the two
noble sanctuaries, which this site's Ottoman and Mamluk pages both record as the
half of the 1517 inheritance Istanbul actually put at the centre of its
self-description. A sovereign who claims that title and does not hold the
sanctuaries is making an empty claim in public, annually, in front of the whole
Muslim world.

Istanbul could not answer it with its own army, which was in no condition to
campaign in Arabia. It gave the commission to somebody who could.
