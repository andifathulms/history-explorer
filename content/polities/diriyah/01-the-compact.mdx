title: The compact

Diriyah in the 1740s was an oasis settlement in the Najd, the central Arabian
plateau: date gardens, a few hundred households, a ruling family called the Al
Saud, and no particular importance. Central Arabia at that date had no state, no
external power governing it and very little that the outside world wanted; the
Ottomans held the Hejaz and the eastern coast and had no interest in the
interior.

Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab was a scholar from Uyayna, the next settlement up the
wadi, who had studied in Medina and Basra and returned preaching a doctrine his
own community eventually found intolerable. He was expelled. In 1744 he arrived
at Diriyah, and he and Muhammad ibn Saud made an agreement.

The terms, as the tradition records them, are the founding document of every
Saudi state since. The amir would defend the doctrine and enforce it; the shaykh
would supply and interpret it; the amir would not be deprived of his authority
and the shaykh would not be asked to give up his teaching. Political power to
the Al Saud, religious power to the Al al-Shaykh — two families, two functions,
one project.

It is a small event with a very long tail. This site records it as a turning
point because the division of authority it created outlasted the emirate that
made it, was reconstituted with the second Saudi state, reconstituted again with
the third, and is still the constitutional arrangement of Saudi Arabia — where
the descendants of Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab have held the senior religious
offices for the better part of three centuries.

What the compact gave Diriyah immediately was a claim. An oasis amir who fights
his neighbours is a raider; an amir enforcing tawhid on behalf of a scholar has
a reason every Muslim in the peninsula is obliged to consider, and a way of
converting submission into obligation. The emirate expanded across the Najd
within a generation.
