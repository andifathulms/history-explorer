The Wahhabi state at its height is usually described through its wars. It is
more informative to look at how it collected money and what it did with the
money, and a Swiss traveller who spent years in Arabia while the state still
stood wrote both down.

Every winter the collectors left Diriyah and dispersed across the whole of the
Wahhabi districts, exacting payment — his word is strictness — and then returned
to the capital with loads of gold and silver.

The rates were fixed and modest. One Spanish dollar for every five camels. The
value of one for every forty sheep or goats. About seven shillings for a horse
or a mare. It was paid in cash, and the ruler had at first insisted on Spanish
and Imperial dollars before consenting to take Turkish coin.

That is *zakat* — the alms every Muslim owes — collected as the central tax of a
state, by officials, annually, in specie, from tribes across most of a
peninsula. The doctrinal claim and the fiscal system are the same instrument.

## Land held for men, not rent

The ruler's own estates were let on terms Burckhardt thought worth explaining to
a European reader by analogy with feudalism: the tenants paid no yearly rent at
all, and instead were obliged to keep a fixed number of armed camel-riders ready
to march at any time.

When an expedition was planned they were told where to meet, and made their own
way there in small parties or singly. Across the Najd the general obligation was
reportedly one man in ten.

<Figure id="camel-column" />

So the state's two revenues were a cash tax on livestock and a standing
obligation of armed men on land, and neither required a treasury clerk to value
a harvest or a standing army to be fed between campaigns. It is an extremely
cheap way to run a country, and it explains how an oasis reached the Hejaz and
Karbala within two generations.

## What it did not command

The traveller is equally clear about the limits, and he had the advantage of
meeting the people concerned in the desert rather than at court.

The northern Anaza had not paid tribute for several years. Every Anaza group he
met on his journey was in rebellion, while keeping up an appearance of good
understanding: their chiefs abstained from tobacco and professed the Wahhabi
faith, and the ordinary people cared little for the new doctrine — they sang and
they smoked — but always mentioned Ibn Saud's name with respect.

That is the true reach of the thing. A ring of formal conformity around a core
that actually paid, with the outer tribes performing the doctrine's visible
prohibitions and ignoring the rest, and everybody careful about the name.

## The library

The last part is the one that contradicts the usual picture hardest.

The Wahhabis had established schools in every village and obliged fathers to
superintend their children's instruction. At Diriyah, learned men had assembled
very valuable libraries collected from all over Arabia, and some of their
scholars were writing treatises of their own.

What they wanted most was history. Whatever historical manuscripts could be
found at Mecca, at Medina and in the towns of Yemen had been bought up and
carried off to the Najd, and Saud's library was, in Burckhardt's judgement,
unquestionably the richest collection of Arabic historical manuscripts then in
existence.

He adds one more thing. Saud had gathered the best poets of the desert at
Diriyah, delighted in poetry, and rewarded those who excelled at it very
liberally.

A movement remembered for destroying tombs and shrines was also, at its capital,
a school system, the largest history library in the Arabic-speaking world, and a
court that paid poets well. Both are true at once, and the second is what an
oasis looks like when it has been receiving loads of gold and silver every
winter for fifty years.
