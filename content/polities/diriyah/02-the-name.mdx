The doctrine is called Wahhabism by almost everyone who is not an adherent, and
its adherents have generally refused the name. They called themselves
*al-Muwahhidun* — the unitarians, those who affirm the oneness of God — and
objected to Wahhabi on the grounds that naming a school after a man is precisely
the error the school exists to correct.

This site has met that problem twice already. The Jafnid record is called Jafnid
rather than Ghassanid because the source distinguishes a ruling family from a
confederacy that later tradition merged with it. The Nizari record is called
Nizari rather than Assassin. In both cases the site took the name the polity or
the scholarship uses rather than the one hostile outsiders supplied.

Here it does not, and the reason should be stated rather than passed over.
Wahhabism is the term the entire secondary literature uses, including the
literature written by scholars sympathetic to the movement and including the
source this page is drafted from; *Muwahhidun* is a claim as well as a name, since
it implies that everybody else is not a unitarian, which is the doctrine's most
contested assertion rather than a neutral label. The site uses the conventional
term in prose and does not put either in a field.

What the doctrine held is narrower than the popular account and more consequential.
Its central proposition is *tawhid*, the absolute oneness of God, and its
practical programme follows from what that rules out: intercession through
saints or the dead, veneration of tombs and shrines, vows made at graves, the
elaborations of popular devotion that had accumulated across the Muslim world
for a thousand years. Ibn Abd al-Wahhab held that these were not lapses within
Islam but *shirk* — association, the one unforgivable sin — and therefore that
those who practised them were not, in the relevant sense, Muslims.

That last step is the one everything else turned on, because a Muslim who is not
a Muslim can be fought. It is what made a reforming doctrine into a basis for
conquest, and it is why the destruction of tombs recurs on every page of this
movement's history — at Karbala in 1802, at Medina in 1806, and in the Hejaz
again after 1925.
