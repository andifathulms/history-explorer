Forty-four years after the bombardment, a European travelling through central
Arabia in disguise came past Diriyah and looked at what was left.

The ruins of an enormous palace, he wrote, and of a scarcely less enormous
mosque, still remained. And then the observation that does the work: the old
capital, amid all its present desolation, displayed traces of much greater
regularity and ornament than Riyadh could boast.

The living capital of the Wahhabi state, in other words, was a shabbier place
than the dead one. Riyadh in 1862 was the seat of a working government with an
army, a treasury and a religious establishment, and it did not look as good as
the town that had been shelled flat in 1818 and abandoned.

## What was destroyed and how

The same traveller collected the account of the end from people who remembered
it, and it is worth having because it explains why nothing was rebuilt.

After the field battles were lost, the last ruler entrenched himself in Diriyah
and Wadi Hanifa lay open. The Egyptians came up and assaulted once and were
thrown back. Their commander did not try again. He ringed the town, cut it off,
and invited it to surrender.

Then twenty days passed in which neither side fired a shot. The defenders were
holding everything back for the final assault and would not sally; they returned
no answer at all to repeated offers of terms. A relief force gathered elsewhere
in the Najd and was intercepted and scattered on the way. On the twenty-first
day the commander sent in a last offer of honourable capitulation, and the
messenger came back without an answer.

The artillery was ranged on the heights. The bombardment began at sunset and
went on without a break until sunrise, six thousand shot and shell into the town
in one night, and morning came up on crumbling walls, heaps of rubble, people
dead and dying under their own houses.

He entered without parley and without opposition.

## The point of pulling it down

Diriyah was not sacked in the ordinary way and left to recover. It was
systematically demolished and its population dispersed, and the Egyptians were
explicit that the object was to make sure nothing could gather there again. The
last ruler was sent to Constantinople and beheaded.

It very nearly worked, and the way it failed is the interesting part.

The state came back six years later, in a different town a short way down the
same wadi, under a cousin of the executed man, with the same doctrine and the same
arrangement between the same two families. What could not be reassembled was the
place. Diriyah stayed a ruin, and the second Saudi state governed from Riyadh
instead — which is why the capital of the Najd for the next century and a half
was a town that had been a provincial settlement when the movement began.

The palace and the mosque that a European traveller stopped to admire in 1862
were the physical record of what fifty winters of tribute had built, and they
were already the best-looking thing in central Arabia because nobody had had
that kind of money since.
