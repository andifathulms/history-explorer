Muhammad Ali of Egypt was the Ottoman governor of a province he had made
effectively independent, and he was building a state on European lines — an
army with French instruction, conscription, a fleet, an industrial base. The
Hejaz commission suited him: it earned Ottoman credit, it occupied his army
usefully, and it put Egyptian troops in control of the holy cities.

His son Tusun landed on the Red Sea coast in 1811 and took Medina in 1812 and
Mecca in 1813, which recovered the sanctuaries and did not touch the emirate.
For that, Muhammad Ali sent his other son.

Ibrahim Pasha's campaign from 1816 is one of the more thoroughly executed
expeditions of the period. He went inland with artillery and engineers,
methodically, taking the fortified oasis towns of the Qasim one at a time
instead of chasing a mobile enemy across a desert, and reduced the Najd town by
town over two years. It is the answer to the strategic problem that had made
central Arabia ungovernable for everyone before him: he did not try to defeat
the tribes, he took the walls and the wells.

The emirate had no reply. Its army was tribal cavalry raised on obligation and
enthusiasm, and it had never faced a regular force with siege guns. The doctrine
that had converted submission into obligation could not raise men against
Muslims fighting under the sultan's authority as readily as it had against
Shi'is and shrine-visitors.

In April 1818 Ibrahim reached Diriyah and besieged it. It held for about five
months and surrendered in September.
