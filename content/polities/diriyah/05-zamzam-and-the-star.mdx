Burckhardt reached Mecca in 1814, a year after the Wahhabis had been pushed out
of it, among people who had lived under them for a decade. His account of what
the occupation had actually done is more interesting than either side's version
of it, because he is interested in money.

<Figure id="burckhardt-title" />

Start with the well. Before the Wahhabis came, Zamzam belonged to the sharif, and
the water — the holiest water in Islam, which pilgrims come a very long way to
drink — was a monopoly, sold at a high price. One of Saud's first orders on
arriving at Mecca was to abolish the traffic, and Burckhardt records that the
water was still being dispensed free when he was there.

<Figure id="haram-tile" />

That is the movement in its most sympathetic form: a reformer arrives and stops a
holy city charging for its holiness. Burckhardt, who had no particular sympathy
for the doctrine, sets it down without comment and then sets down the rest.

<Figure id="mecca-plan" />

The rest is Medina. Burckhardt had already written a history of the Wahhabis and
summarises it here. During the siege, the chiefs of the town seized a great deal
of the treasure of the Prophet's mosque — all the gold vessels — ostensibly to
distribute among the poor, and finally divided it among themselves. When Saud
took the town he went into the Hujra himself, penetrated behind the curtain, and
took everything valuable he found there: vessels set with jewels, earrings,
bracelets, necklaces, offerings sent from every part of the Muslim world, and,
most famous of all, a brilliant star of diamonds and pearls that hung directly
above the tomb, which the Arabs called the Kawkab al-durri.

Then the transaction that says the most. Part of what he took, Saud sold to
Sharif Ghalib of Mecca. The rest he carried to Diriyah.

Burckhardt then does the arithmetic, because the treasure had become a legend and
he distrusted legends. Ghalib valued his purchase at a hundred thousand dollars.
The Medina chiefs' gold was perhaps a hundredweight, worth forty or fifty
thousand. What Saud took away was mostly pearls and coral and was probably worth
no more than Ghalib's purchase. Total, perhaps three hundred thousand dollars —
considerable, he says, but far from the inestimable sum people liked to imagine.
No money was ever kept there, because cash given to the mosque was distributed
among its attendants at once.

He adds the suspicion any accountant would: given how long the offerings had been
accumulating, the total ought to have been far larger, and it would be strange if
the governors of Medina and the guardians of the tomb had not been drawing on it
for centuries already.

Put the three things in a row and the Wahhabi decade in the Hijaz is legible. The
sharif's charge for sacred water was abolished. The shrine's accumulated treasure
was taken, partly sold back to the sharif who had been collecting that charge,
and partly carried into the Najd. And a heavy poll tax was imposed on the
pilgrims who were still allowed to come, while the Syrian caravan was stopped
altogether.

What changed was not whether the holy cities produced a revenue. It was who
collected it, and on what grounds.
