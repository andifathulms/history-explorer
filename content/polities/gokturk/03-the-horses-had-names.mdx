The second khaganate at its height is documented in a way nothing else on the
steppe is: campaign by campaign, in the first person, with the horses named.

The Kul Tegin monument works through his brother's career as a ledger, and the
form is worth noticing before the content. Age, enemy, place, horse, number of
men killed by his own hand, result. It repeats about twenty times.

## The ledger

At sixteen, against the Six Chub and the Sogds; a Chinese commander came with
fifty thousand and Kul Tegin attacked at the run with the footmen, took the
commander prisoner with his spearmen and brought them armed to the qaghan.

At twenty-one, against Chacha Sengün. He attacked riding Tadiq Chur's grey; the
horse was killed. He attacked again on Ishbara Yamtar's grey; that horse was
killed. He attacked a third time on Yegin Silig Beg's brown Kedimlig; that horse
was killed too. *His armour and his moon-diamond ornament they hit with more
than 100 arrows, but not one hurt the plates or his head.*

At twenty-six, against the Kirghiz: they cut a road through snow a lance deep,
went over the Kögmen forest and fell on the Kirghiz as they slept. He rode
Bayirku's white stallion, shot one man, pierced two others one after the other,
and broke the stallion's leg in the charge. They killed the Kirghiz qaghan and
took the realm.

In the same year, against the Türgish, over the Altai and across the Irtysh,
falling on them as they slept as well.

At twenty-seven the Karluks. At thirty-one the Az, whose chief he took
prisoner. Then the Izgil, destroyed, and his horse dead under him again. Then
the Nine Oghuz five times in one year, on the white Azman, on the brown Az, on
the black-brown Az.

## What the ledger is for

None of it is the record of a state. There is no province in it, no tax, no
governor, no city, no law. What is being recorded is a man's personal courage
and the number of times he charged, and the reason is that this was the
qualification.

A steppe empire of this kind holds together because subordinate peoples believe
the centre will win. The monument is not boasting for its own sake; it is
publishing the evidence, in the only currency that mattered, for a house whose
authority rested on nothing else — no descent from a god, no scripture, no
capital, no bureaucracy. Bumin and Istami had been *wise qaghans, stout-hearted*
and so were their officials, the same text says, and that is offered as the
whole explanation of why they could govern so great a realm.

## The white horse and the camp

There is one entry that is not a victory, and it is the one the text builds
towards.

After wintering, the army set out in the spring against the Oghuz and Kul Tegin
was sent back to carry orders home. The hostile Oghuz fell on the camp. He
transfixed nine men, riding his white Ögsiz — *the motherless* — and did not
yield the camp.

> My mother the katun, along with my stepmothers, my aunts, and elder sisters,
> my daughters-in-law, my princesses — all of those that were left alive, would
> have become slave-women, and those of you that were slain would have been left
> lying in the camp or on the road. If Kül tegin had not been there, it would
> have been all over with you all.

That is the peak of the second khaganate, stated by the man who ruled it: an
empire from Manchuria to Bukhara, whose survival on one particular afternoon
came down to one rider on one horse in front of the tents.

## And then the mourning

Kul Tegin died in 731, in his forty-seventh year, and Bilge's account of it
abandons the ledger entirely.

> Now my brother Kül tegin is dead. I have myself mourned. My eyes, though
> seeing, have become as blinded; my thoughts, though conscious, have become as
> without consciousness... But the sons of men are all born to die, so soon as
> Heaven ordains the time.

The funeral was held on the twenty-seventh day of the ninth month, and the hall,
the paintings and the inscribed stone were dedicated on 21 August 732. The
dates are on the monument, which is the only place in this part of the world
where a steppe empire dated anything of its own.
