---
title: A steppe empire in its own words
drafted_from: golden-1992
phase: aside
---

In the Orkhon valley in Mongolia stand stone monuments raised in the 730s for
Kul Tegin and Bilge Qaghan of the second Turk khaganate, carrying long
inscriptions in the runiform Turkic script. They are the oldest substantial texts
in any Turkic language, and the only place in the whole history of the steppe
before the Mongols where a nomadic empire explains itself at length in its own
voice.

They are two great square monoliths that stood in sockets and have fallen; the
second is in four pieces. Three sides of each carry Turkish in runic
characters, and the western side carries Chinese. The Chinese is beautifully
cut and the runic is less elegant, though probably the work of the same Chinese
stonecutters — and the two texts, as Ross notes, differ entirely in their
contents.

That is the artefact worth dwelling on. On one face a steppe empire's account of
itself; on the other, the same monument, a Tang court's account of the same
people. They were carved at the same time by the same hands and they do not
describe the same object.

Everything else in this part of the world survives only in the second kind of
text. The Xiongnu are known through Sima Qian, the Rouran through the Wei
histories, the Khazars through Arabic and Hebrew correspondence, the Mongols
through everyone they attacked. The Orkhon stones are the one place where both
versions sit on the same block of rock, and the difference between them is
measurable.
