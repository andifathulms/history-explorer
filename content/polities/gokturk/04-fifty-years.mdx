The most remarkable passage on the Orkhon stones is not a boast but an
autopsy. Bilge Qaghan explains to his people how the first khaganate was lost,
and he does not blame the Chinese army.

## The sequence

> After them their younger brothers became kagans, and their sons became
> kagans; but the younger brothers were now not like their elder brothers, the
> sons were not like their fathers. It was incapable kagans that mounted the
> throne, it was bad kagans; their buyruks were likewise incapable and they
> were bad.

Then the mechanism: want of harmony between the begs and the people, Chinese
cunning and craft and intrigue, and younger and elder brothers taking counsel
against one another and setting begs against people. That brought the old realm
to dissolution and destroyed its lawful qaghans.

What followed is stated without softening. *The sons of the nobles became the
bondsmen of the Chinese people, their unsullied daughters became its slaves.
The Turkish begs gave up their Turkish names, and bearing the Chinese names of
Chinese begs they obeyed the Chinese Emperor, and served him during fifty
years.*

Fifty years — from about 630, when the eastern khaganate fell to the Tang, to
the revolt of the 680s. In that half-century, the stone says, Turkish armies
campaigned for the Chinese emperor eastward to the sunrise and westward as far
as the Iron Gate, and conquered kingdoms and power for him.

## The complaint that starts the revolt

Then the inscription does something no chronicle does. It quotes the ordinary
people.

> The whole of the common Turkish people said thus: "I have been a nation that
> had its own kingdom; where is now my kingdom? For whom do I win the
> kingdoms?" said they. "I have been a people that had its own kagan; where is
> my kagan? Which kagan is it I serve?" they said.

They rose, and — the text is candid about this too — having risen, they could
not put themselves in order or take proper thought, and submitted again.

## Seventeen men

The recovery, when it came, is counted in a way that reads like a ledger.

Heaven raised up Bilge's father Elterish and his mother Elbilge. *My father the
kagan went forth with seventeen men.* When word spread that he was out, those
in the towns came out and those on the mountains came down, and when they
gathered they were seventy. Then, as Heaven gave them strength, his army was as
wolves and their foes as sheep; making marches east and west he gathered people
until there were seven hundred.

Seventeen, seventy, seven hundred. With that he reorganised a people that had
lost its realm and its qaghan, whose institutions had been broken up, and
divided it again into the Tölis and Tardush wings under a yabghu and a shad.

Then the tally: to the south the Chinese were hostile, to the north the Nine
Oghuz, and the Kirghiz, Kurikans, Thirty Tatars, Kitay and Tatabi were all
hostile too. *Forty-seven times he went campaigning, and fought in twenty
fights.*

## What is being argued

A monument raised by a restored dynasty for its own dead is a political
document, and this one has a case to make: that the collapse was the people's
fault and the recovery was the family's achievement. Every campaign in it is a
victory and every difficulty is somebody's cowardice.

But the analysis it offers is not self-serving in the way you would expect. A
qaghan who wanted only to flatter his house would blame the enemy. Bilge blames
succession — brothers unlike brothers, sons unlike fathers — and then blames
his own subjects for being seduced. He is describing the two failure modes of
a steppe empire, accurately, on a stone he expected to be read for centuries,
and he was right about both.
