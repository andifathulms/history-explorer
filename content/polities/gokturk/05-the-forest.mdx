Bilge Qaghan knew what was going to destroy his people and said so on a stone,
in the imperative, and it did not help.

## The warning

> While I have ruled here, I have become reconciled with the Chinese people.
> The Chinese people, who give in abundance gold, silver, millet and silk, have
> always used ingratiating words and have at their disposal enervating riches.
> While ensnaring them with their ingratiating talk and enervating riches, they
> have drawn the far-dwelling peoples nearer to themselves. But after settling
> down near them these have come to see their cunning.

The argument is not that the Chinese are enemies. It is that they are generous
on purpose. And the counter-policy is geographic:

> If thou then go forth to that land, O Turkish nation, thou wilt come to
> destruction; but if thou stay in the land of Ötüken, and send out caravans,
> thou wilt never suffer any need. If thou stay on in the mountain forest of
> Ötüken, thou shalt ever hold an everlasting kingdom, O Turkish nation, and
> thou shalt be full-fed.

Stay in the forest and trade outward. Do not go and live next to the market.
The Ötükan forest, the stone says twice, has no foreign overlord, and is the
place from which the kingdom is held together.

Then the sentence that gives the whole thing away, because it explains why
nobody listens:

> When thou art hungry, thou dost not remember what fullness is; but once thou
> art full-fed, thou hast no thought of what hunger is.

## The trap he could not close

Bilge could see the mechanism and could not stop it, and the inscription
records that too, in the middle of an address meant to inspire.

Some among the Turks, it says, decided to settle in the south, in the plain
rather than in the Chugay forest, and wicked men encouraged them with the
argument that when they are far away the Chinese give bad gifts and when they
are near they give good ones. *The foolish persons were taken by these words,
and went down to their neighbourhood, whereby many among you have come to
destruction.*

He also had to reconquer his own people repeatedly. The Nine Oghuz, the stone
says, *was my own people*, and they rose against him; within one year he fought
them five times. The Karluks, free and independent, came to war. The Az people
came to war. The Izgil people were destroyed. Every one of those is a
subordinate the empire had taken, and every one of them had to be taken again.

## The end, which is not in the text

The second khaganate outlived Bilge by about ten years. He was poisoned in 734;
his sons did not hold it; in 744 the Uyghurs, with the Karluks and the Basmil,
killed the last qaghan and took the Ötükan.

The monuments stayed where they were. What destroyed the state was not Chinese
silk, in the end, but the ordinary thing — a subordinated people that saw the
centre weaken and did what the inscription itself predicts subordinated peoples
do. Bilge had named the mechanism in his autopsy of the first khaganate and it
came for the second on schedule.

## Who cut the stone

There is one more fact on the monument, and it is stranger than the warning.

> From the Chinese Emperor I have had artists to come, and have set them to
> work. My request has not been refused. They have sent the Chinese Emperor's
> court painters. I have bidden them set up a separate hall, and inside and out
> I have had them to make various remarkable paintings.

The great anti-Chinese manifesto of the steppe was carved by masons and painted
by artists requested from, and supplied by, the Chinese emperor — who also
provided a Chinese inscription for the reverse of the same stone, saying
entirely different things. The Turkish face warns against Chinese gifts. It was
itself a Chinese gift.
