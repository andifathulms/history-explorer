---
title: A steppe empire in its own words
drafted_from: golden-1992
phase: aside
---

In the Orkhon valley in Mongolia stand stone monuments raised in the 730s for
Kul Tegin and Bilge Qaghan of the second Turk khaganate, carrying long
inscriptions in the runiform Turkic script. They are the oldest substantial texts
in any Turkic language, and they are the only place in this site's steppe regions
where a nomadic empire explains itself at length in its own voice.

What they say is unexpected. They are largely a warning against the Chinese: not
against Chinese armies, but against Chinese silk, gold, sweet words and cities.
Bilge Qaghan tells his people that the Turks who settled near China were
destroyed by comfort, that the Chinese give generously precisely in order to
weaken, and that the Turkish people should stay in the Otukan forest and remain
poor and free.

It is a self-aware statement of the dilemma every polity in this corpus's steppe
pages faces, made by a ruler who could see the trap and could not close it. The
same document also records that he had to reconquer his own people repeatedly and
that they scattered when the empire weakened.

The bilingual monuments carry a Chinese inscription on the reverse, composed at
the Tang court, which says entirely different things. The two faces of the same
stone are the best single artefact for the argument on this site's Xiongnu page:
that the steppe as described by its neighbours and the steppe as it described
itself are not the same object.
