Two hundred years after the event, the second khaganate's memorial stones set
down what the founders had done, and the account is a compass:

> Over the sons of men set themselves as rulers my forbears Bumin kagan and
> Istami kagan, and having set themselves as rulers they governed and kept in
> order the Turkish peoples' kingdoms and polity. All the peoples in the four
> quarters of the world were foes to them; but they waged wars against them and
> overcame all the peoples in the four quarters of the world, made them keep
> the peace and bow their head and bend their knee. Towards the East they made
> them settle as far as the mountain forest of Kadirkan, to the West as far as
> Tamir-kapig — the Iron Gate.

The Iron Gate is the pass at Buzgala, south of Samarkand, on the road into
Bactria. Kadirkan is the Khingan range in Manchuria. Between those two points
is about five thousand kilometres, and the claim is that two brothers took all
of it in one generation, starting from a revolt of ironworkers against the
Rouran in 552.

## Why it could be done at all

The Turks were not the first people to hold this ground. They were the first to
hold it as a single command with a working division of labour: Bumin in the
east on the Orkhon, his brother Istami in the west from the Ili valley, each a
sovereign in his own quarter and both parts of one house.

That structure is why the western half could conduct its own foreign policy.
Istami destroyed the Hephthalites in alliance with the Sasanians, fell out with
them almost at once over the silk trade — the Persians wanted the Sogdian
merchants kept out of their market — and in 568 sent an embassy to
Constantinople proposing a joint attack on Persia. The Byzantines sent one
back. That is the moment the steppe stops being a frontier problem for the
Mediterranean and Iran and becomes a third party in the diplomacy between them.

## The inventory of the funeral

The inscription's best evidence for the reach is not a boast. It is a guest
list.

When Bumin and Istami died, the monument says, there came to the funeral and
the mourning, from the east, the far-off Bökli nation, the Chinese, the
Tibetans, the Avars, the Kirghiz, the Three Kurikans, the Thirty Tatars, the
Kitay and the Tatabi.

A century and a half later, at Kul Tegin's funeral in 732, the delegations are
listed again and the list has changed shape: envoys from the Kitay and Tatabi;
from the Chinese emperor, two named men bringing gold and silver by the ten
thousand; from the Tibetan qaghan an official whose Tibetan title the Turkish
scribe transcribed phonetically; from the Sogds, the Persians and the Bukhara
tribes in the west, two more named men; from the Ten Arrows and the Türgish
qaghan, two keepers of the seal; from the Kirghiz qaghan, one envoy.

Read those two paragraphs together and you have the political map of Inner Asia
without a single sentence of narrative. Everyone who mattered from Manchuria to
Bukhara sent somebody, twice, a hundred and fifty years apart, and the Turks
recorded who came because who came was the point.

## What holding it meant

Not administration. The inscriptions have no tax, no province, no governor and
no city. What they have instead is a verb repeated until it becomes a formula:

> From them that had a kingdom we took away their kingdom; them that had a
> qaghan we robbed of their qaghan; we made them bend their knee and bow their
> head.

An empire of this kind is a set of subordinated dynasties, each keeping its own
people and its own ground, each obliged to send men when called and to send an
envoy when the qaghan's brother died. That is a cheap way to hold five thousand
kilometres and it has one structural weakness, which the same stones set out at
length: every one of those subordinated peoples is a rebellion waiting for the
centre to look weak.
