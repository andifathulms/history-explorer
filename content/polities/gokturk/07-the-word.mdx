The khaganate lasted, in its two incarnations, about a hundred and ninety
years. What it left is a noun that is now attached to a language family, a
nation-state, and something like two hundred million people.

## Türk

Before 552 there is no people that calls itself Türk. The word appears as the
name of one confederation of Altai ironworkers who revolted against the Rouran,
and by the seventh century it is in Chinese, Byzantine, Sogdian and Persian
sources as the name of a power. Every later use runs through this one: the
Türgish and the Uyghurs, the Karluks and the Oghuz, the Seljuks who took
Baghdad, the Ottomans who took Constantinople, and the modern Turkic languages
from the Balkans to Siberia.

That is not a claim that all those peoples descend from the Altai revolt. It is
a claim about a name — that a political label attached to one confederation
became the ethnonym of a family, because that confederation was the first to
hold the whole steppe and to be written about by everybody who bordered it.

## The alphabet nobody else used

The runiform script of the Orkhon stones is the other survival, and it is the
odder one.

It has about thirty-eight characters, distinguishes front and back vowels in
its consonant signs, and was written mostly right to left. Where it came from
is argued — Sogdian derivation, Aramaic ultimately, with tamga clan-marks
possibly feeding into the letter shapes — and it is attested from the Orkhon to
the Yenisei and west into the Talas valley.

Then it stopped. The Uyghurs who took the Ötükan in 744 used it at first and
then adopted the Sogdian-derived alphabet instead; Islam brought the Arabic
script to the Turkic west; and the runiform letters were unreadable to everyone
by about the tenth century. They stayed unreadable for nine hundred years, until
Vilhelm Thomsen deciphered them in 1893 — reportedly by working out the words
for *Türk* and *Tengri* first, from the Chinese face of the same stones.

So the first thing any Turkic-speaking people wrote about itself was
inaccessible to Turkic-speaking people for most of the time it has existed, and
came back into the world through a Danish philologist in Copenhagen.

## What it was for

Almost every steppe power before and after this one is known only from the
records of its neighbours: the Xiongnu through Sima Qian, the Rouran through
the Wei histories, the Khazars through Arabic and Hebrew letters, the Mongols
through everyone they attacked.

The Türks are the exception, and the exception is small — two monuments and
some shorter texts, all raised within a decade, all by one dynasty about
itself. It is not much. But it is the difference between a people described and
a people speaking, and there is no other case of it in this part of the world
for another five hundred years.
