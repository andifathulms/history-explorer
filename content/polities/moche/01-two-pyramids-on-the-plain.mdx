A few kilometers from the Pacific, on the southern edge of the
valley that takes its name from the same river, two great earthen
structures rise from a dry plain between the conical hill called
Cerro Blanco and the water's edge. The larger, the Huaca del Sol,
stands free of the hillside, its bulk somewhat recalling the
pyramids of Teotihuacan though less high and less steep; the
smaller Huaca de la Luna is built as a terraced platform directly
against the lower slope of the Cerro itself. Neither name - Sun,
Moon - is likely original to the people who built them.

## Uhle's excavations

The site takes its modern name, and the whole ancient culture along
with it, from the nearby town of Moche. It was here that Max Uhle
excavated for the University of California in 1899, working chiefly
in the great cemetery that spreads across the sandy slopes behind
the Huaca de la Luna and around both sides of the Cerro Blanco. The
pottery he recovered - overwhelmingly red-surfaced, ranging from
coarse utilitarian wares to finely modeled and painted vessels -
became the type collection against which everything else found on
this stretch of coast would afterward be compared. Even today, a
visitor can pick sherds from the same red pottery off the plain
between the two pyramids without much difficulty, evidence of just
how thoroughly this ground was once occupied.
