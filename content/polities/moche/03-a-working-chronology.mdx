Fixing the Mochica period in calendar time occupied Peruvian
archaeologists for decades. Before radiocarbon dating existed, the
only firm anchors available came from the Inca and Chimu dynasty
lists themselves, which placed the founding of the Chimu kingdom
between about A.D. 1300 and 1350 and the Inca conquest of the north
coast between 1462 and 1470. Everything earlier than that had to be
estimated from stratigraphy, from the accumulation rate of guano
deposits, and from comparison with sequences worked out elsewhere -
methods that left North American archaeologists guessing, for years,
that the whole span of Peruvian civilization from the earliest
monumental culture to the Inca had unfolded within the previous two
thousand years.

## Bennett's working dates

By the time the Virú Valley Project's excavators had assembled the
evidence available to them, Wendell Bennett's 1949 estimate had
become the chronology most archaeologists in the field were working
from: a Gallinazo and Mochica period running from about A.D. 300 to
1000, preceded by centuries of Salinar and Cupisnique culture and
followed by what the source itself terms simply the Late Epoch - the
interval from the collapse of Mochica culture until the Spanish
conquest, spanning the Chimu kingdom that eventually rose on the
same stretch of coast. Early radiocarbon dates, still new and
contested when this chronology was compiled, complicated the picture
in places without overturning its basic shape.

## An ending still without a story

What actually brought Mochica rule on the north coast to a close is
a question this chronology answers only in dates, not in events -
a range of years rather than a narrative. What followed, in the
centuries the source calls simply the Late Epoch, was the rise of
the Chimu kingdom on the same stretch of coast the Mochica builders
had once occupied.
