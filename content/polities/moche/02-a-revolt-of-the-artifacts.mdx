In 1910 the German scholar Eduard Seler noted a wall painting inside
the Huaca de la Luna, in the highest of the structure's chambers -
a discovery that was subsequently lost again to drifting sand. It
was not until 1925, and more substantially through 1926, that
excavators cleared several meters of the same walls and found a
whole series of painted scenes, most of them depicting combat.

## Weapons that fight back

The paintings were made by whitewashing the mud-plastered adobe,
incising the outlines of each figure, going over those incisions in
black, and filling the enclosed areas with red, yellow, pink and
light blue. What they show is stranger than ordinary battle scenes:
human figures fighting against personified weapons and implements,
brought to life and turned against the people who had made them - a
"revolt of the artifacts" myth also known from ancient Mexico,
suggesting a shared current of belief reaching across two otherwise
unconnected civilizations.

## A room repeatedly repainted

Traces of earlier coats of plaster beneath the visible paintings show
that these chambers were decorated more than once over time, and one
section of unfinished outline - drawn but never filled with color -
suggests the work, or the room itself, was abandoned before
completion. At the center of the painted wall stood a stepped,
throne-like platform, its risers painted on both their tops and
sides, hinting that whatever stood in the niche it framed - an altar,
an image, a seat - was itself part of the scene the painters were
depicting. The murals rank among the clearest surviving evidence of
how the people who built the twin pyramids of Moche told their own
stories on the walls of their own temple.
