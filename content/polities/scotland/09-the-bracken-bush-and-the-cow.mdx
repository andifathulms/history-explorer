Three Stewart kings in succession learned that ruling Scotland's
nobility by force carried a price. James I, James II and James III
each spent their reigns breaking the power of over-mighty subjects,
and two of the three died for it.

## "The key shall keep the castle"

James I returned from eighteen years as an English hostage in 1424
determined, as he is said to have declared on his first day home, that
"the key shall keep the castle and the bracken-bush the cow" - that
royal authority would reach into every corner of a kingdom that had
drifted for two decades under weak regents. He moved fast: within
months of his coronation he arrested and later executed Duke Murdoch
of Albany, his own cousin and the son of a former regent, along with
Murdoch's sons and father-in-law, on charges the record never
specifies. He broke the power of the Lord of the Isles in the
Highlands, first by mass arrest at a parliament called under false
pretenses at Inverness, then by pitched battle when the humbled chief
rose again. He stripped earldom after earldom from their hereditary
holders on legal technicalities, adding each to the crown's own
estate. The nobility's resentment finally caught up with him in 1437,
when a conspiracy led by his own kinsman the Earl of Atholl - who
believed himself the rightful king - broke into James's lodging at
Perth while he was undressing for bed. He tried to hide in a drain
beneath the floor; his murderers found him anyway and killed him
there, wounding the queen herself in the struggle. Every conspirator
caught was executed with a cruelty that shocked even contemporaries.

## Killed by his own cannon

James II, crowned at six, grew up to govern with the same
determination his father had shown. When the Earl of Douglas refused,
under the king's own safe conduct at a dinner in Stirling Castle, to
break a threatening alliance with two other great earls, James stabbed
him with his own dagger and let his courtiers finish the killing,
leaving the earl dead of twenty-six wounds - an act that provoked
exactly the civil war it was meant to prevent, fought until the
Douglas power was finally broken years later. Fascinated by the new
military technology of
cannon, James besieged the long-contested border fortress of Roxburgh
in 1460 with an artillery train of his own - and was killed instantly
when one of his own bombards burst beside him while he watched it
fire, not yet thirty years old.

## A minority, a revolt, and a king who did not survive his own son's army
 
James III inherited the throne at eight and grew into a king his own
nobility found remote and financially reckless - his fondness for
low-born favorites and his currency debasements were recurring
grievances through his reign. Resentment built for years, flaring
openly in 1482 when barons seized the king at Lauder and hanged his
favorites from a bridge before his own eyes, and finally boiling over
in 1488 into open rebellion backed, in a final humiliation, by James's
own teenage son and heir. The rebel army defeated the king's forces at
Sauchieburn that June; James himself died in the confusion following
the battle, in circumstances the surviving accounts never fully agree
on. His son was crowned within weeks as James IV - the third
consecutive Stewart king to inherit a throne his own family's internal
violence had just emptied.
