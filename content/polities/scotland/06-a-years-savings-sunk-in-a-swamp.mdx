William Paterson had dreamed for years of a Scottish trading colony on
the isthmus of Panama, positioned to profit from trade between the
Atlantic and Pacific. When the Company of Scotland finally committed
to the scheme, the response at home was extraordinary: subscriptions
poured in from across Scottish society until a substantial share of
the nation's entire liquid capital was tied up in a single colonial
venture. Twelve hundred colonists sailed from Leith in 1698 with the
enterprise's founders' own high hopes riding with them.

## Disease, Spanish claims, and an abandoned shore

The colony, named Caledonia, struggled from the outset against tropical
disease and poor planning, and Spain, which claimed prior rights to the
territory, moved to make the claim good by force - capturing supply
ships and preparing an attack on the settlement itself. By the middle
of June 1699, not yet a year after they had sailed with such
confidence, the colonists abandoned the settlement; Paterson himself,
too ill to do more than protest from a sickbed against the retreat,
could not prevent it. Of five ships that had left Scotland, only one
made it home; of the original twelve hundred emigrants, barely nine
hundred even left Darien alive, and few of those reached Scotland at
all.

## What the wreck left behind

The financial loss was catastrophic for a country as small and
capital-poor as Scotland then was, and it fell disproportionately on
precisely the class of landowners and merchants whose support any
future political settlement would need. The disaster did not by
itself cause what came next, but it gave the case for closer union
with England - and access to England's own colonial markets and
capital - an argument its opponents found very difficult to answer.
