By the winter of 1559-60 the Scottish Reformation had become a matter
of open warfare between Protestant lords calling themselves the
Congregation and the French-backed regency of Mary of Guise, mother of
the young Queen Mary then still living in France. French troops under
D'Oysel pushed the Congregation's forces hard through the depths of
that winter, and by January the reformers around John Knox had all but
given up hope - until an English fleet, sent by Elizabeth to block the
Firth of Forth against further French reinforcement, appeared
unannounced off the coast and turned the campaign decisively.

## A treaty, then a parliament

Negotiations that followed produced the Treaty of Berwick, a mutual
defence pact against France that brought an English army into Scotland
to help drive the French garrison out. With French support withdrawn
and Mary of Guise dead that June, the Scottish Parliament that met soon
afterward broke with Rome and adopted a Reformed Confession of Faith -
a change carried through, by later count, with only thirty-five laymen
among the forty-one members of the church assembly that endorsed it.

## Bloodless by the standards of the age

What followed struck contemporaries less than what did not follow.
Compared with the burnings, massacres and decades of civil war that
accompanied the same religious transformation in France and Spain,
Scotland's own Reformation was, by its own historian's later
reckoning, a record of order rather than atrocity - heretics had been
burned before 1560, but in numbers modest by continental standards,
and even at the height of their own triumph the victorious Protestants
carried out not a single execution for religion. Mary and her husband
Francis refused to ratify the Parliament's acts from France, "which we
little regarded," Knox wrote - the new church stood regardless, secure
enough that the refusal changed nothing in practice.
