Church and religion had been kept off the negotiating table
throughout the union talks, and the exclusion frightened the
Presbyterian clergy more than it reassured them. A single Parliament
at Westminster, its overwhelming majority English churchmen, seemed to
most of them certain to impose one form of church government on both
kingdoms sooner or later - and the National Church's ministers made
clear, through the autumn of 1706, that they had the means to wreck
the treaty if nothing was done to answer that fear.

## A promise sworn at accession

What was done, on 12 November 1706, was the Act of Security: a
guarantee, worded as absolutely as legal drafting allowed, that the
Presbyterian settlement made at the Revolution would "continue without
any alteration to the people of this land in all succeeding
generations," and that the four universities, whose professors had to
belong to the National Church, would remain "within this Kingdom for
ever." The Act was never written into the Treaty of Union itself, but
it was made an indissoluble part of it by another device: every future
sovereign would have to swear to uphold it at their accession, before
their coronation rather than during it, a solemnity given to no other
undertaking of the settlement. On the strength of that pledge, the
Church's more moderate leaders backed the Union, even though most of
the country clergy kept opposing it to the end.

## How long "for ever" lasted

The promise survived less than four years intact. In 1710 an
Episcopalian minister in Edinburgh, prosecuted by the Presbytery for
using the Anglican liturgy, appealed a Court of Session ruling against
him all the way to the House of Lords - and won, reversing a Scottish
church court's own jurisdiction from London. Two years later, English
Tories passed an Act of Toleration protecting Scottish Episcopalians
against the discipline of the National Church, followed within days by
an Act restoring lay patronage over ministerial appointments that had
been abolished in 1690. The Church protested each measure as a breach
of the Act of Security's own guarantee of its privileges as they had
stood at the Union; the protests changed nothing, because the security
sworn "for ever" was, in practice, only as durable as the Parliament
that had no other body above it to answer to.

What survived was not the letter of the 1706 guarantee but the
institution it had tried to fix in place. The Church of Scotland kept
its own courts, its own government, and its own claim to be a national
church distinct from England's - contested repeatedly, weakened by the
very Parliament sworn to protect it, but never actually dissolved into
the settlement its ministers had feared in 1706.
