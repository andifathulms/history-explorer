When Edward I of England forced a disputed succession to Scotland's
throne in the 1290s and then set about ruling the kingdom directly
through his own officials, most of the Scottish nobility who might
have resisted him had already submitted. Resistance, when it came in
1297, arose instead from outside that class - William Wallace, whose
own rising against English officials in Lanarkshire grew through the
spring and summer into something close to a national revolt.

## The narrow bridge at Stirling

An English army under the Earl of Surrey marched to put the rising
down, and met Wallace's forces at Stirling, where a single narrow
bridge crossed the Forth. Forced to funnel across it a few men at a
time, the English vanguard was cut off and destroyed on the northern
bank before the rest of the army could support it - a defeat inflicted
by a force built mainly of common spearmen rather than the mounted
nobility that had dominated Scottish warfare in the past.

## A revolt becomes a war

Stirling Bridge did not settle the war; Edward would return in person
the following year and defeat Wallace decisively at Falkirk. But it
reopened a conflict the previous year's submissions had seemed to
close, and it gave the resistance to English overlordship a leader and
a victory it could point to. The wars that followed - fought under
Wallace, then under Robert Bruce after him - would occupy the better
part of the next three decades, and Stirling Bridge was where they
genuinely began.
