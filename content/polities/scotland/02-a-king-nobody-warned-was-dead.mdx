Malcolm II, who won Lothian at the battle of Carham in 1018 and
extinguished the last independent king of Strathclyde the same year,
impressed his own contemporaries as the greatest prince yet to reign
over Alba. One chronicler called him "the head of the nobility of the
west of Europe." The two reigns that followed his death in 1034 show
how little that authority could yet be taken for granted.

## The king Shakespeare invented from nothing

Malcolm was succeeded by his grandson Duncan, whose own reign was as
brief as it was disastrous - and whose real character has almost
nothing in common with the elderly, saintly victim of Shakespeare's
play. Duncan spent his reign fighting wars he mostly lost: Northumbrian
raiders devastated Strathclyde in his own name, an expedition against
Durham ended with his infantry cut down to the last man and their
heads set on stakes in the town square, and a campaign against
Thorfinn of Orkney - a nephew by marriage who refused to acknowledge
any Scottish overlordship - cost him his fleet and, eventually, his
own life. Macbeth, mormaer of Moray and commander of the royal forces,
whose wife Gruoch carried her own claim to the throne through the old
law of alternate succession, killed Duncan in 1040 under circumstances
the sources do not explain in detail, and took the kingdom he would
hold for the next seventeen years.

## Seventeen years the play never mentions

The historical Macbeth's reign was, by the modest standard of his
predecessors, a stable and even pious one. He crushed the one attempt
to unseat him early in his reign, when Duncan's own father marched
against him and lost, and was not seriously troubled again until his
final year. He gave generously to the church, distributing alms among
the poor of Rome and granting lands to the Culdees of Loch Leven in his
own and his wife's names, and in 1054 withstood an invasion by Siward,
Earl of Northumbria, that ended without accomplishing anything the
English earl had set out to do. It was Duncan's own son Malcolm,
old enough by 1057 to press his own claim in earnest, who finally
defeated and killed Macbeth, probably in open battle at Lumphanan.
Later Scottish chroniclers, eager to construct an unbroken royal line
running back to the dawn of the world, recast the usurper who had
actually governed well for seventeen years as a monstrous interloper -
the transformation that would eventually reach Shakespeare by way of
Wyntoun, Boece and Holinshed.
