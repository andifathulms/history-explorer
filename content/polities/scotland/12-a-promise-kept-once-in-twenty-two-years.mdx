James VI had spent years positioning himself for the one prize that
mattered most to him: recognition as Elizabeth of England's successor.
He conciliated Catholic nobles at home, brought the Presbyterian
church more fully under royal control, and carried on careful,
simultaneous correspondence with English Catholics and Protestants
alike, each led to believe his eventual accession would favour them.
By early 1603 the outcome was, in Brown's own phrase, no longer
seriously in doubt to anyone paying attention.

## News in three days

Elizabeth died in the early hours of 24 March 1603. Sir Robert Carey
rode from London to Holyrood in under three days to bring James the
news himself, and within two more days an official proclamation from
the English Privy Council confirmed what everyone had expected: James
VI of Scotland was now also James I of England. He addressed his
Scottish subjects for the last time as their resident king on 3 April,
promising to return every three years, and left for London on the
5th.

## A kingdom that kept everything but a king

He kept that promise once. Of the twenty-two years he reigned after
1603, James revisited Scotland only a single time. What changed that
spring went well beyond who sat on the throne. Scotland retained its
own parliament, its own law, and its own church, but lost, at a
stroke, both an independent foreign policy and a resident monarch -
the crown itself now permanently attached to a larger kingdom whose
capital lay four hundred miles to the south.
