The seventy-two years spanned by Alexander II and Alexander III are,
by Brown's own account, the golden age of medieval Scottish history -
a stretch of almost unbroken peace in which the kingdom's institutions
finally caught up with its own territory, ending in a single accident
on a dark road that undid the security three generations of kings had
built.

## Finishing what earlier kings had started

Alexander II, crowned at seventeen the day after his father's death,
spent his reign completing the consolidation David I had begun.
He brought Argyll under direct royal authority for the first time,
crushed repeated risings in Moray and Galloway with a speed that
showed how thoroughly the crown's power had grown since his
grandfather's day, and in 1237 settled the long-running border dispute
with England by treaty rather than by battle - trading away his
family's old claim to Northumberland for lands in the north of England
worth two hundred pounds a year, a bargain that held for the rest of
the century. His son Alexander III, who succeeded him at not quite
eight years old in 1249, presided over a kingdom that by his own reign
had become, relative to its size, one of the most prosperous in
Christendom - a status only England, among all the great powers of
Europe, could then match. Norway's cession of the Western Isles in
1266, after a failed invasion of its own, completed Scotland's
territorial shape in almost the form it would hold into the modern era.

## Death visits a wedding feast

Alexander's own good fortune turned in the space of a few years. His
younger son died in 1281; within weeks of each other in 1283 his
daughter Margaret, queen of Norway, and his elder son both followed.
Only the infant Margaret, Alexander's granddaughter through the
Norwegian queen, remained to carry the royal line, and the king
summoned his nobility to Scone within a week of his own son's death to
have them formally recognize her as heir. Alexander remarried in 1285,
hoping for a son of his own, but the wedding feast at Jedburgh produced
an omen nobody could shake: a masked dance performed before the
king and queen, in which a figure dressed as Death appeared among the
dancers and struck terror into everyone watching. The omen proved
accurate. On a stormy night in March 1286, riding to rejoin his queen
at Kinghorn against his own advisers' urging, Alexander's horse
stumbled over a cliff in the dark and killed him. A lament recorded by
the chronicler Wyntoun, the oldest fragment of Scottish poetry to
survive, mourned a king who had led the country "in love and law" and
warned that gold had turned to lead.

## Twelve claimants and one judge with his own agenda

Alexander's death left only his infant granddaughter, already Queen of
Scots in name, to inherit a throne six regents now governed in her
absence. When the girl - remembered as the Maid of Norway - died at
sea in 1290 on her way to Scotland at last, the kingdom faced a
succession dispute serious enough that two of the strongest claimants,
Robert Bruce of Annandale and John Balliol, each commanded enough
backing to make an armed contest a real danger. The Scottish
guardians invited Edward I of England to arbitrate - not realizing,
or not able to prevent, that Edward would use the role to establish
himself as Scotland's own feudal overlord first and judge second. At
Norham in 1291 Edward required every claimant to acknowledge him as
Lord Paramount of Scotland before he would even hear their cases; by
the time his court finally ruled at Berwick in November 1292, naming
John Balliol the rightful king on the narrow technical grounds that
the kingdom itself could not be divided among rival claimants,
Edward had already extracted homage from Balliol and every other
competitor, and taken temporary custody of every royal castle in the
country. Balliol was crowned at Scone on the last day of November
1292 - the first Scottish king to owe his own throne, formally and
publicly, to an English king's judgment rather than to Scottish
consent alone.
