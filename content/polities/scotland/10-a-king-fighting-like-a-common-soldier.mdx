James IV, who came to the throne as a sixteen-year-old already
implicated in the revolt that killed his own father, grew into the
most capable of the later Stewart kings - and led his own kingdom
into the worst military disaster in its history over a French alliance
he valued more than his own caution warranted.

## A warning at vespers, and a king who ignored it

By the tradition Brown records, two supernatural warnings preceded
James's final campaign: a stranger who appeared before him at vespers
in Linlithgow church and urged him not to march, then vanished: and a
voice heard at midnight from Edinburgh's mercat cross, calling out by
name the lords who would be dead within forty days. Whatever weight to
give the omens, the actual cause of the war was concrete enough -
James's alliance with France obliged him to open a second front
against Henry VIII while the English king campaigned in France
himself. James crossed the border in August 1513, wasted weeks
besieging minor castles instead of striking at the poorly defended
town of Berwick, and - by one account distracted for a time by the
hospitality of Lady Heron at Ford Castle - let desertion and bad
weather thin his own army before the Earl of Surrey's English force
finally caught up with him.

## A ring of dead men around their king

The two armies met at Flodden on 9 September 1513. James, fighting in
the center of his own line rather than directing the battle from
behind it, held his ground even as his flanks collapsed on both sides
- the Highland right broken and its commanders killed, the left
fought to a standstill. As the English closed in from three directions
in the gathering dark, the remnant of the Scottish army formed a ring
around their king that held until nightfall ended the fighting
outright. When Surrey's men found the field empty the next morning,
they realized what the darkness had hidden: James was dead, along
with twelve earls, fourteen lords, an archbishop, a bishop and two
abbots - a casualty list so total that scarcely a noble family in
Scotland went unmarked by it. Edinburgh, expecting an invasion that
never came, built its first town wall in the disaster's immediate
aftermath.

## A dying man's daughter, and a reckoning renewed

James V, an infant at his father's death, grew up amid the same
factional chaos that had marked every Stewart minority, and came into
his own rule determined to match his father's engagement with the wider
currents of Europe - marrying first a daughter of Francis I of France,
then, after her early death, Mary of Guise. It was the same French
alliance that had killed his father that ended his own reign. In 1542,
drawn into war with Henry VIII's England once again, James found his
own nobles unwilling to follow him across the border - they turned back
rather than invade, at Fala Moor - and a second attempt ended in
catastrophe at Solway Moss, where a Scottish force collapsed with
barely a blow struck against it. James, already ill, received news of
the defeat and of the birth of his own daughter within the same
fortnight; he died within days, leaving the crown to a six-day-old
infant, Mary, whose own reign would open the final act of the
independent Scottish kingdom.
