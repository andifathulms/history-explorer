David I, youngest of Malcolm Canmore and Margaret's sons, came to the
throne in 1124 already shaped by decades at the English court and as
Earl of Cumbria and Lothian - and went on to remake Scotland more
thoroughly than any king before or after him. Brown's own history
calls his reign's transformation unmatched by anything short of the
Reformation four centuries later.

## Crushing the last resistance of Moray

David inherited the same rivalry with the house of Moray that had
troubled his predecessors, and settled it for good. When two grandsons
of Lulach - the king briefly set up after Macbeth's own death - raised
a rebellion during David's absence, his constable crushed them at
Stracathro; when the surviving brother, Malcolm, held out for five
more years, David called on English barons from across the border to
help finish the job. The mere rumor of their approach was enough:
Malcolm's own followers handed him over, and Moray's lands were
divided among Normans and trusted natives, ending its independent
power for good.

## A mast fixed in a wagon

David's own claim on English territory produced the most famous
battle of his reign. In 1138, leading an army of Norwegians, Scots,
Normans, Germans and Galwegian Picts more than twenty-six thousand
strong, he pushed deep into Yorkshire - only to be met near
Northallerton by an English force under the elderly Walter l'Espec,
fighting beneath a standard built around the mast of a ship mounted on
a wagon, hung with the banners of three northern saints and topped
with the consecrated host itself. The Galwegians, granted the right to
form the vanguard by ancient privilege, threw themselves against the
armored Norman line and were cut to pieces by English archery; when a
false rumor that David himself had fallen spread through the ranks,
the whole Scottish army broke. David's own reserve force covered the
retreat to Carlisle, where Prince Henry arrived two days later with
nineteen knights out of the two hundred who had ridden into battle
with him. The defeat cost Scotland nothing lasting: England's own
civil war between Stephen and the Empress Matilda left Stephen too
occupied to press the advantage, and within a year David had extracted
a peace more favorable than before the battle - the earldom of
Northumberland itself, granted to his son Henry.

## A kingdom remade rather than merely ruled

David's real legacy lay less in the battles he won or lost than in
what he built at home. He founded burghs with their own charters and
trading privileges, reorganized the church along the lines of the wider
European reform movement, and settled Norman knights and barons across
his kingdom in numbers that made feudal tenure, rather than kinship,
the basis of landholding for the first time. The transformation reached
deep enough that by David's death in 1153 Scotland had become,
Brown's own account argues, one of the more orderly and prosperous
kingdoms in a Christendom otherwise convulsed by civil war - a
peace and stability his own grandsons, Malcolm the Maiden and William
the Lion, would spend their reigns defending against renewed pressure
from an England that had, by then, learned to take Scotland seriously
as a rival rather than a troublesome neighbor.
