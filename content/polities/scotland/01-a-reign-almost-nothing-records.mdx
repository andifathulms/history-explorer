What actually happened when Kenneth mac Ailpín became ruler of both
the Picts and the Scots of Dál Riata survives in almost no detail at
all. The Chronicle of the Kings of Alba, the one native narrative
source for this period, states only that he received the kingdom of
Dál Riata around 840, and two years later began sixteen years of rule
"over this Pictland" - fighting the Britons and the Danes, invading
England six times, and burning Dunbar and Melrose in the process,
before he died at Forteviot in 858.

## A gap later centuries rushed to fill

That bare outline is nearly everything the earliest surviving evidence
actually says. Nothing in the Chronicle explains how Kenneth actually
brought the Picts under his rule, or why a Dál Riatan king succeeded
where earlier rulers of either people had not. Later medieval
chroniclers, writing centuries afterward, filled the silence with
elaborate stories of conquest and betrayal - a supposed massacre of
Pictish nobles at a banquet among the most vivid - that modern
historians treat as legend rather than history, constructed to explain
an outcome the sources never actually describe happening.

## What the silence itself suggests

Modern scholarship has grown more cautious still about what this
period's near-total absence of documentation actually permits anyone
to claim. Rather than a securely dated act of unification by a
powerful centralizing king, the evidence is at least as consistent
with a weaker, more gradual, and far less centrally directed process -
one the sources are simply too thin to describe with any confidence
either way. What is not in doubt is the outcome eventually visible in
later centuries: a single royal line, tracing its descent from
Kenneth, ruling a kingdom that called itself Alba.
