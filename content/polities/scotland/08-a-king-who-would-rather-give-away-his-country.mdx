Robert Bruce died in 1329 asking that his heart be carried on crusade;
Sir James Douglas died fulfilling the request, killed fighting the
Moors in Spain before he ever reached the Holy Land. It was an
appropriate opening to the reign of Bruce's own son, David II, crowned
at eight years old and fated to spend more of his reign as a captive
or an exile than as a free king in his own country.

## A puppet king and a real one

Within three years of David's coronation, Edward Balliol - son of the
king Edward I had installed in 1292 - landed in Fife backed by English
lords dispossessed of their Scottish estates, crushed the Scottish
regent's army at Dupplin Moor, and was crowned at Scone. Scottish
resistance regrouped fast enough to drive Balliol out within months,
but the English king intervened directly the following year, defeating
the new regent's army at Halidon Hill in 1333 so thoroughly that six
earls died on the field. David and his queen were sent to France for
safety, and for the better part of a decade Scotland fought a war of
recovery under a series of regents while Balliol, propped up by English
arms, ceded Berwick and the whole tract of country between the Tweed
and the Forth to his own patron. Among the resistance's most celebrated
moments was the nineteen-week defense of Dunbar Castle by Black Agnes,
Countess of March, who held the fortress against siege by land and sea
until the attackers gave up.

## Captured, ransomed, and willing to sell the bargain twice

David returned to a country largely cleared of the invader in 1341, but
proved, in the judgment of his own contemporary chroniclers, "young,
stout and jolly" rather than prudent. In 1346, campaigning in England's
interior at France's urging while Edward III was occupied besieging
Calais, David was routed and captured at the Battle of Neville's Cross.
He spent the next eleven years a prisoner in England while Robert the
Steward governed as best he could through a country already strained
by his ransom demands and then struck, in 1350, by the Black Death,
which by contemporary account killed a third of the population. David's
eventual release in 1357 came at a ransom of 100,000 marks payable over
ten years - a burden so heavy that his own subjects never fully paid
it off in his lifetime. In his final years David went further still,
secretly offering Edward III recognition as his own successor should
he die without a son, an arrangement that would have disinherited the
Stewart line entirely had David not died, in 1371, before it could take
effect. The throne passed instead to his nephew, Robert the Steward -
Robert II, the first king of the house of Stewart, whose own descendants
would occupy the Scottish throne for the rest of its independent
existence and beyond.
