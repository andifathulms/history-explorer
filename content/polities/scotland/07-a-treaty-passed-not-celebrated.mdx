Negotiations for a full union of the Scottish and English parliaments
had been attempted before and had failed; this time, after the Darien
disaster had weakened Scotland's bargaining position and English
legislation threatened to treat Scots as foreigners unless a union or
its abandonment was settled, both sides had genuine reasons to reach
an agreement. The treaty that resulted went to the Scottish Parliament
for ratification article by article through the autumn and winter of
1706-07, debated for nearly three months against organized opposition
both inside the chamber and, at times, in the streets outside it.

## A death in the night

The session's strain told on its participants. On the night after the
Parliament passed the treaty's twenty-second article - covering how
Scotland's new representatives would be chosen for the united
Parliament at Westminster - the Earl of Stair, who had worked harder
than any other Scottish statesman to bring the union about, was struck
with apoplexy and died before he could see the treaty he had spent
years pursuing receive its final sanction.

## Ratification

Eight days after Stair's death, on 16 January 1707, the Scottish
Parliament's Commissioner touched the Act of Union with the royal
sceptre, giving it final legal force. Difficult questions - the
distribution of a cash settlement called the Equivalent, the method of
choosing Scotland's members for the new Parliament of Great Britain -
took another two months to resolve before the session finally closed
that March. By the treaty's own terms, the union was not one kingdom
swallowing another: both the Scottish and English Parliaments ceased
to exist, replaced by a single new one. That distinction earned its
architects little public gratitude. Those who had steered the treaty
through, Stair included, were widely denounced by their own
countrymen in the years that followed not as statesmen who had secured
Scotland's future, but as men who had sold their country for their own
advantage.
