Malcolm III, called Canmore - bighead - ruled for thirty-six years as
the contemporary of Edward the Confessor, Harold, William the
Conqueror and William Rufus, and spent most of that reign trying to
push Scotland's southern border past the Tweed by force.

## Five invasions, and a marriage that outlasted them all

Malcolm crossed into England with an army five times, provoking the
construction or repair of four separate border castles - Carlisle,
Durham, Newcastle and Norham - built specifically to check him. His
marriage to the Saxon princess Margaret, sister of Edgar Atheling and
a refugee from the Norman Conquest, was as much a political alliance
as a personal one: it made Malcolm brother-in-law to the last credible
English claimant against William the Conqueror, and gave him a
plausible pretext for the raids he was already inclined to make.
Margaret's own influence reached well beyond the marriage bed - her
piety and her connections reoriented the Scottish court toward
England and the wider currents of reform Christianity, a shift that
would matter more to the kingdom's future than any of her husband's
campaigns.

## Two submissions, and a death Margaret did not survive

Malcolm's raids did not go unanswered. In 1072 William the Conqueror
led a fleet and army north and met him at Abernethy, where Malcolm
became William's man and handed over his own son Duncan as a hostage
for the arrangement. Two decades later William Rufus extracted a
similar submission after a failed invasion of his own, then compounded
the insult: when Malcolm traveled south to Gloucester under a pledge
of safe conduct to press the treaty's terms, Rufus refused even to see
him and treated him as nothing more than an English subject. Malcolm's
response was immediate and characteristic - he raised an army and
raided south a final time, ignoring his own wife's plea, made from her
sickbed, that he stay home. He was killed in an ambush on the banks
of the Alne in November 1093, betrayed, by one account, by a man who
had been counted among his closest friends. Margaret, already
gravely ill, died within days of hearing the news - and the court she
had spent thirty years reorienting toward England would, under her own
sons, complete the transformation her marriage had begun.
