Vijayasena's conquests passed around 1108 to his son Vallalasena,
remembered in Bengal tradition as Ballal Sen, whose own reputation
rests less on further conquest than on remaking the society his father
had won. He is credited with reorganizing the caste system and
introducing the practice known as Kulinism among Brahmans, Baidyas
and Kayasths - a hierarchy of ranked lineages within each caste that
would shape Bengali social structure for centuries after the dynasty
itself was gone. Some accounts credit him with founding the city of
Gaur, also called Lakhnauti, though Smith judges the city likely
predates him; a palace attributed to Ballal Sen is still pointed out
at Rampal near Bikrampur in the Dacca district.

## A Hindu throne against a Buddhist rival

Every Sena king was a Brahmanical Hindu, in explicit contrast to the
Buddhist Pala kings whose own territory the dynasty had taken - a
religious distinction Smith reads as a real source of hostility
between the two houses, sharpened by the Senas' own particular
interest in maintaining caste distinctions the Palas had never
enforced. Ballal Sen's own devotion ran to the Tantric strain of
Hinduism, and Bengali genealogists credit him with sending Brahman
missionaries as far afield as Magadha, Bhutan, Chittagong, Arakan,
Orissa and Nepal - a religious reach that outstripped anything his
father's own military conquests had achieved, and marked the high
point of Sena influence over Bengal's own cultural and religious life,
even as the dynasty's military position would prove, within two
generations, far less durable than its social reforms.
