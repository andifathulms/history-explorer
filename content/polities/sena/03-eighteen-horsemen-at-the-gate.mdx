By the time the throne passed to Lakshmanasena in 1119, the Sena
dynasty had ruled eastern Bengal for a generation as an established,
respected power - Smith records the Muhammadan chronicler's own
account of an aged king so widely revered that his family "was
respected by all the Rais or chiefs of Hindustan," credited with the
rank of hereditary spiritual head of the country and a reputation for
justice and generosity that outlasted the accuracy of the eighty-year
reign popularly, if erroneously, attributed to him. His court at
Nadia, on the Bhagirathi river north of the future site of Calcutta,
was already renowned as a seat of Hindu learning - the town still
carries the name of a college district today.

## A horse-trader's disguise

That standing collapsed in a single afternoon, probably in 1199.
Muhammad, son of Bakhtiyar - already notorious across Bihar for a
raiding campaign that had stormed the fortress-monastery of Bihar
with just two hundred horsemen two years before, slaughtering its
Buddhist monks so completely that no one literate in their own
libraries' books could afterward be found - rode ahead of his main
army with a party of only eighteen horsemen and entered Nadia
unopposed, the population mistaking him for an ordinary horse-trader.
At the gate of the royal palace he drew his sword and struck. The aged
king, caught at his own dinner table, fled barefoot out the rear of
the palace, abandoning his treasure, his household and his throne to
an invader who had taken the Sena capital with a force smaller than a
modest merchant caravan.

## The dishonoured end

Lakshmanasena escaped to Bikrampur in the Dacca district, where he
died still in exile; Muhammad destroyed Nadia itself and established
his own new capital at the ancient city of Gaur, endowing mosques,
colleges and monasteries there with the wealth his raid had seized,
and sending a substantial share of the plunder on to his own overlord,
Qutb al-Din. Smith's own verdict on the episode is unsparing: "the
dishonoured end of the last Hindu kingdom" of Bengal - a dynasty that
had spent a century and a half building a genuinely respected
Brahmanical state, undone not by a matched army in open battle but by
eighteen riders and the element of surprise.
