Political independence ended at Stainmore, but the society Halfdan's
partition had created did not simply revert to an ordinary English
shire once an English king ruled it directly again. More than a
century later, Domesday Book still recorded a countryside across
Yorkshire and Lincolnshire distinguished by an unusually large number
of free, land-owning peasants - sokemen - whose numbers had no real
equivalent in most of southern England.

## Defeats that loosened a hierarchy

Part of the explanation lies in the military disasters this kingdom
suffered across the early tenth century - the Chronicle names two
kings, two earls and at least five holds among the Danish dead at
Tettenhall in 910 alone, and Brunanburh cost five more kings and seven
earls in 937. Losses on that scale repeatedly weakened the local
aristocracy's grip, and gave smaller landowners real opportunities to
escape old obligations and claim fuller ownership of what they held -
a process the dense Scandinavian personal names embedded in later
place-names seem to register directly, marking a change in a
settlement's legal status rather than a new act of colonization.

## Small parishes, sokeland, and a landscape built from fragments

The same fragmentation shows up in the ground itself. Old estates that
survived largely intact elsewhere in England - the Kentish Weald is
the standard comparison - had broken up across the Yorkshire Wolds
into many small, independently held units well before 1066, and the
unusually high number of small parishes across the former Danelaw
descends directly from that same process: a parish, in effect, was
the religious shadow of a private estate, and a landscape of many
small estates produced a landscape of many small parishes. None of
this was planned by any king or conqueror. It was the accumulated
residue of a settlement that had begun as an army dividing up land in
876, and it was still legible in the historical record more than three
centuries after the kingdom that first divided it had ceased to exist.
