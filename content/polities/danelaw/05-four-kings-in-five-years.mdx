Æthelstan's conquest of York did not outlive him. When he died in 939
and his eighteen-year-old brother Edmund succeeded, the Norse of
Dublin and Ireland read the transition as their chance, and Olaf
Guthfrithson led an invading army that occupied York before the year
was out, apparently without resistance. Early in 940 Olaf pushed south
into the midlands, was checked at Northampton, then stormed Tamworth
and laid the surrounding country waste before Edmund finally met him
with an army at Leicester.

## A treaty that gave away twenty years of English rule

The two kings never came to battle. Before the armies engaged, the
archbishops of Canterbury and York brokered a treaty that handed Olaf
the entire region between Watling Street and the Northumbrian border -
the shires later known as Leicester, Derby, Nottingham and Lincoln,
governed by England for two decades. It was, by any measure, a
humiliating settlement: the first serious reverse the West Saxon
monarchy had suffered since Edward the Elder first began his advance
against the southern Danes a generation before.

## A throne that would not stay still

Olaf did not live to enjoy the recovery for long. He died within the
year raiding beyond the Tees, and York passed to his cousin, another
Olaf - Sihtricson, the same claimant Æthelstan had once driven out in
927, now received back as king. Weaker and less warlike than his
predecessor, he lost the Five Boroughs back to Edmund within two
years, and in 943 the Northumbrians themselves drove him out and chose
Raegnald, a brother of the first Olaf, in his place. Within the same
year both former rivals turned up separately at Edmund's own court and
were baptized there, the English king standing sponsor to each of
them in turn - a settlement of sorts, though not one built to last.
