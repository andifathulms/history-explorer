For nearly two decades after Halfdan's departure, the kingdom
centred on York is almost invisible to history. Its one certain figure
is Guthfrith, a Christian king remembered gratefully at Durham for his
friendship with St Cuthbert's clergy, who died at York on 24 August
895 and was buried in the city's minster. Even his own reign had
already gathered a mass of legend by the time later writers described
it, and what lies beneath the legend is now largely unrecoverable. The
kings who followed him are, in the driest sense, names only.

## What a buried hoard can say that a chronicle cannot

The one real window onto this period comes not from any written
account but from a hoard of coins found at Cuerdale, near Preston,
apparently buried early in the tenth century. Among its silver were
pieces struck for two otherwise unrecorded Northumbrian kings, Cnut
and Siefred. Siefred's coins that carry a mint-name were all struck at
York; Cnut's were minted both at York and, remarkably, at Quentovic on
the Norman coast. A king able to strike coin on both sides of the
Channel was ruling a genuine maritime power, whatever else remains
unknown about him - and one of the two, Siefred, is probably the same
raider whom a near-contemporary chronicler records harrying the
English coast in 894, while the Christian Guthfrith was apparently
still alive to see it.

## Two kings, one throne, and no way to reconcile them

Nothing in the coin evidence explains how Cnut and Siefred related to
each other, or to Guthfrith - whether as rivals, co-rulers, or
successive kings whose reigns the surviving record has simply
compressed. It is a problem this kingdom's own history will keep
posing in one form or another: written sources thin to almost nothing
for a stretch of years, and what fills the gap is a scatter of physical
evidence that raises as many questions as it answers.
