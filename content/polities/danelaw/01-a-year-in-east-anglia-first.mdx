The army that would eventually settle a third of England did not
arrive intending to. Brought to England in 865 by Ivar and Halfdan,
sons of the Danish leader Ragnar Lothbrok, it followed the ordinary
Scandinavian custom of shifting its winter quarters each autumn,
seizing a defensible position near a navigable river, fortifying it,
and living off the surrounding country until its people paid to be
left alone. For its first twelve months in England the army stayed in
East Anglia, securing horses rather than territory - a mounted force
would prove far harder to contain than a seaborne one.

## York, taken almost by accident of timing

In the autumn of 866 the army moved north and occupied York on 1
November. What let it hold the city unopposed for four months was not
its own strength so much as Northumbria's condition when it arrived:
the kingdom was in the middle of a civil war, its king Osberht
recently deposed in favour of a rival with no claim to royal birth.
Only when the two rivals had been brought to combine their forces did
a Northumbrian army finally attack the city, on 21 March 867. It broke
through York's poorly repaired Roman walls but could not hold the
ground inside them; both Northumbrian kings died in the fighting,
along with eight of their ealdormen, and the survivors bought their
own peace.

## An army still without a country

Nothing about the events of 865 to 867 yet amounted to a settlement.
The Great Army had won a city and broken a kingdom's leadership, but
it remained what it had always been - a mobile force living off
tribute and plunder, still moving on to its next target rather than
staying to govern what it had taken. That would not change for
another nine years.
