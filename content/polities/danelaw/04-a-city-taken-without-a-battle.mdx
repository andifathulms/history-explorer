York's independence had already survived one determined assault by
the time Æthelstan came to threaten it directly. Edward the Elder and
his sister Æthelflæd had reduced every Danish army south of the
Humber by the 920s, but the Norse kingdom in the north had escaped
their reach entirely - Æthelflæd died at Tamworth in 918 just as she
was receiving an offer of allegiance from York's own leading men, and
no comparable opening ever came to her brother. It fell to the next
generation to finish what the reconquest of the south had left undone.

## A death, a disputed succession, and an invasion

The opening came in 927. Sihtric, Norse king of York, had allied
himself with Æthelstan two years before, marrying his sister at
Tamworth - but Sihtric died before the summer, leaving the throne to
a young son, Olaf, too weak to hold it against the claims of his uncle
Guthfrith and the ambitions of the West Saxon king next door.
Æthelstan answered the succession crisis with an invasion. Both Olaf
and Guthfrith were driven from Northumbria within what can only have
been a short campaign; Guthfrith fled toward Scotland, was pursued as
far as a failed siege of York itself, and eventually surrendered and
was allowed to return to Ireland.

## What conquest looked like in practice

Æthelstan took York without a recorded battle for the city itself,
tore down the fortifications its Danish rulers had built inside its
walls, and distributed whatever treasure he found there among his own
followers. It was less a pitched engagement than the dismantling of an
independent government - the physical apparatus of a Scandinavian
kingdom stripped out of a city that would, for the next twelve years,
answer to a West Saxon king instead. Independent rule from York was
not yet finished for good, as later events would show, but for the
first time since Halfdan's settlement half a century earlier, it had
been broken.
