Edmund's successor Eadred inherited a Northumbria that had accepted
him without opposition - the archbishop of York and the northern
magnates swore fealty to him at Tanshelf in 947 - but the settlement
held for less than a year. Eric, called Bloodaxe, had just been driven
from the Norwegian throne by his own milder brother and turned his
fleet west, arriving in Northumbria with the one credential the local
Norse still valued: descent from the royal house of Norway itself. He
was accepted as king almost at once.

## A crown that changed hands four times in seven years

Eadred's answer was another invasion, one that burned the minster at
Ripon and, on the retreat south, was mauled at the crossing of the
Aire near Castleford - enough of a check that Eadred had to threaten
outright destruction of the country to force the Northumbrians to
abandon Eric anyway. His authority in York lasted only months. Olaf
Sihtricson, already twice a king at York and twice expelled, returned
around 949; Eric came back and drove him out again around 952; for two
years Eric reigned in open defiance of both the Dublin Norse and the
English crown - the last independent Scandinavian king this kingdom
would ever have.

## Stainmore

How Eric's second reign actually ended is one of the least certain
points in the whole record: the Norse tradition that survives reads
like reconstruction rather than memory, and the best contemporary
English source states only, without elaboration, that the
Northumbrians expelled him in 954 and Eadred took the kingdom
directly. A later tradition, current at Durham within two centuries of
the event, adds that Eric was killed afterward, betrayed by the
Northumbrian magnate Oswulf and cut down by a certain Maccus at a
place called Stainmore - a desolate crossing of the old Roman road
between Catterick and Carlisle that reads, in every account that
survives, less like a battle than the last stand of a king with
nowhere left to go.
