By the mid-870s the profits of raiding were thinning. England's most
portable wealth had already changed hands more than once, campaigns
against the Picts and the Britons of Strathclyde brought little reward
to ordinary fighters, and the terms the army had already imposed on
Mercia in 874 show its leaders were beginning to plan something more
permanent than another season of tribute-taking. In 876 that planning
became visible: Halfdan divided the country around York among his own
followers as farmland, in the first of what would become three
comparable partitions across eastern England.

## Land, not loot

The scale of the change is visible in the ground itself. Yorkshire
place-names of Scandinavian origin - many built from personal names
found nowhere else in England - are dense enough across the region to
mark a genuinely intensive settlement, not a scattering of garrisons.
Halfdan himself left England not long after completing the division,
apparently to join Scandinavian fighting in Ireland; the country he
had organized continued under kings whose names survive mostly as
names, their history almost entirely undocumented, while further
north and west the older pattern of English rule persisted under
figures like Eadwulf of Bamburgh, remembered as a friend of King
Alfred's.

## A frontier, not yet a single kingdom

What 876 produced was a settled Danish presence with York at its
centre, not yet a unified Scandinavian kingdom in any formal sense.
The land north of the Tees and west of the Pennines saw no comparable
Scandinavian immigration for another half-century; England, in
effect, now had two frontiers rather than one - a Danish-settled south
and east around York, and an English-held north still finding its own
way to survive alongside it.
