---
title: Ruling a country that was already Christian
drafted_from: macevitt-2008
phase: expansion
---

The standing image of the Crusader states is a Frankish garrison sitting on a
Muslim population. For Edessa that is close to backwards.

The county's people were overwhelmingly Eastern Christian — Armenian and Syriac
Orthodox, with Greek and Muslim minorities — and the Latin element was a few
hundred knights and their households in a country of hundreds of thousands.
MacEvitt's argument, for which Edessa is the central case, is that what
developed there was neither the systematic persecution of the older accounts
nor anything resembling tolerance as a principle. It was an unarticulated
working accommodation: local lords kept their lands, Syriac and Armenian
bishops kept their sees and their disputes, Frankish and Armenian families
intermarried at the top, and nobody wrote down a theory of what was going on
because writing it down would have required deciding questions everyone was
better off leaving open.

The county was administered in Latin, Armenian and Syriac at once, and not as a
courtesy. The charters were Latin. The men who collected the revenue, held the
smaller castles and knew where the boundaries ran were mostly not.

The army says the rest. Fiefs held of the count, in the ordinary Frankish way,
over a Latin fighting strength counted in the hundreds throughout the county's
fifty-two years. Antioch, Tripoli and Jerusalem were all thin. Edessa was thin
and also landlocked, which is the difference that killed it: it was the one
Crusader state that no fleet could reach, four hundred kilometres from the sea,
dependent for reinforcement on a road through Antioch that an enemy holding
Aleppo could cut.

Nobody has published a figure for how much ground it covered, and the figure
would mislead if anybody did. The county was large, and it was empty of the
thing that mattered, which was Franks.
