title: Ruling a country that was already Christian

The standing image of the Crusader states is a Frankish garrison sitting on a
Muslim population. For Edessa that is close to backwards.

The county's people were overwhelmingly Eastern Christian — Armenian and Syriac
Orthodox, with Greek and Muslim minorities — and the Latin element was a few
hundred knights and their households in a country of hundreds of thousands.
MacEvitt's argument, for which Edessa is the central case, is that what
developed there was neither the systematic persecution of the older accounts nor
anything resembling tolerance as a principle. It was an unarticulated working
accommodation: local lords kept their lands, Syriac and Armenian bishops kept
their sees and their disputes, Frankish and Armenian families intermarried at
the top, and nobody wrote down a theory of what was going on because writing it
down would have required deciding questions everyone was better off leaving
open.

This site's `scripts_and_languages` coding for the page records `administration`
as Latin, Armenian and Syriac together, and the value is doing real work rather
than being generous. The charters were Latin. The men who collected the revenue,
held the smaller castles and knew where the boundaries ran were mostly not.

The military coding says the rest. `land-grant` — fiefs held of the count, the
ordinary Frankish arrangement — over a Latin fighting strength counted in the
hundreds throughout the county's fifty-two years. Antioch, Tripoli and Jerusalem
were all thin. Edessa was thin and also landlocked, which is the difference that
killed it: it was the one Crusader state that no fleet could reach, four hundred
kilometres from the sea, dependent for reinforcement on a road through Antioch
that an enemy holding Aleppo could cut.

`reach_km2` on this page is null and will stay null unless a source publishes a
figure. It would be a misleading measurement in any case. The county was large
and it was empty of the thing that mattered, which was Franks.
