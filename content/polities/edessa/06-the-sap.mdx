The Damascene chronicler's account of how Edessa fell is a technical one, and
that is what makes it useful: he is interested in the engineering.

The besiegers drove a mine under the wall, shoring the tunnel as they went with
timbers and special appliances. When it was finished, all that remained was to
set fire to the shoring. Zangi went down into the sap himself to see what had
been done, expressed his admiration, and gave the order.

The fire caught the timbers, the timbers went, and the wall came down with
them. The Muslims forced their way in over the breach with heavy losses on both
sides, and so many of the Franks and Armenians were killed and wounded that the
rest abandoned the town. It was taken by the sword in the early forenoon of
Saturday 23 December 1144.

## What the victor did next

The troops sacked it — killing, capturing and looting — until, Qalanisi says,
their hands were filled with money, furnishings, animals and captives enough to
gladden their hearts.

Then Zangi gave orders to stop the killing and the plundering, and set about
rebuilding what had been destroyed and repairing what was damaged. He detailed
a picked force to govern and defend the city, and reassured the inhabitants
with promises of good treatment and of justice for all of them, near and far.

That sequence is not a contradiction and should not be softened into one. A
storming was expected to be followed by a sack; a ruler who intended to keep a
city stopped the sack when he judged it had gone far enough and started paying
for roofs. Both halves are the same policy, and the second half is why Edessa
was still a functioning city under his successors.

## Everything else fell by itself

He marched on to Saruj, which the Franks had already abandoned, and took it.
After that, Qalanisi notes, there was not a place or a stronghold he passed and
camped in front of that was not immediately handed over.

Only al-Bira held — a castle of the utmost impregnability, he says — and Zangi
sat down in front of it and cut off everything and everyone trying to reach it.

<Figure id="ed-bira" />

That is the measure of what 1144 did. The county of Edessa had held its ground
for forty-six years against everything sent at it; and once the capital was
gone, the rest of it surrendered on sight, in the order in which the army
happened to arrive.

## The proof

The Frankish states in Syria had been founded on the assumption that they could
not be dislodged, an assumption every ruler in the region had been testing for
half a century without success.

The mine under the wall of Edessa ended that. Everything afterwards — the
programme of Nur al-Din, the reconquest under Saladin, and, eventually, the
Mamluk sultans working down the coast city by city — proceeds from a night in
December 1144 when somebody set fire to the props and stood back to watch.
