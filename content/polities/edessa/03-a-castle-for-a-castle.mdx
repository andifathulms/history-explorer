---
title: A castle for a castle
drafted_from: ibn-al-qalanisi
phase: peak
---

The county at its height is not visible in a battle. It is visible in the fact
that the atabeg of Damascus received a letter from the lord of Tell Bashir
proposing a swap of fortresses and a mutual non-aggression pact, and had to
decide what to do about it.

## The proposal

Joscelin of Courtenay had fallen out with his own maternal uncle, Baldwin the
Little, who held Edessa, and had gone over to the king of Jerusalem, who gave him
Tiberias as a fief. The two of them then worked out a plan.

Joscelin would write to Zahir al-Din, atabeg of Damascus, offering friendship and
urging the establishment of peaceful relations. He would hand over the castle of
Thamanin near Jabal Amila, and receive in exchange the castle of Habis in the
Sawad, together with half the Sawad itself. And he would guarantee, on the king
of Jerusalem's behalf, that there would be no further attacks on any province of
Damascus — provided Damascus attacked no Frankish province in return.

That is a completely ordinary piece of twelfth-century diplomacy: a territorial
exchange, a defined frontier, and a bilateral truce, negotiated between a
Frankish count and a Turkish atabeg, with a Frankish king's ratification promised
in advance.

Nothing in it treats the other side as an anomaly. Ibn al-Qalanisi records the
terms the way he records terms between Muslim rulers, because that is what they
were.

## The refusal

Zahir al-Din rejected the proposals — and the reason was not that the Franks were
untrustworthy.

He set out from Damascus with his *askar* to meet the amir Mawdud and join him
in the holy war. They met at Marj Salamiya, agreed to attack Baldwin, and went
together. The atabeg took the whole Damascene army with contingents from Homs,
Hama and Rafaniya, encamped at Qadas on the Feast of Sacrifice, and marched by
Ain al-Jar and the Biqa to Banyas.

So the two things were live at the same moment, in the same council: a
negotiated frontier with the Franks, and a coalition to destroy them. Damascus
chose the second that year. It would choose the first in other years, and did.

## What peace looked like when it held

The chronicle shows the alternative working a decade later, on the northern
frontier, in a passage that is easy to read past.

An armistice was concluded between the lord of Aleppo and the Franks, on
condition that the castle of Atharib be surrendered to the Franks. It was handed
over and they took possession. Then: *friendly relations were maintained on this
footing, the provinces prospered on both sides, and the roads became* safe.

A castle changes hands, the frontier is agreed, trade moves, and a Damascene
chronicler writes it up as good news for everybody.

That is the county of Edessa at its peak — not a crusader outpost besieged in a
hostile sea, but one of half a dozen small powers in northern Syria that fought
each other, allied with each other, exchanged castles, and occasionally kept the
roads open. Joscelin's own career runs on those terms: he quarrels with his
uncle, takes service with a king, negotiates with Damascus, raids when it suits
him, and is eventually taken prisoner at Saruj by Balak ibn Ortuq along with his
cousin and a number of his leaders.

Being captured by a neighbour is a normal risk for a regional lord. It is a very
different condition from the one the county ended in.
