The recurring problem of the county of Edessa was that its rulers kept being
captured.

It was a frontier state with a Latin fighting strength in the hundreds, holding
a long border against Turkish lords who raided constantly and who had learned
that the most valuable thing on the far side of it was a Frankish commander.
Ransoms were enormous. So the counts led from the front, out of necessity, and
were taken prisoner at a rate no other crusader state matched.

## Two in six months

In the autumn of 1122 the Artuqid amir Balak marched against the Franks of
Edessa, fell on them at Saruj and defeated them, taking prisoner their
commander Joscelin, together with his cousin Galeran and a number of their
leaders.

The following April, Baldwin — count of Edessa before he became king of
Jerusalem — moved on Aleppo to deal with Balak, who was besieging a castle. The
armies met near Manzara. The king was defeated and captured, along with a
number of his principal knights, and Balak put him in a dungeon in the castle
of Kharput, together with Joscelin and the rest.

So within six months the same man had taken the count of Edessa and the king of
Jerusalem and had them both in the same fortress, four hundred kilometres from
anywhere either of them governed.

## The escape

What happened next is one of the strangest episodes of the period, and Ibn
al-Qalanisi's text at this point is damaged, which is a pity because he had the
story.

What survives is this: the Frankish prisoners in Kharput devised a stratagem
among themselves, seized the castle, and got out. One of them escaped and could
not be recaptured. On the same day the bishop of al-Bara, imprisoned there too,
got away from his confinement.

Then Balak marched on Kharput with his troops and besieged his own citadel
until he had taken it back from the Franks who had surprised it, and put a
garrison in it with instructions to be more careful.

The prisoners who did not get away were still prisoners, in a fortress they had
briefly captured.

## What it cost

Rulers of Edessa spent years of their reigns in captivity, and the county spent
those years being governed by whoever was left and paying for whoever was not.
Ransoms went east, hostages went with them, and the frontier castles that
required a lord's attention did not get it.

That is the ordinary condition of the county for most of its existence, and the
reason it was the crusader state that could least afford a bad year. When one
finally came, in 1144, there was no reserve of anything: no army beyond the
garrison, no fleet that could reach it, and no ruler in the city.
