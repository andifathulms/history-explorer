Joscelin II had moved his residence west of the Euphrates to Turbessel and was
there in November 1144 when Zengi, atabeg of Mosul and Aleppo, appeared before
Edessa with an army. The city had walls and no field force. The count raised
what he could and did not arrive. Antioch, with whose prince Joscelin was
quarrelling, sent nothing. Jerusalem sent a relief column that got there too
late.

Edessa fell on 24 December 1144, after four weeks.

The county was finished when the city fell — a landlocked state named for its
capital, with the capital gone — but it took six more years to stop existing.
Joscelin held the fragment west of the Euphrates until 1150, when he was
captured; his wife sold what was left to the Byzantines, and Nur al-Din took it
off them within months. The same dynasty did both ends of it.

What 1144 changed is larger than the county. It was the first demonstration
that a Frankish state in the Levant could be destroyed, forty-five years after
the first one was founded, and it converted the counter-crusade from a series
of raids into a programme with a proof behind it. Zangi and Nur al-Din are
usually identified as the point at which the Muslim response to the crusades
acquired an ideology. Edessa is where it acquired the evidence.

In Europe the news produced the Second Crusade, which arrived in 1148, decided
not to attempt Edessa at all, besieged Damascus for four days, and went home.
Damascus was at that moment the one Muslim power in Syria still hostile to the
Zangids. The crusade raised to avenge the loss of Edessa succeeded chiefly in
pushing Damascus into Nur al-Din's hands, and ended the independent Burid
emirate there.
