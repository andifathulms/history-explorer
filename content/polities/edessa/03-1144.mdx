Joscelin II had moved his residence west of the Euphrates to Turbessel and was
there in November 1144 when Zengi, atabeg of Mosul and Aleppo, appeared before
Edessa with an army. The city had walls and no field force. The count raised
what he could and did not arrive. Antioch, with whose prince Joscelin was
quarrelling, sent nothing. Jerusalem sent a relief column that got there too
late.

Edessa fell on 24 December 1144, after four weeks.

The edge from this record to the Zangid page is dated 1144 and typed `conquered
by`, and the span on this page runs four years past it. Both are correct. The
county as a polity ended when the city fell — a landlocked state named for its
capital, with the capital gone — but Joscelin held the trans-Euphrates fragment
until 1150, when he was captured, his wife sold what remained to the Byzantines,
and Nur al-Din took it off them within months. This record's `ended` is dated to
that final extinction and its `by` names the Zangids, who did both.

What 1144 changed is larger than the county. It was the first demonstration that
a Frankish state in the Levant could be destroyed, forty-five years after the
first one was founded, and it converted the counter-crusade from a series of
raids into a programme with a proof behind it. The Zangid page on this site
argues that Zengi and Nur al-Din are the point at which the Muslim response
acquires an ideology. Edessa is where they acquired the evidence.

In Europe the news produced the Second Crusade, which arrived in 1148, decided
not to attempt Edessa at all, besieged Damascus for four days, and went home.
Damascus was at that moment the one Muslim power in Syria still hostile to the
Zangids. The crusade raised to avenge the loss of Edessa succeeded chiefly in
pushing Damascus into Nur al-Din's hands, which is where the Burid record on
this site ends.
