Order, when it came back, came from a city just south of the Fayum that
had been a provincial cult centre since the earliest dynasties and had
never before produced a king. Herakleopolis's own nomarchs succeeded in
placing one of their number on the throne, founding the line Manetho
counted as the Ninth and Tenth Dynasties: eighteen kings in succession,
by his own reckoning, over roughly a century.

Almost none of them left anything. The line's founder, a man Manetho
calls Akhthoes, survives in exactly one anecdote, and it is not a
flattering one: he was, by this account, the most violent king of his
age, and was eventually seized with madness and killed by a crocodile.
Whether that story is closer to history or to the kind of thing later
Egyptians said about a dynasty they had no reason to admire is not
something any source consulted here can settle. What is certain is that
a family from a town with no previous claim on the kingship took it, by
force, from whatever remained of the old Memphite line.

## What order looked like from Siut

The clearest surviving picture of the Herakleopolitan kingdom does not
come from Herakleopolis itself, but from the cliff tombs of a family of
nomarchs a hundred and thirty miles to the south, at Siut. Their
inscriptions are almost the only contemporary record of this dynasty
that survives at all, and what they describe, once the Herakleopolitan
kings had a real grip on the country, is a restoration worth boasting
about:

> Every official was at his post, there was no one fighting, nor any
> shooting an arrow. The child was not smitten beside his mother, nor the
> citizen beside his wife. There was no evil-doer... nor any one doing
> violence against his house.

Another Siut lord put the same claim in terms of a traveller rather than
a household:

> When night came, he who slept on the road gave me praise, for he was
> like a man in his house; the fear of my soldier was his protection.

Whatever these lines exaggerate for a tomb wall's own purposes, they are
making a specific claim, and it is not one the previous chapter's
evidence would have supported: that a period which had begun in
destruction with no author on record had, within a generation or two,
produced a countryside safe enough to sleep in unguarded.

## What the restoration was not

It was not a return to the Old Kingdom's own arrangement. The Siut
nomarchs who describe this security are not describing a bureaucracy
answering to Herakleopolis in the way the nomes had once answered to
Memphis; they are describing their own domain, kept orderly by their own
soldiers, under a king they served but did not depend on for their local
authority. The nomarchs who had broken the Old Kingdom apart were still
there, still hereditary, still armed. What Herakleopolis restored was not
the old centralised state but a working peace between a throne and the
regional lords who now answered to it on their own terms.
