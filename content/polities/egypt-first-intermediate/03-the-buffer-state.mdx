South of Herakleopolis, another regional power had been rising for
longer than anyone at court might have wished to notice. Thebes, then an
obscure provincial town, had produced a family of nomarchs - the Intefs
- ambitious enough that by the time an early Intef held the title
"keeper of the Door of the South," the whole southern stretch of the
valley was answering to Thebes rather than to Herakleopolis. Intef
organised the South in open rebellion, and it was the Herakleopolitan
kingdom's good fortune, rather than its own strength, that its most
loyal vassals happened to sit exactly between the two capitals.

## Tefibi's war

The Siut nomarchs were that vassal. When the Theban confederation pushed
north, Tefibi of Siut - probably the son of the Kheti whose own tomb
first described the countryside made safe - put his own army in the
field against them. He met one Theban force on the west bank and drove
it back, recovering territory as far south as a fortress the inscriptions
call the "Port of the South." A second army advancing on the east bank
was beaten the same way: its fleet driven ashore, its commander forced
into the river, the ships themselves apparently taken as prizes.

For a moment, this looks like a war Herakleopolis might win.

## The king-maker

Tefibi's own son, also named Kheti, inherited both the military command
and the loyalty that came with it. Appointed "commander of the whole
land" and "great lord of Middle Egypt," he suppressed a rebellion on the
southern frontier in his sovereign Merikare's name and then brought the
king south in person, apparently to receive the submission of the
reconquered districts directly. Kheti's own account of the return
journey is proud rather than modest: his fleet, he says, stretched for
miles up the river as it passed his home, and at Herakleopolis, "the
city came, rejoicing over her lord... women mingled with men, old men
and children."

This is the high-water mark the Herakleopolitan side of the war left any
record of - a king escorted home in triumph by a vassal's army, at a
moment when the line that would eventually destroy his kingdom had
already been pushed back once. Nothing in the Siut tombs suggests anyone
at the time thought this was the war's final chapter.
