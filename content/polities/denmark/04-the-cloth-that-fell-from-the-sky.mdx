Valdemar II, called the Victorious, had already conquered Holstein
and the lands south to the Elbe as Duke of Slesvig before he ever
took the Danish throne in 1202, and the Emperor's own recognition of
him as Lord of Northalbingia afterward put Lübeck and Hamburg under
Danish overlordship as well. He pushed further still into the Baltic,
seizing the island of Ösel off Estonia and then, answering an appeal
from the Bishop of Riga, launching a crusade against the pagan
Estonians with a fleet of some fourteen hundred vessels.

## A banner from the sky

Tradition holds that at the Battle of Lyndanise near Reval in 1219,
with the Danish standard lost and the army hard-pressed, a red cloth
bearing a white cross fell from the sky - and the Danes, rallying
around it, won the field. Whether the banner was really a consecrated
gift sent by the Pope for the crusade or a story that grew up around
one afterward, the Dannebrog has been Denmark's national flag ever
since, and it still appears today in the arms of Reval, the city that
grew up around the fortress Valdemar built there. By the early 1220s
Denmark held the whole southern Baltic coast from the Elbe to Lake
Peipus - a dominion, in Stefánsson's own assessment, wider than any
power in Northern Europe held outside England, and one Denmark had
not matched since Cnut the Great very nearly a full two centuries
before.

## One night in May

It came apart in a single stroke. One of Valdemar's own German
vassals, Count Henry of Schwerin, nursed a grievance over a fief the
king had taken from him, and on the night of 6 May 1223, while
Valdemar and his eldest son were hunting on the small island of Lyø,
Henry seized them both asleep in their tent and carried them off to
his own castle at Dannenberg on the Elbe. Valdemar's German vassals
rose against Denmark the moment word spread, and after two and a
half years of captivity the king bought his freedom in November 1225
at a staggering price: forty-five thousand marks of silver, every
jewel the queen owned, costly outfits for a hundred knights, hostages,
and the surrender of every conquest he held except Rügen. Sixty years
of conquest by three successive kings were undone in a single night's
treachery. Absolved by the Pope of the oath his captors had forced on
him, Valdemar tried once more to reverse the loss - and was defeated,
and lost an eye, at the Battle of Bornhöved on 22 July 1227. He
formally ceded Northalbingia to the Emperor that same catastrophic
year, keeping only Rügen of the empire he had built. What Valdemar
turned to afterward was law rather than conquest: he codified the
laws of Zealand himself, and the Jutland law code he commissioned was
finished only days before his death in 1241 - the lasting achievement,
in the end, of a king better remembered by his own century as the
Lawmaker than as the conqueror he had once been.
