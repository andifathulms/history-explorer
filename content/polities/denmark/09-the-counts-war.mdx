Frederick I's death in April 1533 split Denmark between Protestant
and Catholic factions over his succession, and into that vacuum
stepped Jürgen Wullenwever, the radical new burgomaster of Lübeck,
who saw a chance to dismember Denmark under cover of restoring the
exiled Christian II. His hired commander, Count Christopher of
Oldenburg, overran eastern Denmark within weeks in the summer of
1534, while an assembly of Jutland nobles elected the elder,
Lutheran son of Frederick I as Christian III that same July. The
conflict took its name, the Count's War, from Wullenwever's own
proxy. Jutland peasants rose in Christian III's favour under a leader
known as Skipper Clement and beat the nobles at Svenstrup that
October - but the royalist general Johan Rantzau reconquered the
province within a month, stormed Aalborg, and put some two thousand
peasants to the sword there in December; the yeomanry who survived
were reduced to bondage as tenants, and Skipper Clement himself was
executed.

## A year's siege and a fleet destroyed

Rantzau went on to crush Count Christopher's own army at Øxnebjerg in
Funen in June 1535, mortally wounding Archbishop Trolle in the
process, while a combined Danish-Swedish fleet under Peder Skram
annihilated Lübeck's navy at sea. With the Lübeck-backed cause
broken on both land and water, Christian III crossed to Zealand and
laid siege to Copenhagen itself. The city held out for a full year,
hoping for relief from Christian II's son-in-law or from the Emperor
Charles V, and endured what Stefánsson calls the full horrors of
famine before finally surrendering on 29 July 1536. Its defenders
walked bareheaded to the royal camp with white staffs in hand to beg
pardon, and received a general amnesty. Two centuries of Lübeck's
dominance over Scandinavian trade ended with the same defeat.

## One morning in August

With the civil war won, Christian III moved against the Catholic
Church itself, following Gustaf Vasa's Swedish precedent of seizing
episcopal estates - but on his officers' advice he struck in a single
coordinated stroke rather than announcing his intentions first. On
the night before his own birthday, the Archbishop and every prelate
present in Copenhagen were arrested, and by eight o'clock the next
morning, 12 August 1536, the kingdom's temporal councillors had been
compelled to sign away the bishops' civil authority and hand their
estates to the crown. A national assembly of twelve hundred
representatives, the largest Denmark had ever seen, met that October
and formally established a national Protestant church: bishops were
replaced by Lutheran superintendents, church property passed to the
crown, and the king became head of the church in his own right. The
same day's royal charter laid such stress on Christian III's own
hereditary claim that Danish kingship, though still nominally
elective, became hereditary in practice - and the charter went
further still, formally reducing Norway from a separate kingdom to a
mere province of the Danish crown, on the stated grounds that Norway
could no longer sustain a sovereign of its own. The clause proved
more aspiration than fact - Norway kept its own laws and
administration, and state papers went on calling it a kingdom - but
it set the pattern of Danish domination that would govern the union
for the better part of three centuries to come.
