Denmark's alliance with Napoleon, entered in fury after the 1807
bombardment, left the kingdom clinging to a losing side as the tide
of the wars turned. Sweden's new Crown Prince, the former French
marshal Bernadotte, secured Russian backing to claim Norway as
compensation for Sweden's own loss of Finland, and by 1813 Norway
itself was suffering famine under a naval blockade that cut it off
from Danish supply entirely. The Danish heir, Prince Christian
Frederik, crossed to Norway disguised as a fisherman in a small boat
in May 1813 and took up the viceroyalty there, becoming, by
Stefánsson's account, a genuinely popular favourite among a
population increasingly drawn to separatism regardless. Denmark's
own currency had by this point sunk to a fourteenth of its face
value, the kingdom effectively bankrupt.

## The treaty that ended four centuries

After Napoleon's defeat at Leipzig, Bernadotte marched into Holstein,
and Frederick VI, despite a brave Danish stand at Sehested, could no
longer avoid the inevitable. By the Treaty of Kiel, signed 14 January
1814, Denmark ceded Norway itself - though not Iceland, Greenland or
the Faroes, which stayed with the Danish crown - to Sweden, and the
island of Heligoland to Britain, in exchange for Swedish Pomerania,
Rügen, and a million rixdollars (Pomerania and Rügen were themselves
traded to Prussia the following year for the Duchy of Lauenburg and a
further two million). It closed a union between Denmark and Norway
that had lasted, in one form or another, more than four hundred
years. Frederick VI, whom Stefánsson holds largely responsible for
clinging so obstinately to the French alliance that produced this
outcome, attended the Congress of Vienna in person seeking better
terms and came away with none - though he was greeted, on his return
to a humiliated and bankrupt Denmark, with genuine popular sympathy
rather than blame.

## What grew back

The material and political wounds were real - a dismembered kingdom,
a stolen navy, ruined finances - but the years that followed brought
an unlikely compensation. What Danish literary history calls the
Golden Age reached its full maturity in the generation between 1810
and 1830, producing Oehlenschläger, Grundtvig, Kierkegaard and Hans
Christian Andersen within a single national literature at almost the
same moment the kingdom itself had never stood smaller on the map.
Frederick VI, who had no personal interest in literature himself,
spent his remaining years working to restore Danish finances and
heal the war's wounds; the free constitution his own subjects hoped
an enlightened absolutist king might grant them, having already freed
the peasantry from serfdom decades before, remained, for the whole of
his own reign, a promise deferred rather than delivered.
