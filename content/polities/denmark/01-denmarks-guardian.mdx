Denmark appears in written record long before it had a king anyone
could reliably name. Roman writers placed the Cimbri in Jutland
centuries before Christ, and Augustus himself boasted on an
inscription at Ankyra that his fleet had sailed to "the country of
the Cimbrians, to which no Roman had ever penetrated before that
time by sea or by land." The name Denmark itself first appears in
writing far later still, in King Alfred's own translation of
Orosius around 890. Between those two points lie centuries of
half-legendary kings - the medieval chronicler Saxo Grammaticus
names no fewer than fifty of them reigning before the Viking Age
even began, a list no more reliable than the founding genealogies
other early medieval kingdoms produced for themselves once they
wanted an ancient pedigree.

## A queen who built a wall

The first Danish ruler whose own words survive is Gorm the Old, and
they survive because he had them carved in stone. Sometime in the
mid-tenth century, Gorm raised a runic monument at Jelling for his
dead queen, Thyri, with an inscription naming her "Denmark's
guardian" - a title she earned, the tradition held, by organizing
the building of the Danevirke, an earthwork wall stretching nearly
ten miles from the Bay of Slien to the river Eider to defend
Denmark's southern frontier, each province responsible for its own
stretch of the work. It is the earliest occurrence of the name
Denmark found anywhere within Denmark itself.

## The king baptized by fire

Gorm's son, Harald Bluetooth, succeeded him and became, by the
Saxon monk Widukind of Corvey's own account written in 970, the
first king to make all of Denmark Christian. The occasion, as
Widukind tells it, was a trial: a German missionary priest named
Poppo carried red-hot iron in his bare hands before the king,
around the year 960, and was unhurt - proof enough, in the terms
that mattered to a Viking-age court, to bring Harald to baptism.
Archbishop Adaldag of Hamburg had already been quietly building a
Danish church in the decades before, appointing bishops to Slesvig,
Ribe and Aarhus, but it was Harald's own conversion that made
Christianity the religion of the Danish crown itself rather than
merely a mission working at its edges. Harald went on to subdue
southern Norway, and Earl Hakon of Norway became his vassal -
though Hakon himself refused to follow his overlord into the new
faith.
