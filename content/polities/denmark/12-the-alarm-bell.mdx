Frederick III, learned and reserved where his father had been
boisterous, was elected only after signing a charter that curtailed
royal power further still - and spent his early reign disgracing the
aristocratic leaders who had dominated the previous regency, forcing
Hannibal Sehested to surrender his estates over embezzlement charges
and driving Corfitz Ulfeldt into exile on suspicion of a plot against
the crown. A war against Sweden launched while Charles X was
distracted in Poland went badly, but Copenhagen's own heroic defence
- king and commoners together - left the nobility's continued
exemption from taxation looking harder to justify than ever.

## "Slaves who ought to keep within their limits"

When the Estates assembled in September 1660, the burgesses and
clergy demanded that new indirect taxes apply to the nobles as well,
and after bitter resistance forced the point, the nobles calling
them "slaves who ought to keep within their limits" as they yielded.
Sensing the moment, the burgesses and clergy then offered Frederick
III the crown as a hereditary possession in gratitude for his wartime
leadership - and called on the nobles to agree. The noble leaders
refused, until their spokesman, meeting Burgomaster Hans Nansen in
the street, gestured toward the state prison as a threat; Nansen
answered by pointing to the alarm bell of Our Lady's Church, the
signal that called Copenhagen's citizens to arms. With the city
gates closed, the garrison secured, and armed burghers in the
streets, the King pressed the Council for a swift answer, and on 13
October the nobility yielded: Denmark became a hereditary monarchy
under Frederick III and his heirs.

## A constitution that promised everything and appeared once

Frederick surrendered his own coronation charter and was released
from every oath binding him to it, empowered to issue a new
constitution "as to His Majesty should seem best." On 18 October
1660 he received the formal homage of every order and rank in an
amphitheatre before the royal palace, promising a fair and just
constitution to come - a promise never heard of again. The State
Council's own archives were moved into the palace as a sign that the
body itself had ceased to exist, and Denmark's Estates would not meet
again for nearly two centuries. A circulated declaration in January
1661 had subjects across the Danish dominions formally confer
absolute government on the king and his heirs; the constitution
itself, the Lex Regia, was drafted in secret by the king's own
secretary, Peter Schumacher, signed on 14 November 1665, and kept
hidden until Frederick's death five years later. It vested every
legislative, executive and judicial power of the state in the king
personally, acknowledging no earthly superior, bound only by his own
Lutheran faith and a promise to keep the kingdom undivided - and
declared, by its own twenty-sixth article, that any attempt to alter
it was itself high treason.
