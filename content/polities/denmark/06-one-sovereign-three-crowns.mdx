Valdemar Atterdag left no son, and with him the male line that had
ruled Denmark since Sven Estrithson died out. His daughter Margaret,
married at ten to King Håkon VI of Norway and already showing, in
letters written in her own hand by nineteen, a firm grip on matters of
government, secured the election of her five-year-old son Oluf as
King of Denmark in 1376. When Håkon died in 1380, Oluf inherited
Norway too, joining the two crowns in a union that would last, as it
turned out, for four hundred and thirty-four years. Margaret governed
both kingdoms as regent in her son's name - until Oluf himself died
suddenly in 1387, leaving her a childless queen whose formal authority
should, by every normal rule, have ended with him.

## A woman ruling the largest realm in Europe

It did not. Denmark's councillors elected her "Sovereign Lady, the
Guardian of the Realm" within months, Norway followed the year after,
and discontented Swedish noblemen, eager to be rid of their own
unpopular King Albrecht, offered her the same title in Sweden on terms
heavily favouring their own interests. Albrecht tested her at the
Battle of Falköping in 1389 and was taken prisoner outright - "God
gave an unexpected victory into the hands of a woman," one
contemporary chronicle marvelled, while another recorded that all the
nobility of Denmark had come to fear this one woman's wisdom and
strength. A childless queen now ruled, in practice if not yet in
name, the largest monarchy in Europe. She spent the years afterward
consolidating rather than resting on the victory: forcing Swedish and
Danish nobles alike to surrender crown lands they had acquired in
recent decades, leaving the highest offices of state deliberately
vacant so that authority stayed gathered in her own hands, and reforming
a debased currency with new silver coin.

## Crowned three times at once

To bind the three kingdoms together beyond her own lifetime, Margaret
had her grand-nephew Eric of Pomerania proclaimed King of Norway at
seven and elected King of Denmark and Sweden in the years that
followed. In June 1397 she summoned the lords of all three realms to
Kalmar, and on Trinity Sunday, 17 June, the Archbishops of Lund and
Uppsala crowned Eric king of Denmark, Sweden and Norway simultaneously
- one coronation for three thrones, a deliberate symbol of the union
itself. The assembled lords spent weeks afterward drafting terms to
formalize what the coronation had already declared: eternal and
unbroken peace between the three kingdoms under a single sovereign.
Only one of the two resulting documents was ever actually sealed and
ratified; the more detailed constitutional draft survived with just
ten of seventeen councillors' seals attached, seven Swedish, three
Danish, and not a single Norwegian one - an imperfect founding
charter for a union that would nonetheless bind the Scandinavian
crowns together, on and off, for more than a century to come.
