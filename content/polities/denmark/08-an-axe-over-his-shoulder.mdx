Gustaf Eriksson Vasa had been carried off to Denmark as a hostage in
1518, escaped a year later disguised as a horse-dealer, and was in
hiding on his father's Swedish estate when a peasant brought him news
of the Stockholm Massacre: his own father and brother-in-law
executed, his mother and sisters imprisoned, a price set on his head.
At the end of November 1520 he set off on foot into Dalecarlia,
disguised as a labourer with an axe over his shoulder, alone against
what Stefánsson calls the mighty ruler of three kingdoms. He was
hunted from hiding place to hiding place, travelling by hay-cart and
sleeping on forest leaves, and when he finally reached Mora and spoke
to the assembled peasantry of Lake Siljan, they turned him away -
weary of war, and unconvinced that a king interested only in punishing
noblemen posed any threat to them.

## Called back on snowshoes

Gustaf had already set out on snowshoes for the Norwegian border when
further news reached Dalecarlia: Christian planned to march through
the province in person, gallows already ordered raised at every
manor along his route, and had imposed a harsh new tax in kind while
disarming the peasantry outright. Two runners overtook Gustaf near
the frontier and brought him back to Mora, where the leading men of
the province elected him Lord of the Dales and of Sweden in January
1521. What began with two hundred young volunteers grew daily; a
Danish force sent against the rising found peasant pikemen who, in
one bishop's own resigned words, "drink little but water and eat bark
bread" and would sooner die than yield, and Danish cavalry attacking
at Västerås that April broke against unarmed but disciplined pike
formations and lost their own artillery in the retreat. Province
after province did homage to Gustaf through 1521; even Bishop Brask,
once loyal to the crown, came over to him. The Estates of southern
Sweden elected him Regent that August, though Stockholm itself, well
defended, held out under siege for months longer.

## Two kings undone together

Christian's own position in Denmark collapsed in almost exactly the
same span of time. The domestic reforms of his final years in
power - protections for peasants against arbitrary transfer between
landlords, an end to the practice of selling them "as if they were
brute beasts," restrictions on noble privilege - won him no loyalty
from the nobility they targeted, and in December 1522 the bishops and
nobles of Jutland renounced their allegiance outright and offered the
crown to his uncle, Frederick of Holstein. Christian's nerve failed
him: in April 1523 he sailed for the Netherlands with his family and
a handful of loyal servants, abandoning a kingdom whose common towns
and peasantry, unlike its nobility, had largely remained loyal to
him. He spent eight years in exile seeking help that never came,
pawning his own jewels to survive, losing his queen to death in 1526
and his children to a Catholic upbringing he had no power to prevent.
Frederick I laid siege to Copenhagen that June and took it the
following January. In Sweden, word of Christian's flight let a
national assembly at Strängnäs proclaim Gustaf King of the Swedes and
Goths on 6 June 1523, over his own genuine objections that an elder
councillor would serve better; a fortnight later, its people reduced
by hunger and disease to a quarter their former number, Stockholm
itself finally surrendered - ending, in every practical sense though
not quite in name, the union three kingdoms had sworn eternal in
1397.
