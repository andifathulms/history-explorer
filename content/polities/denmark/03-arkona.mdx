Valdemar I, later called the Great, came to the throne in 1157
inheriting a kingdom worn down by civil war and, along its southern
and eastern coasts, by decades of raiding from the pagan Wends across
the Baltic - roughly a third of Denmark lay wasted and depopulated by
their incursions when his reign began. What turned that around was
less the king himself than his foster-brother and lifelong ally
Absalon, Bishop of Roskilde from 1158 and, in Stefánsson's own
judgment, the single greatest figure Denmark ever produced: equally
capable as statesman, churchman and warrior, and the man who spent a
decade leading campaigns against the Wends by land and sea.

## The idol chopped for firewood

The campaign reached its climax in 1169, when Valdemar and Absalon
stormed Arkona, the Wends' near-impregnable temple fortress on the
northern tip of Rügen. Before an audience of hundreds of the shrine's
own priests, the Danes tore down the four-headed wooden idol of
Svantovit, the Wends' chief god, and cut it up for firewood in their
own camp; the Wendish capital at Garz fell soon after, its own idol
destroyed the same way. The Wends were baptized and Rügen was
annexed to the bishopric of Roskilde, closing a threat that had
bled Denmark's coasts for a generation.

## A fishing village becomes a capital in waiting

Absalon's other lasting achievement was less dramatic but, in the
end, more consequential. In 1168 he fortified a small fishing
settlement on the Sound called Havn, first mentioned centuries
earlier in the Knýtlinga Saga, building a stronghold on the site
where Christiansborg Palace now stands. Valdemar granted the growing
town to the see of Roskilde, and its own bishops gave it municipal
privileges that a royal charter later confirmed; traders came to call
it Købmannahavn, "merchants' harbour" - the name that would become,
centuries later, Copenhagen. Valdemar died suddenly at forty-seven,
having already seen his own young son Cnut crowned in 1170 in
Denmark's first royal coronation, held the same day Cnut Lavard, the
king's own murdered father, was formally enshrined as a saint at
Ringsted. His epitaph called him "conqueror and lord of the Slavs,
liberator of the fatherland, keeper of the peace" - and for once, in
an age generous with such inscriptions, the claim was not far wrong.
