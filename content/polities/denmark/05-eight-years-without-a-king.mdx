The century after Valdemar II's death undid nearly everything his own
reign had rebuilt. Civil war between rival claimants ran on and off
for decades, and King Christopher II - remembered, in Stefánsson's own
judgment, as the most faithless and useless ruler Denmark ever had -
was driven from his own kingdom twice and died in 1332 in extreme
poverty, most of his realm already mortgaged to German counts to pay
for wars and ransoms. Count Gerhard III of Holstein, the chief
creditor, occupied Jutland outright and let his mercenaries lay it
waste. For eight years, from 1332 to 1340, Denmark had no king at all
- its provinces parcelled out among foreign mortgage-holders, its
own institutions in abeyance. Gerhard's rule ended only when a
Jutland nobleman, Niels Ebbesen, still remembered in folk song,
cut him down by night in his own camp at Randers in 1340.

## A kingdom with the revenue of one county

Christopher's youngest son, Valdemar IV, was elected king that same
year, not yet twenty years old and with nothing to draw on but the
income of a single Jutland county - a kingdom in name only, still
scattered among the men who held its mortgages. What he had instead
was, in Stefánsson's words, dogged and unscrupulous energy and a cool
determination to recover Denmark by any means available. He sold
Estonia to the Teutonic Knights in 1346 for nineteen thousand marks of
silver and used the money to buy back alienated crown lands; when the
Black Death struck in 1349-50 and provoked a noble revolt in Jutland,
he used the disorder to seize still more estates outright. By 1349 he
had recovered all of Denmark west of the Sound bar parts of Funen and
Jutland, and by 1360 Scania, South Halland and Blekinge had followed -
along with control of the herring fisheries off Skanör and Falsterbo,
where by one contemporary crusader's account some forty thousand
boats and three hundred thousand fishermen gathered each autumn to
salt fish for Lent, a single seasonal fishery that brought the crown
more revenue than all its other income combined.

## The day came again

Valdemar's recovery was not without setbacks - a coalition of
seventy-seven Hanseatic cities, which he derided as "seventy-seven
geese," combined with Sweden, Mecklenburg and Holstein in 1367 to
carve up what he had rebuilt, forcing him abroad for three years and
a humiliating peace at Stralsund in 1370 that barred any future
Danish king from taking the throne without Hanseatic consent. But he
had, by the time of his death in 1375, recovered nearly all of
Slesvig as well, and the byname his contemporaries gave him -
Atterdag, from a Low German oath meaning roughly "day again" - was
literal: with him, after the eight kingless years and the decades of
mortgage and civil war before them, it was day again in Denmark.
