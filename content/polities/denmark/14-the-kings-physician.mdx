Christian VII inherited the absolute monarchy his great-grandfather
had built at seventeen, in 1766 - a boy whose brutal upbringing under
a tutor who beat and punished him had left him, in Stefánsson's own
clinical account, mentally unstable and given to cunning deception
learned as a defence against cruelty. Surrounded at court by dissolute
companions, he descended within months of his accession into public
drunkenness and vandalism, dismissing capable ministers on a whim and
retaining the respect of only one, Count Bernstorff. His marriage to
the English princess Caroline Matilda, George III's own sister, wed
her by proxy at fifteen to a king she had never met; the coronation
followed in 1767, and a Danish public that adored her young charm had
no idea how quickly the marriage itself would curdle. Christian's
drunken companion Count Holck became his closest confidant, and even
the birth of an heir - the future Frederick VI - in 1768 changed
nothing in the king's conduct toward his lonely, spied-upon young
queen.

## A doctor who became a dictator

A German physician named Johann Friedrich Struensee was introduced to
Christian on a royal tour of Europe later that year and so pleased
him that he was retained as court physician on the king's return -
and proved to have a strange, calming influence over the increasingly
unstable monarch. Caroline Matilda disliked Struensee at first, but
warmed to him as she watched him restore some order to the king's
conduct, and by January 1770 he had become her lover as well as the
king's most trusted advisor. Through 1770 and 1771 Struensee
engineered the dismissal of his rivals - Holck, and then the
formidable Bernstorff himself - abolished press censorship to
popular applause, then dissolved the Council of State outright and
made himself the sole channel through which royal authority passed.
By July 1771 he held the title Cabinet Minister with power to issue
orders under royal seal as though they carried the king's own
signature; Christian, lucid only intermittently, signed whatever was
placed before him.

## Reform by decree

In his last thirty-eight weeks of power Struensee issued more than a
thousand cabinet orders, a reforming zeal that abolished judicial
torture and capital punishment for theft, established a proper
finance board, opened public office to qualification rather than
patronage, and modernized the courts - genuine achievements that,
in Stefánsson's judgment, outlasted his own fall even though they
were pushed through too fast and with too high a hand, dismissing
whole department staffs without pension along the way. He outraged
public sentiment just as thoroughly: decriminalizing adultery,
converting a chapel into a hospital for venereal disease, licensing
public gambling and masquerades that scandalized a still-devout
capital. When the queen bore a daughter in 1771, congregations
across Copenhagen walked out of church rather than sing the
thanksgiving hymn ordered for the birth, certain the child was
Struensee's own - a suspicion the king himself, in his more lucid
moments, seemed to share.
