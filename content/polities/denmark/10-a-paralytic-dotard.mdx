Christian IV came to the throne at ten years old in 1588 and grew
into what Stefánsson calls a full-blooded Renaissance prince: restless,
energetic, personally courageous, gifted in both military and
artistic matters, and prone to the same excesses that marked his
pleasure-loving private life. He explored his own kingdom's furthest
reaches, sailing round the North Cape into the White Sea, founded and
rebuilt towns across Denmark, Norway, Scania and Holstein, laid out
the plans for Norway's new capital at Christiania with his own hand,
and tripled the strength of his navy from designs he drew himself.
His domestic life brought him less credit: his morganatic marriage to
Christine Munk, after his first queen's death, produced twelve
children before he sent her away for infidelity, and the resulting
feuds between his legitimate and illegitimate offspring caused him
lasting grief.

## Trading insults with a dying king

When Charles IX of Sweden claimed the title King of the Lapps over
territory reaching into northern Norway and granted trading rights
there to the newly founded city of Gothenburg, Christian forced his
own reluctant State Council into war. In the Kalmar War of 1611-13,
Danish forces took the fortress of Kalmar after a three-month siege;
an enraged Charles IX challenged Christian to personal combat, sword
in hand, warning that refusal would mean he was no honourable king or
soldier, and Christian's reply advised the "paralytic dotard" to stay
by his fireside with his nurse. Charles did not long outlive the
insult, and though his young successor Gustavus Adolphus offered
concessions, Christian rejected peace and pressed the war further,
taking the fortress guarding Sweden's only western outlet in 1612.

## Lutter and the wolves of Wallenstein

Christian's greater ambitions proved far costlier. Hoping to control
the mouths of the Elbe and Weser and encouraged by England and France
to champion the German Protestant cause, he invaded the Empire in
1625 with a mainly German army - only to be defeated decisively by
Tilly and Wallenstein at Lutter am Barenberg on 27 August 1626, his
German allies abandoning him in the aftermath. The following year
Wallenstein's mercenaries overran Holstein and Slesvig and then the
whole Jutland peninsula, plundering at will while an increasingly
powerless Christian, mired in disputes with his own State Council,
could only watch from Funen. The Emperor now aimed at nothing less
than mastery of the Baltic, naming Wallenstein "General of the Baltic
and Oceanic Seas" - a design broken only when the besieged port of
Stralsund, reinforced jointly by Denmark and Sweden despite their own
long rivalry, held out against Wallenstein's boast that he would take
it "though it were slung with chains between earth and heaven." Peace
came at Lübeck in 1629 on comparatively merciful terms: Denmark
recovered its occupied provinces, but only by renouncing the
secularized German bishoprics it had claimed and abandoning, for
good, its leadership of the Protestant cause in Germany.
