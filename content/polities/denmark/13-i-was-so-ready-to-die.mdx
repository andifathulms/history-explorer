Peter Schumacher, the son of a wealthy Copenhagen citizen, spent the
years 1654 to 1662 studying abroad - at Queen's College, Oxford,
where he witnessed the English Restoration firsthand; in Germany and
Holland; and in Paris, where he watched Louis XIV lay the foundations
of the centralized monarchy the rest of Europe would spend the
following century imitating. He returned to Denmark to become
librarian of the newly founded Royal Library and then the king's own
secretary, steadily climbing the only ladder that mattered under the
new absolutism: royal favour. On his deathbed in 1670, Frederick III
told his son and successor, Christian V, to "make a great man of
Schumacher, but do it slowly."

## The self-made architect of absolutism

Christian V's very first act was to hand Schumacher the secret Lex
Regia that only Frederick III had known of, and Schumacher's rise
from there was rapid: Privy Councillor, ennobled as Count Griffenfeld
after the griffin on his own coat of arms, Knight of the Order of the
Elephant - an honour otherwise reserved for royalty - and finally
Grand Chancellor in 1673. He built a genuinely efficient
administration out of the new absolute monarchy's raw materials,
consolidating government boards and pursuing a deliberately cautious
foreign policy meant to recover Denmark's prestige through alliances
and subsidies rather than through the wars of reconquest against
Sweden that Christian V and his generals were impatient to fight. It
was that same caution that turned the king against him: after
Christian V personally handed his chancellor a list of fifteen points
on which he wanted more circumspect conduct, Griffenfeld was arrested
on 11 March 1676.

## A pardon that arrived on the scaffold

The formal charges were embezzlement, but the piece of evidence that
did the most damage was a private diary entry noting that the king
"talked like a child to the ambassadors." Neither his written appeal
for mercy nor his own defence before a court packed with his enemies
saved him: he was sentenced to lose life, honour and property, and
his coat of arms was formally broken on the scaffold as he waited for
the executioner's axe. The king's pardon, commuting the sentence to
life imprisonment, arrived only as he lay there expecting the blow.
"May God forgive you," Griffenfeld said, "I was so ready to die." He
spent twenty-two years in prison, denied even writing materials,
before his death - a statesman Louis XIV himself once called without
equal in Europe, broken by the same absolute monarchy he had spent
his career building into an efficient instrument of state. Among the
quieter achievements of these same years, largely untouched by the
court's own convulsions, was the astronomer Ole Rømer's measurement
of the speed of light, and in 1683 Denmark's laws were codified and
published as a single body for the first time.
