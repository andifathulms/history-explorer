Christian II inherited the Kalmar Union's Danish crown determined to
make its claim on Sweden real rather than nominal, and by 1520 his
armies had fought their way to Stockholm itself, defended through a
brutal siege by the widowed Christina Gyllenstierna after Swedish
resistance under the Sture family collapsed. Christina held out
through a hard summer of siege before autumn storms brought Christian
to the negotiating table; her price was a full, explicit amnesty
covering everyone who had fought against him, and on those terms she
surrendered Stockholm's keys on 7 September. Christian entered the
city in triumph, was crowned hereditary sovereign of Sweden that
November with an envoy of the Emperor Charles V investing him with
the Order of the Golden Fleece before the high altar - and three days
later summoned the Senate, the City Council, Christina herself, and
the assembled nobility and clergy to the royal palace for what the
Swedish reformer Olaus Petri would later call "a banquet of another
kind."

## The document that could not save them

Archbishop Trolle, whom the Stures had once imprisoned and whose
castle they had razed, stood before the enthroned king and demanded
punishment for those responsible as heretics who had violated canon
law. Christina rose and produced a decree proving the acts had been
authorized by a national assembly whose members, herself included,
had all declared themselves jointly responsible - a document that
should have spread the guilt too widely for any selective punishment
to proceed. It changed nothing. After dark, armed men worked through
a prepared list, arresting everyone named on it while the king had
already withdrawn to settle, in private, exactly who was to be seized.
The next morning an ecclesiastical court under Trolle's own
presidency declared the prisoners manifest heretics, and at midday
they were beheaded in the central square under guard, the executions
continuing into a second day until the streets themselves ran with
blood. The bodies lay unburied until Saturday, when they were burned
in a heap - Sten Sture's own corpse, along with that of a child born
to his widow during the interdict, dug up from its grave to be burned
with them.

## A cruelty its author could not admit to

What made the Stockholm Massacre distinctive, in Stefánsson's own
judgment, was not only its violence but Christian's dishonesty about
it afterward: he told the Swedish people the executions had been
necessary to head off a new papal interdict, while privately writing
the Pope that two of the murdered bishops had been killed by mistake
during a plot against his own life. His journey home through Sweden
that December was marked by gallows at every stop, and he believed,
wrongly, that he had broken the country's spirit for good. Christina
Gyllenstierna was sent to Denmark as a prisoner. Among the Swedish
nobles who had escaped the palace that November night, having been
carried off as a hostage to Denmark two years earlier under
altogether different and, as it turned out, luckier circumstances,
was a young nobleman named Gustaf Eriksson - of the family Vasa.
