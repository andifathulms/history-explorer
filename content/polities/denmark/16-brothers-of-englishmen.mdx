Denmark's attempt to stay neutral in the wars convulsing Europe after
1793 pulled it steadily toward confrontation with Britain regardless.
Joined with Russia, Sweden and Prussia in a League of Armed
Neutrality meant to protect neutral shipping from British search and
seizure, Denmark found its own frigates fighting British cruisers
outright by 1800, and when Britain answered by laying embargo on
every Danish and Norwegian ship in January 1801, the response was a
fleet of fifty-three sail under Admiral Hyde Parker and Nelson.

## The hottest fight Nelson ever fought

Nelson's squadron of thirty-five ships attacked Copenhagen's harbour
defences on 2 April 1801 - blockships, floating batteries, and the
fort of Trekroner, manned largely by raw recruits and university
students under Olfert Fischer. Nelson himself later called it the
hottest fight he had ever been in; after five hours of fighting left
six of his own ships of the line aground and exposed to Trekroner's
guns, he sent the Danish Crown Prince a letter proposing a truce,
addressed simply "To the Brothers of Englishmen, the Danes." The
Crown Prince agreed to a day's ceasefire. Losses on both sides ran
close to even - over a thousand Danish casualties, twelve hundred
English - and among the defenders was an eighteen-year-old named
Peder Willemoes, who fought Nelson's own flagship from a small
gunboat for four hours and lost two-thirds of his crew; Nelson
himself later said the young officer deserved promotion above any
man in his own fleet.

## A fleet stolen before it could be used

Denmark's neutrality bought only six years' peace. In August 1807, an
English fleet carrying thirty thousand troops under Lord Cathcart,
with Arthur Wellesley - the future Duke of Wellington - as second in
command, arrived in the Sound with an ultimatum: hand over the entire
Danish navy for the duration of the war, or face attack. Denmark's
Crown Prince refused, and Copenhagen, garrisoned by thirteen thousand
mostly untrained volunteers, was bombarded from 2 to 5 September,
burning the university, the cathedral and three hundred houses before
the city capitulated. Britain carried off seventeen ships of the
line, twelve frigates and dozens of smaller vessels - the entire
Danish fleet, seized from a nation Britain was not formally at war
with until the attack itself provoked one. Furious at what
Stefánsson calls an unprovoked attack, the Crown Prince allied
Denmark with Napoleon that October, a decision whose consequences
would not become clear for another seven years.
