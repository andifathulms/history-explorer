Sweden's chancellor Axel Oxenstierna judged, by the early 1640s, that
Denmark stood as the last real obstacle to Swedish dominance of the
North, and sent secret orders to his general Torstensson to strike
while the Thirty Years' War still occupied every other power in
Europe. Torstensson crossed into Jutland in December 1643 and
overran the entire peninsula within weeks - a danger Denmark
survived only through the personal exertions of Christian IV himself,
now sixty-seven years old and spending days and nights equipping his
navy and raising troops in a kingdom his own war minister could no
longer defend by conventional means.

## The gun on the quarterdeck

Christian took to the sea himself. In April 1644 he beat back a Dutch
fleet sent to ferry Torstensson's army across to the Danish islands,
forcing it home to Holland, and in June he met a forty-ship Swedish
fleet on the same errand in a ten-hour battle off Kolberger Heide. A
gun exploded on the quarterdeck where he stood; wood and metal
splinters wounded him in thirteen places, cost him an eye, and felled
him to the deck. He got up at once, insisted he was unhurt, and stayed
on deck directing the battle until the Swedish fleet withdrew into
Kiel Bay - an episode later celebrated in the Danish national hymn.
The tactical victory did not hold: the blockaded Swedish fleet
escaped regardless, and Christian had the Danish admiral responsible
shot for it. A combined Dutch-Swedish force went on to destroy or
capture fifteen of seventeen Danish ships off Lolland that autumn,
outnumbering the Danes more than two to one.

## "They care not for God, King, or country"

Christian was forced to sue for peace at Brömsebro on 13 August 1645.
The terms stripped Denmark of the long-disputed provinces of
Jämtland and Härjedalen, the islands of Ösel and Gotland, and Halland
for thirty years as security against Swedish customs exemption at the
Sound - a toll whose revenue fell by three-quarters as a result.
Christian, whose own son-in-law Corfitz Ulfeldt had turned against
him and taken open satisfaction in his humiliation, blamed the
nobility bitterly for the disaster: "They care not for God, King, or
country," he said, "but only for their own selfish interests." He
died in 1648 after fifty-two years on the throne, seventy-one years
old - a king whose personal courage and genuine mastery of
shipbuilding and naval command, in Stefánsson's own final judgment,
stood out as the one bright thread running through a reign that
otherwise closed in disaster after disaster for the kingdom he had
ruled longer than any other Danish monarch before him.
