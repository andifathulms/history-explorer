Sven Forkbeard, Harald Bluetooth's own son, spent years raiding
England before finally becoming its master in 1013 - only to die
within months, at Gainsborough in February 1014. His son Cnut, elected
king by the Danish army already in England, was forced to withdraw and
then fight his way back to the English throne over the following two
years, a struggle settled only by the deaths of both Æthelred the
Unready and his son Edmund Ironside in 1016. Cnut was twenty-two years
old when he finally secured England outright, and he governed it, in
Stefánsson's own judgment, not as a foreign conqueror but with greater
wisdom and justice than many of its native-born kings before him - he
sent most of his Danish army home, kept only his household troops, the
housecarls, as a standing force, and let Englishmen like Godwine rise
to be his chief advisers in place of the Danish earls who had brought
him to power.

## Three crowns

Cnut inherited Denmark itself in 1018 on his brother Harald's death,
and in 1028 he sailed to Norway with a fleet of some 1,400 ships and
took it without a battle. When the exiled Norwegian king Olaf
Haraldsson tried to reclaim his throne two years later, Norwegian
farmers themselves killed him at the Battle of Stiklestad. By the
early 1030s Cnut ruled England, Denmark and Norway together, an
Anglo-Scandinavian empire with no real precedent in the North. In
1027 he became the first Scandinavian king to enter Rome, walking out
of St Peter's alongside the newly crowned Emperor Conrad II after his
coronation - and wrote home to his English subjects a letter that
reads less like a Viking war-leader's boast than a Christian king's
statement of principle: "I have vowed to God to rule my kingdoms
justly and piously... My officers shall administer justice to all,
rich and poor, nor do wrong for fear or favour of any man, on pain of
losing my friendship and their own life and goods."

## An empire too young to outlast him

The same reign had its older, harder edge. At Christmas 1026, Cnut
quarrelled with his own brother-in-law, Earl Ulf, over a game of
chess at Roskilde; by the next morning he had ordered Ulf killed, and
the earl was run through while kneeling in the choir of Trinity
Church. Cnut's later pilgrimage to Rome was, by his own admission,
partly meant to expiate that sin. He built churches and founded
Denmark's earliest monasteries, brought English bishops to staff a
Danish church he wanted answerable to Canterbury rather than Bremen,
and struck the first regular Danish coinage of his reign. None of it
gave the empire roots deep enough to survive him. Cnut died still a
young man, and what Stefánsson calls his Anglo-Scandinavian empire -
three crowns held by one Danish king at once, never achieved again -
crumbled within a few years of his death, split back apart among heirs
who could hold no more than a fraction of what he had ruled together.
