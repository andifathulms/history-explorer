---
title: Written from Timbuktu

Two Arabic chronicles compiled at Timbuktu in the seventeenth century, the
*Ta'rikh al-Sudan* and the *Ta'rikh al-Fattash*, are the main narrative sources
for Songhai. They were written by members of the scholarly families of the city,
after the empire had been destroyed, and they carry that position throughout.

Sunni Ali, who took Timbuktu in 1468 and treated its scholars harshly, is
remembered in them as a tyrant and a doubtful Muslim. Askia Muhammad, who
overthrew Sunni Ali's son, made the pilgrimage and patronised the same scholarly
families, is remembered as the model Muslim ruler. Both judgements come from the
constituency each ruler treated differently, and modern work reads Sunni Ali
rather more sympathetically as a ruler balancing an urban Islamic elite against a
rural population whose practice was not theirs.

It is the oldest source problem there is: the surviving account is written by a
group with a stake in it, after the fact, and there is nothing to check it
against.

What makes this case unusually clean is that the men writing were not the
victors. They had lost. The Moroccan conquest of 1591 had put a foreign garrison
in their city and destroyed the state they had served, and both chronicles were
written under that occupation by people describing a world that had stopped
inside their own lifetimes.
