Songhai is the third empire of the middle Niger and the last, and the thing that
distinguishes it from the two before it is a fleet.

Gao sits on the Niger about five hundred kilometres downstream of Timbuktu, and
the Songhai were river people — fishers, boatmen, traders along a waterway
navigable for something like sixteen hundred kilometres of its middle course. The
empire that grew out of Gao ran along that river rather than out from a centre,
and it was held together by an inland navy under an officer the chronicles call
the *hi-koi*, the master of the water.

Nothing else in this corpus is shaped that way. The Niger empires are usually
described as a sequence — Ghana, then Mali, then Songhai — as though they were
the same state under different management, and the first two were built on the
trade routes crossing the river while this one was built on the river itself.

## What it inherited

A great deal, and this record's second chapter is about the man who took it.

Gao had been a Malian province in the fourteenth century. When Mali's frontier
gave way — this corpus's Mali record dates the first substantial loss to 1433,
when Tuareg took Timbuktu — Songhai was the power that filled the space, and it
inherited the two things the region ran on: the trans-Saharan routes at their
southern terminus, and the towns.

The towns matter more than the routes for what this record can say about itself.
Timbuktu and Jenne were not merely markets. They held mosques, madrasas, a
book trade, a jurist class, and a body of Arabic literacy that had been
accumulating for two centuries, and the whole of this record's surviving
narrative comes from that class writing about the empire two generations after it
ended.

## Two audiences again

The Mali record in this region observes that its dynasty maintained two
genealogies for two audiences — one held by hereditary praise-singers, one
written in Arabic for Cairo and Fez.

Songhai has the same split in a sharper form and it runs through the whole
record. The founder of the empire, Sunni Ali, is a hero in Songhai oral tradition
and a tyrant and unbeliever in the Timbuktu chronicles. The man who overthrew his
son is a usurper in the first and the restorer of Islam in the second.

Both accounts are about the same twenty years, and this site's sources are the
chronicles, because the chronicles are what was written down. The fifth chapter
is about what that costs.
