Songhai taxed the Saharan trade and took tribute from its provinces, and the
chronicles are more specific about a third source of income than about either of
those: the royal estates.

The *Ta'rikh al-Fattash* describes agricultural establishments along the Niger
worked by settled slaves — communities with a name, a headman, a fixed obligation
in sacks of grain, and in some accounts hundreds of workers on a single estate.
They fed the court, the army and the towns. And they were hereditary: the people
on them were slaves by descent, in categories the chronicles name, and were not
manumitted in the ordinary way.

This is not taxation. A state taxing agriculture stands outside the farm and
takes a share; this was direct production on crown land by an unfree workforce,
which is closer to the state agriculture of Ur III or the fortress estates of
Urartu than to anything the word tax describes.

## What it rested on

The Niger empires ran on slavery, and it is worth stating plainly because the
older literature did not.

Slaves worked the estates. Slaves fought in the armies — Mali's rulers had
already found that free troops came through intermediaries who could withhold
them, and a purchased soldiery could not be withheld. Slaves staffed the
households. And slaves moved north across the Sahara as the region's oldest
export, for a thousand years before the Atlantic trade and alongside it
afterwards.

Gomez's argument is that the categories of caste, servility and descent that
West Africa carried into the modern period were formed here, in this period, and
not by later European contact. Whether or not one follows him the whole way, the
alternative is an account of these empires that leaves out most of the people who
lived in them.

## The other side of the same century

None of it made the state weak, which a modern reader may half expect.

Songhai in the middle of the sixteenth century was the largest polity West Africa
had produced — some eight hundred thousand square kilometres — with a river
fleet, a working judiciary in the towns, a class of jurists corresponding with
Cairo and Fez, and a book trade substantial enough that the *Ta'rikh al-Sudan*
mentions a scholar's library of sixteen hundred volumes.

It was destroyed in an afternoon by four thousand men with guns.
