title: Askia Muhammad's pilgrimage

Songhai rose at Gao on the middle Niger as Mali receded, and under Sunni Ali took
Timbuktu in 1468 and Djenne in 1473 — the two cities that made the middle Niger
the intellectual centre of West Africa.

Sunni Ali's relationship with the scholars of Timbuktu was hostile; the Arabic
chronicles written by their descendants remember him as a persecutor and barely
a Muslim. His successor Askia Muhammad, who seized power in 1493, went the other
way entirely: he made the pilgrimage to Mecca in 1496, obtained recognition as
caliph of the Sudan from the Abbasid shadow caliph in Cairo, and built his
legitimacy on Islamic scholarship and its patronage.

That detail is worth pausing on, because it connects to another page here. The
shadow Abbasid caliphate the Mamluks kept at Cairo — an office with no territory,
no army and no authority — was still, two hundred and thirty-five years after
Baghdad fell, worth travelling across the Sahara to be recognised by. It is the
clearest available demonstration of what the caliphate had become and why it
persisted.

Timbuktu under Songhai held the Sankore mosque and a scholarly community whose
libraries survived into the present. The empire was destroyed in 1591 by a
Moroccan expedition equipped with firearms, sent across the Sahara by a sultanate
that is not on this site.
