In 1591 a Moroccan expeditionary force crossed the Sahara — something in the
order of four thousand men, with pack animals, a train, and firearms — and met
the Songhai army at Tondibi north of Gao.

The Songhai force was many times larger. It had cavalry, infantry and, by the
chronicles' account, a herd of cattle driven at the Moroccan line to break it.
The cattle turned when the guns went off and went back through their own army.

The battle lasted a short time. Gao and Timbuktu fell within weeks.

## The clearest gunpowder case in this corpus

This corpus's Saadi record carries the same day from the winning side and this
record carries the loss, and between them the two pages are the site's plainest
instance of firearms deciding an engagement outright rather than contributing to
one.

The Ottoman record has Chaldiran in 1514, where this site's Safavid record's
third chapter argues the guns mattered less than the Ottoman infantry discipline.
The Mughal record has Panipat. In both, the guns are one factor among several
and the numbers were not wildly unequal.

Here a force outnumbered by a large multiple crossed sixteen hundred kilometres
of desert, arrived depleted, and won in an afternoon, and the only variable
anybody has proposed is the arquebus.

## What Morocco got

Not an empire. A garrison.

The Saadi state could not administer the middle Niger from Marrakesh across the
Sahara, and within a generation the expeditionary force and its descendants — the
*Arma* — were an autonomous military caste governing the towns on their own
account and paying Morocco nothing. This record's `successor_claims` is zero
because they made no claim on Songhai's legitimacy; they held authority as an
occupying garrison and said so.

What the conquest destroyed was the thing that had made the region legible to the
rest of the world: the empire that guaranteed the roads, the courts that applied a
known law, and the scholarly establishment that wrote in a language Cairo could
read. Ahmad Baba, the most eminent Timbuktu jurist of the period, was arrested,
his library confiscated, and he was deported to Marrakesh, where he spent twelve
years and wrote — among other things — a treatise on the legality of enslaving
West Africans, arguing against much of what was being done.

## The end of a system, not a state

This record's `ended.type` is `conquest` and it is accurate, which makes it
unusual in this region: the Aksumite record ends in a `gradual absorption` with
no conqueror and the Mali record in a `fragmentation` with an empty `by`.

Songhai is the one African record here with a date, a battle and a named power in
the field, and the reason is that the power was on the other side of the Sahara
and left an account.

What ended with it was larger than the polity. The trans-Saharan trade system had
been running for something like eight hundred years and had supported three
successive empires; by 1591 the Atlantic had already begun taking the gold west
to European ships, and the Moroccan conquest removed the political structure that
had held the southern end of the old system together. There was no fourth empire
of the Niger.

The two Timbuktu chronicles that supply almost everything on this record were
written in the decades afterwards, by men living under the Arma, describing a
world that had just stopped. The fifth chapter on this record is about what it
means that this page is built almost entirely out of elegy.
