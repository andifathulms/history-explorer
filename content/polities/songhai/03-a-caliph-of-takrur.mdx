In 1493 a general named Muhammad Ture overthrew Sunni Ali's son, took the title
*askia*, and founded a dynasty. In 1496 he set out for Mecca.

The pilgrimage is the second one this region carries — this corpus's Mali record
has Mansa Musa's of 1324 — and the two are usually told as the same story about
West African gold arriving in Egypt. They were not doing the same thing.

## What he came back with

An appointment.

At Cairo, Askia Muhammad obtained from the Abbasid caliph — the shadow caliph in
Mamluk keeping, whose office this corpus carries as its own record, `abbasid-cairo`
— designation as *khalifa* of the land of Takrur. He also acquired, on the
chronicles' account, a cap and a sword as the insignia of it, and the counsel of
the Egyptian jurist al-Suyuti.

This record's `legitimation` field reads `caliphal-investiture, conquest`, and
the pairing is the whole of the man's position. He had taken the throne by
beating the previous dynasty's heir in the field, which is the second value and
was not a claim anybody could build on. What he went to Cairo for was the first.

The Abbasid caliph at Cairo had no army, no territory and no power of any kind;
this corpus's record for that office has a chapter called *what an empty office
was for*, which sets out the transaction in detail — the diploma, the robing, the
public reading. Coding rule 8 says a grant from a powerless conferrer counts,
because what is recorded is that the ruler thought it worth having.

Askia Muhammad crossed the Sahara twice to get one.

## And this site does not draw an edge for it

There used to be one — `abbasid -> songhai`, type `claimed legitimacy of`, dated
1496 — and it was wrong in three ways, so it has been removed.

It pointed at the wrong record. The Abbasid caliphate of Baghdad ends in 1258 on
this site; the office Askia Muhammad dealt with is the Cairo one, which this
corpus carries separately and whose span covers the year.

It was the wrong type. Coding rule 1 counts a *later* polity asserting an
*earlier* polity's legitimacy. These two were contemporaries, and a ruler
receiving an appointment from a living authority is not claiming a defunct one's
legacy — he is holding office from somebody, which is what the `legitimation`
field's `caliphal-investiture` value exists to record and does record here.

And it was inconsistent. Twenty-eight records in this corpus carry
`caliphal-investiture` and twenty-four of them have no edge from either
caliphate; the ones that do have `vassal of` or `seceded from`, because those
polities were actually Abbasid governors. Songhai was not. Keeping one edge that
the site's own practice contradicts twenty-four times over is worse than having
none.

The Cairo record's own chapter on what an investiture was already said this — it
names the Askia in the Niger valley and observes that the Songhai page records
the transaction as legitimation rather than as a curiosity. The edge had been
sitting underneath a page that argued against it.

## What it bought at home

Legitimacy against the men he had displaced, and an alliance with the scholars
his predecessor had persecuted.

Sunni Ali had treated the Timbuktu jurists as a political faction — killed some,
driven others to Walata, and by the chronicles' account never pretended to be
anything other than hostile. Askia Muhammad recalled them, endowed them, appointed
their leading families to judgeships, and applied the law visibly.

The chronicles that damn the founder of the empire celebrate the man who
overthrew his son, and they were written by the descendants of the people on the
receiving end of both policies. That is the fifth chapter's problem and it is
worth flagging before this chapter's admiration is taken at face value.

## And what it did not fix

The succession.

Askia Muhammad ruled thirty-five years, went blind, and was deposed in 1528 by
his own son, who put him on an island in the Niger where he spent nine years
before another son brought him back to die in Gao. That son had already been
deposed by a brother.

This record's `succession_rule` is null and the data comment is unusually blunt
about what followed: something close to a coup a decade for the rest of the
century. A caliphal title obtained from Cairo settled the dynasty's standing
against outsiders and settled nothing at all inside the family.
