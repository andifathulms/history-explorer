Songhai lasted a hundred and twenty years as an empire and spent most of them
having a succession crisis.

Askia Muhammad, who had taken the throne by overthrowing Sunni Ali's son, ruled
thirty-five years, went blind, and was deposed in 1528 by a son of his own, who
put him on an island in the Niger. He was there nine years. Another son brought
him back to Gao to die; that son had already deposed the first one.

What follows is close to a coup a decade for the rest of the century. Brothers
deposed brothers, sons deposed fathers, and the *askiyas* were made and unmade by
factions of the military and court aristocracy who had learned exactly how it was
done, because Askia Muhammad had shown them.

## Why there was no rule

Nobody ever wrote one down, and by the time it mattered there were two competing
principles in the field and no way to choose between them.

The dynasty was a usurping one. Askia Muhammad had taken the throne on a caliphal
investiture and a religious argument against his predecessor, not on descent, and
a dynasty founded that way cannot easily insist that descent is binding on
everybody else. Meanwhile the older Songhai political tradition, which was oral
and which the Timbuktu chroniclers had no interest in recording, presumably had
arrangements of its own that the Askias had overridden.

So the empire's own historians, who were Muslim jurists in Timbuktu writing about
a court in Gao, record the coups without ever stating what the coups were
violations of.

## 1588

The one that mattered came three years before the Moroccans did.

A disputed succession turned into open civil war between factions of the army,
fought in the field, with substantial casualties among exactly the cavalry that
would be needed shortly afterwards. The winner took the throne as Askia Ishaq II.

Three years later he was at Tondibi, with an army that had been fighting itself
in living memory, against four thousand men who had crossed the Sahara with
firearms.

## What the pattern cost

It is easy to overstate this and worth stating carefully.

Songhai was not weak in 1591. It was the largest state in West Africa, it had
just fought a civil war and reunified, and it outnumbered the Moroccan force
several times over. A stable succession would not have supplied it with
arquebuses, which is what it actually needed.

What the succession pattern did cost was time and attention over sixty years —
sixty years in which the Saadis were arming with Ottoman muskets and a Songhai
court was preoccupied with which brother would rule. Morocco had asked for the
salt mines at Taghaza repeatedly and been refused; the possibility of a crossing
was not a secret; and nobody in Gao appears to have done anything about
firearms.

The empire did not fall because of the coups. It fell because it was busy.
