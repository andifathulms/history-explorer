Sunni Ali ruled from 1464 to 1492 and made the empire. He took Timbuktu in 1468
and Jenne in 1473 — the two cities that made the middle Niger the intellectual
centre of West Africa — pushed the Tuareg back into the desert, campaigned
against the Mossi in the south, and built the river fleet the first chapter
describes.

He is also, in the only narrative sources this record has, a monster.

## The chronicles' verdict

The *Ta'rikh al-Sudan* and the *Ta'rikh al-Fattash* were compiled at Timbuktu in
the seventeenth century by men descended from the scholarly families Sunni Ali
had persecuted, and they do not moderate it. He is a tyrant, a shedder of blood,
a man who killed so many of the learned that God would not forgive him, and a
Muslim only in name — praying without ablution, combining the prayers, mixing
Islam with the practices of the ancestral religion.

Some of this is checkable and some of it is a genre. What is not disputed is
that he took Timbuktu by force from the Tuareg, treated its jurists as a hostile
faction rather than as an asset, killed a number of them and drove others to
Walata, and governed for twenty-eight years without seeking the approval of
anybody in Cairo.

## The other tradition

Songhai oral tradition remembers him differently: as the founder, a figure of
enormous power, and a man of the river.

This record's `legitimation` field carries `caliphal-investiture` and `conquest`,
and both values belong to his successor rather than to him. There is no coded
value on this page for what Sunni Ali actually ruled on — the assent of a Songhai
military aristocracy and a religious authority the chronicles will only describe
as sorcery.

That is a gap of a particular kind and this corpus meets it repeatedly. The
Median record says it is disputed whether there was a state to have institutions.
The Mitanni record has one coded field because nobody has found the capital. Here
the polity is not in doubt and the description of it is monopolised by people who
found its founder's religion illegitimate, so the field records the successor's
arrangements and not the founder's.

## Why the successor's version won

Because the successor's people wrote.

Askia Muhammad, the general who overthrew Sunni Ali's son a year after his death,
rebuilt the alliance with the Timbuktu scholars and went to Cairo for a title —
the next chapter. The consequence for this record is that the empire's founder is
described by the beneficiaries of the coup that displaced his line, and the man
who took the throne by force is the one the sources call the restorer of Islam.

The fifth chapter on this record is about that problem directly. It is worth
noticing here that the problem is not that the chronicles are unreliable. They
are careful, detailed and often checkable. It is that they are the only voice,
and they belonged to one party in a quarrel this record's first two reigns were
about.
