Morocco won at Tondibi and could not keep what it had won, and what happened
next is a demonstration of why crossing a desert to conquer somebody is a bad
idea even when it works.

The expeditionary force could not be supplied from Marrakesh. Sixteen hundred
kilometres of Sahara is not a line of communication; it is an obstacle that
happens to be crossable. Reinforcements arrived at intervals and then stopped
arriving. Within a generation the soldiers and their descendants — the *Arma*,
from the Arabic for musketeers — had married locally, stopped remitting anything
to Morocco, and become an autonomous military caste governing Timbuktu, Gao and
Djenné on their own account.

They ruled for two hundred years, as a pashalik in name and a self-perpetuating
garrison in fact, electing and deposing their own pashas at a rate that makes the
Askias look stable.

## What they did not do

They made no claim on Songhai at all.

No Arma pasha called himself Askia, invoked the dynasty, married into it, or
argued that he was continuing anything. They held the towns as an occupying force
descended from an occupying force, and their own descendants remembered
themselves as Moroccans for generations after Morocco had forgotten them.

That is genuinely unusual. A conqueror who intends to stay almost always finds a
reason why he is the rightful heir — it is cheaper than governing by force
forever. The Arma did not bother, and the reason is probably that they never
thought of themselves as ruling a country. They were holding three towns on a
river, which is what they went on doing until the Bambara and then the Fulani
jihads reduced them.

## Ahmad Baba

The most eminent jurist of Timbuktu was arrested after the conquest, his library
was confiscated, and he was deported across the desert to Marrakesh, where he
spent twelve years.

He said afterwards that his own library — the smallest, he claimed, of any of his
family's — had held sixteen hundred volumes.

While in Morocco he wrote, among a great deal else, a treatise on the legality of
enslaving West Africans, arguing that a person's origin does not make him
enslavable and that unbelief is the only ground the law allows — which meant that
the Muslims of the Niger bend, whoever else might be taken, could not lawfully be.
He was writing in the country whose army had just conquered his.

## And the chronicles

The two great Timbuktu chronicles were written under the Arma, by men who had
watched the empire end.

Everything anybody can say about Songhai — the reigns, the campaigns, the
administration, Sunni Ali's atrocities and Askia Muhammad's pilgrimage — comes
out of two books composed in a defeated city, under a foreign garrison, by
scholars from the class that had been the empire's judiciary and had every reason
to remember it as better than what replaced it.

That is not a reason to discard them. It is a reason to read them as what they
are, which is what men write when the thing they belonged to has just stopped and
they are the only ones left who remember how it worked.

There was no fourth empire on the Niger. The trans-Saharan system that had
carried three of them was already being undercut by ships on the Atlantic coast,
and after 1591 there was no political structure holding its southern end
together. What the Arma inherited was three towns on a river that no longer led
anywhere important.
