The chronicle that preserves the Songhai empire's history was written in
Timbuktu in the 1650s by a scholar of the city, and its chapter on the man who
built the empire opens like this, in Houdas's French:

> As for that master tyrant, that famous scoundrel, Sonni Ali … he was a man
> endowed with great strength and powerful energy. Wicked, libertine, unjust,
> oppressive, bloodthirsty, he put to death such a quantity of men that God alone
> knows the number. He persecuted the learned and the pious, attacking their
> lives, their honour or their standing.

Es-Sa'di goes on in that register for a chapter. Then, without changing tone, he
records that Sonni Ali reigned twenty-seven or twenty-eight years, spent them in
campaigns and conquests, took Jenne and stayed a year and a month, conquered
Bara and the country of the Sanhaja, took Timbuktu and every mountain stronghold
but one.

## Why the chronicler hates him

Because of who was writing. Es-Sa'di was a Timbuktu jurist from a Timbuktu
family, and the *ulama* of Timbuktu were the people Sonni Ali had destroyed.

The chronicle is specific. He hastened to kill or humiliate every scholar who had
stayed in the city, giving as his pretext that they were friends and courtiers of
the Tuareg and that this was why he was angry with them. He imprisoned the mother
of one jurist and had her two brothers, both jurists, put to death. *In short, he
did not cease to inflict miseries upon miseries and humiliations upon
humiliations on all the learned.* One day he ordered thirty virgins, daughters of
scholars, brought to him as concubines, and being at the river port at the time,
required them to make the journey on foot.

Es-Sa'di's parenthesis after that catalogue — *God preserve us from such a fate!*
— is the voice of a man writing about his own professional ancestors.

## The two anecdotes

The chronicle's charge of impiety is not a general accusation. It is two stories,
and they are better than any denunciation.

The first is about prayer. Sonni Ali would put off his five daily prayers until
the night or the next day, and then perform them all at once, sitting down, going
through the gestures several times over and naming each prayer in turn as he went.
Then he gave a single closing salutation for the lot and said:

> Now divide all that among yourselves, since you know one another well.

The second is about killing. He would order a man's death — even one of the men
he most valued — for no motive and no necessity whatever; and afterwards he
sometimes repented. His servants, knowing this, took to hiding anyone whose
execution seemed likely to be regretted. When the regret appeared they would tell
him: *we have spared him, he is not dead.* And he was delighted.

Es-Sa'di names the servant who did this most often. It was Askia Muhammad — the
commander who overthrew Sonni Ali's son the year after he died, took the throne,
went on pilgrimage, was invested by the sharif of Mecca, and restored the
scholars of Timbuktu to everything they had lost.

## What the chronicle is

That last detail is the reason to read the chapter as a document rather than as
a verdict.

Es-Sa'di is writing a hundred and fifty years after the events, in a city under
Moroccan occupation, in a scholarly tradition whose founding grievance is what
Sonni Ali did to it and whose founding benefactor is the man who replaced him.
The portrait of a blasphemous, capricious murderer and the portrait of his pious
secretary saving lives behind his back are the same story told from one side.

What it cannot hide, because the chronicler is honest about the facts even when
he is not neutral about the man, is that the tyrant took Jenne and Timbuktu and
the river and built the largest state West Africa had yet seen, and that the
scholars' hero inherited it from him.
