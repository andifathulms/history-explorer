In 1493 a general named Muhammad Ture overthrew Sunni Ali's son, took the title
*askia*, and founded a dynasty. In 1496 he set out for Mecca.

It was the second great pilgrimage out of the Niger bend. Mansa Musa's
has Mansa Musa's of 1324 — and the two are usually told as the same story about
West African gold arriving in Egypt. They were not doing the same thing.

## What he came back with

An appointment.

At Cairo, Askia Muhammad obtained from the Abbasid caliph — the shadow caliph in
Mamluk keeping — the shadow Abbasid caliphate of Cairo
— designation as *khalifa* of the land of Takrur. He also acquired, on the
chronicles' account, a cap and a sword as the insignia of it, and the counsel of
the Egyptian jurist al-Suyuti.

The investiture is the point, and
the pairing is the whole of the man's position. He had taken the throne by
beating the previous dynasty's heir in the field, which is the second value and
was not a claim anybody could build on. What he went to Cairo for was the first.

The Abbasid caliph at Cairo had no army, no territory and no power of any kind;
the transaction was entirely
standardised — a diploma, a robing, a public reading — and the office conferring
it had held no power of any kind for two hundred and fifty years. What made it
worth having was not that the caliph could do anything. It was that everybody in
the Islamic world agreed he was the caliph.

Askia Muhammad crossed the Sahara twice to get one.

## What it bought at home

Legitimacy against the men he had displaced, and an alliance with the scholars
his predecessor had persecuted.

Sunni Ali had treated the Timbuktu jurists as a political faction — killed some,
driven others to Walata, and by the chronicles' account never pretended to be
anything other than hostile. Askia Muhammad recalled them, endowed them, appointed
their leading families to judgeships, and applied the law visibly.

The chronicles that damn the founder of the empire celebrate the man who
overthrew his son, and they were written by the descendants of the people on the
receiving end of both policies. That bias is worth flagging before the
admiration in them is taken at face value.

## And what it did not fix

The succession.

Askia Muhammad ruled thirty-five years, went blind, and was deposed in 1528 by
his own son, who put him on an island in the Niger where he spent nine years
before another son brought him back to die in Gao. That son had already been
deposed by a brother.

Nobody ever wrote down how the throne was supposed to pass, and what followed was
close to a coup a decade for the rest of the century. A caliphal title obtained from Cairo settled the dynasty's standing
against outsiders and settled nothing at all inside the family.
