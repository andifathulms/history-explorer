This record's `revenue_basis` reads `trade-toll, tribute`, and there is a third
thing the chronicles are more specific about than either: the royal estates.

The *Ta'rikh al-Fattash* describes agricultural establishments along the Niger
worked by settled slaves — communities with a name, a headman, a fixed obligation
in sacks of grain, and in some accounts hundreds of workers on a single estate.
They fed the court, the army and the towns, and they were hereditary: the people
on them were slaves by descent, in categories the chronicles name, and were not
manumitted in the ordinary way.

## Why it is not a coded value

There is no value for it in this site's seven-item `revenue_basis` vocabulary.

`land-tax` means a state taxing agriculture it does not itself run. What this was
is direct production on crown land by an unfree workforce, which is closer to the
Ur III record's state agriculture or the Urartian fortress estates than to
anything the word tax describes — and both of those records code `land-tax` for
it, which is the vocabulary being stretched in the same direction twice before.

So the field carries the two values it can carry and this chapter carries the
third. That is this site's standard treatment and it has been used more than a
dozen times, and it is worth naming here specifically because of what is being
left out of a field: an institution that a substantial part of the empire's
population lived inside.

## Gomez, and what the older accounts left out

This record's `military_basis` is coded from Gomez rather than from Hunwick, and
the reason is that his book is direct about this in a way the earlier literature
was not.

The Niger empires ran on slavery. Slaves worked the estates, fought in the
armies, staffed the households and moved north across the Sahara as the region's
oldest export. Mali's record in this region carries `slave-soldier` for the same
reason, and Gomez's argument — that categories of caste, servility and descent in
West Africa were formed in this period and not by later European contact — is why
both records now say so in a field rather than only in prose.

A corpus that codes what a state's army rested on and where its money came from
will keep arriving at this answer for these two records, and the alternative to
saying it is a page that describes an empire without describing most of the
people in it.

## The other side of the same century

None of that made the state weak, which is the thing a modern reader may expect
it to have done.

Songhai in the middle of the sixteenth century was the largest polity in West
Africa's history to that point, with a reach figure of eight hundred thousand
square kilometres, a river fleet, a working judiciary in the towns, a class of
jurists corresponding with Cairo and Fez, and a book trade substantial enough
that the *Ta'rikh al-Sudan* mentions a scholar's library of sixteen hundred
volumes.

It was destroyed in an afternoon by four thousand men with guns, which is the
last chapter.
