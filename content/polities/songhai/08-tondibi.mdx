In 1591 a Moroccan expeditionary force crossed the Sahara — something in the
order of four thousand men, with pack animals, a train, and firearms — and met
the Songhai army at Tondibi north of Gao.

The Songhai force was many times larger. It had cavalry, infantry and, by the
chronicles' account, a herd of cattle driven at the Moroccan line to break it.
The cattle turned when the guns went off and went back through their own army.

The battle lasted a short time. Gao and Timbuktu fell within weeks.

## The clearest gunpowder case there is

Most of the famous gunpowder battles of the sixteenth century are less decisive
than they look. At Chaldiran in 1514 the Ottoman guns mattered less than Ottoman
infantry discipline; at Panipat in 1526 the artillery was one factor among
several, and the numbers were not wildly unequal in either.

Here a force outnumbered several times over crossed sixteen hundred kilometres of
desert, arrived depleted and sick, and won in an afternoon. Every account from
both sides says the same thing about why. The Songhai cavalry had never faced
massed firearms; the horses would not stand it; and no amount of numbers or
courage substituted for a weapon the other side had and this side did not.

## What Morocco got

Not an empire. A garrison.

The Saadi state could not administer the middle Niger from Marrakesh across the
Sahara, and within a generation the expeditionary force and its descendants — the
*Arma* — were an autonomous military caste governing the towns on their own
account and paying Morocco nothing. They made no
claim on Songhai's legitimacy of any kind: they held the towns as an occupying
garrison and said so, which is unusual, since a conqueror who intends to stay
almost always finds a reason why he is the rightful heir.

What the conquest destroyed was the thing that had made the region legible to the
rest of the world: the empire that guaranteed the roads, the courts that applied a
known law, and the scholarly establishment that wrote in a language Cairo could
read. Ahmad Baba, the most eminent Timbuktu jurist of the period, was arrested,
his library confiscated, and he was deported to Marrakesh, where he spent twelve
years and wrote — among other things — a treatise on the legality of enslaving
West Africans, arguing against much of what was being done.

## The end of a system, not a state

Songhai is unusual among the African empires in having an ending anybody can
date: a battle, a day, a named enemy, and accounts from both sides. Ghana, Mali,
Aksum, Kush and Kanem all end in slow contractions nobody recorded, because there
was nobody outside with an interest in writing it down. Songhai ends precisely
because the power that ended it came from the other side of the Sahara and kept
records.

What ended with it was larger than the polity. The trans-Saharan trade system had
been running for something like eight hundred years and had supported three
successive empires; by 1591 the Atlantic had already begun taking the gold west
to European ships, and the Moroccan conquest removed the political structure that
had held the southern end of the old system together. There was no fourth empire
of the Niger.

The two Timbuktu chronicles that supply almost everything anybody knows about
this empire were written in the decades afterwards, by men living under the Arma,
describing a world that had stopped within their own memory. Nearly all of what
can be said about Songhai is therefore said in the register of elegy, by people
who had watched it end.
