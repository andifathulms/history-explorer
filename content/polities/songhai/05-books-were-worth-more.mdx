Around 1510 a young Granada-born Moroccan travelled to Timbuktu, and about
fifteen years later, having been captured by corsairs and presented to the pope,
wrote down what he had seen. Leo Africanus is the only outsider who described
Songhai at its height.

The city he found was not built of stone. The houses, he says, are cottages of
chalk thatched with straw. But there is a stately temple with walls of stone and
lime, and a princely palace *built by a most excellent workeman of Granada* —
which is to say by a Spanish Muslim architect, brought fifteen hundred miles
south across the desert to build for an African king.

There are shops of artificers and merchants, and especially of weavers of linen
and cotton. The women, except maidservants, go veiled and sell all the food.
Corn, cattle, milk and butter are plentiful. Salt is not: it comes five hundred
miles overland from Taghaza, and Leo saw a single camel-load sold for eighty
ducats.

## The court

The king had gold sceptres and plate, some pieces of which Leo says weighed
thirteen hundred pounds — a figure to be read as *a great deal* rather than
weighed.

He kept three thousand horsemen and a large number of footmen with poisoned
arrows. He rode a camel, led by one of his noblemen, while his soldiers rode
horses. When merchants arrived in town with horses for sale, the king had a
number brought before him, chose the best for himself, and paid, Leo says, a most
liberal price.

And the protocol, which is the passage most often quoted:

> Whosoeuer will speake vnto this king must first fall downe before his feete, &
> then taking vp earth must sprinkle it vpon his owne head & shoulders.

Ibn Battuta had described the same ceremony at the Malian court a hundred and
sixty years earlier and disapproved of it. Leo reports it without comment, as one
of the customs of the place, observed by everybody who had not saluted the king
before and by ambassadors from other princes.

## Where the money went

Two sentences of Leo's have carried the reputation of Timbuktu for five hundred
years, and they are worth quoting exactly:

> Here are great store of doctors, iudges, priests, and other learned men, that
> are bountifully maintained at the kings cost and charges. And hither are
> brought diuers manuscripts or written bookes out of Barbarie, which are sold
> for more money than any other merchandize.

Books were the most profitable thing to carry to Timbuktu. Not salt, which came
five hundred miles and sold for eighty ducats a camel-load; not cloth; not
horses. Manuscripts, imported from north Africa, into a city on the edge of the
Sahara, bought by scholars whose salaries the king was paying.

That is a description of a state deliberately buying an intellectual
establishment, and it says more about what the Askias thought they were doing
than any amount of campaign narrative.

## The other side of the same page

Leo does not soften what paid for it, and the sentence sits four lines above the
one about the books.

The king's forces, he says, have frequent skirmishes with those who refuse to pay
tribute — *and so many as they take, they sell vnto the merchants of Tombuto.*

Captives taken from tributaries who had not paid were sold in the market of the
city whose scholars were being maintained at the king's expense. Both facts are in
the same paragraph of the same account by the same man, and any description of
this empire that keeps one and drops the other is not describing it.
