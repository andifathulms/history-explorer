The Safavids were a Sufi order before they were anything else. Shaykh Safi
al-Din, who died in 1334, founded a tariqa at Ardabil in Azerbaijan; it was
Sunni, it was respectable, it attracted endowments, and for four generations its
shaykhs were religious figures with a large following and no army.

<Figure id="ardabil-shrine" />

Somewhere in the fifteenth century that changed, and the change is the whole
explanation of what happened in 1501. The order's following moved from settled
Persian-speaking devotees to Turkmen tribes in Anatolia and Azerbaijan — the
same human material as the two sheep, from the same pastures — and the doctrine
moved with it, towards an Alid messianism that scholars call *ghuluww*,
exaggeration: the belief that the imam's authority was present in a living man,
that the end of the world was near, and that the shaykh was the one who would
bring it.

Babayan's book is about that world, and its argument is that the Safavid state
did not emerge from a religious movement so much as it emerged as one and then
had to be converted into a government. Shaykh Junayd and his son Haydar led
their followers into battle in the 1450s and 1460s and were both killed doing
it; Haydar gave them the red twelve-gored cap that got them their name,
Qizilbash, red-heads.

<Figure id="ardabil-carpet" />

Isma'il was Haydar's son, and he was raised in hiding as the object of that
expectation. His Turkish poetry, written under the pen-name Khata'i, does not
gesture at it. He tells his followers that he is Alexander and Khidr and the
eye of God, that Adam's clothing was changed and he is Faridun and Khusraw and
Jesus, and that whoever follows him will not die.

Rulers claim divine sanction all the time and mean something modest by it. This
is not that. A young man is telling armed men that he is God's eye and that
following him confers immortality, in verse, under his own name, and they are
believing him well enough to charge Ottoman artillery on the strength of it.
