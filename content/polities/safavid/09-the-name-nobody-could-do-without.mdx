The Safavid state fell in 1722. For the next fourteen years everybody who
governed any part of Iran did so in the name of a Safavid, including the men who
had no intention of giving anything back.

## The puppets

Tahmasp II got out of Isfahan before it fell and was proclaimed in the north. He
had almost nothing — no army, no treasury and no province he could hold — and he
was, for four years, the only legitimate authority in the country, because he
was the only Safavid at liberty.

Nadir Quli took service with him in 1726 and did the work: beat the Afghans out
of Isfahan in 1729, pushed them back to Kandahar, fought the Ottomans in the
west. Then he deposed Tahmasp in 1732 in favour of Tahmasp's infant son, and
governed for four more years as regent for a baby called Abbas III.

That is the measure of what the dynasty had become. A commander who had
personally reconquered the country still could not rule it under his own name,
so he found a Safavid who could not yet speak and ruled as his deputy.

## Mughan

In 1736 he called an assembly on the Mughan plain and had himself proclaimed
shah, on the condition — he made a point of the condition — that the Safavid
line was finished and nobody would restore it.

Hanway, writing seventeen years later from what he could gather in Russia and
Persia, treats the whole sequence as a usurpation dressed up as an election, and
he is not wrong about the mechanics. What he registers less clearly is why the
theatre was necessary at all. Nadir had won every battle he had fought. He
needed an assembly because the alternative was to be one more general who had
seized a throne, and Iran had a two-hundred-year habit of believing that thrones
belonged to one family.

Safavid claimants kept appearing afterwards, and were used by anybody who needed
one. The Zands governed for decades without ever taking the title of shah,
styling their ruler the deputy — the *vakil* — which is a way of saying that the
office still belonged to somebody else.

## What actually survived

Not the family. The confession.

The Safavids found a country that was mostly Sunni and left one that was
overwhelmingly Twelver Shi'i, and no subsequent change of dynasty touched that.
The Afghans who took Isfahan were Sunni and could not alter it in seven years.
Nadir tried, seriously and for reasons of state — proposing that Twelver Shi'ism
be recognised as a fifth school of Sunni law so that he could rule Iranians and
Ottomans and Afghans without a confessional quarrel — and got nowhere with
anybody.

The clergy the dynasty had imported and endowed outlasted the dynasty by a long
way. It had its own revenues, its own courts, its own schools, and a doctrine
about where authority sits in the absence of the imam that did not require a
shah at all.

A family that claimed its founder was the eye of God ended with an infant regent
and a general's assembly on a plain. What it built instead — a clerical
establishment with an independent income and a theory of legitimacy — is still
there.
