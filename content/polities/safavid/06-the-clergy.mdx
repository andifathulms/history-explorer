title: The scholars they imported

In 1501 there was no Twelver Shi'i clerical establishment in Iran, because there
had been no Twelver Shi'i state in Iran to employ one. The dynasty had to build
it, and the way it did so has a long consequence.

The Safavids imported jurists — from Jabal Amil in what is now Lebanon, from
Bahrain, from Iraq — gave them stipends, endowments, appointments and land, and
put them in charge of law, education and the administration of religious
property. Within a century the imported scholars and their Iranian students were
an established class with their own institutions, their own revenues from
endowment, and their own doctrine of who held the imam's authority in his
absence.

That doctrine is the interesting part, because the dynasty had an answer to the
question and the scholars developed a different one. The Safavid answer, in the
first decades, was that the shah held it: Isma'il was the imam's presence, or
something even less qualified than that. The clerical answer, worked out over
the following two centuries, was that in the imam's absence his authority
devolved on the qualified jurists — which locates it in a class of scholars
rather than in a king, and is the ancestor of the doctrine an Iranian
revolution used against a different monarchy in 1979.

By the late seventeenth century the leading clerics were powerful enough to set
policy. Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, a vast compiler of hadith and the most
influential of them, held the senior religious office under the last effective
shah and pressed campaigns against Sufis, philosophers, wine and the religious
minorities the earlier Safavids had tolerated — including, at the end of the
period, the Sunnis of the eastern provinces, which is not unrelated to the
Afghan rising that finished the dynasty.

This is the pattern this site records on the Mamluk and Abbasid pages in a
different vocabulary. A ruler who cannot generate legitimacy himself imports an
institution that can supply it, and the institution outlives him and acquires
interests of its own. The Safavid version is the most complete: the clergy the
dynasty created to make itself lawful is still there, and the dynasty is not.
