---
title: Abbas breaks his own army
drafted_from: newman-2006
phase: peak
---

Abbas I came to the throne in 1588 at sixteen, over a state that had lost Tabriz
and much of the north-west to the Ottomans and Khurasan to the Uzbeks, and that
was governed in practice by Qizilbash chiefs who had made his dynasty and
expected to be paid in provinces for it. His first act was to make peace with
the Ottomans on humiliating terms, conceding the territory, in order to have one
war at a time.

Then he dealt with the Qizilbash, and how he did it is the most consequential
piece of institution-building on this page. He built a standing corps of
*ghulams* — Georgian, Armenian and Circassian captives from the Caucasus
campaigns, raised as Muslim converts, trained, paid, and owing loyalty to the
shah personally rather than to any tribe. He gave them the senior governorships
and the army commands. And he paid for them by converting provincial land from
tribal assignment into crown domain administered for the treasury, which took
the revenue away from the Qizilbash at the same time as it took the offices.

This site has now recorded that solution five times — the Abbasids at Samarra,
the Samanids, the Ghaznavids, the Ottomans, and here — and has recorded its
recurring failure four times: an army that can make a ruler eventually makes one
it prefers. The Safavid ghulams did not seize the throne. What happened instead
was subtler and, on Matthee's reading, worse: the corps became a court interest
group like the one it replaced, the crown lands it was paid from could not be
returned to tribal assignment when the money ran short, and the frontier
provinces lost the tribal cavalry that had defended them without gaining a
standing garrison that could.

With the new army he took everything back: Khurasan and Herat from the Uzbeks by
1603, Tabriz and the north-west from the Ottomans, Baghdad in 1623, Hormuz from
the Portuguese in 1622 with English ships. At his death in 1629 the empire was
larger and better governed than at any other point in its history.

He also had his eldest son executed and two others blinded, and ended the
practice of sending princes to the provinces to learn to govern. His successors
were raised in the harem.
