---
title: Fifty-one years, eleven of them indoors
drafted_from: italian-travels-grey
phase: peak
---

Tahmasp reigned for fifty-two years, which is longer than any other Safavid and
longer than most European reigns of the period, and almost nothing in the
standard account of the dynasty happens during it. In 1571 a Venetian envoy
named Vincentio d'Alessandri reached his court and wrote a report for the
Senate, and it explains the silence.

D'Alessandri had been sent to find out whether Iran was worth an alliance
against the Ottomans, so he wrote the kind of report a government wants: the
provinces and what they produce, the revenue and the expenditure, the number and
quality of the *sultans* — who, he notes for Venetian readers, are nothing but
commanders of soldiers — and the person of the king.

The king was in his sixty-fourth year and the fifty-first of his reign, of
middling stature, well made, dark, thick-lipped, with a grizzled beard, and
more melancholy than anything else. D'Alessandri says this is known by many
signs and principally by one: he had not come out of his palace for eleven
years, and had not gone once to the chase or to any other amusement in that
time.

The consequence is the part a Venetian would notice, because it is
administrative. By the custom of the country a subject presented a petition to
the king in person and justice was got by being seen; with the king indoors, it
could not be done. So people came and shouted for justice in front of the palace
day and night, sometimes a thousand of them, and the king heard the noise and
gave orders to send them away, saying that there were judges appointed in the
country and that justice was their business. What he did not take into account,
writes d'Alessandri, is that the complaints were against those judges and
*sultans*, who were in the habit of having people killed in the street — which
he adds he had seen himself, as had many others.

The rest of the report is a study in what a treasury does when the ruler will
not leave the harem. Tahmasp deliberated affairs of state with his women, and
was miserly by nature except with them. He bought cloth in Khurasan and Aleppo
and issued it to his soldiers as pay at ten times its value. He accepted any
present however small and did not always make one in return. He paid a tribal
chief on the eastern frontier four hundred *talleri* a year not to interfere with
the caravans from India.

And the succession was already arranged in the worst possible way. Of eleven
sons, the one the whole country wanted was Ismail, whom his father had put in
prison and kept there; the *sultans* were afraid of him for his pride, and
d'Alessandri's assessment is that if he ever came to the throne he would have to
replace a great many of the commanders and fight all his brothers, who had taken
possession of large parts of the kingdom. Ismail said he would bear his own
imprisonment patiently but would not have children to be prisoners too. The
third son, Haydar, was eighteen, small, beautiful, a fine speaker and horseman,
too delicate for war, and the father's favourite.

Tahmasp died in 1576. Ismail came out of prison, took the throne, killed most of
his brothers including Haydar, and was dead within eighteen months. Everything
d'Alessandri predicted happened, and it happened in a year and a half.

That is the reign the dynasty's greatest age was built on top of. It is not
nothing — the Ottoman wars were survived, the frontier was stabilised at Amasya
in 1555, the capital was moved to Qazvin, and the state that Abbas would rebuild
still existed to be rebuilt. But the report is a description of a monarchy that
has stopped performing the one function its subjects could see, and the
twelve years of dynastic murder that followed Tahmasp's death were not an
interruption of it.
