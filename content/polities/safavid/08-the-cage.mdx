Abbas I built the Safavid state and also, by the account of a man who lived at
the court a century later, installed the mechanism that took it apart. He was
afraid of his sons, and the arrangement he made about them became the rule.

## What Abbas did about his children

Krusinski — a Jesuit who spent years at Isfahan and was there when the Afghans
came — sets it out as an established practice with a named origin.

Abbas had his eldest son killed and blinded two others. Then he had his grandson
educated in a manner which was afterwards a rule for the education of all the
king's sons: shut up in the harem, with no conversation but with the eunuchs,
taught only to read and write, his only diversions shooting with a bow and
riding an ass in the gardens.

And Krusinski adds the detail that makes it a policy rather than a cruelty.
Abbas was so much on his guard against this child — who was far too young to
attempt anything against him — that it was said he made him take opium every day
to stupefy him, for fear the boy had more wit than he wished him to have.

## What it produced

Consider what that arrangement selects for over a hundred years.

A prince raised in that room has met no soldiers, no provincial governors and no
foreign ambassadors. He has no faction of his own, because he has never been
outside to build one, and he owes his survival entirely to the eunuchs and the
women who arranged it. When he is brought out and put on the throne he is the
only man in the palace who does not know how the empire works, and everybody who
does know is somebody who had a hand in choosing him.

Krusinski's judgement on one of the shahs of that century is the pattern in a
sentence: a very bad king in peacetime, covetous of his subjects' wealth,
committing crying acts of injustice for trifling profit, abandoning the
government wholly to his ministers while he devoted himself to ease and
pleasure, shut up in his harem — where he once stayed ten years together and
never went abroad.

That same man, he adds, conducted himself capably in war, and would not have
been much to blame if he had behaved as well in peace.

## Why this is not the whole explanation

It is worth resisting the version of this story in which a great empire is
destroyed by degenerate princes, because that is the account the eighteenth
century liked and it does too much work.

The Safavid state had structural problems that no sequence of able shahs would
have solved: a large, dry, thinly populated country whose revenues could not
support an empire of that extent; a silk trade increasingly bypassed by sea; and
a standing army that had to be paid whether or not there was a war. Abbas's own
solution to the Qizilbash — converting tribal cavalry into salaried ghulams — had
made the state permanently more expensive to run.

What the cage did was remove the one thing that might have addressed any of it.
A shah who has never left the harem cannot lead an army, cannot inspect a
province, and cannot arbitrate between the factions at court because he is the
creation of one of them. The empire could survive incompetence at the top for
decades. It could not survive it being systematic.

By 1722 the man on the throne was a pious, gentle and entirely unprepared ruler
who had been raised exactly as Abbas prescribed, and there were twenty thousand
Afghans outside the walls.
