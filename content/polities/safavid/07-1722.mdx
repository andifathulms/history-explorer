The standard account of the Safavid seventeenth century is a decline story with
a moral shape: weak shahs raised in the harem, wine, eunuchs, a clergy turning
intolerant, and a state that rotted until anyone could push it over. Matthee's
book is an argument against it, and this page follows him.

His account is structural. Iran is large, dry and thinly populated, and its
economic base could not generate the revenue an empire of that extent needed;
the silver that had come in through the silk trade began going out again as the
trade shifted; the state's composition was a network of tribal and provincial
alliances that had to be renegotiated continuously and never became an
administration; and the ghulam system that had replaced the Qizilbash left the
frontiers without the cavalry that had held them. The shahs after Abbas I were
not obviously worse men than the shahs before him. They inherited a state whose
solutions to its first century's problems had become its second century's
problems, and they had less to work with.

The end came from Kandahar, a province the Safavids had spent a century
contesting with the Mughals, where the Sunni Ghilzai Afghans rose in 1709 over
the government's treatment of them. In 1722 a Ghilzai force of perhaps twenty
thousand under Mahmud Hotak crossed Iran, beat a much larger Safavid army at
Gulnabad outside Isfahan, and then simply sat down around the capital. There was
no assault. The siege lasted six months, the city starved — the reported
mortality is enormous — and Shah Sultan Husayn walked out and handed the crown
to Mahmud in person.

That is the fall, and it should be read the way Matthee reads it: not as a
verdict on Safavid civilisation but as what happens to a state with a small
fiscal base, no standing field army it can concentrate, and a capital it cannot
provision. Isfahan had been built as a commercial instrument and could not feed
itself under blockade.

The dynasty formally continued for fourteen more years as other men's
authorisation. Nadir Shah, a Turkmen commander from Khurasan, drove the Afghans
out and ruled in the name of Safavid puppets until 1736, when he had himself
proclaimed and ended the line. This site's `ended.by` is empty because neither
the Hotaki Afghans nor the Afsharids are in this corpus, and putting one of them
in the field would resolve to nothing.
