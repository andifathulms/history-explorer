Abbas I inherited a state losing wars to the Ottomans and the Uzbeks and governed
by Qizilbash tribal chiefs who had made his family and expected to keep the
returns. He made peace on bad terms, dealt with the Qizilbash, and only then
fought.

Dealing with them meant building an alternative: a standing army of *ghulams*,
Georgian, Armenian and Circassian captives raised as converts and loyal to the
shah personally, paid from crown lands converted out of tribal assignment. This
site has now recorded that solution five times — the Abbasids at Samarra, the
Samanids, the Ghaznavids, the Ottomans, and here — and its consequence four
times.

He moved the capital to Isfahan in 1598 and rebuilt it around a vast rectangular
maidan with a royal mosque at one end, a bazaar at the other, and a palace and a
private mosque on the flanks. It is one of the most complete surviving pieces of
imperial urban planning anywhere, and it was built as a commercial instrument as
much as a monument: Abbas ran the silk export as a crown monopoly, relocated
Armenian merchants from Julfa to a new suburb to operate it, and dealt directly
with English and Dutch companies to route silk around Ottoman territory.

The dynasty declined through the seventeenth century and fell in 1722 to an
Afghan force from Kandahar — a province it had spent a century contesting with
the Mughals — after which Nadir Shah ruled in the name of Safavid puppets before
ending the line in 1736.
