Songtsen Gampo died in 650, and the throne passed to a grandson still
too young to rule. For the next two decades the empire was run by the
minister his grandfather had trusted with the Chinese marriage
negotiations in the first place - a man the Tang court's own historians,
writing about a rival power, still called an excellent regent.

## An old ally in charge

Gar Tongtsen - Lutungtsan to the Chinese chroniclers, of the Gar clan -
had been the envoy who traveled to Chang'an to receive Princess
Wencheng on Songtsen Gampo's behalf. When the emperor Taizong offered
him a bride of his own, a granddaughter of the imperial house, Gar
Tongtsen turned it down: he already had a wife chosen for him by his
own parents and could not send her away, he said, and in any case it
would not do for a minister to marry before his own sovereign's bride
had even met her husband. Taizong was impressed rather than offended.

That same tact ran the empire for the better part of twenty years after
Songtsen Gampo's son died young and left the throne to an infant
grandson - the ruler later called Mangsong Mangtsen. Illiterate but
"naturally wise, resolute, strict and honourable," in the Tang
histories' own description of a man they had every reason to resent,
Gar Tongtsen held the regency and pushed the empire's frontier further
than Songtsen Gampo himself had managed.

## The Tuyuhun absorbed

His target was the Tuyuhun, the Xianbei-descended kingdom that
controlled the Koko Nor basin and, with it, the trade routes into the
Tarim. Tibet and the Tuyuhun had traded rival memorials with the Tang
court for years, each accusing the other of provocation, while the
Chinese government declined to arbitrate. Under Gar Tongtsen's
direction Tibetan forces exploited the opening: a defecting Tuyuhun
minister handed over the kingdom's weaknesses, and by the late 660s the
Tuyuhun state had collapsed outright. Its last prince fled to Chinese
territory with the Tang princess who had married into his own house,
begging Chang'an for refuge rather than face the Tibetans who now held
his former kingdom.

The conquest did more than add territory. It gave Tibet direct access
to the Silk Road oases of the Tarim basin for the first time, and a
forward base from which Tibetan and Chinese garrisons would contest
Kashgar, Khotan, Kucha and Karashahr for the next century.

## What outlived him

Gar Tongtsen did not live to see the full consequence of what he had
built. He died before the conquest was entirely secured, but the
regency did not end with him: his five sons inherited his authority
the way a Tang court, watching uneasily from across the frontier, might
have expected a hereditary ministry to work rather than a foreign
court's own succession. The eldest, Gar Trinring, took over the central
administration; his brothers divided the outlying commands between
them, one of them holding the eastern frontier facing China itself for
more than thirty years. For a generation after Gar Tongtsen's death,
the family he had built continued running an empire that its own royal line was, for now, too young or
too preoccupied to run itself.
