---
title: The third power
drafted_from: beckwith-1987
phase: peak
---

For roughly two centuries there were three great powers in inner Asia, not two.
The Tibetan empire fought the Tang for the Tarim basin oases and the Gansu
corridor, fought the caliphate's eastern frontier in Transoxiana and the Pamirs,
and at times allied with one against the other. In 763 a Tibetan army took
Chang'an, the Tang capital, and installed an emperor of its own choosing before
withdrawing.

This is the piece routinely missing from accounts of the period written from
either the Islamic or the Chinese side, because it sits between them. The Abbasid
frontier in Central Asia was not simply the far edge of the caliphate; it was one
face of a three-cornered contest, and the Karluk, Turgesh and Uyghur groups in
between made and unmade alliances with all three.

The empire's other legacy is religious and script-based. Songtsen Gampo's reign
saw an alphabet devised for Tibetan on an Indic model, and Trisong Detsen made
Buddhism the state religion and founded the monastery at Samye in the 770s,
importing Indian monastic Buddhism directly rather than through China. Both are
counted on this page: a new writing system created for a language that lacked
one, and a tradition propagated well beyond the polity's own prior boundary.

It broke apart after Langdarma's assassination in 842, into a fragmentation
recorded here without a conquering agent, because there was not one.
