The empire fell apart in the 840s and Tibet did not become a state again for
four hundred years. What the empire had built that survived the empire was a
translation.

## What the vocabulary did

The decreed vocabulary is the unglamorous part and it is the reason the whole
enterprise worked. It made the body of translation internally consistent across
thousands of volumes and hundreds of translators, and it made the Tibetan
versions reversible: a scholar can often reconstruct the Sanskrit from the
Tibetan word by word, because the same term was never rendered two ways.

Korea and Japan received their Buddhism through Chinese translations, which are
not reversible in that way, having been made by many hands over centuries with no
authority standardising them.

## Why that turned out to matter to everybody else

Buddhism disappeared from India. Over the centuries after the empire fell, the
monastic universities were destroyed, patronage went elsewhere, and a very
large part of Indian Buddhist literature was lost in its original language.

The Tibetan canon has it. For a great many Indian Buddhist works — including
central texts of Madhyamaka and Yogacara philosophy, and much of the tantric
literature — the Tibetan translation is the only complete version that exists,
and it is exact enough to be used as evidence for what the Sanskrit said.

So a decision taken by a Tibetan king in the eighth century about which of two
neighbours to import a religion from is the reason a substantial part of Indian
intellectual history is recoverable at all. It is not a Tibetan legacy in the
ordinary sense. It is an Indian legacy that Tibet happens to be holding.

## The four hundred years

The intervening period is usually called the era of fragmentation and it is
badly served by the name, which suggests nothing happened.

The imperial line did not end; a branch of it went west and founded the kingdom
of Guge in Ngari, which in the eleventh century paid for the second wave of
translation and invited Atisha from Bengal. The monastic orders that dominate
Tibetan history afterwards — Kadam, Sakya, Kagyu — all form in that period, out
of the second transmission, working from and adding to the books the empire had
paid for.

What did not come back was the state. When Tibet next appears as a political
unit under a single authority, in the thirteenth century, it is as a
territory administered for the Mongols by the abbots of Sakya — a monastery
governing a country on behalf of a steppe empire.

That is the empire's real afterlife and its strangest one. It had made Buddhism
the instrument of the Tibetan state; four centuries later the Buddhist
establishment was the Tibetan state, and the arrangement lasted, in one form or
another, until 1959.
