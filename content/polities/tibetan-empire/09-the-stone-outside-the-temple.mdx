In 821 and 822 the Tibetan empire and the Tang concluded the last of their
sworn treaties, and the text is the clearest statement anyone made of what the
two centuries of fighting had established.

## The terms

The oath was sworn on an altar in the western suburb of the Chinese capital,
with a pit dug on the north side; the victims were sacrificed and buried with
the written text.

> What the Chinese now rule shall have the Tang as the sovereign, and the
> country of their western race shall have the great Fan as ruler. From this
> time henceforward both shall put by weapons and armour, forget their
> differences and old grievances, and respect the honoured kinship of their
> sovereigns and the ancient bonds of mutual aid. The frontier guard-houses
> shall be left ungarrisoned, and the watch-fires no longer lighted... the
> barrier stations and fortifications shall be disused and invasion and plunder
> shall cease.

And then the verse the ministers proclaimed:

> The frontier mountains are lofty, the River flows on unceasingly: on a
> propitious day and favourable season have we fixed the two boundaries, the
> west to belong to the great Fan, the east to be ruled by the grand Tang: the
> great ministers, holding up the sworn treaty, proclaim it afar to the autumn
> country.

Two sovereigns, two territories, one border, no tribute either way. That is
not how the Tang concluded arrangements with frontier peoples. It is how two
empires conclude a peace.

The treaty was ratified in Tibet and cut on a stone pillar, which was set up in
Lhasa outside the Jokhang temple and is still standing.

## Twenty years

The empire had about twenty years left.

What ended it is domestic and badly documented. Langdarma was assassinated in
842; the succession was disputed between two claimants and never resolved; the
great families that had supplied the empire's ministers took their own regions
and stopped taking orders; a revolt of the common people in the 860s finished
whatever central administration was left, and the imperial tombs were dug up.

There is no conquering power in it. Nobody took Tibet in 842 or afterwards. The
Tang, which had spent two hundred years failing to, was itself sixty years from
its end and in no condition to try. The Uyghur khaganate on the northern steppe
had collapsed two years earlier, in 840, for reasons of its own.

## What went with it

The state religion went. Buddhism had been made the court religion by Trisong
Detsen and endowed on an imperial scale, and imperial endowment is what
monasteries live on. When the court stopped, the monastic establishment stopped
with it, for something over a century.

The empire's other creation did not stop. The translation programme had already
produced a library, and libraries do not need a government.

What came back in the eleventh century was built on the books the empire had
paid for, by men who no longer had any idea how to raise an army.
