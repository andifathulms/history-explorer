Trisong Detsen made Buddhism the religion of the Tibetan court in the second half
of the eighth century and founded the monastery at Samye in the 770s. Then he had
to decide whose Buddhism it was going to be, and the choice was between his two
largest neighbours.

## The question

Tibet sat between India and China and could have taken the religion from either.
The Chinese route was the obvious one: the empire had been dealing with the Tang
for a century and a half, it had captured Chinese territory full of Buddhist
establishments, and Chan teachers were already in the country. The Indian route
meant bringing scholars over the Himalaya and working in a language nobody in
Tibet had grown up speaking.

The tradition records a debate at Samye, placed in the 790s, between an Indian
monk arguing the gradual path of study and accumulated merit and a Chinese Chan
master arguing sudden enlightenment. The Indian side is recorded as winning.

Whether it happened as a single staged event is doubted, and the sources for it
are Tibetan and later and have an interest in the outcome. That a choice was made
in that direction is not in doubt at all, because everything downstream of it is
visible.

## What a king was choosing

It is worth being clear that this was not only a doctrinal question, because the
doctrinal question is the part that has survived and the political one is the
part that would have decided it.

Trisong Detsen was at war with the Tang for most of his reign; his armies took
Chang'an in 763 and he spent decades holding the Gansu corridor against Chinese
attempts to recover it. Adopting the religious establishment of the state he was
fighting, staffed by its clergy and reading its commentaries, would have put a
Chinese-trained institution at the centre of his court.

India was not a rival. There was no Indian empire on his frontier, no Indian army
he was going to have to fight, and no Indian court whose prestige his own
nobility might prefer to his. Importing from India meant importing scholars
without importing a patron.

## What it committed the state to

Everything that followed was expensive.

Taking the religion in Sanskrit rather than in Chinese translation meant a
translation programme built from nothing: Indian *panditas* brought in and
maintained, Tibetan translators trained in a language with no prior tradition of
scholarship in it, and — the decision that made the whole thing work — a
standardised technical vocabulary decreed by the court, so that a given Sanskrit
term produced the same Tibetan word in every text by every translator.

A state deciding by edict what words mean, and enforcing it across a body of
translation running to thousands of volumes, is an administrative act on the
scale of a tax reform. It was paid for out of the same revenue as the armies in
the Tarim basin.

Nobody involved was buying an institution that would outlast the court. That is
simply what they got.
