The Gar family had run the empire for two generations when the
Tibetan throne finally moved against them - and it did it the same way
Gar Tongtsen himself had once out-argued a Tang envoy: with patience,
and while the family's own head was somewhere else.

## Thirty years too many

Gar Trinring, eldest of Gar Tongtsen's five sons, had held the central
administration for decades by the time Tridu Songtsen came of age -
long enough that the Tang court's own chroniclers, describing a
negotiation over disputed frontier garrisons, recorded an exchange in
which a Chinese envoy accused him outright of betraying his father's
loyalty. Trinring's answer survives in the same source: his family
had held the empire together through decades of vigilance, he said, and
territorial demands were about frontier security, not appetite. It is,
strikingly, the fullest first-person political argument any Tibetan
minister is recorded making in a source of this era - preserved only
because it happened to be made to a Chinese audience.

In 699, with Trinring away from court, the young tsanp'u called a
secret council with a minister of his own choosing, announced he was
leaving on a hunting trip, and used the muster to seize and execute
more than two thousand of Trinring's own relatives and retainers.
Summoned home to answer for it, Trinring raised what troops he could,
was deserted by them before battle was joined, and killed himself; more
than a hundred of his own household followed him in death the same
day. One brother came in to submit and was given an honorary command
against the family's own former rivals; within the year he too was
dead. Four generations after Gar Tongtsen had talked his way out of an
unwanted royal marriage, the dynasty he had spent his life serving
destroyed the family he had founded in a single orchestrated purge.

## A throne too young again

Tridu Songtsen did not enjoy his recovered authority for long. He died
on campaign against a border revolt, and the succession dispute that
followed took months to settle before the country agreed on a seven
year old son of the dead tsanp'u's own line. Real power passed, in the
same pattern that had governed the empire for half a century now
already, to whoever could actually hold it on a child's behalf - this
time not a minister's family, but the boy's own grandmother.

## The grandmother's peace

Bushell's translation never gives her a name of her own - she appears
only as "the grandmother of the tsanp'u," or by the Chinese transliteration
of her title, k'otun - but her diplomacy is on the record in more
detail than most Tibetan ministers of the previous century. She
managed the empire's relations with the Tang court through Empress Wu
Zetian's own final years and the chaotic reigns that followed her, and
in 710 she sent an embassy asking for a bride for her grown grandson,
exactly as an earlier Tibetan court had done for Songtsen Gampo seven
decades before. The Tang emperor granted it: an adopted daughter, given
the title Princess of Jincheng, dispatched west with an escort large
enough that the sovereign himself rode out from the capital to see her
off and, by his own court's account, wept doing it.

It was the same mechanism Gar Tongtsen had once negotiated and Gar
Trinring had once argued over - marriage alliance as the visible face
of a relationship actually built on which side could field the larger
army - run now by a grandmother regent rather than a hereditary
ministry, for a grandson who would spend his own long reign fighting
the wars the Chang'an campaign of 763 would bring to a head.
