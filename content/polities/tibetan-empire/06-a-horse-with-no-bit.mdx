The two empires swore treaties at each other for two centuries. The Tang
histories record the ceremonies in detail, including the negotiations about what
to kill and the occasion on which the ceremony was an ambush.

## What to sacrifice

At the peace of 783 the two delegations agreed the shape of the thing in advance:
two thousand men each, half armed and drawn up two hundred paces from the altar,
half unarmed below it; seven men a side in court costume to go up and swear.

It had been agreed that the Chinese would sacrifice an ox and the Tibetans a
horse. Then the Chinese envoy, Zhang Yi — who, the history says, was *ashamed of
his part in the ceremony* and wanted to depreciate the rites — proposed a
substitution:

> The Han cannot cultivate the ground without oxen, the Fan cannot travel without
> horses, I propose therefore to substitute a sheep, pig and dog as the three
> victims.

The Tibetan minister agreed. But there were no pigs to be had outside the
frontier, so he took a wild ram instead and the Chinese took a dog and a sheep.
They were killed on the north side of the altar, the blood mixed in two vessels
and smeared on the lips of the swearers.

An envoy embarrassed to be treating with barbarians downgrades the victims on a
pretext about agriculture, and the other side, being on the wrong side of a
frontier with no pigs, improvises. That is what two empires negotiating as equals
looked like from close up.

## Pingliang

Four years later they arranged to swear again, and the Chinese chose the ground
carefully.

The Tibetan minister had proposed a valley called Tulishu. The general Ma Yulin
reported that the Tulishu country was full of difficult passes and that he feared
a secret ambush; Pingliang, by contrast, was a plain open on every side and close
to a Chinese garrison town. The court agreed and moved the ceremony there, and
had to send after the Tibetan envoy, who had already left, to tell him.

At Pingliang the two commanders agreed three thousand men a side drawn up east
and west of the altar, four hundred unarmed men below it, and mounted scouts on
both sides to watch each other.

Then the Tibetan minister massed several tens of thousands of picked horsemen
west of the altar. His scouts rode *through and through* the Chinese ranks. When
the Chinese general sent sixty horsemen into the middle of the Tibetan army they
were seized and bound on the spot.

> Hsien had provided for nothing.

A message came across asking that the minister and his subordinates put on their
official robes and hats, swords and jewels, and wait for instructions — *this was
to induce them to dismount from their horses so that they might be captured*.
They went into the tent together, calm and unsuspecting. The Tibetan commander
had the drums beaten three times and his army came on shouting.

## The ride

The Chinese commander Hun Jian got out of the back of the tent, caught a loose
horse and rode.

> The horse had no bit in its mouth, and Hsien had to lean over its mane to guide
> it with his hands, and it was only after he had ridden for several miles that
> he got the bit into its mouth, and it was owing to this that the arrows of the
> pursuing horsemen passed by without wounding him.

One of his generals collected a few hundred men on a hillock to the north and
fought until the whole army surrounded him. The treaty officials were taken.

## Why the record kept it

A court history is not obliged to preserve an episode in which its own side
chooses the ground for safety, agrees the numbers, walks into the tent, and is
taken. The Tang histories preserve this one in circumstantial detail, down to the
horse's missing bit.

Part of the reason is that Hun Jian survived to report it and somebody had to be
blamed; the sentence about providing for nothing is a verdict. But the larger
part is that the treaty system itself mattered. The Tang had been making sworn
agreements with the Tibetans since the seventh century and would make another
thirty-five years later — the one cut on stone in Lhasa, with its two sovereigns
and its single border. A record of what happened when the ceremony was used as a
trap is exactly what a chancery preserves, and reads.
