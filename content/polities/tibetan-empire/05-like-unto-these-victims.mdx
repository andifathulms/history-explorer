The Tang histories carry a description of how the Tibetan state worked, compiled
by people who had to deal with it and who wrote down the parts a foreign ministry
needs to know. It is the only account of the empire's institutions written while
it existed by anybody who was not inside it.

## The machinery

The sovereign is the *tsanpo* — from *tsan*, a famous hero, and *pu*, a man. His
consort is the *momeng*. Under him are ministers styled great *lun* and small
*lun*, and the histories set out the whole establishment: a chief minister and
his assistant, a commander-in-chief, a chief minister of the interior with an
assistant and a lesser minister, a chief consulting minister with the same two
grades under him, the whole body named collectively in one Tibetan phrase the
Chinese transcribe and do not translate.

Then the observation that a Chinese archivist would find worth making:

> Although there are officers, they are not constantly employed, being only
> appointed when there is stress of business.

An establishment that exists on paper and is staffed when required is a
description of a state run by an aristocracy rather than a bureaucracy, from the
point of view of the most bureaucratic government on earth.

The communications are the part they admired. A gold arrow seven inches long
served as a sign of office and was used to call warriors out. There was a post
station every hundred *li*; if the war was important the courier carried a silver
hawk on his breast, and if it was urgent, several hawks. When the country was
invaded, smoke-fires were lit from towers a hundred *li* apart.

## No writing

The same passage states, flatly, that the Tibetans have no written characters,
and that notched pieces of wood and knotted strings are used in covenants.

That cannot be true of the empire the Tang were fighting. An alphabet was devised
for Tibetan on an Indic model under Songtsen Gampo; the treaty of 821 was cut in
Tibetan on a stone pillar; and the imperial chancery was producing documents in
Tibetan that survive.

What the sentence preserves is an earlier notice, copied forward into an account
of a later state — which is how these compilations were made, and a useful
warning about the rest of the description.

## Punishment and hospitality

Two details sit next to each other and are worth keeping together.

> Their punishments are most severe, and even for small crimes the eyes are
> scooped out, and the nose cut off, or stripes inflicted with a leather whip.
> They differ according to caprice, there being no fixed code. They imprison men
> in holes several tens of feet under the ground, and release them only after two
> or three years.

And:

> When they entertain envoys from foreign countries, they always bring out a yak
> for the guest himself to shoot, the flesh of which is afterwards served at the
> banquet.

The first is an accusation — a Chinese historian's standard complaint about a
government that has punishments without a written code, which is the thing his
own state most prided itself on having. The second is a piece of protocol
somebody experienced and reported, and it is the only glimpse anywhere of what
being received at a Tibetan court was like.

## The oath

The central institution, and the one the Chinese describe at most length, is a
ceremony.

Once a year the officers were assembled for the lesser oath of fealty. Sheep,
dogs and monkeys were sacrificed — their legs broken first, then killed, the
intestines exposed and cut into pieces. The sorcerers were summoned and called
on the gods of heaven and earth, of the mountains and rivers, of the sun, moon,
stars and planets:

> Should your hearts become changed, and your thoughts disloyal, the gods will
> see clearly and make you like these sheep and dogs.

Every third year came the great ceremony. All assembled in the middle of the
night on a raised altar spread with savoury meats. The victims were men, horses,
oxen and asses, and the prayer ran:

> Do you all with one heart and united strength cherish our native country. The
> god of heaven, and the spirit of the earth, will both know your thoughts, and
> if you break this oath they will cause your bodies to be cut into pieces like
> unto these victims.

## What that is a description of

Not barbarism, whatever the compiler thought. It is a constitutional mechanism.

An empire with no standing bureaucracy, whose officers are appointed when there
is business and whose army is summoned by sending out a gold arrow, is held
together by the personal obligation of a few hundred aristocrats to one man. The
Chinese state bound its servants with an examination system, a code and a
salary. The Tibetan state assembled them once a year and, every third year at
midnight, showed them what they would look like if they broke their word.

For two centuries it worked better than the alternative. When it stopped working
— when the great families took their own regions after 842 and stopped taking
orders — there was nothing underneath it, because the oath *was* the
administration.
