title: Which Buddhism
drafted_from: beckwith-1987

Trisong Detsen made Buddhism the state religion and founded Samye in the 770s,
and then had to decide which Buddhism. The tradition records a debate at Samye,
probably in the 790s, between an Indian monk arguing for the gradual path of
study and accumulated merit and a Chinese Chan master arguing for sudden
enlightenment. The Indian side is recorded as winning, and Tibetan Buddhism took
its Indian monastic and scholastic form thereafter.

Whether the debate happened as a single event is doubted; that a choice was made
in that direction is not. Its consequence is that Tibet imported Indian Buddhism
directly, in Sanskrit, with a vast translation programme and a standardised
vocabulary decreed by the court — rather than receiving it through China, as
Korea and Japan did.

That is why the Tibetan canon preserves Indian Buddhist texts whose Sanskrit
originals were lost when Buddhism disappeared from India, and why it is a primary
source for Indian intellectual history rather than only for Tibetan.

The empire fell apart after Langdarma's assassination in 842 and the monasteries
went with it for over a century, because imperial endowment is what they lived
on. What returned in the eleventh century was built on the translations the
empire had commissioned — which is an odd shape for a legacy to have. The thing
the state propagated took its lasting form two hundred years after the state
that propagated it had ceased to exist.
