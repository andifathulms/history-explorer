In the tenth month of 763 a Tibetan army walked into the capital of the Tang
empire, deposed the dynasty, appointed a new emperor with a new reign title and
a full slate of ministers, and went home a fortnight later.

The Tang histories record all of it, because they had to.

## How it happened

The empire was still broken from the An Lushan rebellion. Tibetan forces took
Jingzhou, whose governor Gao Hui surrendered to them; the next month they
invaded Binzhou. The great general Guo Ziyi was sent west to hold them, and had
to turn back when two hundred thousand Tuyuhun and Tangut troops came in from
another direction.

Then the emperor's carriage left for Shangzhou and the capital was abandoned.
The surrendered governor led the Tibetans in.

Inside the city, with the Tibetan commander-in-chief, they raised the Prince of
Guangwu to the throne, gave his reign the title Dashe, and appointed the various
officers of state.

## What the empire looked like that autumn

Bushell's translation keeps the detail, and the detail is the humiliation.

When the emperor first went east, the officers and their families fled south or
went into the mountains to hide, and the soldiers of the imperial armies broke
up into armed bands blocking the roads. Guo Ziyi — the most celebrated soldier
of the dynasty — was in a mountain valley with a few hundred followers, his
wives, children and slaves, and some hundreds of camels, horses, carts and oxen,
not knowing which way to turn, until two of his officers came and told him he
ought to be ashamed of himself.

He agreed, and took a back road because the main one would be watched. His
column of a thousand men was strung out over a hundred li because the mountain
track was too narrow, and two of his officers left by a different route in case
of pursuit, since in a defile the van could not help the rear.

Meanwhile the general of the six armies had fled the capital with his own
standard and spent several days plundering the fleeing court officials and
robbing the townspeople of money, saddles and horses, until he too was talked
into behaving.

That is the strongest empire in the world, described by its own historians, in
the fortnight after a Tibetan army took its capital.

## Then they left

The Tibetans occupied the city fifteen days and retired, and the imperial army
came back.

It is easy to read the withdrawal as a failure of nerve, and it was not. An army
of this kind could not garrison Chang'an: it was months from its own supply,
in a river valley in autumn, with a hostile countryside and no interest in
administering a Chinese province. What it could do was demonstrate — publicly,
in front of every polity in Inner Asia — that the Tang could be reached.

That demonstration is why the empire spent the following century holding the
Gansu corridor and the Tarim oases at Chinese expense, and why the Uyghurs
could charge the price they charged for cavalry.

## Three powers, not two

The period between about 660 and 840 is usually told twice, once from Chang'an
and once from Baghdad, and each account has a frontier that stops where the
other begins. Between them was a third empire fighting both.

Tibetan armies took Tang oases in the Tarim, met the caliphate's forces in the
Pamirs and Transoxiana, and at different moments allied with each against the
other. The Karluks, Türgish and Uyghurs in between made and unmade arrangements
with all three.

Neither surviving imperial tradition has an obvious place for that, which is
why a fortnight in 763 tends to appear in accounts of the Tang as a raid, and
in accounts of Islam not at all.
