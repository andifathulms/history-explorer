The Tibetan empire enters the written record in the seventh century as a
frontier problem in a Chinese archive, and the way the Tang historians tell it
is worth reading exactly, because they are describing a man they thought they
had absorbed.

## How to obtain a princess

Songtsen Gampo — Lungtsan, in Bushell's transcription of the Tang text — asked
for a marriage alliance and was refused. So he invaded, carried off the herds
of a neighbouring people, and stayed in the field for several years without
going home; his chief ministers begged him to return, and when he would not
listen, eight of them killed themselves.

Then he sent a mission to apologise for his misdeeds and asked again, with five
thousand ounces of gold and several hundred precious articles. In 641 the
emperor gave him the Princess Wencheng.

That is a Chinese court history admitting, in its own words, that a marriage
alliance was extracted by force and then paid for.

## What he did with her

The account of what followed is the part a Chinese historian is least likely to
have invented, because it is flattering to China and factually specific.

He met her escort in person and received the presiding prince with the rites
due from a son-in-law. Afterwards, the history says, *he praised the costume of
the great empire, and the perfection of their manners, and was ashamed of the
barbarism of his own people.* He told his relatives that no ancestor of his had
ever been allied to the sovereign empire, and that he would build a walled city
for the princess to proclaim his glory to later generations — and did, with a
palace for her inside the walls.

She disliked the Tibetan custom of painting the face red, so he ordered his
people to stop, and they stopped. He put off felt and skins and put on brocade
and silk. He sent the sons of his chiefs and his rich men to ask admission to
the Chinese national schools to study the classics, and invited scholars from
China to compose his official despatches to the emperor.

## The goose

The despatches were worth having. When Taizong came back from campaigning in
Korea, Songtsen Gampo's minister was sent with congratulations and this:

> The wild goose flies most swiftly, but not when compared with the speed of the
> emperor. His slave and unworthy son-in-law, rejoicing a hundred-fold above
> ordinary barbarians, in that the common goose is not unlike the wild goose,
> has made a gold goose, and respectfully offers it.

The goose was moulded of pure gold, seven feet high, and held about ten gallons
of wine.

Read the letter and the object together and they are not the same document. The
letter is elaborately abject — *his slave and unworthy son-in-law*, *above
ordinary barbarians* — in the precise register a Chinese chancery expects, which
is exactly what you would produce if you had hired Chinese scholars to write
your despatches. The gift is a seven-foot wine vessel of solid gold from a
country the Tang had just refused a princess.

## What he was actually building

Within seven years of the marriage, in 648, a Chinese envoy on his way to India
was robbed there, and it was Tibetan troops who marched into India with him,
defeated the offenders and sent word of the victory to the Chinese emperor. The
next emperor gave Songtsen Gampo the title Prince of the Western Sea and two
thousand pieces of silk. He replied offering to lead his warriors into China
personally to punish any officer who proved disloyal.

That is not a client. That is a power positioning itself as the guarantor of a
neighbour's order, in the vocabulary of a son-in-law, six years after being
told it was not good enough for a princess.

Under the same reign an alphabet was devised for Tibetan on an Indic model, and
the country acquired the ability to write itself down — which is the more
consequential of the two things Songtsen Gampo imported, and the one the Tang
historians do not mention.
