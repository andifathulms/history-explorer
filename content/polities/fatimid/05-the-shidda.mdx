---
title: The years the Nile did not come
drafted_from: brett-2017

Al-Mustansir reigned for fifty-eight years, the longest reign in Islamic
history, and this site lists him as the peak ruler of the polity. That is
defensible for the first half of it and grimly misleading about the second,
because the break is inside his reign rather than after it.

Three things failed at once in the 1060s. The Nile flood came in short for
several years running, and in a country whose entire agriculture is a function
of that flood, a run of low years is not a bad harvest but a famine. The army —
Turkish regiments, Sudanese infantry and Berber contingents, recruited
separately and loyal separately — stopped being an army and fought each other
through the streets of Cairo for control of what revenue remained. And the
treasury emptied, along with the palace: the *shidda*, the hardship, is the
period in which the caliph's own libraries and treasures were sold, looted or
used to pay soldiers, and the Fatimid book collection, one of the largest in
the world, was dispersed.

What ended it was a soldier. In 1074 al-Mustansir sent for Badr al-Jamali, an
Armenian commander governing Acre, who came to Egypt with his own troops in
winter, killed the faction leaders, restored order, and took for himself the
vizierate together with command of the armies, the judiciary and the mission —
every civil and religious office the state had.

That is the hinge, and it is worth being exact about what turned. Badr saved
the dynasty; Egypt recovered; the caliphate lasted another ninety-seven years.
It did so as a state in which the caliph reigned and the vizier ruled, with the
army answering to the vizier's household rather than to the palace. The
Abbasids arrived at the same arrangement under the Buyids a century earlier and
this site's Abbasid page describes it as the caliphate becoming a currency
rather than a government. Cairo reached it by a different road, and the office
that saved the dynasty in 1074 is the office that ended it in 1171.
