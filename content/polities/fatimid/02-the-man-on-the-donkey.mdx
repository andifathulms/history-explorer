Thirty-three years after the mahdi declared himself caliph, the state he
founded came within one siege of not existing. The threat was not Baghdad. It
was a Kharijite preacher riding a donkey, and for two years he came close to
finishing what he started.

## An old heresy, revived

Abu Yazid began preaching in the Aurès mountains in February 944, calling the
Berber tribes of the interior to holy war against the Fatimids on Ibadi
Kharijite doctrine — the same argument that had produced the oldest and most
stubborn opposition to every caliphate that came before this one: that
leadership belonged to the most righteous Muslim and not to a claimed
descent. His own descent was the opposite of grand, and he preached it as a
virtue; the donkey was deliberate, and the name stuck to him because of it.

The Kutama army that had made the Fatimid state was built for conquest, not
for holding down a hostile interior, and it broke almost at once. Kairouan
fell on 14 October. Abu Yazid destroyed the main Fatimid field army in a night
attack two weeks later. By the new year his forces were outside al-Mahdiyya
itself, the fortified capital on its promontory, the one place the founder had
built specifically to be unconquerable.

## The eight months

The siege ran from January to September 945. Al-Qa'im, the second caliph, had
a wall and a ditch thrown up to shelter the refugees crowding into the
suburbs, and refused every entreaty from his own court to appear before them
or send one of his sons in his place. He stayed inside the walls he had
inherited and let the city itself be the answer.

It held. Abu Yazid's following was a peasant army that could not sustain a
siege — men drifted home to their fields between assaults, and Fatimid
sorties sometimes found the enemy camp standing empty. In September, Arab
contingents in the rebel force defected in the middle of a battle outside the
walls, and Abu Yazid broke off the siege and pulled back to Kairouan. He found
the city had turned on him too, worn out by his own Berbers' exactions, and
shut its gates against him.

## The caliph who was already dead

What happened next was concealed for a year, and the concealment is the most
revealing part of the whole crisis.

Al-Qa'im died on 17 May 946, with Abu Yazid still in the field and the war
still undecided. His son Isma'il did not announce it. Every proclamation, every
order to the army, every public ceremony continued to be issued in his dead
father's name, while Isma'il governed as heir-designate for a caliph who no
longer existed — a deception made easier by the fact that al-Qa'im had spent
his whole reign, siege included, refusing to be seen. A state that had just
proven it could survive an army at its gates now had to survive not having a
caliph at all, and solved it by pretending the fact away until the war was
won.

Isma'il ran that war himself for nearly two years after the siege broke,
pursuing Abu Yazid's retreating forces through mountains his own troops did
not know, against a man whose peasant following melted away and reassembled
and melted away again. He cornered him at last in the fortress of Kiyana in
the spring of 947, forced a breakout, and had him brought in grievously
wounded after a fall. Abu Yazid died under interrogation four days later, on
19 August 947.

Isma'il proclaimed his own caliphate — taking the title al-Mansur — and his
father's death, on the same day he announced his enemy's. The Kharijite's
skin, by the account this chapter follows, was salted, stuffed, dressed in a
heretic's tall cap and paraded through every town the victorious caliph passed
on the way home, with trained monkeys set on it to pull its beard and strike
its head.

## What it proved

The Fatimid state that reached Egypt in 969 is usually read backward from
that success, as though the outcome had been likely all along. For two years
in the 940s it was not: a peasant revolt built on the oldest rival doctrine in
Islam took the capital's own countryside, destroyed the field army, and
besieged the caliph in his own unconquerable city while the caliph himself,
unknown to everyone outside the palace, was dead.

It survived because the son was better at the war than the father had been at
the siege, and because the state could keep functioning in the name of a
caliph who was not there — which is the same lesson, learned early and the
hard way, that later Fatimid viziers would use to run Cairo through children
and absentees for a century after Badr al-Jamali. Kharijism never recovered
its standing in the Maghreb after Kiyana. Neither claim — that the office
belonged to the worthiest man, or that a raided province could out-organise a
Kutama army — got a second chance to test the Fatimids again before Jawhar
sailed for Egypt.
