Al-Mustansir was proclaimed caliph in 1036 at the age of seven and reigned for
sixty years, which Ibn Khallikan notes was longer than any Abbasid or any other
Fatimid ever managed. The high point of the whole Fatimid claim falls in the
middle of it, and it happened two thousand miles from Cairo without a Fatimid
army being involved at all.

## Fifty-eight weeks

Arslan al-Basasiri was a Turkish commander who had acquired great power in
Baghdad. In 450 of the hijra — 1058 — he substituted, in the Friday prayer, the
name of al-Mustansir for that of the Abbasid caliph al-Qa'im.

Ibn Khallikan gives it in a sentence that is worth reading slowly: *during twelve
months the khutba was recited, in the name of al-Mustansir, from all the pulpits
of Baghdad.*

The Abbasid caliph left his own capital. The city that the Abbasids had built,
in which the office had sat for three centuries, spent a year publicly
acknowledging a Shi'i imam in Cairo as the commander of the faithful.

It did not last, and the reason it did not is instructive: what restored the
Abbasid name was not an Abbasid recovery but Tughril Beg and the Seljuk army.
The Fatimids had reached Baghdad through a disaffected general and could not hold
it, because holding Iraq would have required the thing they had never built — an
army that could operate that far east.

## What else that reign held

Ibn Khallikan lists the Baghdad *khutba* as one of several things that happened
in al-Mustansir's reign the like of which had never happened to any other prince
of the family, and the others fill out the shape of the claim at its widest.

Ali ibn Muhammad al-Sulayhi took Yemen and had the prayer offered for
al-Mustansir from every pulpit in the country — an event, Ibn Khallikan says,
so well known that details are unnecessary.

And the western half of the inheritance was going the other way at the same time.
When al-Mu'izz left for Egypt he had appointed Buluggin ibn Ziri to hold Ifriqiya
for him, and the prayer there continued to be said for the Fatimids as usual —
until al-Mu'izz ibn Badis, Buluggin's descendant, stopped saying it.

## A caliphate measured in pulpits

Put those together and the nature of this polity comes into focus.

At its widest the Fatimid name was pronounced from the pulpits of Egypt, Syria,
the Hijaz, Yemen, and for one year Baghdad — and it was being withdrawn in
Ifriqiya, which the dynasty had actually ruled and where its founder was buried.

That is a fair description of what the Fatimids were: not primarily a territorial
empire but a claim with an army attached, propagated by a missionary organisation
and registered in the only place a caliphal claim could be registered, which was
the Friday sermon. It was capable of reaching further than any army it had, and
of losing ground it physically held.

The reign that contained the widest extent of that claim also contained the worst
thing that ever happened to the state underneath it, and the two were seven years
apart.
