---
title: The apparatus that outlived the state
drafted_from: halm-1996

The two chapters before this one mention the *da'wa* and move on. It deserves
its own, because the mission mattered to this polity more than the army ever did,
and because it is the part of the Fatimid enterprise that lasted longest.

The *da'wa* was a hierarchy rather than a department. It had ranks, a curriculum,
and a chief — the *da'i al-du'at* — who sat in Cairo, and it operated
overwhelmingly outside Fatimid territory: in Abbasid Iraq, in Iran and Khurasan,
in Sind, in Yemen. Its agents were in the business of persuading the subjects of
a rival caliph that the caliph they lived under was a usurper. The *dar al-ilm*,
founded in Cairo in 1005 and usually described as a library, is also where those
agents were trained before being sent out. This is the institution the previous
chapter's religion-carried count is resting on, described.

It worked well enough to produce states. Yemen came over in the eleventh century
under the Sulayhids, who ruled in the Fatimid caliph's name from Sana'a and
funded the mission further east.

Then it did what an apparatus does when the head fails. Al-Mustansir died in 1094
and the succession was disputed between his elder son Nizar and the vizier's
candidate al-Musta'li. The *da'wa* split along the same line. Hasan-i Sabbah, who
had taken the fortress of Alamut in northern Iran four years earlier as a Fatimid
agent, backed Nizar, broke with Cairo, and ran the eastern network as something
independent of the caliphate that built it.

The arithmetic of that is the reason this chapter exists. Cairo fell to its own
vizier in 1171. Alamut fell to Hulegu in 1256, two years before he took Baghdad —
which this site records on the Ilkhanate's page. The state lasted two hundred and
sixty-two years; the organisation it sent out lasted eighty-five years longer,
and in Yemen and later India longer still.
