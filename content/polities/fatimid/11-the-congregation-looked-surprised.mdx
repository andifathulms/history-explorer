On Friday 10 September 1171 the preachers in the mosques of Cairo named the
Abbasid caliph instead of the Fatimid one.

That was the whole of it. Saladin had spent the previous year founding three
orthodox colleges to prepare opinion, and had waited until he was sure of it.
The congregations, Lane-Poole writes, looked merely surprised. Two centuries of
Isma'ili caliphate in Egypt ended in the interval between two sentences of a
sermon, and nobody objected.

The last caliph did not find out. Al-Adid had been a recluse in his palace
since Saladin arrived, and he was dying when his name was suppressed; the news
was kept from him, and he died three days later without knowing he had been
deposed.

## Every luxury except posterity

His relations — eleven sons, four sisters, four wives and the rest, a hundred
and fifty-two people — were shut up in different buildings of the palace, the
sexes separated, and kept in comfort. The phrase for the arrangement is exact:
they were indulged with every luxury except posterity.

It did not entirely work. They contrived to rear grandsons of the caliph, and
the family was not extinct in 1260. A magic cup made at Cairo in 1175 carries
the name of an imam with a regnal title, which may be one of al-Adid's sons,
whose claim the pro-Fatimid party went on conspiring over for years afterwards,
hoping either to restore the dynasty or to profit from restoring it nominally.

## The books, twice

The library was the part that could not be replaced, and it was destroyed
twice.

The first time was in 1068, during the famine and the mutiny of the Turkish
soldiery, when over a hundred thousand volumes were sold off or carried away
against arrears of pay. It had been kept in locked presses labelled by subject,
run by a librarian, two copyists and two servants. It held two thousand four
hundred illuminated Qur'ans, manuscripts in the hand of Ibn Muqla and other
famous calligraphers, thirty copies of the great dictionary called the *Ayn*,
and twenty copies of Tabari's history including the author's own autograph.

Manuscripts that scholars would now give anything for were used to light fires.
Bindings were cut up to mend the shoes of Turkish officers' slaves. Torn
volumes were thrown out and buried in the sand, and the mounds near Abyar were
long known as the hills of the books. The luckiest were the ones exported
abroad.

Then the dynasty started again, with such energy that Saladin found a hundred
and twenty thousand volumes in the palace a century later. He gave them away —
to his chancellor, al-Qadi al-Fadil.

## The palaces

Saladin kept nothing of the treasure for himself. Some went to his followers,
some to his own sovereign in Syria, and the rest was sold for the treasury. He
did not move into the caliph's halls either; he stayed in the vizier's house
and handed the palaces to his army officers.

They were not royal residences any more, so they decayed, and not a fragment of
them survives. The poet Umara, who had written for the dynasty and who three
years later would be crucified with the other leaders of a conspiracy to bring
it back — a plot that reached as far as the kings of Sicily and Jerusalem and
the master of the Assassins — said the thing worth ending on: censure my love
for the sons of Fatima if you like, and then come and weep with me over the
desolate halls of the two palaces.
