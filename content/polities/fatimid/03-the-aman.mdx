Egypt was not stormed. It was negotiated, and the negotiation began inside
Egypt.

Kafur, the eunuch who had governed for the Ikhshidids, died in 968. The officers
settled the succession on a boy, with a cousin as his lieutenant, a general over
the troops and the vizier Ibn al-Furat over the revenue — and then the pay fell
short and the customary gratuities stopped, and the soldiers became turbulent.
At which point, Ibn Khallikan records, a number of men of high rank in Fustat
wrote to al-Mu'izz in Ifriqiya inviting him to send an army and take the country.

## Sending Jawhar

The man he sent had been his own slave. Jawhar ibn Abd Allah, called the Greek
scribe, was a freedman of the caliph, and he was so ill when the order came that
nobody expected him to live. Al-Mu'izz visited him and told him that he would not
only survive but conquer Egypt.

The send-off Ibn Khallikan describes is the most revealing thing in the account.
Jawhar had a hundred thousand horse and more than twelve hundred chests of money.
Al-Mu'izz came out to say goodbye, leaned down from his horse's neck to speak
with him privately, and then ordered his own sons to dismount and salute Jawhar —
which forced every great officer of the kingdom to dismount too. Jawhar kissed
the caliph's hand and the hoof of his horse. When al-Mu'izz got back to his
palace he sent Jawhar every garment he had been wearing, keeping only his drawers
and his seal-ring, and wrote to his governor of Barqa ordering him to go out and
kiss Jawhar's hand. The governor offered a hundred thousand dinars to be excused
that ceremony, and had to do it anyway.

A dynasty that claimed the whole caliphate as the descendants of Fatima
choreographed the humiliation of its own nobility in front of a freedman, because
the freedman was carrying the claim to Egypt.

## The document

When the army was close, Fustat panicked. The vizier Ibn al-Furat wrote asking
for terms; the city sent an ambassador, a sharif named Abu Ja'far Muslim, on the
condition that a body of citizens went with him. They met Jawhar at a village
near Alexandria in June 969. He granted every demand and put it in writing.

Then the arrangement nearly failed — not because Jawhar broke it but because
Fustat could not agree to accept it. The Ikhshidid loyalists, Kafur's old
officers and part of the army hid their valuables, camped outside the city and
refused the peace. The sharif arrived with the *aman*, the written promise of
protection, and read it out; Jawhar had attached to it individual answers
granting the fiefs, money and governorships that various Egyptians had privately
written to ask for, and a letter to the vizier addressed to him as vizier. The
meeting broke up without agreeing.

So there was a battle after all, at Giza, and it was brief. Jawhar took the ford
at Munyat Shalqan; some Egyptian troops came over to him by boat and surrendered;
when the rest guarded the ford he stripped to his trousers, crossed in a boat
while his men forded the river, and broke them on the far bank. The Ikhshidids
left Fustat in disorder that night.

## The date that mattered

Jawhar had Fustat on 16 Sha'ban 358. Three days later he went up into the pulpit
and pronounced the *khutba* with a prayer for his master al-Mu'izz.

That is the conquest. Not the battle at Giza — the sentence from the pulpit, in
which the Friday prayer of Egypt named a Shi'i imam in Ifriqiya as the rightful
caliph of all Islam. It took another month for the news to reach al-Mu'izz.

Jawhar governed Egypt with absolute power for five years until his master came,
and kept his rank until 974, when al-Mu'izz removed him from the bureaux, the
revenue and the control of affairs. He lived another eighteen years, and Ibn
Khallikan says there was not a poet of the time who failed to compose verses
lamenting him.
