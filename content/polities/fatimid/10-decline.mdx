The vizierate saved the dynasty in 1074 and spent the following century
becoming the only thing in it that mattered. Badr al-Jamali was succeeded by
his son; caliphs were installed as children by viziers who had chosen them; and
the succession dispute of 1094, which split the mission and is described
three chapters earlier, was itself settled by a vizier putting his own
candidate on the throne over the claim of the elder son.

<Figure id="aqmar-mosque" />

The end came through the same office. Competing viziers in the 1160s each
invited outside help against the other — the Crusader kingdom of Jerusalem on
one side, Nur al-Din of Damascus on the other. Nur al-Din's expeditionary
commander Shirkuh won the contest and became vizier in 1169; when he died two
months later his nephew Saladin took the post, dismantled the Isma'ili
establishment, and in 1171 had the Friday sermon in Cairo preached in the name
of the Abbasid caliph. Al-Adid died days later.

It is not a conquest. No foreign army took Cairo. The dynasty was ended by its
own chief minister, which is what the vizierate had made structurally possible
a century earlier.
