---
title: A storeroom in Fustat
drafted_from: goitein-1967
phase: aside

Almost everything else on this site is reconstructed from chronicles: written
later, by men attached to courts, about rulers and battles and successions.
The Fatimid period is the one exception in this corpus, and the reason is a
disposal problem.

Jewish practice forbids destroying documents that might bear the name of God,
so the Ben Ezra synagogue in Fustat, old Cairo, kept a *geniza* — a storeroom
into which written paper went instead of being thrown away. Because the
community wrote in Hebrew script but often in Arabic, and because nobody
sorted the sacred from the ordinary, the room accumulated roughly a thousand
years of everything: letters, court records, marriage contracts, dowry lists,
bills of lading, business partnerships, prescriptions, school exercises,
complaints, a child's alphabet practice.

Its densest period is the eleventh and twelfth centuries — the Fatimid
century and the one after it. What comes out of it is a picture of the economy
these chapters describe from above, seen from inside: merchants in Fustat with
partners in Qayrawan, Palermo, Aden and the Malabar coast; credit extended
across the whole of that distance on reputation and correspondence; India-trade
cargoes financed in Cairo; a Mediterranean that functioned as one commercial
space regardless of which caliph claimed which coast.

Two things it does to this page. It confirms from documents what chapter two
asserts from chronicles — that the Fatimid state's wealth came from the Nile
and from a deliberately cultivated Red Sea and Indian Ocean trade — which is a
rarer kind of confirmation than it sounds. And it does something no chronicle
does at all: it records people the chronicles never mention, in a period where
this site otherwise knows only rulers, viziers and the men who killed them.

It also sits awkwardly beside the previous chapters, and should. A dynasty is
easy to narrate as a sequence of caliphs and a hard problem when the evidence
starts describing what its subjects were actually doing, which in Fustat was
mostly business, conducted with people on the other side of a religious
frontier that the chronicles treat as the organising fact of the age.
