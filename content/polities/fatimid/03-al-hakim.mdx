---
title: The caliph the sources cannot agree about
drafted_from: walker-2009
phase: peak

Al-Hakim became caliph in 996 at eleven and vanished in 1021 at thirty-six. He
went out at night to the Muqattam hills above Cairo, as he often did, and did
not come back; the search found his donkey and his bloodstained clothes and
nothing else.

Between those two facts sits the most contested reign in this corpus. The
hostile tradition gives a tyrant: the destruction of the Church of the Holy
Sepulchre in Jerusalem in 1009, part of a wider campaign against Christian and
Jewish worship; humiliating dress requirements for non-Muslims; a stream of
public-order decrees reaching into what could be eaten, sold and done after
dark, several of them reversed within a few years of being issued. The
favourable tradition gives an ascetic and an imam, patron of the *dar al-ilm*
and of the astronomers, who walked the streets himself and heard petitions.

Walker's position, which this chapter follows, is that both portraits are
real and neither is disinterested — they are what two constituencies made of
the same man, and the chronicles that survive were written by people who
belonged to one or the other. The reversals are the awkward fact for both. A
ruler who restores what he demolished and rescinds what he decreed is doing
something the mad-tyrant account cannot explain and the ideal-imam account does
not want to.

Two consequences outlived him regardless of which portrait is preferred. The
first is that around 1018 a group of missionaries began preaching that al-Hakim
was a manifestation of the divine — a doctrine he does not seem to have
authorised and that Cairo repudiated — and after his disappearance they became
a closed community holding that he had not died but withdrawn. That community
is the Druze, and it exists now.

The second is the Holy Sepulchre. The church was rebuilt, on Byzantine money,
under a later agreement; but the memory of its destruction was in western
circulation when the preaching of the First Crusade began ninety years later,
which is a long fuse from an Egyptian decree to a Frankish army — and to the
chapter after next, in which the Fatimids lose Jerusalem to that army.
