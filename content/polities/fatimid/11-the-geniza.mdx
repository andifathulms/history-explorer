---
title: A storeroom in Fustat
drafted_from: goitein-1967

Almost all medieval Islamic history is reconstructed from chronicles: written
later, by men attached to courts, about rulers and battles and successions. For
the Fatimid centuries there is an exception, and the reason for it is a
disposal problem.

Jewish practice forbids destroying documents that might bear the name of God,
so the Ben Ezra synagogue in Fustat, old Cairo, kept a *geniza* — a storeroom
into which written paper went instead of being thrown away. Because the
community wrote in Hebrew script but often in Arabic, and because nobody sorted
the sacred from the ordinary, the room accumulated roughly a thousand years of
everything: letters, court records, marriage contracts, dowry lists, bills of
lading, business partnerships, prescriptions, school exercises, complaints, a
child's alphabet practice.

Its densest period is the eleventh and twelfth centuries — the Fatimid century
and the one after it. What comes out of it is a picture of the economy these
chapters describe from above, seen from inside: merchants in Fustat with
partners in Qayrawan, Palermo, Aden and the Malabar coast; credit extended
across the whole of that distance on reputation and correspondence; India-trade
cargoes financed in Cairo; a Mediterranean that functioned as one commercial
space regardless of which caliph claimed which coast.

Two things follow. It confirms from documents what the chronicles assert — that
the Fatimid state's wealth came from the Nile and from a deliberately
cultivated Red Sea and Indian Ocean trade — which is a rarer kind of
confirmation than it sounds. And it does something no chronicle does at all: it
records people the chronicles never mention, in a period otherwise known
through rulers, viziers and the men who killed them.

It also sits awkwardly beside the previous chapters, and should. A dynasty is
easy to narrate as a sequence of caliphs and a hard problem when the evidence
starts describing what its subjects were actually doing, which in Fustat was
mostly business, conducted with people on the other side of a religious
frontier that the chronicles treat as the organising fact of the age.
