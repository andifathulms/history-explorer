The Fatimids took Egypt in 969 after three failed attempts, and founded a new
city north of Fustat: al-Qahira, the Victorious. It was a palace city for the
caliph, his Kutama troops and his administration, closed to the general
population at first — the same instinct that built Samarra and Baghdad, and
Mahdiyya before it.

Egypt made them rich in a way Ifriqiya never had. The Nile's agriculture, plus
the Red Sea trade that the Fatimids deliberately cultivated as an alternative to
the Gulf routes running through Abbasid Iraq, gave them a revenue base that
funded a gold dinar of famous reliability and a navy. Al-Azhar was founded in
970 as a mosque and became a college for Isma'ili teaching; the *dar al-ilm*
followed in 1005.

The empire at its height held Egypt, Ifriqiya, Sicily, the Levantine coast, and
the Hijaz with the holy cities — which meant the Friday sermon at Mecca and
Medina was preached in the Fatimid caliph's name, the single most valuable piece
of symbolic ground in the Islamic world.

What it did not do was convert Egypt. The population remained largely Sunni and
substantially Coptic Christian throughout, and the regime made little sustained
attempt to change that. Isma'ilism was the creed of the ruling establishment and
the *da'wa* directed outward — which is precisely why this site's
religion-carried count for the Fatimids is Isma'ili Shi'ism and rests on the
missionary organisation rather than on the conquest of Egypt.
