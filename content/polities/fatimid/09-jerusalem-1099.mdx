The Fatimids took Jerusalem back from the Seljuks in 1098 and held it for about
a year. The following July the armies of the First Crusade came over the wall
and took it from them, and the Fatimid garrison that surrendered the citadel
was one of the few groups in the city allowed to leave.

<Figure id="jerusalem-citadel" />

This is a distinction worth drawing, because the Crusade is usually narrated
against the Seljuks. The Turks had taken Jerusalem from Cairo in the 1070s and
lost it again to Cairo eleven months before the Franks arrived, so the power
the Crusaders actually dispossessed in the city was the Isma'ili caliphate, not
the Sunni sultanate they had been recruited against.

What followed was a coastal war Egypt could not win. The Fatimid navy and the
ports of the Levant — Acre, Tyre, Sidon, Ascalon — had been the outer edge of
the empire and the thing that made the Red Sea trade a Mediterranean business
as well. They fell one after another over the next thirty years, the last of
them in 1153, and the Italian fleets that took them kept the sea lanes
afterwards. A caliphate that had preached the Friday sermon at Mecca and
Medina, held Sicily, and disputed the whole Islamic world with Baghdad became a
power whose reach ended at its own delta.

<Figure id="ascalon" />

The internal consequence was worse than the territorial one. Egypt's defence
now depended entirely on whoever commanded the army, and the office that
commanded it was the vizierate, which had been where the actual government sat
since 1074. Every later Fatimid crisis is
a quarrel between viziers with an outside army invited in by one of them, and
the last of those quarrels ends the dynasty.
