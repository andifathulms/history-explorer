Every other dynasty on this site took a province and left the caliphate standing.
The Fatimids took the caliphate.

The movement was Isma'ili — a branch of Shi'ism holding that the imamate had
passed through Isma'il, son of the sixth imam — and it had spent decades running
a clandestine missionary organisation, the *da'wa*, across the Islamic world from
a hidden centre. Its agent Abu Abdallah al-Shi'i converted the Kutama Berbers of
the Kabylie, raised them as an army, destroyed the Aghlabid emirate of Ifriqiya
in 909, and then summoned his master.

Abd Allah declared himself caliph and *mahdi* at Raqqada in 909, claiming
descent from Fatima and Ali and therefore the whole office — not a rival
governorship but a statement that the Abbasids in Baghdad were usurpers of an
imamate that belonged to the Prophet's line. He had Abu Abdallah, who had built
the entire thing, executed in 911.

This is what makes the Fatimids structurally different from everything else in
this site's caliphates region. The Rashidun, Umayyads and Abbasids are one office
passing between houses. The Fatimids are a second office, asserted against the
first, and for two centuries there were two caliphs in the Islamic world who each
regarded the other as illegitimate.
