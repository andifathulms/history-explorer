Ambitious dynasties normally took a province and left the caliphate standing.
The Fatimids took the caliphate.

The movement was Isma'ili — a branch of Shi'ism holding that the imamate had
passed through Isma'il, son of the sixth imam — and it had spent decades
running a clandestine missionary organisation, the *da'wa*, across the Islamic
world from a hidden centre. Its agent Abu Abdallah al-Shi'i converted the
Kutama Berbers of the Kabylie, raised them as an army, destroyed the Aghlabid
emirate of Ifriqiya in 909, and then summoned his master.

<Figure id="kairouan-mosque" />

Abd Allah declared himself caliph and *mahdi* at Raqqada in 909, claiming
descent from Fatima and Ali and therefore the whole office — not a rival
governorship but a statement that the Abbasids in Baghdad were usurpers of an
imamate that belonged to the Prophet's line. He had Abu Abdallah, who had built
the entire thing, executed in 911.

<Figure id="mahdia-port" />

This is what makes the Fatimids structurally different from every caliphate
before them. The Rashidun, the Umayyads and the Abbasids are one office passing
between houses. The Fatimids are a second office, asserted against the first,
and for two centuries there were two caliphs in the Islamic world who each
regarded the other as illegitimate.
