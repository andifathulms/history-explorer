On 30 July 1419, a procession of Hus's followers carrying the
sacrament through Prague's New Town was met with a stone thrown from
a window of the town hall. The enraged crowd, led by a royal courtier
named Jan Žižka, stormed the building; the burgomaster and several
councillors were thrown from its windows, and those who survived the
fall were killed by the crowd waiting below. It was Bohemia's first
defenestration, and it marked the moment reform stopped being a
matter of preaching and became open insurrection against the crown
itself.

## A one-eyed courtier becomes a general

Žižka, already in his forties and blind in one eye from earlier
fighting, had no formal claim to military command beyond years of
hard experience and an evident genius for it. What he built out of
peasants, small farmers, and townsmen armed with little more than
iron flails and short spears was a style of war Europe's mounted
nobility had no real answer for: the wagon-fort, chains of armoured
carts formed into a mobile fortress, its best marksmen posted beside
each driver, able to close into a defensive square in retreat or
wheel outward to outflank an enemy in attack. On Bohemia's open
plains, largely free of the ditches and hedges that would have broken
up such a formation elsewhere, it let an army with almost no cavalry
of its own fight cavalry-heavy armies to a standstill and, again and
again, to defeat.

## The hill that took his name

Sigismund answered the growing revolt by proclaiming a crusade, and
by the end of June 1420 an army drawn from across Latin Christendom -
contemporary chroniclers counted contingents from as far as Aragon
and England alongside the German princes who made up its core, with
estimates of its total strength running as high as 150,000 - had
gathered around Prague. Žižka had already fortified a steep hill east
of the city, hastily entrenched with what defences his men could
raise in the time available. When Sigismund's forces attacked on 14
July, the fighting at the hill was desperate enough that when
Taborite defenders briefly gave ground, one woman among them refused
to retreat, declaring that a true Christian should never yield to
Antichrist, and was killed where she stood. Žižka fought in the front
rank himself, and the crusaders were eventually driven back down the
slope in disorder, many killed or drowned trying to cross the Vltava
in flight. The hill has been called Žižka's Hill ever since. Dissent
within the crusading army's own ranks - German nobles openly
distrustful of the Bohemian Catholics fighting alongside them -
did the rest of the work: Sigismund's siege of Prague collapsed
within weeks, and a peasant army that all of Europe had expected to
scatter at the first real battle had instead broken a crusade.
