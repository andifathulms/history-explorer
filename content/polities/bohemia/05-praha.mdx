John of Luxembourg, King of Bohemia since 1310, had gone blind years
before he rode out with the French army against the English at Crécy
in August 1346 - a battle his own advisors had counselled against and
one he watched, in effect, only through the reports of the men around
him. When the French line began to break, Bohemian nobles at his side
urged him to retreat with the rest. John refused: "So will it God, it
shall not be that a King of Bohemia flies from the battlefield." He
had two of his knights lash their horses to his own to guide him, gave
the watchword "Praha," and charged into the thick of the English
ranks. He and fifty of his chief nobles died there, cut down almost to
a man. Edward III, on being shown the blind king's body that evening,
wept and said, "The crown of chivalry has fallen today; never was any
one equal to this King of Bohemia."

## An heir already tested

Charles, John's own son and already his regent and diplomatic proxy
across a kingdom his father's own attention had often drifted from,
had fought at his father's side that same day and was himself
severely wounded before the few Bohemian knights still standing
forced him off the field, unwilling to risk losing both their kings
at once. Elected King of the
Germans the same year as his father's death and crowned King of
Bohemia in 1347, Charles IV set about rebuilding the kingdom's
institutions with a thoroughness his predecessors had rarely matched.
In 1348 he summoned the Estates to Prague, confirmed the privileges
earlier kings had granted but let lapse since Ottokar II's fall,
fixed Moravia's status under his brother John Henry, and formally
attached Silesia and Upper Lusatia to the Bohemian crown. Succession
was to run by primogeniture, with the Estates' old right of election
preserved only against the day the royal line itself might fail.

## A capital built to match an empire

At that same Diet, Charles announced his intention to found a
university at Prague - securing the Pope's consent before he had even
returned from campaign, and personally overseeing its organization
once the doors opened. It was the first university anywhere in
Central Europe, and Charles, already effectively ruling as the
Empire's strongest prince, meant Prague to be worthy of the role: a
capital that could draw scholars the way Paris did, in a kingdom that
a single defeat at Crécy had shown could not survive on the old
nobility's loyalty alone.
