The Bohemian crown passed out of any native dynasty's hands in 1526,
when the Diet elected the Habsburg Archduke Ferdinand king after a
contested vote decided as much by bribery as by conviction - the
start of a rule that would last, with one violent interruption, into
the twentieth century. Religious tension between the kingdom's
Protestant majority and its Catholic rulers built for decades until
Emperor Rudolph II, cornered by an Estates revolt he had no means to
suppress, signed the Letter of Majesty on 9 July 1609: it recognized
the Bohemian Confession, gave Protestants supervision of the
university, and let them appoint "Defenders" charged with guarding
the rights the Letter granted. Two royal councillors, Martinič and
Slavata, alone among the Bohemian nobility refused to sign it - a
refusal that marked them as the two most hated men in the kingdom
when the crisis finally broke.

## Out the window again

On 23 May 1618, Protestant nobles under Count Thurn marched in full
armour to Prague Castle to confront the royal governors over reports
that the Letter of Majesty's protections were about to be violated.
After a furious exchange in which the two councillors were accused
of poisoning royal policy against their own countrymen, the assembly
seized Martinič and Slavata and threw them from the castle's windows
into the moat some forty feet below - along with a secretary who had
tried to intervene. All three survived, astonishingly close to
unhurt; Catholic writers afterward called it a miracle, while the
Protestants who watched them stagger away simply fired shots after
them and missed. It was Bohemia's second defenestration, and all of
Europe would come to know it as the opening act of the Thirty Years'
War.

## A battle of a few hours

The Bohemian Estates deposed their Habsburg king and elected the
Calvinist Frederick of the Palatinate in his place, but Frederick's
reign - later remembered as barely lasting a winter - collapsed
almost as soon as it was tested. On 8 November 1620, a combined
Catholic League and Imperial army met the Bohemian forces on a
low ridge outside Prague called the White Mountain. The Bohemian
army, unpaid for months and on the edge of mutiny, broke within
hours; Hungarian cavalry sent to support it fled toward the Vltava
and drowned trying to cross by the hundreds, and only a handful of
isolated units - Moravian troops making a last stand in a nearby
park, the Palatinate guard dying near to a man around their king's
own colours - fought with anything like the discipline that had once
broken a crusade at Žižka's Hill two centuries before. Frederick
himself was hosting English ambassadors at a banquet in the castle
when news of the rout reached him; by the next morning he and his
queen had fled the city, and within days the whole of the Bohemian
crown's lands had submitted. Lützow's own explanation for the
contrast is stark: the free peasant armies that had fought under
Žižka no longer existed by 1620, worn down over two centuries into a
serfdom that had nothing left in it to defend. The battle lasted a
few hours. It ended Bohemia's existence as an independent kingdom.
