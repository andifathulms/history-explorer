By the early 1430s the Hussite movement Žižka had once led as a
single fighting force had split into rival camps. Moderate Utraquist
nobles and the burghers of Prague, willing to negotiate a settlement
with the Church that stopped short of the radical Taborites' full
programme, faced off against Tábor and its allied "Orphan"
brotherhoods, who held out for a more complete break with Rome. Both
sides had learned Žižka's own wagon-fort tactics, and both fielded
armies that had, within living memory, beaten every force sent
against Bohemia from outside it. In May 1434 they met each other
instead.

## The plain of Lipány

Near the village of Lipany, an army of Bohemian nobles roughly 25,000
strong, led by Bořek of Miletínek - who had once served under Žižka
himself - faced some 18,000 Taborites and townsmen under the two
Prokops. Miletínek won by a feigned retreat: his vanguard pretended
to break and flee, the Taborites left their wagon-entrenchment to
pursue what they took for a real rout, and Miletínek's cavalry cut
them off from their own fortified lager before they could return to
it. What followed was less a battle than, in the judgment of
Bohemian historians since, an extermination: some 13,000 Taborites
died, several hundred prisoners were burned alive in the huts where
they had been penned, and both Prokops were killed. A movement no
German army had managed to break in fifteen years of open war had
been broken by other Bohemians.

## Peace at Jihlava

The military defeat of Tábor cleared the way for a settlement long
under negotiation with the Council of Basel. Talks dragged on for two
more years over the Bohemians' insistence that their own elected
Archbishop, John of Rokycany, be recognized by Rome - a demand the
Council never fully granted - but on 5 July 1436, Bohemian delegates
at Jihlava formally accepted the Compacts of Basel, permitting
communion in both bread and wine for those who wished it and ending
two decades of excommunication and open war between Bohemia and the
rest of Latin Christendom. Sigismund, the same king whose broken
safe-conduct had sent Jan Hus to the stake at Constance twenty-one
years earlier, made his triumphal entry into Prague on 23 August 1436
as Bohemia's undisputed, Estate-recognized king. He was sixty-eight
years old, and would reign over the kingdom he had spent two decades
trying to subdue for only a few more months before his death.
