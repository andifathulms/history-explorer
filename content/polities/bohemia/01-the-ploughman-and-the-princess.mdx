The story Bohemian chroniclers told about their own kingdom's
founding is one of the most vivid in medieval legend. Krok, first
ruler of the Slavic tribes settled along the Vltava, left three
daughters: Kazi, skilled in medicine and poisons; Teta, who taught
the people the worship of nature spirits; and Libuše, youngest but
wisest, chosen by the people as their ruler and judge. Libuše is
said to have founded Prague at the foot of the Vyšehrad and
foretold its greatness - but when a noble she ruled against
complained that no other land endured the shame of a woman's rule,
she told the people to choose a man instead, promising to marry
whomever they chose. She sent them to a farm at Stadice, where they
found a peasant named Přemysl ploughing his field with two spotted
oxen, and declared that his descendants would reign forever. The
chronicler Cosmas of Prague, writing this account down centuries
after the events it claims to describe, is its only real source, and
later Bohemian historians who elaborated it into detailed royal
biographies were, in Lützow's own judgment, writing pure invention.

## Where the record actually begins

The first Přemyslid ruler real evidence can attest to is Bořivoj I,
a duke drawn into the politics of his more powerful neighbour,
Svatopluk of Moravia, in the 870s. Svatopluk had thrown off Frankish
overlordship after a decisive victory in 871 and secured his gains
in the 874 Treaty of Forchheim, and it was in the years that followed
- the exact date is not recorded - that Bořivoj and his wife Ludmila
were baptized by followers of Methodius, the missionary who had
brought Slavic-language Christian worship to Moravia. Bořivoj is
credited with building Bohemia's first churches, including the
earliest known church on the Hradčany hill in Prague, and Ludmila's
own reputation for piety helped Christianity take root among a
people the chroniclers otherwise tell us almost nothing about.

## A kingdom still in another's shadow

For years after Bořivoj's death, Bohemia remained a junior partner
in Svatopluk's Moravian empire, which at its height claimed
supremacy over territory reaching from Kraków to the outskirts of
Magdeburg. It was only after Svatopluk's own death in 894, and the
succession quarrels that tore Moravia apart afterward, that Bořivoj's
son Spytihněv broke free of Moravian overlordship and reestablished
Přemyslid rule over the whole of Bohemia on its own account - a
independence that proved durable in a way Svatopluk's own empire,
destroyed by Magyar invasion within a generation, never would.
