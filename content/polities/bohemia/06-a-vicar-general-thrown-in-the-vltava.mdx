Charles IV's son Wenceslas inherited Bohemia in 1378 and, within a
year, the German crown as well - the German princes recognized him as
king of the Romans in 1379, extending his father's own dominance over
the Empire into a second generation. He did not extend his father's
judgment along with it. Where Charles had built alliances patiently,
Wenceslas ruled by sudden, ungovernable temper, and the clearest
evidence of it came in a quarrel with Prague's own archbishop over a
vacant abbey Wenceslas meant to suppress and fold into a new bishopric
of his own creation. When the archbishop's vicar-general, John of
Pomuk, quietly frustrated the plan by confirming a rival abbot's
election, Wenceslas had him and three other clerics seized and
tortured. Three gave in and promised obedience; John refused, and was
tortured until his death was certain, then thrown into the Vltava in
1393 - a killing the Church would eventually recognize with its own
sainthood, John of Nepomuk becoming one of Bohemia's most venerated
martyrs.

## A crown lost in Germany, kept at home

Wenceslas's temper cost him more than a vicar-general's life. His
prolonged absences and growing unpopularity encouraged rivals within
the Empire, and in 1400, with Pope Boniface IX's active support, the
Empire's ecclesiastical electors deposed him as German king outright,
choosing Rupert of the Palatinate in his place. Bohemia itself he kept
- but only after years of struggle against a league of his own nobles
who had, at one point, imprisoned him directly and forced him to
govern through officials of their own choosing rather than his.

## Betrayed by the brother he trusted to free him

The bitterest of those betrayals came from inside his own family. When
Wenceslas's younger brother Sigismund, King of Hungary, was himself
seized and imprisoned by his own nobles, Wenceslas spent heavily to
win his release - and was repaid by having Sigismund seize him in turn
during a journey meant to lead to an imperial coronation in Rome,
installing a bishop as Bohemia's governor and trying to strip Wenceslas
of his own throne. Wenceslas escaped custody in 1403 and returned to a
kingdom that, disgusted by Sigismund's extortions in his absence,
received its erratic king back more warmly than it had ever treated
him before. It was the same Sigismund - a man who had already broken
one promise of safety to his own brother - whose word Jan Hus would
trust a decade later, on a much more consequential journey to
Constance.
