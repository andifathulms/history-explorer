Boleslav II, "the Pious," inherited his father's strength in 967 and
used it to pry Bohemia's own church loose from German oversight. In
973, with the emperor Otto's consent, Bohemia was separated from the
diocese of Regensburg and Prague made the seat of its own bishopric -
a genuine concession, though the pope insisted the new see not be
read as any continuation of the old Moravian archbishopric, and that
it use the Latin liturgy rather than the Slavonic rite Cyril and
Methodius's own mission had once planted in the region. Thietmar, a
German long resident in Bohemia and fluent in its language, became
the first bishop; his successor, the Bohemian noble Adalbert, worked
to root Christianity more deeply among a people German priests before
him had never fully reached, before dying a martyr in 997 while
preaching to the pagan Prussians - killed not long after Boleslav
himself had ordered the execution of Adalbert's own brothers in a
feud between rival noble houses.

## From feuding voyvodes to a single prince

That feud, and Boleslav's ruthless resolution of it, marked the end of
an older arrangement entirely. Early Přemyslid princes had directly
governed only the country immediately around Prague, leaving local
voyvodes to rule the rest of Bohemia under a loose and often-contested
supremacy - an arrangement that produced, by Lützow's own account,
"innumerable feuds" not worth individually retelling. Boleslav I and
II broke that pattern for good: once the last independent voyvode
house fell, Bohemia was reorganized into districts run by burgraves
the prince himself appointed, a new service nobility replacing the old
territorial one, and the assemblies that had once voiced the will of
independent lords narrowed into gatherings that simply heard the
prince's own decisions announced.

## Two centuries the record barely bothers to individualize

Boleslav II's death in 999 ended that centralized strength as suddenly
as it had arrived. The following two centuries saw the throne pass
through a long and frequently violent succession of dukes - rival
brothers, Polish intervention, and repeatedly renewed dependency on
whichever German king could back one claimant over another - a period
Lützow's own account groups under a single chapter heading spanning
nearly two hundred years rather than following duke by duke. What
finally changed the terms of the relationship was not any Bohemian
victory but a German civil war: when the German throne itself fell
into disputed succession in 1197, Přemysl Ottokar I found himself
courted rather than commanded. Backing Philip of Swabia's claim in
exchange for concrete concessions, Ottokar secured Philip's
renunciation of any future say in choosing Bohemia's own dukes,
renounced imperial claims over the appointment of Prague's bishops,
and won something no Přemyslid had held outright before: a hereditary
royal title, conferred on the Bohemian sovereigns "for all time," that
made Ottokar's own 1198 coronation at Mainz the foundation every later
king of Bohemia's own legitimacy would trace back to.
