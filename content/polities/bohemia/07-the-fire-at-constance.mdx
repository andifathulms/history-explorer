Jan Hus, rector of Prague's university and the most forceful preacher
of a Bohemian reform movement that had spent years attacking clerical
corruption and insisting that Scripture alone, not papal authority,
was the true ground of Christian belief, was summoned in 1414 to
answer for his teaching before a General Council of the Church at
Constance. King Sigismund, hoping the Council could settle the
religious turmoil dividing his brother Wenceslas's kingdom, gave Hus
a promise of safe conduct: he was to come to Constance unmolested,
receive a free hearing, and return unharmed even if he refused to
submit to the Council's judgment. Hus set out that October trusting
the promise.

## A safe-conduct that did not hold

He was imprisoned almost as soon as he arrived, chiefly through the
influence of John "the Iron," Bishop of Litomyšl, one of reform's most
determined enemies. Sigismund, arriving at Constance weeks later, did
nothing effective to free him - his private advice to the cardinals,
by his own later admission, was to have Hus burned if he would not
recant and to distrust him even if he did. Hus's actual trial did not
open until June 1415, and even then he was refused any real chance to
defend his positions point by point; the Council demanded a blanket
retraction of every heretical opinion attributed to him, including
several he had never held. He refused, preferring, as he put it, to
die rather than deny with his lips what he held true in his heart.
Condemned as a recusant heretic on 6 July 1415, he was led directly
from the cathedral to a meadow outside the city walls and burned that
same day; his ashes were thrown into the Rhine specifically so that
no relic of him could be carried back to Bohemia. His friend and
fellow reformer Jerome of Prague, arrested on his own way home from
visiting Hus at Constance, was burned by the same Council the
following spring.

## What Bohemia made of it

News of the execution reached a kingdom that had already come to see
Hus as its own greatest living son, and the reaction was not
theological restraint but open revolt against the Church that had
killed him. Within Bohemia, reformers and their Roman Catholic
opponents alike agreed on one thing: whatever else Hus had been, no
impartial account of his sincerity, his personal purity of life, or
his willingness to die for what he actually believed could honestly
be denied him. The Council of Constance had meant to close a
theological dispute by fire. It opened, instead, the Hussite Wars.
