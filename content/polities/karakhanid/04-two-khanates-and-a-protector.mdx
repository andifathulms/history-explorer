---
title: Two khanates, and somebody else's protection
drafted_from: ei2-karakhanids
phase: contraction
---

The Karakhanids never had a single ruler in the sense the other polities on
this page did. Authority was held by the family collectively and distributed
among its adult men as appanages, with a graded ladder of titles through which
a prince rose as he acquired seniority and territory: senior khagans in the
east and west with subordinate khans beneath them, and a working assumption
that a man's holding changed as his rank did.

It is a steppe arrangement and it does what steppe arrangements do. Around 1040
the two halves stopped acknowledging a common head, and the khanate became two:
an eastern one based on Balasaghun and Kashgar, a western one on Samarkand and
Bukhara. This site's Mongol page describes the same logic dividing a much
larger empire two centuries later, and its Seljuk and Ayyubid pages describe the
same thing in Turco-Iranian and Kurdish versions. The record on this page
carries no `last` ruler for exactly this reason: after the split there were two
lines and naming one final khan would assert a unity the dynasty had given up.

What followed was a long subordination. From 1089 the western khanate was a
Seljuk vassal, with the sultans appointing and removing its rulers. In 1141 the
Seljuk sultan Sanjar was destroyed at the Qatwan steppe by the Qara Khitai, a
Khitan power displaced westward from north China, and Transoxiana passed under
their protection instead. The Karakhanids continued as tax collectors for an
overlord who was not Muslim — Buddhist and shamanist, ruling from Balasaghun,
and by most accounts indifferent to what its subjects believed.

That last arrangement is stranger than the chapters around it make it sound. A
dynasty whose whole standing rested on having been the first Turkish Muslims,
and whose courts had just produced the founding works of Islamic Turkic
literature, spent its final seventy years remitting taxes to a non-Muslim
steppe empire and being left alone by it.

The Khwarazmshah finished the western line in 1212 and the eastern one had gone
the year before. The record types the ending `conquest` because that is the
last act the sources describe, and the century of subordination that made it a
formality is in this prose rather than in the field. The closed vocabulary
describes how a polity stopped, not how long it had been over.
