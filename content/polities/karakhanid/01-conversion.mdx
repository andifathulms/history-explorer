The Karakhanids emerged from the Karluk confederation of the Semirechye, and
somewhere around the middle of the tenth century their ruling house converted to
Islam. The conversion story attached to Satuq Bughra Khan is hagiography of a
later date, but the fact is not in doubt: by the 960s a Turkish steppe dynasty
on the far side of the Samanid frontier was Muslim, and it was the first.

<Figure id="satuq-bughra-khan-mazar" />

This inverted the frontier. The Samanids had built their prestige on being the
Islamic world's wall against the pagan Turks, raiding the steppe for slaves and
endowing the frontier ribats where volunteers came to fight unbelievers. When
the people on the other side of that wall became Muslims, the wall stopped
meaning what it had meant, and the Samanid state lost the argument that
justified it before it lost the territory.

What the Karakhanids carried was not conquest followed by conversion but
conversion followed by conquest, and what came with it was institutional: the
mosques and madrasas of Kashgar and Balasaghun, and a Turkish Islamic literary
culture that produced the *Qutadghu Bilig* within a century of the dynasty's
arrival in Transoxiana.
