---
title: Invited in
drafted_from: barthold-1928
phase: expansion
---

The first Karakhanid conquest of Transoxiana met almost no resistance, because
most of the people who should have resisted it had asked for it.

Isfijab went first, occupied by the khan of Balasaghun in the 980s. Then Bughra
Khan Harun, grandson of the Satuq of the conversion story and styled Light of
the Empire and Support of the Summons to the Faith, came down the road to
Samarkand and Bukhara without a battle worth recording. He had already agreed in
secret with Abu Ali, the Samanids' own commander in Khurasan, on how to divide
the kingdom: Transoxiana to the khan, everything south of the Oxus to Abu Ali.
Invitations came in as well from the *dihqans*, the landholders who did much of
the real work of the Samanid state and were thoroughly tired of its government.
Barthold cannot say what the religious classes made of the invasion, but notes
that the chroniclers dwell on Bughra Khan's piety and on the piety of his two
chief backers — which suggests the case for him was being made in the right
quarters before he arrived.

One of his advisers was a man named al-Wathiqi, who claimed descent from the
caliph Wathiq and had failed to extract either the pension or the post such a
claim was supposed to yield; he went over to the Turks, acquired an extraordinary
hold over the khan, and pushed him into the campaign. Tha'alibi thought him the
author of the Samanid collapse. After Bukhara fell he took to going about with
three hundred *ghulams* and expecting to be proclaimed caliph himself, with
Bughra Khan governing Transoxiana and Khurasan as his vassal.

Nuh's defence collapsed twice. The hajib Ayach was sent out, beaten, and taken
prisoner. Faiq, in disgrace and then pardoned and received at Bukhara with
honour, was sent to Samarkand and was beaten at Kharjang, which contemporaries
put down to treason and probably rightly: when the khan reached Bukhara in May
992 Faiq came out to meet him, submitted, and was given Tirmidh and Balkh to
govern. Nuh had already left. Bughra Khan installed himself in the Ju-i Muliyan,
the garden palace outside the city that the Samanids had made famous, and
according to Bayhaqi found the treasury still worth looting.

What undid it was Bukhara itself. The fruit and the climate gave the khan
haemorrhoids, and he pulled back to Samarkand, taking the Samanid vizier with
him and leaving a Samanid prince, Abd al-Aziz, on the throne with a declaration
that he was restoring it to its rightful owner. The retreat was not orderly. The
Turkmens whom Nuh had brought over, and the people of Bukhara, followed the
retiring army, destroyed its rearguard and plundered the baggage. Nuh was back in
his capital on 17 August 992 and had Abd al-Aziz blinded. The khan's illness got
worse at Samarkand and he died on the road home, at a place called
Quchqar-bashi.

So the first conquest lasted three months and ended with the khan dead and the
old dynasty restored. It had also demonstrated, to everybody who had been
watching, that the Samanid state could be entered at will.
