The last western Karakhanid was Uthman of Samarkand, and Juzjani, writing at
Delhi a generation later, explains how he changed sides. It was over a woman he
was not given.

## The proposal

By 1209 Transoxiana had been under Qara Khitai protection for nearly seventy
years, with the Karakhanids collecting the taxes and remitting them to a
Buddhist overlord at Balasaghun who left them alone. Uthman had asked the Gur
Khan — the Qara Khitai ruler — for the hand of his daughter, and had been
refused.

So when the Khwarazmshah Muhammad sent him an agent, he was, in Juzjani's
phrase, already disaffected, and became secretly a zealous ally.

The Khwarazmshah at that point had decided to stop paying the tribute his father
had agreed to send the Qara Khitai, which he had long considered dishonourable
to his sovereignty. He took Bukhara, where an upstart named Sanjar Malik was
holding the city and who, the historian says without elaborating, met with his
deserts. Then he consulted with Uthman — hitherto styled the Sultan of Sultans —
and went back to Khwarazm to prepare a campaign.

## The other man waiting

Meanwhile the Gur Khan had a guest.

Kuchlug the Naiman, son of Tayang Khan, had fled west from Chinggis Khan and
sought protection at the Qara Khitai court. He was there, Juzjani says, ready to
take advantage of any outbreak against his protector. And when the Khwarazmshah
moved, Kuchlug opened secret negotiations with him: whoever could first take
Kashgar and Khotan from the Gur Khan should have them, and Kuchlug would have
the rest.

Both men were living off the same host and had agreed in advance how to divide
him.

## What the battle was like

Juzjani's account of the engagement that followed is a good corrective to any
idea that this was a clash of civilisations.

The Khwarazmshah had traitors in his own camp. Two of his great vassals — the
governor of Samarkand, and the *ispahbad*, which is the title the maliks of
Tabaristan and Rustamdar carried — had sent agents secretly to the Gur Khan
offering to desert on the day of battle with their troops, in exchange for
Khwarazm and Khurasan afterwards. It was guaranteed. When the armies were drawn
up, the two nobles and their men withdrew from the right wing and the
Khwarazmian right was broken.

Then the Khwarazmian left broke the Qara Khitai right, and the two centres met.

## The end of it

The Qara Khitai protectorate came apart between the Khwarazmshah in the west and
Kuchlug in the east, exactly as the two of them had planned. The eastern
Karakhanid line went in 1211 and the western one in 1212, when the Khwarazmshah
disposed of Uthman.

A dynasty that had held Transoxiana for two hundred and thirteen years ended as
a piece of a transaction between a Turkish sultan, a Naiman refugee, and a
Buddhist protector, over territory none of the three of them was from — and the
thing that set it moving was a marriage proposal that had been turned down.
