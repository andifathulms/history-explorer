Juzjani, introducing the last Karakhanid in a sentence, gives him a pedigree:
Uthman, Sultan of Samarkand, of the race of Afrasiyab, and of the family of
Bughra Khan, the antagonist of the later Samanid princes.

The second half of that is ordinary — Bughra Khan is a real Karakhanid title and
the dynasty really did fight the Samanids. The first half is the interesting
part, and it is what the Persian historical tradition settled on calling them:
*Al-i Afrasiyab*, the house of Afrasiyab.

## Who Afrasiyab is

He is the villain of the *Shahnama*.

Afrasiyab is the king of Turan, the country beyond the Oxus, and the great
enemy of Iran through the mythological half of the Persian national epic. He
kills Siyavush. He is the reason there is a frontier. Firdawsi finished the poem
around 1010, at the court of the man who had just taken Khurasan from the
Samanids, and within a century the standard Persian way of referring to the
Turkish dynasty north of the Oxus was as Afrasiyab's descendants.

Nobody appears to have meant it as an insult, and the Karakhanids seem to have
been content with it. That is the striking thing. A dynasty which had made its
entire reputation out of being the first Turkish Muslims — which held Bukhara
and Samarkand, endowed madrasas, and whose courts produced the founding works
of Islamic Turkic letters — was slotted by the literature of the country it
governed into the role of that literature's hereditary antagonist, and answered
to it.

## What the two books were doing, seen from here

Set that beside what Yusuf of Balasaghun and Mahmud al-Kashgari had done a
century and a half earlier and the picture sharpens.

Yusuf wrote his mirror for princes in Turkic, in the metre of the *Shahnama* —
using the form of the poem in which his patrons' ancestors are the enemy, to
argue in his patrons' own language about how to rule. Kashgari wrote to the
caliph in Arabic to explain that men who dealt with government would now need
Turkish, because God had given the Turks the rule.

Both of them are answers to the same situation, which is that the prestige
language and the prestige literature belonged to somebody else and the power did
not. One answer is to take the form and change the language. The other is to
address the old centre in its own tongue and tell it the world has moved.

## And the country stayed

The dynasty was disposed of in 1212 and its work was not.

Transoxiana had been the Persian-speaking heartland of the Samanid state. Under
two centuries of Karakhanid rule and the Turkish migration that came with it,
Mawarannahr became a Turkish country, and it is one now. The city where Rudaki
sang in Persian for a Samanid amir is in Uzbekistan.

That is a larger result than anything in the political record of these khans,
who fought their neighbours to no particular effect, split their state in two,
and spent their last seventy years remitting taxes to a Buddhist. What they
changed was who lived in the country and what language was spoken there, which
is not the kind of thing a chronicle notices until it is finished.
