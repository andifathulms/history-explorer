The Cirebon sultanate did not end in a siege, a conquest or a single
dramatic act. It ended in a piece of colonial administrative paperwork,
which is the appropriate ending for a state whose actual authority had been
draining away for more than a century before the paperwork made the fact
official.

## A century of increasing dependency

From soon after the 1677 division, the competing Cirebon courts found it
increasingly necessary to bring disputes — over succession, over territory,
over precedence between the rival lines — to the Dutch East India Company
for arbitration, because none of the courts individually had the standing to
compel the others to accept a settlement. Each appeal to Company mediation
strengthened the Company's position in Cirebon's internal affairs a little
further and weakened the sultans' own authority to settle their own house's
business without outside involvement.

By the eighteenth century's end, the sultans of Cirebon retained their
titles, their palaces, their religious prestige and a share of local
revenue, but the actual work of governing — justice, administration, the
disposition of territory — increasingly ran through Company and then, after
the Company's dissolution, through Dutch colonial officials rather than
through the courts themselves.

## Daendels' regulation

In 1809, Herman Willem Daendels, the Governor-General sent to reorganise
Java's administration under direct Netherlands government rule, issued the
Reglement van Bestuur voor de Cheribonsche Landen — a regulation for the
government of the Cirebon lands. It formalised what had already largely
happened: the sultans' remaining administrative authority was removed, and
governance of the former sultanate's territory passed fully into the
colonial administrative structure.

Nobody was conquered and no palace was stormed. The three Cirebon courts
kept their titles, their compounds, and — as the previous chapter's material
suggests was never really in doubt — their religious standing. What they
stopped being, by regulation rather than by battle, was rulers of anything
at all.
