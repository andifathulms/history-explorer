Mataram, the inland Javanese power built from the old kingdom of the Brantas
and Solo valleys, spent the early seventeenth century bringing the whole of
Java's north coast under its authority, port by port. Cirebon's turn came in
1613.

## Why Cirebon did not resist the way Surabaya did

Mataram's conquest of the coast was not uniform. Some ports — Surabaya
prominent among them — held out for years against sieges and blockades
before submitting. Cirebon's own accession to Mataram's overlordship came
without the drawn-out resistance the eastern ports mounted, and the reason
is not hard to locate in what the previous chapters describe: a state whose
rulers had built their authority on religious prestige rather than on
military strength had comparatively little capacity to resist a determined
inland power, and comparatively little to lose by accepting a nominal
overlordship that left its ruling house and its religious standing intact.

Mataram, for its part, had reasons to prefer a client Cirebon to a
destroyed one. The port's trade, though never on Banten's scale, was worth
taxing rather than ruining, and a ruling house with genuine religious
standing across the coast was more useful left in place, answering to
Mataram, than removed and replaced with a governor who carried none of that
prestige.

## What vassalage actually changed

Cirebon kept its sultans, its territory in day-to-day administration, and
its position as a pilgrimage centre. What it lost was independent foreign
policy and, increasingly, the ability to settle its own succession disputes
without an outside power's involvement — a vulnerability that would matter a
great deal within the following two generations, when Mataram's own
authority came apart from the inside and Cirebon's ruling house came apart
along with it.
