Cirebon sat at a crossing point other Javanese courts did not: between the
Sundanese-speaking west and the Javanese-speaking centre and east of the
island, between a Hindu-Buddhist past it had only recently left and an
Islamic present it was actively building, and on a coast that had traded
with China for centuries before Islam ever arrived there. Its court culture
kept all of it rather than choosing one strand and discarding the rest.

## What the mixing produced

The mask-dance tradition associated with the Cirebon courts, topeng, draws
on pre-Islamic Javanese performance forms and continued to be patronised
under a Muslim ruling house that in principle might have discouraged
figurative performance of this kind and did not. The batik patterns
associated with Cirebon incorporate motifs — a cloud pattern known as mega
mendung is the best known — that later scholarship traces to Chinese textile
and ceramic influence carried by the same trade that had made the port worth
having in the first place, worked into a decorative tradition otherwise
descended from older Javanese practice.

None of this was unique to Cirebon in kind — every port on this coast
absorbed Chinese, Indian and older Javanese material into its own culture to
some degree — but the degree to which Cirebon's rulers treated the mixture
as their court's own particular character, rather than as a set of separate
imported curiosities, is what later observers single the city out for.

## The tomb

Gunung Jati's own tomb on Gunung Sembung, above the city, became and
remained the single most consequential religious site the dynasty produced,
outlasting in importance any specific political achievement of his
successors. Pilgrims travelled to it from well beyond Cirebon's own
shrinking territory throughout the period the following chapters describe,
including from parts of the archipelago no Cirebonese ruler had held
political authority over for generations.

That is worth setting beside the political decline the chapters after this
one trace. A dynasty can lose its independence, then its unity, then its
governing authority altogether, and still keep the one thing its founder
built into the office from the very first year: a claim to sanctity that
outside power could tax, divide or overrule but never actually take away.
