Most of the states this coast produced disappeared as physical institutions
along with their political authority: a palace abandoned, a court dispersed,
a title nobody bothered claiming a generation later. Cirebon's three courts
did not disappear. They kept existing, without governing anything, through
the rest of Dutch colonial rule, the Japanese occupation, and Indonesian
independence.

## Titles without territory

The Kasepuhan and Kanoman lines, and the smaller Kacirebonan line that had
split from one of them, continued to install sultans, maintain their
palace complexes, and conduct the ceremonial functions attached to their
houses throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, under Dutch rule
and then under an independent Indonesian republic that had no more reason
than the Dutch colonial government to restore them any political authority.
What each court retained was what Gunung Jati had built into the office
from the very beginning and what a regulation from a Dutch governor-general
had never been able to touch: descent from a recognised Islamic saint, and
a claim to religious and cultural standing that outlasted every specific
government that ruled the territory around it.

## What that made the palaces

The kraton complexes of Kasepuhan, Kanoman and Kacirebonan continued, and
continue, to function as centres of exactly the syncretic court culture
described earlier — the mask-dance traditions, the batik
patterns, the annual ceremonies tied to the Islamic calendar and to older
Javanese observance both. Gunung Jati's tomb went on drawing pilgrims
through every political change the region underwent, because nothing about
a change of government touched what the site had always actually been for.

## What ended and what did not

The Sultanate of Cirebon, as a governing state, ended in 1809. The
institution Gunung Jati actually founded — a ruling house whose authority
rested on a claim to sanctity rather than on force — never depended on
holding political power to begin with, and so a regulation that removed the
political power left the institution itself, on its own terms, standing.
