Mataram's own crisis, not Cirebon's, is what split the sultanate in two.

## Trunajaya's rebellion and a rescue that was also a takeover

From 1674 the Madurese lord Trunajaya led a rebellion that came close to
destroying the Mataram state outright, sacking the Mataram capital itself
and throwing the whole of central and eastern Java into war. Cirebon's
ruling family, still nominally Mataram's vassals, were caught inside that
collapse: two princes of the Cirebon house ended up in Mataram's hands amid
the fighting, in circumstances the sources describe as closer to hostage-
taking than to ordinary court residence.

Sultan Ageng of Banten, ruling the one major independent Muslim power left
on the coast and no friend of either Mataram or the Dutch East India Company
then beginning to entangle itself in Mataram's affairs, took the two
Cirebonese princes under his own protection during the chaos and had both of
them crowned as sultans in 1677.

## Two sultans, one city

The result was not a restoration of Cirebon's old unity under new
management. It was a formal division: Prince Martawijaya became Sultan
Sepuh, ruling from what became the kraton Kasepuhan, and his brother Prince
Kartawijaya became Sultan Anom, ruling from the kraton Kanoman, both
courts standing in the same city, both claiming descent from Gunung Jati,
and neither subordinate to the other. A third, smaller princely line split
off from one of the two main houses in the following century, adding a
further court to a city that had once had one throne and would from this
point permanently have several.

## What a divided sultanate could no longer do

A single ruling house speaking for Cirebon as one polity had been able,
within the limits vassalage under Mataram already imposed, to present a
united front to whichever outside power it was dealing with. Two, and later
three, competing courts in the same city could not. Each line's rulers had
an obvious incentive to seek outside backing against the others, and the
outside power increasingly available to provide it, as the following
chapter describes, was the Dutch East India Company rather than a fading
Mataram.
