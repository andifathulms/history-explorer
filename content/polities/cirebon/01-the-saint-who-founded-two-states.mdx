Cirebon was a fishing settlement on the north coast of West Java before it
was anything else, standing in the sphere of the Hindu-Buddhist kingdom of
Sunda to its west. What made it a state in its own right was one man's
decision to convert it and keep it.

Syarif Hidayatullah — remembered afterwards as Sunan Gunung Jati, after the
hill where his tomb stands — was, by the traditions Graaf and Pigeaud treat
as broadly reliable, descended from the Sundanese royal house through his
mother and connected by marriage to the sultan of Demak, the senior Muslim
power on Java's coast at the time. That combination of a local claim and an
outside alliance is exactly the one this coast's other Muslim foundations
also used, and Gunung Jati used it twice: he took Cirebon for himself around
1479, breaking it from Sunda's still-Hindu court, and some years later sent
his son-in-law to found Banten on the same basis further west.

## What made Cirebon different

Every other founder on this stretch of coast was, first and foremost, a
soldier, a merchant, or both. Gunung Jati's standing was religious before it
was political. He is counted among the Wali Songo, the nine saints credited
with converting Java to Islam, and his tomb on Gunung Sembung became a
pilgrimage site in its own right, drawing visitors from well beyond the
territory his political descendants actually governed.

That gave Cirebon a kind of authority Demak and Banten, built by soldiers and
traders, did not have in the same degree: a ruling house whose legitimacy
rested partly on a claim to sanctity rather than on conquest or commercial
success alone. It is a distinction later Cirebonese rulers would keep making
long after their political power had shrunk to almost nothing, because it
was the one asset the decline described in the chapters that follow never
touched.

## A port among ports

Materially, Cirebon was what every north-coast Javanese port of the period
was: a customs point on the shipping lane linking Java's ports to the
Melaka strait and the spice trade beyond it, sitting on a rice-growing plain
that fed it and paid its taxes. What set it on its own path rather than
Sunda's or Demak's was the specific man who took it, and the specific claim
he built into the office from the start.
