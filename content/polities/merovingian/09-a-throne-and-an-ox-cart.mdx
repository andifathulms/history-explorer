Dagobert's arrangement - a minor king, a trusted mayor of the palace
running the government in his name - was meant as a temporary
expedient. It became, over the following decades, the permanent shape of
Frankish kingship. Dagobert's own son Clovis II succeeded him as a child,
and the pattern repeated with nearly every king after him: short reigns,
early successions, and mayors of the palace - the chief officers of each
royal household - accumulating the actual work of government, the
command of armies and the granting of land, while the kings whose names
appeared on their charters grew up as figureheads before they could grow
into anything else.

## A dynasty remembered for its hair and its oxen

Einhard, writing two generations later at the Carolingian court that
replaced this arrangement outright, described what Merovingian kingship
had become in withering terms: a family that had "long since been devoid
of vital strength," with nothing left to a king but his title, his long
hair and flowing beard - the traditional mark of Merovingian royal blood
- and a single country estate too small to support even a modest
household. A Merovingian king's only real function, in Einhard's account,
was ceremonial: to receive foreign ambassadors and dismiss them with
answers written for him in advance, and once a year to be driven by
ox-cart, peasant-fashion, to the general assembly of the Franks, preside
over it in appearance, and be driven home again. The mayor of the palace
managed everything else, at home and abroad, that a king's government
actually required doing.

## One family's office by nearly hereditary right

The office of mayor was not, in law, hereditary, but by the time
Einhard was writing it had belonged to one family, the Pippinids, for
three generations running: Pepin the Elder, then his descendants after
him, holding the position as though it passed by blood rather than by
royal appointment. What had begun as Chlothar II and Dagobert's own
practical solution to the problem of a child on the throne had become,
within two generations of Dagobert's death, the actual constitution of
the Frankish kingdom - a throne that conferred no power, and an office
that conferred all of it, held more and more openly by men who answered
to no Merovingian king in anything but name.
