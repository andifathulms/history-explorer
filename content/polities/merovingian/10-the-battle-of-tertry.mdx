By the 680s the mayoralty itself had split along the same old fault line
that had once divided Frankish kings: Austrasia and Neustria each had
their own mayor, and the two men's rivalry stood in for whatever
authority the kings they served no longer exercised in their own right.
In Neustria, a small and hot-tempered man named Berchar held the office
after his own predecessor's death, ignoring the advice of the Frankish
nobles around him until enough of them abandoned him for Pepin of
Herstal, the Austrasian mayor, to make Berchar's own position
dangerously exposed.

## A battle whose winner never wore a crown

Pepin gathered his Austrasian warbands and marched swiftly against
Berchar and the Neustrian king Theuderic III whose name Berchar governed
in. The two forces met near Saint-Quentin at a place called Tertry, and
Pepin's Austrasians won decisively; Theuderic and Berchar were both put
to flight, and Berchar himself was murdered soon after by his own
associates at his mother-in-law's instigation. Pepin pursued the
victory rather than the crown: he took Theuderic's own person and
treasury into his keeping, reorganized the government of the whole
realm to his own satisfaction, and then returned to Austrasia without
ever claiming the royal title Theuderic still nominally held.

## One family, one office, the whole kingdom

Tertry did not end the Merovingian dynasty - Theuderic III kept his
throne, and kings of his line would go on succeeding one another for
another sixty years - but it ended any real contest over who governed
in their name. From 687 onward the mayoralty of the palace, previously
split between rival courts the way the kingship itself had once been,
belonged to Pepin of Herstal and, after him, to his own descendants
alone. Pepin's two sons by his wife Plectrudis, Drogo and Grimoald,
were raised as the heirs to an office that had, in all but name, already
become the thing that actually ruled the Franks.
