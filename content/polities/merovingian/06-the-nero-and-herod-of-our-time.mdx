Sigebert's murder in 575 left his half-brother Chilperic and his queen
Fredegund unrivalled in Neustria, and for the next nine years Chilperic
ruled with a cruelty his own contemporary bishop, Gregory of Tours,
recorded in blunt terms: he taxed his people ruinously, mutilated men on
suspicion rather than proof, broke wills made in favor of the Church, and
mocked bishops he considered inconvenient. Gregory calls him, without
qualification, "the Nero and Herod of our time" - a Frankish king
literate enough to write his own verse and theology, and cruel enough
that Gregory judged both worthless.

## A blade in the dusk

In the autumn of 584, while his own armies were still campaigning and
plundering the countryside, Chilperic went hunting at his villa of
Chelles outside Paris. Returning at dusk and dismounting with one hand
resting on a servant's shoulder, he was stabbed under the arm by an
unknown assailant, then stabbed again in the belly; he died within
moments, blood flooding from his mouth and from the wounds themselves.
Gregory names no killer with confidence, though he notes that Chilperic's
own court immediately suspected the reach of enemies he had made in
plenty. He had been loved by no one in life, Gregory writes, and was
abandoned by nearly everyone the instant he was dead - it was left to a
single loyal bishop, who had waited three days outside his tent hoping
for an audience he never received alive, to wash the body, dress it
properly, and see it buried by boat in the church of Saint-Vincent at
Paris.

## An infant king and an unfinished war

Chilperic's death left Fredegund regent for their infant son, Chlothar
II, and did nothing to end the war with Austrasia: Brunhild, now regent
in her own right for Sigebert's son Childebert II, remained Fredegund's
sworn enemy, and the two queens would go on directing their kingdoms'
rivalry through proxies, assassinations and shifting alliances for
another three decades before either court's own line of succession
settled the matter for good.
