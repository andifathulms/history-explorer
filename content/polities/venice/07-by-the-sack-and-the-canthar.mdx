In the spring of 1494 a Milanese canon in his sixties came to Venice to catch a
pilgrim galley to Jerusalem, and was kept waiting five weeks. He filled the time
by looking at things and writing them down. Pietro Casola was not interested in
politics, had no opinion about the constitution, and mentions no doge; he was
interested in how much of everything there was, and in whether it was better than
at Milan.

That makes him the most useful witness the city has, because the year is 1494.
Vasco da Gama rounded the Cape four years later, and the sea route to the spices
that Venice existed to distribute was open within a decade. Casola is describing
the top.

## The warehouses

He begins by admitting he cannot do it.

> Indeed it seems as if all the world flocks there, and that human beings have
> concentrated there all their force for trading.

He was taken round the warehouses, starting with the German one, which he thought
would suffice by itself to supply all Italy with the goods that came and went;
and then so many others that he calls them innumerable. Then the sentence that
does the work:

> I see that the special products for which other cities are famous are all to be
> found there, and that what is sold elsewhere by the pound and the ounce is sold
> there by canthar and sacks of a moggio each.

Shops so well furnished that they looked like warehouses themselves — tapestry,
brocades and hangings of every design, carpets of every sort, camlets of every
colour and texture, silks of every kind; warehouses full of spices, groceries and
drugs, and beautiful white wax. *These things stupefy the beholder.* He notes,
with a canon's honesty, that although he wanted to see everything he saw only a
part, and that by forcing himself.

## The vegetable boats

The passage that stays with a reader is not about spices.

Having nothing else to do, Casola went several times very early in the morning to
St Mark's and to the Rialto to watch the boats being unloaded. There were so many
full of big beans, peas and cherries — not, he concedes, of every kind as at
Milan, but every day in such quantity that it seemed as if all the gardens of the
world must be there.

> The number was so great that I declare that after seeing them, when I turned my
> back I hardly believed my eyes.

Vegetables of every kind, cheaper than anywhere he had ever been, brought from
twenty-five miles away and looking as if they had just been picked. He adds that
he knows how this sounds, because he used not to believe it himself when people
told him.

That is the thing the trading empire was actually for. A city on mud with no
arable ground fed a hundred thousand people better and more cheaply than the
agricultural capital of Lombardy, by boat, every morning, from a lagoon shore
twenty-five miles off.

He is not uncritical. The fish, he says, was never good the whole time he was
there, although his hosts took great trouble; the wines were abundant beyond
belief but not so perfect as Lombard wine, and dear because of the duties.

## The churches

On St Mark's he is careful. At first sight it seems a small thing, he says, but a
man who examines everything about it carefully will find it a grand church, and
he thinks it has no equal for its size, adorned within and without with so many
beautiful and subtle mosaics — beginning with the four horses in metal over the
great door. The treasure in relics and the altarpiece he cannot value: *it seems
to me infinite.*

On the parish churches he gets cross, and the crossness is the point. He is
afraid to describe them in detail, he says, because he could not do it without
offending somebody, *especially at Milan*. Then he says it anyway: the poorest
parish church of Venice is more ornate than the finest at Milan.

He does not connect the horses over the door to anything. They had been carried
off from Constantinople by the fleet Venice built for the Fourth Crusade, and by
1494 they had been over that door for nearly three centuries and were simply part
of the church. The money that gilded the parish churches came through the
warehouses he had just walked round.

## Why there was so much of it

Newett's note explains the warehouses, and the explanation is a statute. Venetian
merchant galleys were not allowed to sail directly from the port where goods were
bought to the port where they were sold. Every voyage had to begin and end at
Venice. The city was therefore the compulsory place of deposit for everything,
until at least the next year's sailings, and foreign merchants crowded there to
buy. What stupefied Casola was the physical consequence of a shipping regulation.

The same note carries the other half of it. A few years after his visit the news
came that Portuguese ships had rounded Africa, reached India, and returned with
spices that were being sold cheaply at Lisbon. A Venetian diarist of the time
wrote it down:

> All the city of Venice was greatly impressed and alarmed, and the wisest men
> held that this was the worst news that could ever come to the city … Because
> the spices which came to Venice, passed through the whole of Syria and the
> countries subject to the Sultan, paying exorbitant duties in every place, so
> that when they arrived in Venice the value of an article which, in the
> beginning, was worth a ducat, was raised as high as sixty and even a hundred
> ducats.

Casola walked through the warehouses at their fullest, wrote that the quantity
was beyond description, and sailed for Jerusalem. Nothing in the diary suggests
that anybody in the city thought the arrangement had an end.
