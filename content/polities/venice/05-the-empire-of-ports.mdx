The state built the ships. The Arsenal, walled, guarded and enlarged repeatedly
from the twelfth century, was at its height employing something in the order of
sixteen thousand people and holding a reserve of galley hulls that could be
fitted out at short notice; the standing account, from a visiting Spanish envoy
in 1436, is of a hull being towed past successive windows and receiving its
cordage, arms, ballistas, oars and provisions from each in turn, and emerging
complete in an hour.

The state also ran the shipping. The *muda* were convoys of state-built merchant
galleys, auctioned annually to private operators, sailing on fixed routes to
Alexandria, Beirut, Constantinople, the Black Sea, Flanders and England, on
dates the Senate set, in company, armed. A merchant did not need a fleet; he
needed a share in a voyage, and the auction and the convoy made that available to
anybody with capital.

## What Venice exported

Not a religion, not an alphabet, and not a claim on anybody's throne. Venice
converted nobody, founded no successor state that invoked it, and left no
script. What it exported was technique, and the technique is still in use. Double-entry bookkeeping is not a Venetian
invention but the Venetian version is the one Luca Pacioli printed in 1494 and
the one Europe learned from. Marine insurance as a contract distinct from a loan.
The *colleganza*, a partnership form separating capital from labour on a single
voyage with the risk shared, which let a man with no money and a man with no
inclination to sail both take a position. A funded public debt with tradable
shares — Venetian government bonds were being bought and sold as investments in
the thirteenth century, which is the first securities market in Europe. Quarantine,
the forty days a ship from a plague port waited at the Lazzaretto, and the word.

A city that never ruled more than a few hundred thousand people wrote the
accounting, the insurance, the partnership form, the government bond and the
quarantine that the rest of Europe then used. It is a strange kind of legacy,
because none of it looks like a monument and all of it is still running.

## The subject empire

It is worth being specific about what governing an empire of ports meant,
because the romance obscures it.

Crete was held for four hundred and sixty-five years and revolted at least
fourteen times. The Venetian settlement there was a colonial one — Venetian
feudatories on granted estates, a Latin church established over an Orthodox
population, and the Cretan Orthodox hierarchy suppressed — and the largest revolt,
in 1363, was led by the Venetian colonists themselves, who raised the flag of
Saint Titus rather than Saint Mark and had to be put down by an army from Italy.

The sugar of Cyprus and Crete was grown by slaves, and the Italian republics
were among the largest slave traders in the medieval Mediterranean, moving
people out of the Black Sea to Egypt and to Italy. The Genoese share of that
traffic ran through Caffa in the Crimea, and its cargo included the Kipchak boys
sold into Egypt who grew up to be the Mamluk sultans — who then signed
commercial treaties with the republics that had shipped them.

The slave trade of the Black Sea supplied the manpower of a Muslim state in
Cairo and the sugar of a Christian one in Crete, and the same Italian hulls
carried both ends of it.
