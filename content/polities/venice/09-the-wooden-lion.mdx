Venice was fought over for the next seventy years by everyone except the
Venetians.

Austria took the city in January 1798 and held it to 1805. The French took it
back after Austerlitz and held it to 1814. Austria took it again and held it to
1866. Under the first Austrian occupation the population was pleased to see them
arrive, because the democrats installed by the French had spent the interim
arresting people and sequestrating property, and because the French had carried
off everything in the city they took a fancy to, including the bronze horses
over the door of St Mark's.

Trevelyan is unkind about the switching and it is hard to argue: a people, he
says, as fickle as a mob in Shakespeare, throwing themselves at Napoleon's feet
in 1806 and forgetting that the same man had pillaged, outraged and sold their
city eight years before.

The French period left the deeper mark, for an unromantic reason. It trained
people. Italian officers commanded Napoleon's Italian regiments; native lawyers
and administrators filled the highest places on the bench and in the
bureaucracy. A city whose government had been the private property of a few
thousand registered families for five hundred years acquired, under a foreign
occupier, a professional class with experience of running things.

## The lawyer named after the last doge

Daniele Manin was born in Venice in 1804, seven years after the republic
dissolved itself.

He was not descended from the doge whose name he bore. His father came of a
Venetian Jewish family called Fonseca which had turned Christian in the
eighteenth century, and had been given the name Pietro Manin at the font after
his sponsor — the brother of the last doge. The name came from a godparent, not
from blood.

Pietro Manin passed his son one thing above all others, which was rage. He could
not bear being saddled with the surname of the man who had surrendered the
heritage of a thousand years without a blow, and he raised the boy to be a
Venetian patriot. It worked. Trevelyan, turning over hundreds of pages of praise
and abuse of Manin, could not find a single instance of anyone taunting him with
being a Jew, or even discussing it: he seemed to everybody entirely Venetian.

Manin trained as a lawyer, agitated against Austria, and was in prison in the
early months of 1848. It was there, he said afterwards, that he first had the
idea of proclaiming the republic.

## The Arsenal, again

The revolution of March 1848 turned on the same building the republic had been
built around.

The Arsenal under Austria was a naval dockyard with a resentful hereditary
workforce. The *arsenalotti* were a caste: employment had gone from father to
son since the early republic, and they had just petitioned to be admitted to the
Civic Guard on the ground that under the old constitution they had crewed the
state barge and formed the doge's bodyguard. Under Austria they were dockhands,
ill-treated, in permanent dispute with a superintendent who saw his job as
driving a hard bargain against them and who was hand in glove with the police.
They were convinced he was preparing to bombard the city, and on 22 March they
killed him.

Manin took the Arsenal that day, and with it the fleet, and the ships in the
port outside ran up the Italian tricolour. He came out by the Lion steps and
raised, for the first time, the cry of *Viva San Marco*. He was calculating on
what that phrase would do in that city, and the roar that answered him told him
he had calculated right.

By the time he reached the Piazza the Austrian governor's authority was already
being negotiated away. Standing on a table with a banner in one hand and a drawn
sword in the other, he proclaimed the republic:

> We are free, and we have a double right to boast of it because we have become
> free without shedding a drop of blood, either our own or our brothers', for I
> call all men brothers.

He went home at five o'clock and fell asleep exhausted. At half past six the
Austrian commander signed the capitulation.

## Fifty-one years

That evening the crowd carried the proclamation through the city, over the
Rialto and into the western parishes, and men ran down the alleys shouting that
the republic was restored. Gondoliers raced to the outer islands with the news
until Malamocco and Chioggia and Burano and long-abandoned Torcello were
repeating the old name across the water.

In the Campo San Polo, after the proclamation had been read, a very old man —
one of the poorest people there — stood bare-headed through the ceremony, then
took a battered wooden carving of the lion of St Mark out of his shirt and
showed it to the people standing round him.

> I knew well that Venice would rise again. For fifty-one years I have lived in
> that faith and preserved this lion, to bring it out when the Republic came
> back. To-day I have nothing else to ask God and can die content.

The republic he was waiting for lasted about seventeen months. Manin held it
together for twelve of them after Italy's hopes elsewhere had already been
destroyed, in constant physical pain, refusing throughout to flatter the city's
worse instincts or give way to its less sensible demands; Trevelyan calls the
period his dictatorship and also his martyrdom, and means both. Austria took the
city back, and he went into exile.

He died in Paris on 22 September 1857, having lost his wife and his daughter
there. On his deathbed he signed the articles of the Italian National Society,
having already published a declaration that the republican party would give way:
*Make Italy, and I am with you. If not — no.*

Venice was freed in 1866, nine years after his death, and joined a kingdom
rather than resuming a republic. His son Giorgio, wounded at Calatafimi
fighting beside Garibaldi among the Thousand, lived to see it and then lived
withdrawn from the politics of the liberated city, treated by everyone as
something apart.

The old man in the Campo San Polo had been wrong about the form and right about
the fact. He had kept a wooden lion inside his shirt for fifty-one years on the
chance, and he got to take it out.
