Venice was at its wealthiest in the fifteenth century and spent the following
three hundred years getting smaller, which is a long time to be declining and is
one of the reasons the word is unhelpful.

Three things happened, in this order.

**The mainland.** From 1405 Venice took Padua, Verona, Vicenza, Brescia and
Bergamo, and became a territorial power in northern Italy for the first time.
The reasons were good — control of the food supply, the trade routes over the
Alps, and the rivers — and the consequence was that a republic whose whole
strategic position had been that it could not be reached by an army acquired a
long land frontier and enemies with cavalry. In 1509 a coalition of most of
Europe beat the Venetian army at Agnadello and the entire mainland was lost in
weeks. It was recovered, and the lesson taken was not the one the fifteenth
century had suggested.

**The Ottomans.** This site's Ottoman record and this one fought seven wars
between 1463 and 1718. Negroponte went in 1470, Modon and Coron in 1500, Cyprus
in 1571 — the war in which the Holy League destroyed the Ottoman fleet at
Lepanto, five months too late to save the island — and Crete in 1669 after a
siege of the capital lasting twenty-one years, which is this record's last
turning point.

**The route.** Vasco da Gama reached India in 1498. The traditional account has
the Portuguese route round Africa destroying the Venetian spice trade
immediately, and the traditional account is wrong: the Levant trade recovered
within a generation and the sixteenth century was not a disaster for it. What the
Atlantic did was slower and worse. The centre of European commerce moved to
Antwerp, then Amsterdam and London, and a Mediterranean republic found itself on
a periphery.

## What a state does when it is no longer the centre

It goes on functioning, which this record's coded fields register and its
reputation does not.

Venice in the eighteenth century had a working constitution, an intact legal
system, a functioning bureaucracy, a large manufacturing sector, a network of
resident ambassadors whose reports are among the best diplomatic sources in
Europe, and the best-organised tourist industry on the continent. It was neutral
in almost every war after 1718 and prosperous in a diminished way. It was not
misgoverned and it was not collapsing.

That is the ordinary shape of a long ending and this site has almost no way to
show it. The `span` field runs to 1797 and treats the last century as part of the
polity, which it was. The `reach_km2` field is null. The turning points stop at
1669 because coding rule 4.2 wants hinges and there were none — a state that is
stable and shrinking generates no dated events, and a record with nothing in the
last hundred and twenty-eight years is telling the truth about that.

## 1797

Napoleon arrived in northern Italy in 1796, Venice tried to stay neutral, and in
the spring of 1797 he told the Venetian envoys that he wanted no more Inquisition,
no more Senate, and that he would be an Attila to the Venetian state.

On 12 May the Great Council met without a quorum and voted five hundred and
twelve to twenty to abolish itself and hand power to a provisional municipality.
Ludovico Manin took off the ducal cap and gave it to his servant, saying it would
not be needed again. French troops entered four days later. In October Napoleon
traded the city to Austria at Campo Formio for territory elsewhere.

This record's `ended.type` is `conquest` and `by` is empty, and the data comment
explains both: no battle was fought, and a vote taken with an army on the
mainland is not a constitutional act, and Revolutionary France has no page here.

Eleven hundred years, and the last decision was taken by a body that did not have
enough members present to take it.
