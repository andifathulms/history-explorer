In 1201 the Venetian government contracted to build and crew a fleet to carry
thirty-three thousand crusaders to Egypt, and suspended most other commercial
activity for a year to do it. The crusaders raised about half the agreed sum and
arrived in numbers far below the contract. Venice had a fleet, no payment, and a
year's trade forgone.

What followed is the best-documented sequence of decisions in this record. The
crusade worked off part of the debt by taking Zara on the Dalmatian coast for
Venice — a Christian city, which got the whole army excommunicated. It then
accepted an offer from a Byzantine claimant who promised money and troops if
restored. It restored him; he could not pay; the city rioted and killed him; and
in April 1204 the army stormed Constantinople and sacked it for three days.

## Enrico Dandolo

This record names him as its peak ruler and he is not a typical doge, which the
data comment on the field says.

He was in his nineties and blind. He negotiated the transport contract, took the
cross himself, led the assault on the sea walls from the bow of his own galley,
and was the dominant figure in the partition afterwards. He is buried in Hagia
Sophia, or was — the marker is there, and the remains were probably scattered in
1261.

Whether he engineered the diversion from the start is the oldest argument in
Venetian historiography and Lane's position is that he did not: the sequence is
better explained by a bankrupt crusade making one expedient decision after
another, each of which suited Venice. The debate does not change the outcome and
this record does not adjudicate it.

## Three-eighths

The partition treaty assigned Venice three-eighths of the Byzantine empire and
gave the doge the title lord of a quarter and half a quarter of the empire of
Romania, which is three-eighths written the long way.

What Venice took was chosen with unusual clarity. Not the land: the French and
Flemish knights got Thrace, Macedonia and Greece and had to hold them against
Bulgarians and Greeks. Venice took the ports, the islands and the straits —
Modon and Coron at the tip of the Peloponnese, Negroponte, the Aegean islands,
the Adriatic coast, and Crete, which it bought from Boniface of Montferrat for a
thousand marks because he had no way to use it.

That is the map of a trading empire rather than a territorial one, and it is
what this record's `core_region` field describes. A chain of harbours a day's
sail apart from the lagoon to the eastern Mediterranean, each with a castle, a
Venetian governor and a lion over the gate, and almost no interior anywhere.

## What it cost the other pages

This site's Byzantine record carries 1204 as a turning point and its ninth
chapter is about what the sack did: a city that never regained its population, a
treasury that never recovered, and a customs revenue that passed to the Italian
republics permanently. Its Nicaean record is the government in exile that
resulted. Its Trebizond record is a second one.

Four records on this site describe the same three days, and this is the one that
describes them as an acquisition.
