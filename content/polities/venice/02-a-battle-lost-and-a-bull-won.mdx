For two and a half centuries after Saint Mark's relics reached the
lagoon, Venice remained what it had always been - a nominal Byzantine
dependency trading on its own initiative, without the kind of formal
privilege that would let it outcompete its rivals in the eastern
Mediterranean by right rather than by daring. What changed that was
not a Venetian victory but a Venetian defeat, and the desperate
gratitude of an emperor who needed the Republic's fleet more than he
could admit.

## A doge deposed for losing to the Normans

Robert Guiscard, the Norman adventurer who had already carved a
kingdom out of southern Italy, crossed the Adriatic in the 1080s and
laid siege to Durazzo, the Byzantine Empire's own gateway city on its
western coast. The Emperor Alexios I, unable to relieve the siege
alone, appealed to Venice, promising to cover the cost of any
expedition and reward it richly for success. Doge Domenico Selvo led
the fleet himself and won a real victory at sea, sinking Norman ships
with weights dropped from the yardarms onto their decks and relieving
Durazzo from the water side - only for Alexios's own impatience to
throw the advantage away. The Emperor insisted on a land battle
against Guiscard's veterans despite Venetian warnings, suffered a
ruinous defeat, and watched Durazzo fall to the Normans regardless. A
second joint effort ended in a further defeat at Corfu, and Selvo, his
policy repudiated at home, was deposed by his own Republic.

## A second fleet, and a debt finally paid

His successor, Vitale Falier, rebuilt Venice's naval strength with
striking speed and sailed against the Normans again, this time by
prior arrangement with Alexios over what victory would be worth. The
Venetians met Guiscard's fleet off Corfu and won a real if
inconclusive engagement; the wider war against the Normans ended
shortly afterward with Guiscard's own death in 1085. Alexios, freed
from the immediate threat, honored his debt in full. His chrysobull -
the Golden Bull - granted the Doge the Byzantine court title of
protosebastos, gave Venetian merchants free access to every harbor of
the Empire, exempted them entirely from imperial customs duties, and
handed them lands, warehouses, and a quarter of their own in
Constantinople itself. As a final touch, the Emperor taxed the
merchants of Amalfi, Venice's leading Italian rival in eastern trade,
to help fund the rebuilding of St Mark's.

## What a tax exemption actually built

The concessions of 1082 cost the Byzantine treasury nothing it counted
as urgent at the time and gave Venice something no purely military
victory could have: a structural advantage over every competitor
still paying the standard ten percent. A trading city that pays
nothing where its rivals pay a tenth does not merely turn a better
profit - over generations, it displaces them outright, and Amalfi's
long eclipse as an eastern trading power began almost exactly here.
The bull is, in the most literal sense, the founding document of the
Venetian commercial empire the Fourth Crusade would later turn into
outright territorial dominion - and the same customs concessions that
built Venetian wealth were, eventually, what it cost the Byzantine
Empire the revenues of its own straits.
