Napoleon came into northern Italy in 1796 and fought the Austrians across
territory Venice had held for four hundred years. Venice tried to stay neutral,
which meant supplying both sides and being trusted by neither.

In the spring of 1797 he received the Venetian envoys and told them what he
wanted: no more Inquisition, no more Senate, no more oligarchy. He said he
would be an Attila to the Venetian state.

## The vote

On 12 May the Great Council met.

It did not have a quorum. Under its own rules it could not lawfully transact
business, and it transacted the last business it would ever have. By five
hundred and twelve votes to twenty it abolished itself and handed power to a
provisional municipality, on the reasoning that a French army was on the
mainland, no ally was coming, and a city built on mud had never had a way of
resisting one.

Ludovico Manin took off the ducal cap, handed it to his servant, and said it
would not be needed again. French troops entered four days later.

There was no siege. There was no battle. The eleven-hundred-year-old republic
that had survived Genoa at the mouth of its own lagoon, a coalition of most of
Europe on its landward side, and seven Ottoman wars, ended in an afternoon by a
show of hands that its own constitution did not authorise.

## And then he sold it

The last indignity was not the vote.

In October, at Campo Formio, Napoleon handed Venice to Austria in exchange for
territory he wanted elsewhere. The city that had been talked into dissolving
itself in the name of liberty was traded five months later as a diplomatic
counter, to a monarchy, by the general who had demanded the dissolution.

Eleven hundred years, and the last sovereign act was taken by a body that did
not have enough members present to take one.
