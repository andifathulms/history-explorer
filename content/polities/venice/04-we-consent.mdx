In the spring of 1201 six envoys from the barons of France rode to Venice to ask
for ships. One of them was Geoffrey of Villehardouin, marshal of Champagne, and
he afterwards wrote the crusade up. He is the participant with the largest
personal interest in the outcome looking defensible, and he is also the man who
recorded the terms.

## The contract

The doge took a week to consult and then gave them his figures. Transports for
four thousand five hundred horses and nine thousand squires; ships for four
thousand five hundred knights and twenty thousand sergeants of foot; food for
all of them, men and horses, for nine months. Four marks a horse, two marks a
man. The covenants to hold for a year from the day the fleet sailed.

> Now the sum total of the expenses above named amounts to 85,000 marks.

Then the clause that decided everything afterwards. For the love of God, the
doge added, Venice would put in fifty armed galleys as well — on condition that
so long as the two parties acted in company, of all conquests in land or money,
whether at sea or on dry ground, Venice should have the half.

The envoys consulted overnight and accepted.

## How Venice agreed

Villehardouin's account of the ratification is the best description anybody wrote
of the republic making up its mind, and it has nothing to do with the ten rounds
of lot and ballot.

The doge went first to the great council, forty of the wisest men in the land,
and brought them to agreement. Then a hundred. Then two hundred. Then a
thousand. Then he assembled ten thousand of the people in St Mark's, had a mass
of the Holy Ghost sung, and told them to pray for counsel.

After the mass the envoys were brought into the church, curiously looked upon by
people who had not seen them before, and Villehardouin spoke for them. The
barons of France had chosen Venice, he said, because they knew full well there
was no other people having so great power on the seas; and they had commanded
their envoys to fall at the Venetians' feet and not to rise until they consented.

> Then the six envoys knelt at the feet of the people, weeping many tears. And
> the Doge and all the others burst into tears of pity and compassion, and cried
> with one voice, and lifted up their hands, saying: "We consent, we consent!"
> Then was there so great a noise and tumult that it seemed as if the earth
> itself were falling to pieces.

It is a staged scene and Villehardouin knows it. The doge had already secured the
forty, the hundred, the two hundred and the thousand; the ten thousand in the
basilica were being shown a decision, not asked for one. What the ceremony
produced was not consent but commitment — a public, collective, witnessed one,
which mattered because the republic was about to stop most of its other business
for a year to build the fleet.

## The old man on the reading-desk

A year and a half later, with the fleet built and the crusaders unable to pay,
Enrico Dandolo went up into the same reading-desk on a Sunday before High Mass.

> Signors, you are associated with the most worthy people in the world, and for
> the highest enterprise ever undertaken; and I am a man old and feeble, who
> should have need of rest, and I am sick in body; but I see that no one could
> command and lead you like myself, who am your lord. If you will consent that I
> take the sign of the cross to guard and direct you, and that my son remain in
> my place to guard the land, then shall I go to live or die with you and with
> the pilgrims.

Villehardouin explains the detail that makes the offer extraordinary: although
the doge's eyes were unclouded, he saw nothing, having lost his sight through a
wound in the head. He came down from the desk, knelt weeping before the altar,
and had the cross sewn onto the front of a great cotton hat he wore, *because he
wished that all men should see it*. Up to that day very few Venetians had taken
the cross. After it they took it in great numbers.

A blind man in his nineties put a badge where it could be seen and the
recruitment problem solved itself. Whatever else Dandolo was, he understood what
a crowd in a basilica was for.

## What the contract did

The rest follows from the arithmetic. The crusaders who arrived were far fewer
than the contract named and could raise about half the money; Venice had spent a
year building ships for an army that did not come. The debt was worked off first
against Zara, a Christian city on the Dalmatian coast, and then against
Constantinople.

Villehardouin was there for all of it and the chronicle never quite says that
anybody chose. It presents each step as the only thing that could have been done
next, and treats the men who left rather than attack Christians as deserters. He
is arguing a case. But the case rests on the numbers, and it was Villehardouin
himself who knelt on the floor of St Mark's to get them agreed.
