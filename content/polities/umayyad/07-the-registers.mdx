---
title: Seek another profession
drafted_from: baladhuri-futuh
phase: aside
---

For nearly seventy years after the conquest, the tax registers of Syria were
kept in Greek, by the same clerks and the same families who had kept them for
the emperors. The conquerors had taken the provinces and left the accounting
where it was, because they could not do it themselves.

In the year 81 — 700 in the Christian reckoning — Abd al-Malik ordered the
language changed.

## The inkstand

Baladhuri gives a reason for the decision, and it is worth repeating exactly
because of what it is doing. A Greek clerk, wanting to write something and
finding no ink, urinated in the inkwell. Abd al-Malik heard of it, punished the
man, and gave orders for the registers to be converted.

Whether or not anything of the sort happened, the story is how the change was
remembered: not as policy but as an insult, and the insult is the clerk's
contempt for the arrangement he was employed in. A conquest state that cannot
read its own accounts is being administered by people who despise it, and
everybody involved knew it.

The conversion was contracted out like any other job of work. Sulayman ibn Sa'd
asked, as his fee, for the *kharaj* of the province of Jordan for one year; Abd
al-Malik agreed and made him governor of Jordan into the bargain. The year
ended, the registers were finished, and he brought them in.

## What it was worth

The revenue Sulayman took as his payment is recorded, and so are the
neighbouring provinces, which is a rare thing to have: Jordan yielded 180,000
dinars, Palestine 350,000, Damascus 400,000, and Hims with Qinnasrin between
700,000 and 800,000.

That is the price of the change of language, and it was worth paying, because
what it bought was the end of an entire profession's hold on the state's money.

## Sarjun

Abd al-Malik then called in Sarjun and showed him the new arrangement.

Sarjun — Sergius — was the head of the Greek financial administration, from the
family that had run Damascus for the emperors before the conquest and had gone
on running it for the caliphs afterwards; his son is the theologian known as
John of Damascus. He went away in distress, and when he met some of the Greek
clerks he told them to find their living in some other profession than this
one, because God had cut it off from them.

It is the most exact sentence in the whole business. A bureaucratic caste that
had outlived the empire it was created for, and had made itself indispensable
to the empire that replaced it, was dispensed with in a single year's work by a
man who was paid in tax revenue to do it. Everything the dynasty is admired for
building — its own coinage, its own architecture, its own theological claims —
belongs with this. So does everything it is blamed for, since a state that can
count in its own language can also press its provinces harder and more
uniformly, and did.
