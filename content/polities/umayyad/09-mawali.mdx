The conquest settlement assumed a small Arab military class taxed lightly and
paid stipends, living on the revenue of a large non-Arab, non-Muslim population
taxed heavily. It worked as long as the two categories stayed the same size.

They did not. Non-Arabs converted, attached themselves to Arab tribes as
clients — *mawali* — and then discovered that conversion did not reliably move
them into the paying-less category, because the provincial treasuries could not
afford it if it did. A convert who moved to a garrison town to claim a stipend
was a double loss: revenue gone from the land register, expenditure added to
the *diwan*. Governors responded by continuing to tax converts, which was
defensible fiscally and indefensible in any other terms, since the community's
own claim was that Islam was what mattered.

<Figure id="mosque-mosaic" />

Umar II's reforms in 717–720 attempted the honest version — convert, pay Muslim
rates — and the fiscal arithmetic promptly failed. Later governors quietly
reverted. The grievance did not.

By the 740s the caliphate was carrying that grievance in every province at
once, alongside a Syrian army stretched across a frontier that had stopped
producing booty, tribal factionalism between Qays and Yaman that had become the
organising principle of provincial politics, and a succession of short reigns
and disputed accessions after 743. The third civil war began as an
intra-Umayyad quarrel. It finished as something else.
