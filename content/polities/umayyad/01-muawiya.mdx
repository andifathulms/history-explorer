Mu'awiya had governed Syria for twenty years before he was caliph, and he ruled
as caliph the way he had governed: through the Syrian Arab tribes whose loyalty
he had spent two decades building, and through the Byzantine fiscal
administration he had inherited intact and never dismantled.

This is the arrangement that made the Umayyads and eventually unmade them.
Syria's tribal levies were reliable, close to the capital, and paid. Iraq's
garrisons at Kufa and Basra were larger, further away, unreliable, and
convinced — with reason — that Iraqi revenue was funding Syrian soldiers. Every
Umayyad crisis for the next ninety years runs along that seam.

<Figure id="umayyad-mosque" />

Mu'awiya's other innovation was to have his son Yazid acknowledged as successor
in his own lifetime, taking oaths of allegiance from the provinces in advance.
Nobody had done this. It was not defended as a principle and no theory was
offered for it; it was presented as a practical measure against another civil
war, by a man who had just won one. The theory never really arrived, but the
practice held for three generations, which is how a caliphate elected by
acclamation at Medina became a dynasty ruling from Damascus without any moment
at which the change was announced.

Hawting's warning belongs here rather than in a footnote: nearly everything
written about this dynasty was written afterwards, under the dynasty that
overthrew it, by people with an interest in the Umayyads having been impious
kings rather than caliphs. The hostility is not evidence of the charge.
