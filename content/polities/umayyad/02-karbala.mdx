---
title: Karbala, and the war that remade the dynasty
drafted_from: hawting-2000
phase: formation
---

Mu'awiya died in 680 having spent his last years arranging for his son to
succeed him. The arrangement held everywhere except where it mattered.

Husayn ibn Ali, the Prophet's grandson and the son of the fourth caliph,
refused the oath to Yazid and set out for Kufa, whose garrison had invited him.
The invitation evaporated as the governor's grip tightened, and Husayn was
intercepted in the desert near Karbala with a party of perhaps seventy — family,
household, a few followers — by a force sent from Kufa in the caliph's name.
On the tenth of Muharram he and almost all the men with him were killed, and
the women and children of the Prophet's house were taken to Damascus as
prisoners.

Militarily it was nothing: a skirmish that took an afternoon. Politically it is
one of the few events in this corpus whose consequences are still legible
thirteen centuries later. It gave a constituency that had so far been a
political tendency a founding injustice, a martyr, a place, and a date, and it
was inflicted by the caliph's own troops on the Prophet's own grandson, which
is not a charge any subsequent settlement could answer. The Umayyads did not
become illegitimate to the Shi'a because of anything they did afterwards. They
had already done it.

The war that followed was longer and messier than the first fitna. Abd Allah
ibn al-Zubayr declared a caliphate at Mecca and was for several years the more
widely recognised of the two claimants. Kharijite movements held ground in
Arabia and southern Iran. Mukhtar rose at Kufa in the name of another son of
Ali, on a platform that put the *mawali* — the non-Arab converts — on the same
footing as Arabs, which is the grievance the sixth chapter of this page is
about, appearing forty years early with an army behind it.

The Umayyads survived it by changing branch. The Sufyanid line ended with
Yazid's son, and at Marj Rahit in 684 the Syrian tribes acclaimed Marwan of the
rival Marwanid branch — a victory won by mobilising the Yaman confederation
against the Qays. That worked, and it also converted a tribal rivalry into the
permanent organising principle of Umayyad provincial politics. Every governor
for the next sixty years was a Qaysi or a Yamani appointment, and the factional
arithmetic outlived the dynasty.

Ibn al-Zubayr held Mecca until 692, when al-Hajjaj besieged the city and killed
him. The caliphate that emerged was territorially the same and institutionally
a different thing, and the next chapter is what it did with itself.
