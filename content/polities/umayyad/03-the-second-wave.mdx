---
title: Three frontiers in one decade
drafted_from: kennedy-2007
phase: expansion
---

At its height the caliphate covered eleven million square kilometres, and most
of that was taken in a single decade. The Rashidun conquests had taken Syria,
Egypt and Iran. Everything beyond that — the Maghreb, Spain, Sind, Transoxiana
— is Umayyad, and most of it happened in about ten years under one caliph.

Al-Walid I ruled from 705 to 715, and in that decade three offensives ran at
once on three continents, three thousand miles apart, nominally directed from
Damascus: Qutayba ibn Muslim beyond the Oxus, Muhammad ibn al-Qasim on the lower
Indus, and Tariq ibn Ziyad across the strait that now carries his name. The two
chapters after this one follow the eastern and the western campaigns
separately, because they were run by different men on different principles and
the only thing they had in common was the treasury they paid into.

It is worth being precise about what that directed. The armies were tribal
contingents under governors, and the governors were often given wide latitude
and a share of what they took. The centre supplied legitimacy, appointments,
and reinforcement from Syria, and it took a share of the proceeds. This is a
conquest system rather than a general staff, and its coherence came from the
fact that everyone in it was being paid out of the same expanding revenue.

Which is the thing that stopped. The northern frontier was the first to say so:
Maslama ibn Abd al-Malik laid siege to Constantinople in 717 with a fleet and a
large Syrian army and was destroyed there over the following winter, by Greek
fire, hunger and a Bulgar attack on the besiegers. The Arab armies never
seriously tried again, and the Taurus frontier that resulted stood for three
hundred years — long enough that Byzantine writers came to treat it as a
permanent feature of the world rather than as something that had been won.

After that the arithmetic ran the other way. Blankinship's argument is that a
state whose stipends, loyalty and legitimacy all assumed continuing conquest
could not simply stop conquering and carry on. Under Hisham, from 724 to 743,
the frontiers were defended rather than advanced, in Anatolia, against the
Khazars in the Caucasus, in Transoxiana against the Türgesh, and in the west;
and defence is expensive without being profitable. The grievances that follow — the converts' and the
Berbers' — are what a fixed revenue looks like when it meets a population that
expected a growing one.
