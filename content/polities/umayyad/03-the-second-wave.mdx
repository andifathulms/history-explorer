---
title: Three frontiers in one decade
drafted_from: kennedy-2007

This page carries a reach figure of eleven million square kilometres and, until
now, no chapter about how it got there. The Rashidun conquests took Syria, Egypt
and Iran. Everything beyond that — the Maghreb, Spain, Sind, Transoxiana — is
Umayyad, and most of it happened in about ten years under one caliph.

Al-Walid I ruled from 705 to 715. In that decade Qutayba ibn Muslim took
Bukhara, Samarkand and Khwarazm, putting an Arab garrison on the far side of
the Oxus in the country this site's Iranian Intermezzo region is about;
Muhammad ibn al-Qasim took Sind and the lower Indus; and in 711 Tariq ibn Ziyad
crossed the strait that now carries his name with an army that was
overwhelmingly Berber and destroyed the Visigothic kingdom in a single
campaigning season. Three offensives, on three continents, three thousand miles
apart, directed from Damascus.

It is worth being precise about what that directed. The armies were tribal
contingents under governors, and the governors were often given wide latitude
and a share of what they took. The centre supplied legitimacy, appointments,
and reinforcement from Syria, and it took a share of the proceeds. This is a
conquest system rather than a general staff, and its coherence came from the
fact that everyone in it was being paid out of the same expanding revenue.

Which is the thing that stopped. The northern frontier was the first to say so:
Maslama ibn Abd al-Malik laid siege to Constantinople in 717 with a fleet and a
large Syrian army and was destroyed there over the following winter, by Greek
fire, hunger and a Bulgar attack on the besiegers. The Arab armies never
seriously tried again, and the Taurus frontier that resulted stood for three
hundred years — long enough that this site's Byzantine page treats it as a
settled fact rather than as an event.

After that the arithmetic ran the other way. Blankinship's argument, which this
site's revenue coding for the Umayyads follows, is that a state whose stipends,
loyalty and legitimacy all assumed continuing conquest could not simply stop
conquering and carry on. Under Hisham, from 724 to 743, the frontiers were
defended rather than advanced, in Anatolia, against the Khazars in the Caucasus,
in Transoxiana against the Türgesh, and in the west; and defence is expensive
without being profitable. The grievances of the two chapters after this one are
what a fixed revenue looks like when it meets a population that expected a
growing one.
