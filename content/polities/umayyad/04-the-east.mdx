The eastern conquests under al-Walid are usually given as a list of cities:
Bukhara, Samarkand, Khwarazm, and the lower Indus. Baladhuri's eastern volume
gives them as a list of transactions, and the difference matters, because it is
the transactions that explain how so little manpower took so much ground.

## Bukhara, and how it was done

Qutayba ibn Muslim crossed to Paykand in 706. The town rebelled and called on
the Soghdians for help; he besieged it, refused terms and took it by force. The
following year he raided Tumushkath and Karmuniya, made peace after taking a few
small forts, and reduced Bukhara by treaty.

That is one account. Baladhuri also records another, from Ibn al-Muthanna, and
does not choose between them.

In this version Bukhara defended itself successfully, and Qutayba asked only to
be let into the city to pray two *rakas*. Permission was given. He had men
concealed in ambush; when he entered, they overpowered the gatekeepers and the
army came in behind them. Baladhuri's comment on the money Qutayba took is
flat and without excuse: he obtained much of it, *dealing perfidiously with the
inhabitants*.

He then attacked Soghd, and killed Nizak in Tukharistan and crucified him —
Nizak, who had been campaigning alongside him at Paykand two years earlier.

## Khwarazm, bought

Khwarazm was not conquered at all. It was purchased, by its own king.

The king was weak and had been overpowered by his brother Khurrazadh. He wrote
to Qutayba offering gifts and the keys of the country on condition that Qutayba
put him back on the throne. The money and the keys arrived. Qutayba sent his
brother Abd al-Rahman against Khurrazadh, killed him, took four thousand
captives and put them all to death, and restored the king exactly as agreed.

Then the people of Khwarazm, observing that their king was weak, killed him.
Qutayba appointed another brother, Ubayd Allah ibn Muslim, as governor.

The whole sequence took a local succession dispute and ended with the province
administered from Merv. Nobody in Khwarazm had intended that, and the man who
started it was dead within the year.

At Samarkand there was a real battle — the king of Soghd called in the king of
Chach, and Qutayba broke them with a charge — and then the usual instrument:
Ghurak capitulated on terms of twelve hundred thousand dirhams a year.

## Sind, and the thread and needles

The Indus campaign began with a piracy incident. A ship carrying women home was
taken by raiders off Daybul, and one of the women, of the Banu Yarbu, cried out
*O Hajjaj!* Al-Hajjaj, governor of Iraq, is said to have answered aloud: *Here
am I.* He demanded their release; Dahir replied that pirates were not under his
control. Two expeditions sent to punish Daybul lost their commanders.

The third was Muhammad ibn al-Qasim, and what Baladhuri records about the
preparation is more revealing than the campaign. Al-Hajjaj reinforced him with
six thousand Syrians, told him to sit at Shiraz until everything was assembled,
and supplied him with everything he asked for — *even to thread and needles*.
Knowing that vinegar was scarce in Sind, al-Hajjaj soaked clean cotton in it,
dried it in the shade, and sent it out with instructions to steep the cotton in
water, heat it, and season the food with that.

An empire that could not always say who its next caliph would be was
nevertheless a state where a provincial governor solved a condiment problem for
an army two thousand miles away, in advance. The conquests were not carried by
enthusiasm. They were carried by treaty, by ambush, by buying one brother
against another — and by quartermasters.
