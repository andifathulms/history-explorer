---
title: The revolution came from Khurasan
drafted_from: hawting-2000
phase: end
---

The movement that ended the Umayyads was organised in Khurasan, the far
north-eastern province, and it was organised for twenty years before it moved.

Its agents propagated in the name of an unnamed member of the Prophet's family
— *al-rida min al Muhammad*, the acceptable one from Muhammad's house — a
formula vague enough to hold Shi'i expectation and general Umayyad grievance in
the same coalition without either being told which one had won. Its recruits
were the Arab settlers of Khurasan, long resident, intermarried, and holding
land, plus the local Iranian gentry and the *mawali* the settlement had never
accommodated. This was not a conquered population rising against Arabs. It was
a provincial society that no longer fitted the categories Damascus taxed it by.

Those settlers were themselves Iraq's own overflow. Fifty thousand families had
been shipped out of the overcrowded garrisons of Basra and Kufa to Khurasan in
the 670s, given land at the empire's furthest edge rather than a stipend at its
centre — Iraqi manpower, resettled rather than paid off. Two generations on
they were as fixed in the province as anyone, and no happier than Basra or
Kufa had ever been about what Damascus took from provinces it did not garrison
itself.

They were also still Qaysi and Yamani, and that quarrel did some of the
revolution's work for it. The governor Nasr ibn Sayyar spent his last years
trying to hold Khurasan's two factions together and failed: when war broke out
between him and the Yamani leader Juday al-Kirmani, Abu Muslim moved his own
men between the two camps and let the province's oldest grievance finish
weakening the man who might have stopped him. Kirmani was killed; his son
joined the revolution rather than avenge him on the governor's side. Marwan II,
fighting Kharijites of his own in Iraq, had nothing to spare for Khurasan.

Abu Muslim raised the black banners in 747. The Khurasani army moved west, took
Kufa in 749, and destroyed Marwan II's forces at the Great Zab in 750. Only
when Kufa was held was the unnamed candidate named, and he turned out to be an
Abbasid — a descendant of the Prophet's uncle al-Abbas, not of Ali. The Shi'i
element of the coalition had been used and now discovered it.

The Umayyad house was hunted down. One grandson of Hisham escaped to
al-Andalus, where he founded an emirate at Cordoba that lasted longer than the
caliphate his family had lost, and whose rulers eventually claimed the caliphal
title outright in 929 — the one recorded successor claim on this polity's page,
and one of the few things in this section that is a claim rather than an
inheritance.
