The Abbasids did not merely take the caliphate from the Umayyads. They set out
to make the family harmless, and they were thorough about it.

The dynasty was outlawed and its estates seized. The chief scene of what
followed was Syria, where Abdallah ibn Ali held the command, and Wellhausen is
careful about the responsibility: it was not the doing of the Khurasanian
troops, who were disciplined and did nothing without orders, but was ordered
from above.

## The graves

Even the dead were punished. The tombs of the caliphs and other Umayyads at
Damascus, at Dabiq, at Rusafa, at Qinnasrin and elsewhere were broken open and
what was in them was violated, where anything remained to violate.

Two were spared. One was Umar II, the caliph the pious tradition had already
decided to approve of. The other, more surprisingly, was Mu'awiya.

The worst of it fell on Hisham, who had died recently enough for the hatred to
be personal. His body — of which, it was said, only the nose was still intact —
was scourged, crucified, burned, and the ashes scattered.

## The dinner

At Abu Futrus, where Abdallah ibn Ali stayed after driving out the last caliph,
more than eighty Umayyads are said to have been brought in under promises of
safety and invited to a meal. Verses were recited; he was roused to fury by
them; the guests were clubbed down, leather covers were spread over the bodies,
and the dinner was served on top of them, to the sound of the dying underneath.

Wellhausen doubts the staging and not the substance. The recited verse as a
trigger and the murder of enemies at a feast are motifs that turn up elsewhere
in the same literature, and other versions name other Abbasids in Abdallah's
place. The great slaughter itself he takes as entirely authentic. Masudi dates
it to 15 Dhu al-Qa'da 132, which is 25 June 750.

## Who was left

The survivors did not dare show themselves. They hid, lived meanly, and went in
constant fear of being recognised and declared outlaws.

One grandson of the caliph Hisham got out to Spain, and there made himself a
sovereign.

That is what was left, ninety years on, of a family whose writ had run from the
Atlantic to the Indus: some frightened men in hiding, a set of broken tombs, and
one young man at the far western end of everything they had taken, starting
again.
