---
title: The revolution came from Khurasan
drafted_from: hawting-2000
phase: end
---

The movement that ended the Umayyads was organised in Khurasan, the far
north-eastern province, and it was organised for twenty years before it moved.

Its agents propagated in the name of an unnamed member of the Prophet's family —
*al-rida min al Muhammad*, the acceptable one from Muhammad's house — a formula
vague enough to hold Shi'i expectation and general Umayyad grievance in the same
coalition without either being told which one had won. Its recruits were the
Arab settlers of Khurasan, long resident, intermarried, and holding land, plus
the local Iranian gentry and the *mawali* the settlement had never accommodated.
This was not a conquered population rising against Arabs. It was a provincial
society that no longer fitted the categories Damascus taxed it by.

Abu Muslim raised the black banners in 747. The Khurasani army moved west, took
Kufa in 749, and destroyed Marwan II's forces at the Great Zab in 750. Only when
Kufa was held was the unnamed candidate named, and he turned out to be an
Abbasid — a descendant of the Prophet's uncle al-Abbas, not of Ali. The Shi'i
element of the coalition had been used and now discovered it.

The Umayyad house was hunted down. One grandson of Hisham escaped to al-Andalus,
where he founded an emirate at Cordoba that lasted longer than the caliphate his
family had lost, and whose rulers eventually claimed the caliphal title outright
in 929 — the one recorded successor claim on this polity's page, and one of the
few things in this section that is a claim rather than an inheritance.
