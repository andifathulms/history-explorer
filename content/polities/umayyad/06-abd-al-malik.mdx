Abd al-Malik inherited the second civil war in 685, holding little more than
Syria and Egypt, and had won all of it by 692. What he did afterwards
is the reason his reign, and not the conquests of the one before it, is the
peak of this polity.

He replaced Byzantine and Sasanian coin with a purely Islamic coinage: no
portrait, no cross, no fire altar, only Arabic inscription.

<Figure id="dinar" /> He made Arabic the
language of the tax registers in Syria, Iraq and Egypt, displacing the Greek
and Persian administrations that had run continuously since before the
conquest. He built the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem and put on it an
inscription addressed pointedly to Christians about the nature of Christ.

<Figure id="dome-of-rock" />

Each of these is the same act. A conquest state that taxes through somebody
else's bureaucracy in somebody else's language, using somebody else's money, is
an occupation collecting rent. After Abd al-Malik it was a state with its own
administrative language, its own currency, and its own public architecture
making theological arguments. This is the point at which the caliphate stops
being explicable as the last phase of late antiquity and becomes a thing of its
own.

The cost was legible immediately. Running an Arabic-language administration
meant a class of Arabic-literate officials; standardising coin and tax meant
pressing the provinces harder and more uniformly; and both meant that the
question of who counted as a full member of the community — specifically,
whether non-Arab converts owed the land tax — became a fiscal question with a
constituency attached. That question is what the *mawali* grievance grew out of.
