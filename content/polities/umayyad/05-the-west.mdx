The conquest of Spain took a single campaigning season and produced a quarrel
over loot that outlasted it by centuries. Ibn Abd al-Hakam, writing in
ninth-century Egypt, gives the earliest Arabic account of both, and he tells
them as one story, which is the right instinct.

## Getting across

The version he transmits begins with a betrayal. Ilyan, lord of Ceuta and a
subject of Roderic, had sent a daughter to Toledo to be educated and she came
back pregnant by the king. *I see for him no other punishment or recompense,*
Ilyan is made to say, *than that I should bring the Arabs against him.*

Tariq ibn Ziyad, governor of Tangier, did not simply accept. He said he could
not trust Ilyan without a hostage, and Ilyan sent his two remaining daughters,
who were held under close guard at Tlemcen for the duration.

The crossing itself was done by a trick of tempo. Ilyan's vessels carried a
party over in the evening; they hid by day, and the ships went back for the
next group after dark. Because the same boats were shuttling back and forth on
a strait where trading vessels did exactly that, nobody on the Spanish side
counted them. Tariq came over with the last division.

## A rumour, deliberately made

What Ibn Abd al-Hakam records next is not a battle. It is a piece of
psychological warfare, reported without embarrassment.

On an island the Muslims found only vinedressers, and took them prisoner. They
killed one, cut him up and boiled him in a cauldron, in view of the others,
while meat boiled in the other cauldrons. When it was cooked they threw the
man's flesh away where nobody could see, ate from the other pots, and released
the survivors — who went and told the people of Andalus that the Muslims ate
human flesh.

An invading force too small to win a fair fight manufactured a story about
itself and then let its own prisoners deliver it. The chronicler does not
condemn it or dwell on it; it is simply how the thing was done.

Then the fighting, and it was short. A force from Cordoba came out, despised
the small numbers, and was broken and pursued to the city walls. Roderic came
down from Toledo and met Tariq in the valley the Arabs afterwards called the
valley of Umm Hakim, after Tariq's slave. Roderic was killed there with his
companions, and the Visigothic kingdom effectively ended in an afternoon.

## The leg of the table

Musa ibn Nusayr, Tariq's superior, crossed the following year, and the two men
spent the rest of their lives arguing about who had conquered Spain.

The dispute crystallised around a jewelled table taken among the spoils. Musa
carried the treasure to Damascus and presented the table to the caliph al-Walid
as his own find. Tariq said he had found it. Musa called him a liar.

So Tariq asked the caliph to send for the table and examine it. One leg, he
pointed out, did not match the others. Musa, questioned, said he had found it
that way. Tariq then produced the missing leg, which he had removed when he
found the table precisely so that this moment could happen.

A general who dismantled part of his own loot as evidence, years in advance,
against the day his commander claimed it, tells you what the relationship
between the conquerors and their governors actually was.

The scramble around it was worse. Walid was dying while Musa was still on the
road; the caliph wrote urging him to hurry, and Walid's brother Sulayman wrote
urging him to slow down, so that the treasure would arrive after the succession
and fall to Sulayman instead. Musa got as far as Tiberias before the news of
Walid's death reached him, and handed the spoils to Sulayman, who was delighted
with them.

## What to believe

Not all of this is history and the source itself is the reason we can tell.

Ibn Abd al-Hakam also reports a padlocked house in Toledo to which every king
added a lock, opened at last by Roderic, who found inside portraits of Arabs
and a written warning that when the door was opened these people would invade.
That is a folk motif, and it sits in the same paragraph sequence as everything
above.

Ilyan is contested too. Jones, translating in 1858, notes that Masdeu and most
Spanish critics concluded he never existed, and that Gayangos observed no
writer before the eleventh century mentions his quarrel with Roderic. Jones's
answer is that this holds for Christian authors and not for Muslim ones: Ibn Abd
al-Hakam and Ibn al-Qutiyya both wrote in the ninth century, both name Ilyan,
and both give his quarrel as the cause of the invasion.

What survives all of that is the shape. A frontier governor with Berber troops
crossed on local shipping, exploited a Visigothic succession quarrel, killed the
king in one engagement — and then spent the rest of his career fighting his own
side over the proceeds.
