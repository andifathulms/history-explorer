The fall of the Tang has a foreign witness, because the rebellion that broke it
destroyed the largest foreign merchant community in Asia, and one of the survivors'
countrymen wrote down why the silk had stopped coming.

## Abu Zayd

Around 916 Abu Zayd al-Sirafi added a continuation to the merchant's account of 851,
explaining what had happened to the China trade in the meantime.

His answer is that it had been destroyed, and he begins by saying so in terms of
navigation: the troubles which broiled the affairs of that empire, and put a stop to
the justice and righteousness formerly practised there, *have, in fine, interrupted
the ordinary Navigation from Siraf to China*.

Then he names the cause. An officer of no royal family, considerable in his
employment, who revolted, marched about the country to the great loss of the
inhabitants, won a party over by his liberalities, and gathered a multitude of
vagabonds and abandoned people into a considerable body of troops.

He calls him Baichu. He is Huang Chao.

## Khanfu

The rebel marched on Canfu — Guangzhou — *one of the most noted Cities in China,
and, at that Time, the Port for all the Arabian Merchants*. The citizens shut the
gates. The siege lasted a long while. At last he took the city and put the
inhabitants to the sword.

Abu Zayd then gives a figure and, more unusually, gives his reason for trusting it.

> There are Persons fully acquainted with the Affairs of China, who assure us, that
> besides the Chinese who were massacred upon this Occasion, there perished one
> Hundred and twenty Thousand Mohammedans, Jews, Christians, and Parsees, who were
> there on Account of Traffic. The Number of the Professors of these four Religions,
> who thus perished, is exactly known; because the Chinese are extremely nice in the
> Accounts they keep of them.

That last clause is worth pausing on. The number is credited because the Tang
registered its resident foreigners by religion, and the register was available. A
merchant in the Persian Gulf is citing Chinese household registration as the
warrant for a casualty figure — which is both the best argument for the number and
an unintended confirmation of everything the earlier account said about passes and
counting.

## The trees

Then the detail that explains why a Baghdad reader would care.

> He also cut down the Mulberry Trees, and almost all the Trees of other Kinds; but
> we speak of the Mulberry in particular, because the Chinese carefully cultivate it
> for the sake of its Leaf, wherewith they subsist and propagate their Silk-worms.
> This Devastation is the Cause why Silk has failed, and that the Trade which used to
> be driven with it, in the Countries under the Arabs, is quite stagnated.

A commodity shortage in the caliphate, correctly traced across five thousand miles
of ocean to an orchard.

## The rest of it

Having sacked Canfu he took other cities one after another, the emperor being unable
to stop him; he advanced on the capital, Cumdan — Chang'an — and the emperor made a
confused retreat to a city on the Tibetan frontier. The rebel then went on
demolishing cities and killing most of the inhabitants, intending in the general
butchery to catch every branch of the royal blood, so that nobody should survive to
dispute the empire with him.

That is the Huang Chao rebellion as it reached the Persian Gulf: accurate in
outline, accurate about the flight to Sichuan, and accurate about the ambition.

## What the account is evidence of

Two things the Chinese sources cannot give.

First, the scale of the foreign presence. A hundred and twenty thousand Muslims,
Jews, Christians and Zoroastrians resident in one southern Chinese port for the
purposes of trade is a figure that reframes what the Tang was. That is not a trading
post. It is a quarter of a great city, registered, taxed, and killed in a single
sack.

Second, the interdependence. The Tang collapse is normally told as a Chinese
catastrophe with Chinese causes, which it was. It was also the event that stopped
the silk reaching Baghdad, emptied the Gulf shipping lanes, and ended the direct sea
route between China and the Islamic world for a century and a half.

When an empire that size comes apart, the people who notice first are the ones who
were buying from it.
