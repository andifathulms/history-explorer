The Tang left something unusual: not a claimant, not a language, not a religion,
but a template that three other countries adopted deliberately, in writing,
while it was still running.

## Copied on purpose

Japan sent embassies for two centuries specifically to study the arrangements
and bring them home. What came back was the *ritsuryō* system: the Tang legal
codes, the ministries, the provincial structure, the land allotment, the court
ranks, the calendar. Nara and then Heian were laid out on Chang'an's grid — a
walled rectangle with a palace at the north end and a chequerboard of wards,
built at a scale the Japanese state could not fill and never did.

Korea's Silla and then Goryeo took the ministries and the ranks. Vietnam took
the examinations and was still running them in the nineteenth century, in
Chinese, on the same canon.

None of that was conquest. Japan was never part of the Tang empire, and Silla
fought a war against it. The transmission is a set of foreign governments
deciding that this was how a state should be built, and sending people to copy
the plans.

## What travelled with it

The characters, which all three adapted — *kana* out of cursive forms, later
*hangul* in a deliberately different direction, *chữ Nôm* built out of Chinese
graphs for a language of another family entirely.

Buddhism in its Chinese forms — Chan became Zen and Seon and Thiền, and the
schools that shaped Japanese Buddhism for a millennium were imported from Tang
China by monks who had gone to study there.

And the poetry. Tang verse became the classical standard for literate people
across the whole region, and stayed the standard longer than any political
arrangement it travelled with.

## Why none of it is a claim

Nobody afterwards claimed to *be* the Tang. There is no equivalent of the
Ottoman claim on Rome or the German claim on the Roman empire — no later state
asserting that it was the Tang continuing.

That is a real difference and it is worth naming rather than treating as an
absence. The mandate doctrine made succession exclusive: at any moment exactly
one dynasty holds the mandate, and the previous one has lost it. There is no
room in the theory for a state that is the old empire persisting, because if it
persisted the current one would be illegitimate. Every Chinese dynasty succeeds
the last; none continues it.

So what the Tang exported went out as a design rather than as a title, and the
countries that took it took it as their own rather than as an inheritance. Japan
did not become a Tang successor. It became a state built on the Tang plan, with
its own emperor, and it kept the plan for eleven hundred years after the dynasty
that drew it was gone.

## The one thing that came back

In 1868, when Japan rebuilt its government on European lines, one of the words
it coined for the new institutions was assembled from Chinese characters —
and a good many of those coinages were then borrowed *into* Chinese, in the
other direction, to name things the classical vocabulary had no word for.

The traffic ran one way for a thousand years and then reversed inside a
generation, using the same script the Tang had exported, which is the only kind
of afterlife an institution ever really gets.
