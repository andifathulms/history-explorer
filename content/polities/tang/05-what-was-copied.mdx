Both of this record's other influence counts are zero, and both zeroes need
defending, because this is the polity whose institutions the rest of East Asia
took.

## `descendant_scripts: 0`

Chinese characters reached Japan, Korea and Vietnam, and all three adapted them:
*kana* derived from cursive character forms, *hangul* devised later in a
deliberately different direction, *chữ Nôm* built out of characters for a
language of another family entirely.

Coding rule 3 puts an adaptation on the record of the script that was adapted,
and this site's Han record carries the clerical script — the standardised form
that is the ancestor of everything written in Chinese characters since. The count
goes there. This corpus's Dai Viet record sends chữ Nôm to exactly the same
place, and says so.

## `successor_claims: 0`

Harder, and more deliberate.

The Tang state model was copied wholesale. Japan's Taihō codes are Tang codes.
The capital plans of Nara and Heian are Chang'an's grid. Korea's Silla and Goryeo
took the ministries; Vietnam took the examinations and was still running them in
the nineteenth century. There is nothing else in this corpus that was imitated at
that scale by that many independent states.

None of it is a successor claim. Rule 1 counts a later polity asserting an
earlier one's authority, and adopting somebody's civil service is not claiming to
be them — Japan in particular adopted the apparatus while explicitly refusing the
subordination, and its rulers used a title for themselves that the Chinese court
regarded as an impertinence.

The rulebook was written to make the counts mean the same thing on every record,
including where the answer is inconvenient. This is one of the inconvenient ones:
the most influential polity in East Asian institutional history reads zero in
every influence column but religion.

What the site can say instead is what it always says when the schema will not
hold something — put it in a chapter and name the absence. That is the fourth
time in this pass alone.
