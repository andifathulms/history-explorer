Xuanzong took the throne in 712 meaning to be remembered as a second founder,
and for its first three decades his reign gave every appearance of delivering
on it.

## The reforms

He cut the court's own expenses and led by his own example in the sumptuary
laws he passed - a welcome change to a population that had just spent years
under the tax-farmers Wu's own extended contest for power had left in place.
He overhauled the army through a programme of reviews that doubled as military
games, keeping the force that actually held the empire together engaged and
watched. Astronomy and the systematic observation of natural phenomena, a
science China had pursued since its earliest records, were placed on a new
footing under his patronage. And in a gesture as much about restoring the
dynasty's own intellectual prestige as about religion, he had Confucius
formally proclaimed a prince and given the title King of Literature - an honor
the sage himself, a near-contemporary chronicler notes dryly, would likely
have valued above the secular rank.

## What the reforms did not fix

The empire Xuanzong inherited still had frontiers to defend, and here his own
later record complicates the case for a second Taizong considerably. War with
Tibet ran through most of his reign with no side gaining a lasting advantage:
a Chinese army recovered lost ground in the 720s, a treaty in 730 bound both
sides to stop raiding, Xuanzong broke it himself when Tibet moved against the
small tributary state of Poulin, and the fighting that followed cost tens of
thousands of lives without settling anything the earlier treaty hadn't
already tried to settle. The siege of Chepouching in 749 alone is recorded as
costing more than thirty thousand lives to take.

Elsewhere the record is starker still. An entire Chinese army of thirty
thousand men was destroyed in the Gobi fighting the Khitan and allied Turkic
tribes; a separate campaign against the southwestern kingdom of Nanzhao lost
thirty-three towns in a single year and, three years later, a second army
outright. The reign's total military losses are put, in total, at something
close to two hundred thousand men - a toll that fell on the same frontier
garrisons whose discontent would shortly produce the general who broke the
dynasty from within.

## A verdict earned rather than assumed

Later tradition remembers Xuanzong's early reign, the Kaiyuan era, as the
high point of the whole dynasty, and by the measure of court culture and
domestic reform that reputation has real substance behind it. But the same
reign that produced the reforms also produced the frontier failures that fed
directly into what followed: a court confident enough in its own greatness to
overlook how thin the army actually protecting it had become, and a general
named An Lushan who was about to demonstrate exactly how thin.
