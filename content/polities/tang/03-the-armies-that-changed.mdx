This record's `military_basis` carries two values, `conscript` and
`standing-professional`, and the change between them is the political history of
the dynasty.

The Tang began with the *fubing*: militia units of farmer-soldiers who held land
under the equal-field allotment system, served in rotation at the capital or the
frontier, provided their own kit, and went home. It cost the state almost
nothing, and it was designed so that no commander had a permanent army.

By about 740 it had stopped working. The equal-field system it rested on had
eroded the way such systems do — land accumulated, registers went stale,
allotments were not there to be reallocated — and rotating farmers to the far
side of the Tarim basin for a season was not a way to hold a frontier against
Tibet and the Türks anyway.

What replaced it was ten regional commands on the frontier, each under a
*jiedushi*, with long-service paid troops, its own supply, and eventually its own
revenue.

## The name of the second chapter

An Lushan held three of those commands at once and about a third of the empire's
soldiers. In 755 he marched on the capitals.

This record's second chapter is about what the rebellion did, and the short
version is that the court survived it by granting the commanders who put it down
the same autonomy the rebel had — so the last hundred and fifty years of the Tang
are a central government negotiating with provinces that remit what they choose.

The point of putting it in the institution field rather than only in prose is
that the same transition, in the same field, appears on this corpus's Han record
seven hundred years earlier: a militia resting on smallholding, the land system
eroding, professional regional armies replacing it, one of their commanders
taking the capital. Two records, two values in one field, one mechanism.

Both are coded that way now. Before this pass neither of them had an institutions
block at all.
