In 751 an Abbasid army and a Tang army met on the Talas river in what is now
Kyrgyzstan. The Tang force was defeated, partly because its Karluk allies changed
sides during the battle.

Its importance is usually overstated and occasionally dismissed. It did not
decide the fate of Central Asia — the An Lushan rebellion four years later did
that, forcing the Tang to withdraw its western garrisons permanently to deal with
a civil war that may have been the most destructive in world history to that
point. Talas is the marker rather than the cause.

What it does mark is the only direct contact between the two largest states on
earth. Tang China at Xuanzong's height and the caliphate under the early
Abbasids were comparable in extent, in revenue and in the sophistication of their
administration, and their frontiers touched exactly once, in a battle neither
government treated as important. The tradition that Chinese papermakers captured
at Talas brought paper to Samarkand and thence to Baghdad and Europe is a good
story of uncertain support; paper does appear in the Islamic world at about this
time, and Baghdad had a paper mill by the 790s.

The empire that fought at Talas was the most cosmopolitan in Chinese history —
Sogdian merchants, Turkish generals, Persian refugees from the Sasanian collapse,
Nestorian and Manichaean and Zoroastrian communities in Chang'an. An Lushan, who
nearly destroyed it, was a general of Sogdian and Turkic parentage.

## What the Tang was actually doing out there

The battle is easier to place if the western position is described rather than
assumed.

By the 740s the Tang held a line of garrison commands — the Four Garrisons of
Anxi — at Kucha, Khotan, Kashgar and Karashahr, with a protectorate over the
Sogdian city-states beyond the Pamirs and clients as far as the Oxus. This was not
frontier defence. It was an imperial system a thousand miles beyond the Jade Gate,
administered by Chinese officials, garrisoned by professional soldiers on long
service, and supplied along a road of post stations.

It existed because of the Türk empires and then the Tibetans. Whoever held the
Tarim held the flank of the route to the west and the approaches to Gansu, and the
Tang had spent a century taking it precisely so that nobody else would.

The commander at Talas, Gao Xianzhi, was a Korean by descent — which is worth
noting, because it is entirely characteristic of how that army was officered.

## What the defeat cost, and when

Not the Tarim. The Tang went on holding the Four Garrisons after Talas.

What took them was An Lushan four years later, when the professional armies of the
west were recalled to fight the civil war and never came back, and the Tibetan
empire moved into the vacuum and took Chang'an itself in 763. The garrisons in the
Tarim were left without relief or orders and held out, in some cases, for decades —
a detail known because their coinage and their dated documents kept being issued
long after the road home was closed.

That is why Talas is a marker rather than a cause. The Chinese position in Central
Asia was not lost to the Arabs in a battle. It was withdrawn to deal with a general
of Sogdian and Turkic parentage who had been given three army groups on the northern
frontier, and it was never reinstated.
