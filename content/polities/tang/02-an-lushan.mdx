An Lushan was a frontier general of Sogdian and Turkic parentage who commanded
three of the empire's northern military regions and perhaps a third of its army.
He revolted in 755, took Luoyang and then Chang'an, and drove the emperor
Xuanzong into flight to Sichuan. The rebellion took eight years to suppress and
required Uyghur cavalry to do it.

The census figures before and after show a fall of about two thirds. Almost
nobody now reads that as the death toll — the registration system collapsed
along with the state's ability to run it, and uncounted is not dead — but the
disruption was real and prolonged, and the argument about how much of the drop
was mortality is unsettled.

What is not disputed is the political consequence. To fight the rebellion the
court granted regional commanders control over their own taxation and troops,
and never took it back. The second half of the Tang is a central government
negotiating with semi-autonomous provinces that remit what they choose. The
western garrisons were withdrawn permanently and the Tarim basin passed to the
Tibetans on this site's inner-asia page.

The comparison worth drawing is with the Abbasid fourth fitna fifty years later,
which this site's Abbasid page describes: two halves of an empire fighting with
their own provincial armies, after which the capital's garrison was never trusted
again. Both empires survived their civil war and neither recovered the authority
it had before.
