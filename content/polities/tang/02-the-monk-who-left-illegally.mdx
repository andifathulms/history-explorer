In 629 a Chinese monk left the empire without permission — the frontier was
closed, and the prohibition on private travel west was in force — and came back
sixteen years later with a description of everything between Chang'an and
Bengal. The emperor, who had spent the intervening years conquering a good deal
of it, received him personally.

Xuanzang went for scriptures. What he wrote is a gazetteer.

## The road as he found it

The entries have a shape: distance, direction, terrain, the circuit of the
capital in *li*, the soil, the climate, the character of the people, the number
of monasteries and monks. It is the register of a man who has walked it.

Going north-west from Aksu, across a stony desert, comes the Ice Mountain, the
northern plateau of the Pamirs.

> Both hills and valleys are filled with snowpiles, and it freezes both in
> spring and summer; if it should thaw for a time, the ice soon forms again. The
> roads are steep and dangerous, the cold wind is extremely biting.

Travellers on that road, he adds, should not wear red garments or carry
loud-sounding gourds: the least forgetfulness entails certain misfortune, a
violent wind rises with flying sand and gravel, and those who meet it sink from
exhaustion and are almost sure to die.

The advice is superstition. The wind is not.

## The khan's deer

At the great lake beyond — Issyk-Kul — he records that the khan of the Türks
comes every year to escape the heat, and that the place is full of deer wearing
bells and collars, tame and unafraid of people, which the khan is fond of and
has forbidden anyone to kill on pain of death without remission.

That is the western Türk khaganate at its height, described by a passing monk
in one paragraph about a deer park.

## Three hundred houses at Talas

The entry that repays the most reading is the shortest.

Talas is a town eight or nine *li* round where merchants from all parts live
with the local people. Ten *li* to the south, Xuanzang writes, is a little
deserted town that once had about three hundred houses, occupied by people of
China. They had been carried off violently by the Türks; afterwards they
gathered a number of their countrymen and occupied the place in common.

> Their clothes being worn out, they adopted the Turkish mode of dress, but they
> have preserved their own native language and customs.

A colony of Chinese captives on the Talas river, keeping the language and losing
the clothes, in about 630. A hundred and twenty years later a Tang army and an
Abbasid army fought a battle at the same river.

## What the empire did with him

He came back in 645, at the point when Tang armies had destroyed the eastern
Türk khaganate, taken Turfan, and were pushing into the Tarim.

Taizong received him, and the sources agree on what he wanted: not the sutras.
He wanted the countries — their rulers, their roads, their distances, their
strength — and he offered Xuanzang a post in the government, which was
declined. What he got instead was the *Record of the Western Regions*,
presented to the throne in 646, compiled at imperial request.

That is the transaction worth noticing. A monk broke the law to leave, and the
state that had banned his journey commissioned its results. The most useful
survey of Central Asia and India produced anywhere in the seventh century was
made by a man on a religious errand and written up for a war ministry.
