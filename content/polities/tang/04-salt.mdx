The An Lushan rebellion destroyed the tax registers. A state that assessed
households by head and by allotted land, and could no longer say who lived where
or held what, had no way to collect.

What kept the Tang alive for a hundred and fifty years afterwards was salt.

From 758 the state bought salt at the production sites, resold it to licensed
merchants at a large markup, and let them distribute it. It required no
registers, no census and no control of the countryside — only control of a few
hundred coastal pans and inland wells. By the 780s that one indirect tax was
providing more than half of central government revenue.

This corpus's Han record's third chapter is about the same commodity in the same
country nine hundred years earlier, and about the court debate of 81 BC over
whether a state should be in that business. The Tang did not reopen the argument.
It reopened the monopoly, and the reason is on this page: it was the only tax a
government that had lost its records could still assess.

## Two-tax

The other half of the answer is the reform of 780, which is this record's third
turning point. Yang Yan abandoned assessment by head and by allotment and taxed
households twice a year on what they actually held, in cash.

It is one of very few fiscal reforms anywhere on this site that outlived the
dynasty that made it. In outline that is how China was taxed until the sixteenth
century — until, in fact, the reform this corpus's Ming record carries as its
third turning point, which consolidated the descendants of Yang Yan's two taxes
into one payment in silver.

## And Huang Chao

`revenue_basis` here reads `land-tax`, `poll-tax`, `monopoly`, and the last
turning point on the record is a man who was both of the things the first and
third values produced: a failed examination candidate and a salt smuggler.

Huang Chao's rising crossed the empire twice, sacked Guangzhou, and took both
capitals. A monopoly high enough to fund a state is high enough to make smuggling
worth an army, and the examination system that this corpus's Han record's second
chapter describes the origins of produced, every three years, a cohort of
literate men who had been told the empire was open to talent and had then been
failed.

The dynasty survived him by twenty-three years and was destroyed by the general
who put him down.
