The Tang is the state whose institutions the rest of East Asia took, and by the
usual measures of influence it scores nothing. Both halves of that are worth
setting out, because the reasons are different.

## The script

Chinese characters reached Japan, Korea and Vietnam, and all three adapted them:
*kana* derived from cursive character forms, *hangul* devised later in a
deliberately different direction, *chữ Nôm* built out of characters for a
language of another family entirely.

None of that is a Tang creation. The writing system those countries adapted is
the clerical script the Han standardised, which is the ancestor of everything
written in Chinese characters since. The Tang transmitted it; it did not make
it.

## The claim nobody made

Harder, and more interesting.

The Tang state model was copied wholesale. Japan's Taihō codes are Tang codes.
The capital plans of Nara and Heian are Chang'an's grid. Korea's Silla and Goryeo
took the ministries; Vietnam took the examinations and was still running them in
the nineteenth century. Very few states anywhere have been imitated at that scale
by that many independent governments.

And not one of them claimed to be the Tang. Adopting somebody's civil service is
not claiming to be them: Japan in particular took the apparatus while explicitly
refusing the subordination, and its rulers used a title for themselves that the
Chinese court regarded as an impertinence.

That is the shape of Chinese influence generally, and it is why it is so easy to
undercount. It travelled as a design that other people made their own, rather
than as a title somebody inherited — and a design, unlike a title, leaves no
claimant behind to be counted.
