The Tang was finished by a failed examination candidate with a salt licence.

## Who he was

Huang Chao came from a family of salt merchants — which after the reforms of the
760s meant a family in the smuggling business, because the state monopoly had
made salt the most heavily taxed commodity in the empire and smuggling the most
profitable trade in it. He sat the examinations, repeatedly, and failed.

That combination is the dynasty's two great structural problems in one
biography. The salt monopoly had become the state's main revenue and had
created an armed criminal economy underneath it. The examinations had become the
only respectable route to office and were producing far more qualified
candidates than there were posts, in a system where the great families still
took most of what there was.

## What he did

He joined an existing rising in 875 and within a few years was leading it. What
followed was not a regional revolt. He marched the length of the empire twice —
south to Guangzhou, where his army sacked the port and, on the report of an
Arabic source, massacred a very large foreign merchant community; then north
again, taking Luoyang and, in 880, Chang'an.

The emperor fled to Sichuan, as an emperor had fled in 756 and another would in
881. Huang Chao proclaimed his own dynasty and held the capital for two years.

He was driven out by an alliance of provincial commanders and a Shatuo Turkic
cavalry force under Li Keyong, and was killed in 884.

## What was destroyed

The capital. Chang'an had been sacked in 763 by the Tibetans, in 881 by Huang
Chao, and in 883 by the armies retaking it; the poet Wei Zhuang's *Lament of the
Lady of Qin* describes the last of these, and the city never recovered its
position. It was formally abandoned as a capital in 904 and has never been one
since.

The southern trade. Guangzhou's foreign merchant community was the eastern
terminus of the Indian Ocean network, and the sack of 879 is followed in the
Arabic sources by a redirection of that trade to the Malay ports, where
merchants met Chinese junks instead of sailing to China themselves.

And the aristocracy. The great clans of the north-west that had supplied Tang
officialdom for two centuries — the families whose genealogies the dynasty had
compiled and ranked — were destroyed in the fighting and the massacres of the
880s and 890s as a class, not as individuals. They do not reappear.

## 907

The rest is bookkeeping. Zhu Wen was one of Huang Chao's generals who defected
to the imperial side, was rewarded with a governorship, and spent the 890s
eliminating his rivals. In 903 he massacred the eunuchs. In 904 he moved the
emperor to Luoyang and had him murdered. In 907 he deposed the boy who had
replaced him and took the throne.

The dynasty had been governed by whoever held the emperor for about twenty
years by then, which is the same condition the Han was in for the thirty years
before 220 — and it ended the same way, with an abdication ceremony, for the
same reason. The name was worth something to the man taking it.

What followed was fifty-three years of five dynasties in the north and ten
kingdoms in the south, and then a reunification under the Song that put the
military governors permanently under civil officials, because everyone had just
watched what happened when it was the other way round.
