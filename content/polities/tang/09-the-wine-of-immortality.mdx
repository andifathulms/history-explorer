By 805, when Xianzong came to the throne, the shape of the second half of the
dynasty was already set: a court negotiating with provinces that answered to
their own military governors rather than to Chang'an. Xianzong's fifteen-year
reign is remembered as the one real attempt to reverse that arrangement rather
than simply manage it.

## Putting down two governors

Within his first year, the governor of Sichuan, Liu Pi, tested how far the
new emperor's patience would stretch, expanding his own authority well past
what the court had tolerated from his predecessors. Xianzong tried
accommodation first, overlooking conduct he might reasonably have punished -
and found, as rulers usually do with that approach, that overlooking it only
encouraged more of it. He sent an army instead. Liu Pi's rebellion collapsed,
and the province that had been drifting out of the court's reach was
reattached to it.

A larger test followed in Shandong, where the governor Li Shidao held out with
something close to open independence for years. When the imperial army
finally moved against him, his own following judged the cause lost before a
battle was even fought and refused to defend him; Li Shidao and his two sons
were taken from their own palace and executed in the public square. It was
the clearest demonstration in a generation that a rebellious jiedushi could
still be broken by force rather than merely tolerated - and for a court that
had spent thirty years negotiating with exactly this kind of governor since An
Lushan, that was no small thing to prove.

## The emperor's own weaknesses

Xianzong is remembered, fairly, as well-meaning rather than brilliant, and his
own court showed the same faults that had weakened every Tang emperor since
Xuanzong's decline: an excessive trust in the palace eunuchs, whom he
protected even when his own advisers warned him what that trust would cost his
successors, and a genuine credulity about Buddhist and Daoist claims to
prolong life through alchemical preparation. The second weakness killed him.
In 820, after an overdose of what the court called the "wine of immortality"
left him in a fury that cost several eunuchs their lives, the eunuch Chen
Hongzhi finished what the elixir itself had started, poisoning the same cup
Xianzong kept returning to.

## What the restoration actually restored

Xianzong's reign did not undo the settlement An Lushan's rebellion had forced
on the dynasty - the great provincial commands, largely self-taxing and
self-arming, remained the basic shape of Tang government for the rest of its
existence. What it proved was narrower and, in its own way, more useful: that
a court willing to commit force decisively, rather than negotiate
indefinitely, could still bring an individual rebellious governor back under
its own authority. It was a lesson his own less capable successors would
prove no better able to apply than his own eunuchs were to be trusted.
