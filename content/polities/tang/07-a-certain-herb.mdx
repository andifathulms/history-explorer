An Arab merchant wrote an account of China around 851, and it is the earliest
description of the Tang state by somebody from outside it. It is also the first
description of tea in any language other than Chinese.

## The herb

The emperor, he says, reserves to himself the revenues of the salt mines, and of

> a certain Herb which they drink with hot Water, and of which great Quantities
> are sold in all the Cities, to the amount of great Sums. They call it Sah, and it
> is a Shrub more bushy than the Pomegranate-tree, and of a more taking Smell, but
> it has a kind of Bitterness with it. Their way is to boil Water, which they pour
> upon this Leaf, and this Drink cures all sorts of Diseases.

That is tea, in 851, described by a man who has clearly drunk it and has no word
for it. Everything lodged in the treasury, he adds, comes from the poll tax and the
duties on salt and on this leaf — which is an accurate statement of late Tang public
finance, arrived at by a foreigner from what he could see of the customs houses.

## What he noticed about the taxes

There is no tax on land. There is a tax on men, levied according to their condition
and capacity. Arabs and other foreigners in the country are taxed in proportion to
their substance.

He puts fifty thousand dinars a day into the treasury from the poll tax at Canton
alone — *although this City be none of the largest in China*, which is the detail
that shows he understood the scale of what he was looking at.

## The granaries

> When any Dearth makes Necessaries very dear, then does the King open his
> Storehouses, and sell all Sorts of Provisions much cheaper than they are to be had
> at Market; and hence no Dearth is of any long Continuance among the Chinese.

An ever-normal granary system, working, described by a merchant from a part of the
world that had nothing of the kind. He does not editorialise; he states it as a fact
about how prices behave in China.

## The bell on the string

The most remarkable thing he records is a machine for petition.

In each city a small bell hangs on the wall above the governor's head. A string runs
from it for about three miles, crossing the highway, so that anybody can reach it.
When the string is pulled the bell strikes over the governor's head, and he orders
the person who has demanded justice to be brought in, and the complainant states his
case in person and the wrongs he suffers.

*And the same Practice is in use throughout all the other Provinces.*

Whether every city in Tang China really had a three-mile bell-rope is not the point.
What the merchant is recording is that he was told this, everywhere, as the normal
arrangement — a state that advertised to foreigners that any subject could compel
the attention of the governor.

## The passes

And against that, the other half of the same system.

To travel from one place to another a man must carry two passes: one from the
governor, which permits him to set out and records his name; and one from the
eunuch, the emperor's own officer in the province. Both are inspected on the road.

A state that will hear any man's complaint and will not let any man move without
papers. The merchant reports both without finding them contradictory, and they are
not: they are two expressions of the same thing, which is a government that intends
to know where everybody is.

## Why the account matters

Because it is outside evidence for what the Tang was like at ground level, from a
man with no interest in flattering it and no access to its official record.

The dynastic histories describe the salt monopoly, the granaries, the household
registration and the travel permits, and a sceptical reader may reasonably wonder
how much of that was real and how much was the paperwork of an ambitious
bureaucracy. Here is a Persian Gulf trader, a century before the dynasty ended,
who had gone there to buy and sell, reporting that the granaries opened, that the
passes were demanded, that the tax was on heads rather than land, and that the
government was making a fortune out of a bitter leaf infused in hot water.
