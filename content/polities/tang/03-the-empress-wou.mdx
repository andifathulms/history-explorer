For more than forty years, the person who actually ruled Tang China was a
woman the dynasty's own historians could never quite forgive for it.

## From convent to consort

Wu had been one of the previous emperor Taizong's own concubines, and after his
death had retired, as custom required, into a Buddhist convent. His son and
successor Gaozong brought her back to court and, in 655, deposed his own
reigning empress to install her in the position instead - over the strenuous
objection of ministers who saw exactly where the precedent led. It went
further than they feared. Wu is recorded disposing of the two women she saw as
rivals for Gaozong's favour by having their hands and feet cut off and their
bodies thrown into a wine vat; whatever the literal truth of the detail, it is
the story contemporaries told about how she cleared her own path.

She did not stop at the harem. Ministers hostile to her interests were removed
from office and, in the accounts that survive, disposed of by the sword or the
poisoned cup; her own son was named heir apparent; and Gaozong, whom the
record credits with real capacity but no appetite for the daily labour of the
throne, found her quick grasp of state business so useful that he let her
handle more and more of it, until - in substance, years before it was ever
formalised - she was ruling and he was reigning.

## A dynasty renamed

Gaozong died in 683 after a nominal reign of thirty-three years. Wu had his
eldest son proclaimed emperor and deposed him within days, installing a
second son as a figurehead while she conducted the government openly in her
own name - receiving petitions, filling offices, building ancestral temples to
her own family, and eventually declaring a new dynasty altogether, breaking
the fiction of a puppet Tang emperor she had kept up for years. No woman had
ruled China in her own right before. She built temples, wore the regalia
reserved for an emperor, and offered the sacrifices only an emperor was
supposed to offer.

The opposition this provoked was real, and she met it without hesitation.
Several risings and assassination plots against her all failed, and her own
solution to the flood of accusations her spies generated was characteristically
direct: an edict punishing false accusers with death, applied so rigorously
that on one occasion eight hundred and fifty of a thousand charges brought
before her were found to be fabricated - and their authors executed for it.
Executions of the great nobles she distrusted were carried out publicly in the
streets of the capital, occasions the chronicler compares, in their effect on
the watching city, to the public scaffolds of Tower Hill.

## Governing the case for her own rule

None of this was the whole of her reign. She fought Tibet to a standstill on
the western frontier, recovering ground lost earlier in the dynasty; she made
and then had to fight a Turkic khan she had tried to buy as an ally, after he
turned the very arms she had sent him against her own borders; and she worked,
deliberately, to build the positive case for her own legitimacy alongside the
punitive one - commissioning books celebrating her administration, and
instructing the empire's religious establishment to preach her own
indispensability to the state's welfare. It is not the record of an idle
usurper. Even the chronicler most inclined to condemn her, the record
concedes, cannot show her failing any test ordinarily demanded of a competent
sovereign.

## The fall

In 704, in her eightieth year, illness kept her from her ministers for months
at a stretch. Her enemies took the opening: a faction struck down her closest
relatives and presented themselves at the palace in force. She had no army
left to resist them with, and handed over the imperial seal with what the
hostile chronicler himself concedes was real dignity. She died the following
year. The son she had deposed decades earlier was restored to the throne, and
the Tang dynasty she had displaced resumed its own name - though not, as the
next chapters show, its own competence.
