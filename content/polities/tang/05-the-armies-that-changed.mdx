The Tang changed how it raised soldiers once, in the middle of its life, and the
change is the political history of the dynasty.

It began with the *fubing*: militia units of farmer-soldiers who held land under
the equal-field allotment system, served in rotation at the capital or on the
frontier, provided their own kit, and went home. It cost the state almost
nothing. It was also designed so that no commander ever had a permanent army.

By about 740 it had stopped working. The equal-field system underneath it had
eroded the way such systems do — land accumulated, registers went stale,
allotments were not there to be reallocated — and in any case rotating farmers to
the far side of the Tarim basin for a season was not a way to hold a frontier
against Tibet and the Türks.

What replaced it was ten regional commands on the frontier, each under a
*jiedushi*, with long-service paid troops, its own supply, and eventually its own
revenue.

## 755

An Lushan held three of those commands at once, and about a third of the empire's
soldiers. In 755 he marched on the capitals.

The court survived the rebellion by granting the commanders who put it down the
same autonomy the rebel had enjoyed, so the last hundred and fifty years of the
Tang are a central government negotiating with provinces that remitted what they
chose.

## The same thing, seven hundred years earlier

The Han had done all of this before.

A militia resting on smallholding. The land system eroding underneath it.
Professional regional armies raised to replace it. And then one of their
commanders taking the capital.

Two dynasties, seven centuries apart, on the same ground, running the same
sequence in the same order. Whatever else the Tang collapse was, it was not
unforeseeable — the precedent was in the histories the court's own officials had
been trained on.
