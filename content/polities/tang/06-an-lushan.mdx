An Lushan was a frontier general of Sogdian and Turkic parentage who commanded
three of the empire's northern military regions and perhaps a third of its army.
He revolted in 755, took Luoyang and then Chang'an, and drove the emperor
Xuanzong into flight to Sichuan. The rebellion took eight years to suppress and
required Uyghur cavalry to do it.

The census figures before and after show a fall of about two thirds. Almost
nobody now reads that as the death toll — the registration system collapsed
along with the state's ability to run it, and uncounted is not dead — but the
disruption was real and prolonged, and the argument about how much of the drop
was mortality is unsettled.

What is not disputed is the political consequence. To fight the rebellion the
court granted regional commanders control over their own taxation and troops,
and never took it back. The second half of the Tang is a central government
negotiating with semi-autonomous provinces that remit what they choose. The
western garrisons were withdrawn permanently and the Tarim basin passed to the
Tibetan empire.

The comparison worth drawing is with the Abbasid civil war of 811 to 813, fifty
years later: two halves of an empire fighting with their own provincial armies,
after which the capital's garrison was never trusted again. Both empires
survived their civil war and neither recovered the authority it had before.

## Why a frontier general had that much army

Because of a reform that looked sensible at the time.

The Tang had begun with the *fubing* system — militia soldiers who held land in
return for service, mustered in rotation, living at home between turns. It worked
when campaigns were short and near. It could not garrison the Tarim basin, the
Korean frontier and the Ordos simultaneously, because a farmer cannot be sent three
thousand kilometres for three years and still be a farmer.

So from the 710s the militia was replaced on the frontiers by long-service
professionals under permanent regional commands, the *jiedushi*, each with its own
recruitment, its own supply and eventually its own revenues. By the 740s nine of
these commands held the great majority of the empire's effective troops, and the
interior of China was garrisoned by almost nobody.

An Lushan held three of the nine.

This is the standing problem of every empire with a long land frontier, and the
number of solutions is small. Rome ended with frontier armies that made emperors.
The Abbasids imported slave soldiers and were governed by them within two
generations. The Tang professionalised its frontier and lost control of it in
forty years.

## What eight years of it did

The court survived by borrowing. Uyghur cavalry was hired to retake the capitals,
and was paid in silk, in the right to plunder Luoyang, and in a trade in horses at
prices the Uyghurs set — an obligation that bled the treasury for a century and
which the Tang could not get out of because it could not fight the people it owed.

Rebel commanders who surrendered were confirmed in their commands rather than
punished, because there was no force available to punish them with. Those
commands became hereditary. Three of the north-eastern provinces were in practice
independent from 763 onward, sending nothing and obeying nothing, and the dynasty
went on formally appointing the men who already held them for another hundred and
fifty years.

The An Lushan rebellion did not destroy the Tang. It converted it into a different
kind of state: a court with a rich southern tax base, a salt monopoly, a diplomatic
relationship with its own provinces, and no army of its own worth the name.
