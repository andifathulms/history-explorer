Wuzong took the throne in 840 by the same method the eunuchs had used to make
and unmake emperors for a generation - they passed over his predecessor's own
sons and installed him instead, expecting a pliable client. They got, in this
one instance, something closer to the opposite.

<Figure id="tg-wuzong" />

## An able ruler by the standard of his own age

Wuzong protected the frontiers with an energy the court had not shown in
years, defeated a persistent Tangut tribal challenge in Shaanxi through his
own lieutenants, and granted asylum within his dominions to a displaced people
from Central Asia rather than turning them away. He was, by his contemporaries'
own account, a genuine huntsman and a man given to military exercise rather
than to court ceremony - closer in temperament to the dynasty's early rulers
than to the figures who had occupied the throne since Xianzong's death twenty
years before.

## The edict

It was this same practical, unsentimental cast of mind that produced the
reign's defining act. Chinese Buddhism by 845 supported an establishment on a
scale the state had never systematically measured or taxed - close to four
thousand monasteries and something like a quarter of a million monks and nuns,
by the count later chroniclers settled on, all of it standing outside the
ordinary registers of land and labour the rest of the population was taxed
and conscripted through. Wuzong's own reasoning, as the record presents it,
was not theological. A monk was, in his own blunt phrase, an able-bodied man
living in idleness on the credulity of his neighbours, and it took no
particular flaw in the faith itself to explain why so many had found their way
into a monastery's comfortable retirement. In 845 he ordered the monks and
nuns of the empire's religious houses to leave their orders and return to
their own families - and, with them, to the tax rolls and the corvée labour
their monastic status had shielded them from.

## What it did and did not undo

The suppression was a fiscal and administrative act dressed in religious
language, and it worked on its own narrow terms: land and labour that had
been outside the state's reach for generations came back within it in a
single year, at exactly the moment a court starved of revenue by decades of
provincial autonomy needed every registered household it could find. It did
nothing to the faith itself outside China's own borders - Buddhism had
already spread through Japan and Korea by way of Chinese teaching and
scripture decades earlier, and no domestic edict in Chang'an could touch what
had already propagated abroad.

<Figure id="tg-foguang" />

Wuzong did not live to manage what he had unsettled. He died the following
year, his reign judged by the record itself to have been too brief for the
good it might otherwise have done, and the eunuchs who had expected a
compliant nominee in 840 went back to the same kingmaking they had practiced
before him - proof, if any more were needed, that one capable reign was not
enough to break a pattern that had already outlasted three of them.
