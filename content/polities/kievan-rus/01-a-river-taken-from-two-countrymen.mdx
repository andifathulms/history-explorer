The Book of Annals, the medieval compilation that is the main narrative
source for this period, tells a founding story that starts with an
invitation rather than a conquest. Around 862, it says, the Slavic and
Finnic peoples of the north had fallen into such disorder fighting among
themselves that they sent across the sea for a Varangian chieftain named
Riurik to come and rule them, and Riurik settled at Novgorod with his
brothers. Whether a real Riurik stands behind the story, or whether it is
a founding legend built around a genuine Varangian presence in the region,
is disputed among historians; what happened next is better attested.

<Figure id="kr-varangians" />

## Two Varangians already held the prize

Riurik died leaving an infant son, and it was Oleg, ruling as regent, who
made the decision that actually founded this Kyiv-based polity. Two other
Varangians, Askold and Dir, had already made themselves masters
of Kyiv, far to the south on the Dnieper, independently of Riurik's own
authority. Around 878 Oleg moved south, took the city from them, and
shifted the centre of the whole enterprise there - trading Novgorod's
access to the Baltic for a capital on the river road that ran all the way
to the Black Sea and Byzantium.

<Figure id="kr-askold" />

## What the river was for

The whole point of the position was the water. Furs, honey, wax and
slaves moved down the Dnieper toward Byzantine markets; silver and
manufactured luxuries moved back up it. Oleg spent much of his reign
extending control over the Slavic tribes along that route and drawing
them into paying tribute to Kyiv rather than to the Khazar khaganate, whose
own reach had previously extended into the same territory. A war against
Magyar raiders passing through the region around 890 was, by the
chronicle's own account, indecisive - a reminder that the new Kyiv-based
polity was one power on a contested frontier rather than an unchallenged
one.

## An old man's death, and a house that would rule for centuries

Oleg died in 913, according to the chronicle tradition, and was succeeded
by Riurik's own son. Every prince who would later hold any part of this
loose federation, for the whole three and a half centuries down to the
Mongol conquest, claimed descent from that same house - a single
genealogical thread run through a political structure that would prove,
in practice, to be anything but single.
