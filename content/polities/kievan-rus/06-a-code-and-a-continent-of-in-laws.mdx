Iaroslav did not come to Kyiv's throne peacefully. Vladimir's death in
1015 set off a war among his sons in which one brother, Sviatopolk,
seized power first and was accused by later chroniclers of murdering
several of his own siblings to secure it. Iaroslav, ruling from Novgorod,
was the one brother who fought back, and it took the better part of a
decade of intermittent civil war - with both sides hiring outside troops,
Iaroslav's Varangians against Sviatopolk's Pecheneg allies - before he
held Kyiv without a serious rival.

<Figure id="kr-sophia" />

## What he did with an uncontested throne

Once secure, Iaroslav governed for close to two decades in relative peace,
and used it to give Rus' its first written body of law: the Russkaia
Pravda. It set compensation payments for injury and death by precise rank
and circumstance, regulated commercial disputes, and gave Rus' a legal
code of its own generations before many of the polities that would later
outlast it had anything comparable. His sons expanded it further after
his death, adding provisions that reflect a more developed commercial
economy than his own reign had known.

<Figure id="kr-daughters" />

## Constantinople's princesses, in reverse

The most striking measure of the kingdom's standing under Iaroslav is
who wanted to marry into it. Where his father Vladimir had needed an army
to win a single Byzantine bride, Iaroslav's own daughters were sought by
ruling houses across Europe on ordinary diplomatic terms. Elizabeth
married King Sweyn II of Denmark. Anna, in 1051, married Henry I of
France and was crowned queen - the marriage arranged specifically because
the French court, needing an heir after Henry's first wife died childless,
concluded that the prince of Kyiv's own family was worth approaching.
Widowed in 1060, Anna served as a power behind her young son Philip I's
regency and signed at least one surviving document "Anna Regina" in
Slavic characters, a queen of France still writing in the alphabet Rus'
had taken from Bulgaria seventy years before.

## What the marriages actually measured

None of this reflected Kyiv projecting power abroad in any military
sense. It reflected the opposite: a kingdom secure and prosperous enough,
under one uncontested ruler, that distant courts thought a marriage tie to
it worth having on its own terms. That kind of reputation depends entirely
on the stability that produces it, which is exactly what Iaroslav's own
death would remove. The division of authority he arranged among his
surviving sons was meant to preserve what he had built. It became, within
a generation, the mechanism that undid it.
