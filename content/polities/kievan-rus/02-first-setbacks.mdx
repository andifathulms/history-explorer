Oleg had opened the Dnieper trade route and beaten favorable terms out
of Constantinople twice. His successor Igor spent three decades finding
out how much harder it was to hold what Oleg had won than to win it -
and died proving the lesson to his own tribute-payers in the worst
possible way.

## Two disasters and a treaty

Igor's own reputation with the chroniclers was never generous - they
call him greedy where they call Oleg shrewd - and his major campaigns
explain why. In 941 he sailed against Byzantine Anatolia, plundering
Bithynia and Paphlagonia as far as Nicomedia before the Byzantine fleet
caught his returning ships and burned them with Greek fire. Two years
later a second Rus' force, quite possibly led by Igor's own son, sacked
the Caucasian city of Bardaa in Azerbaijan and held it for months
before a Muslim army wore it down; the commander was killed retreating
to the ships.

Undeterred, Igor mounted a third expedition in 944, hiring Varangians
from Scandinavia and Pecheneg cavalry to reinforce his own druzhina.
This time Constantinople chose not to fight: Byzantine envoys met him
on the Danube with gifts and a peace offer, which Igor's own council -
still wary of Greek fire - voted to accept. The resulting treaty of 945
confirmed and expanded the commercial privileges Oleg had won, though
it dropped the earlier exemption from customs duties, a sign of how
much less leverage a failed campaign left him with at the negotiating
table.

## The double collection

The treaty brought Igor prestige but not enough plunder to pay off the
Varangians and Pechenegs he had hired to win it. He settled the
Varangian commander Sveneld's claim by granting him the right to
collect tribute from two subject tribes directly - and his own
druzhina, watching Sveneld's retainers grow rich on it, complained
openly: "the servants of Sveneld are adorned with weapons and fine
raiment, but we are naked." Igor led them to collect tribute from the
Drevlyans himself, took what Sveneld's collection had already covered,
and then - against his own retinue's advice - went back a second time
with only a handful of men to round out his personal share.

The Drevlyans had had enough. Their prince Mal put it to his people in
a proverb the chronicle preserved: "if a wolf comes among the sheep, he
will take away the whole flock one by one, unless he be killed." They
attacked Igor's small escort and killed them all. It was 945, and the
Rurikid house's first ruler to die by the hand of his own subjects left
behind a widow and an infant son to answer for it.
