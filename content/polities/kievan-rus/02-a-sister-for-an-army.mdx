Vladimir did not become a Christian because a missionary convinced him.
He became one because the Byzantine emperor Basil II was, in the winter of
987-88, in genuine danger of losing his throne to a rebel general who had
seized most of Anatolia and was approaching Constantinople itself, and
Basil needed an army Vladimir alone could supply on short notice.

## What was actually being traded

Byzantine envoys reached Kyiv in January or February 988 to ask for six
thousand Varangian troops. What they offered in return was extraordinary
by the standards of Byzantine court protocol: the hand of Princess Anna,
Basil's own sister, in marriage. Anna was "porphyrogenete" - born in the
imperial palace to a reigning emperor - and a Byzantine court that had,
within living memory, refused a similar request from the German emperor's
own son had never given a woman of that rank to a foreign ruler. Vladimir
accepted, and his one obligation in return was to be baptised.

## The soldiers arrived. The bride did not, at first.

Vladimir kept his side of the bargain in full: six thousand Varangian
troops reached Constantinople that summer, tipped the military balance
against the rebellion, and helped destroy the rebel army the following
spring. Vladimir was baptised, apparently in February 988, and took the
Christian name Basil in honour of the emperor whose throne he had just
saved.

Basil, once the danger had passed, found himself much less eager to
actually send his own sister north. Vladimir's answer was to attack the
Byzantine possessions in Crimea directly, applying exactly the kind of
pressure that finally produced the marriage the treaty had promised -
and with it, most historians believe, the beginnings of an organised
Christian church answering to Kyiv rather than simply to Constantinople.

## What arrived along with the treaty

Christianity came to Rus' bundled with an entire administrative and
cultural apparatus: a written liturgy in Slavonic rather than Greek, an
alphabet already adapted for Slavic speech by earlier missionaries to the
Bulgarians, and a model of sacred kingship that let a prince who had just
paid for his own bride with an army present his rule as divinely
sanctioned rather than merely won by force. Vladimir's own reputation in
later Rus' memory rests less on the transaction that produced his
conversion than on what he did with the authority it gave him - but the
transaction is what actually happened, negotiated in the same terms as any
other alliance of the age.
