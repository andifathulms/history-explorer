Iaroslav's arrangement for his succession was, on its own terms, a
reasonable answer to a real problem. Rather than name one son to inherit
a single throne and leave the others to fight for scraps, he divided
authority among his surviving sons collectively, on the principle that
Kyiv and its lesser cities belonged to the whole princely house rather
than to any one branch of it. The senior member of the family, by age
within the generation, held Kyiv; the next senior held the next most
important city; and when a prince died, everyone below him was supposed
to move up a rung.

## What made the ladder impossible to climb in order

The rule assumed a family willing to wait its turn and princes content to
hold whatever rung their birth order gave them. Neither assumption held
for long. A prince who died before reaching Kyiv left sons who believed
they had been cut out of an inheritance their father would eventually have
received, and those sons rarely accepted exclusion quietly. Within a
couple of generations the "ladder" was less a working system than a
standing justification both sides in any given war could invoke for their
own claim, and Rus' politics from the mid-eleventh century on is a
near-continuous record of princely coalitions fighting each other over
whose reading of seniority was the correct one.

## A capital taken and abandoned in the same campaign

The clearest sign of how far the old order had come apart happened in
1169, when Andrei, prince of the northeastern city of Suzdal, led an army
against Kyiv itself over exactly this kind of succession dispute. His
forces took "the mother of Russian cities" - the honorific by which
Kyiv was still known - and sacked it without restraint. Then Andrei did
something no earlier winner of Kyiv had done: he went home, ruling from
his own northeastern capital rather than moving to occupy the throne he
had just captured by force.

## What the shrug meant

Kyiv kept its ceremonial primacy as the notionally senior seat of the
whole princely house for another seven decades. It never again carried
the political weight that title had once implied. Power in the Rus' lands
had visibly begun shifting toward the northeast, toward Novgorod's own
trading networks, and toward the southwestern principality of
Galicia-Volhynia - three separate centres of gravity rather than one,
each capable of fielding an army but none capable, any longer, of holding
the others in a single working system. It was that fragmented federation,
not the unified realm Iaroslav had once ruled, that the Mongols would
find when they arrived.
