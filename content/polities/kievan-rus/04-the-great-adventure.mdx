Sviatoslav announced his wars the way he lived: without excess. Before
attacking anyone he sent a single message ahead of his army - "I am
setting forth against you" - and then moved fast enough that the
warning rarely helped. A Byzantine historian who watched him cross the
Danube for a peace conference in 971 left the fullest physical
description of any Kievan ruler: shaven head but for a single lock of
hair, a long mustache, an earring set with two pearls and a ruby, white
clothing indistinguishable from his own soldiers' except for its
cleanliness. He carried no tent and no cooking kettles on campaign,
sleeping on a saddle-cloth with his saddle for a pillow and eating meat
sliced thin and roasted on coals.

## Sarkel, and the road down the Volga

His target from the start of his own reign was the Khazar khaganate,
whose control of the lower Volga and the Caspian trade blocked Kyiv's
access to both. In 963 he struck first at Sarkel, the Khazar fortress
on the lower Don, and took it; the Ossetians and Circassians of the
Kuban submitted soon after, and Sviatoslav entered Tmutorokan as its
acknowledged overlord. Two more campaigns followed in quick succession:
the Vyatichi, Khazar tributaries who blocked the road north, submitted
around 964, and the Volga Bulgars' own capital fell to a Rus' assault
the following year.

The Khazar khaganate itself - already reduced to its Volga-delta
heartland by these blows - did not survive what came next. While
Sviatoslav's own attention had turned to a Byzantine-financed campaign
in Bulgaria, a Rus' force descended the Volga from the captured Bulgar
capital in 968 and sacked both Atil, the Khazar capital, and Samandar
further south. Arab geographers recorded it as the end of an empire
that had stood on the steppe for three centuries; the Rus' chronicles,
oddly, say nothing about the campaign at all; what survives of it comes
entirely from the Muslim side.

## The Bulgarian war, and a prince who wanted to move

Sviatoslav's Balkan ambitions outlasted the Khazar campaign by years.
Hired by Byzantium to fight Bulgaria in 967, he occupied the northern
half of the Bulgarian tsardom and told his own mother and boyars in
969 that he intended to move his capital to Pereyaslavets on the
Danube outright - "that is the center of my realm," he said, listing
the goods that passed through it: Greek silk and wine, Hungarian silver
and horses, and furs, wax, honey and slaves coming the other way out of
Rus' itself. Byzantium, alarmed at having created a rival on its own
frontier rather than a mercenary, turned against him once the new
emperor John Tzimiskes secured his own throne. Besieged at Dorostol in
971 after a string of defeats, Sviatoslav negotiated a peace that gave
up his claims to Bulgaria and the Crimea in exchange for safe passage
home.

## Death at the cataracts

He did not make it. Word reached the Pechenegs that Sviatoslav was
returning with a small escort and considerable plunder, and they
blocked the Dnieper cataracts to wait for him. He wintered at the
river's mouth rather than force the crossing immediately, which gained
him nothing: the ambush caught his column in the spring of 972. Part of
the Rus' force broke through, but Sviatoslav himself was killed. The
Pecheneg khan Kurya had his skull made into a gold-plated drinking cup.
