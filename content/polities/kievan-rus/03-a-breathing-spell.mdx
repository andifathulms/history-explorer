Olga answered her husband's murder before she answered anything else
about how to govern - the chronicler records her revenge on the
Drevlyans with a relish that later centuries read as admiration rather
than horror. What she did afterward mattered more: she spent the rest
of her regency making sure no prince of her house would ever again be
killed collecting tribute in person the way Igor had been.

<Figure id="kr-revenge" />

## The reform Igor's death made necessary

Olga abolished poliudie outright - the winter circuit in which the
prince and his retinue rode from tribe to tribe extracting tribute
directly, the same practice that had put Igor alone in Drevlyan
territory with too few men. In its place she divided the realm into
fixed districts, each called a pogost, under a permanent agent or local
board responsible for collecting a set tax rather than whatever a
prince's retinue could extract on the spot. Autonomous tribute
negotiated tribe by tribe became a uniform administrative system - the
first real centralization of Kievan finance, tested first on her own
extensive private estates before she extended it to the Drevlyan lands
and the north.

## A baptism, and a diplomacy that went nowhere

Olga was baptized, most likely at Kyiv around 955, taking the Christian
name Helena after the reigning Byzantine empress. It did not convert
her realm. Her own son and heir Sviatoslav, coming of age as the head
of the pagan party at court, refused every attempt she made to
instruct him in the new faith. When she pressed Constantinople to
grant the Rus' church its own autonomous hierarchy rather than
subordinate status under the Patriarch, the negotiations went nowhere;
she then tried Otto I of the Holy Roman Empire instead, requesting
bishops from the west. Otto's own candidate arrived prepared to
organize the Rus' church as an ordinary eparchy under German clerical
control - not the autonomy Olga had asked for - and the mission
collapsed within the year.

## What she bought her grandson

Olga's own diplomatic failure mattered less than her administrative
success. She held power long enough for Sviatoslav to grow from an
infant orphaned by a tribal ambush into a warrior capable of leading
his own campaigns, and she kept Kyiv itself intact against a Pecheneg
siege in 968 by getting a message through to recall her son from
Bulgaria. She died in 969, only days after Sviatoslav agreed to delay his own
plan to move the capital to the Danube until after her death - a
regency that ended, in the end, exactly on its own terms.
