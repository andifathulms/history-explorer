Septimia Zenobia — Bat-Zabbai in Palmyrene — was Odaenathus's widow, and on his
death she ruled in the name of their surviving son Vaballathus, who was about ten.
She held the position for five years, and in that time Palmyra did the things it
is remembered for.

<Figure id="tower-tomb" />

In 270 Palmyrene forces took Egypt, defeating the Roman garrison and holding
Alexandria. In the same period they moved north and west into Anatolia as far as
Ankara and possibly Chalcedon, opposite Constantinople. At its widest the
territory administered from Palmyra ran from the Nile to the Bosphorus and east
to the Euphrates, which for a city of perhaps a hundred thousand people in a
desert is one of the more remarkable things any city ever did.

The intention is genuinely disputed and will not be resolved here. On the
coins, until 271, Vaballathus appears with Aurelian, junior and deferential, in a
formula that claims inherited authority rather than independence — the argument
being that Zenobia was asserting her son's right to his father's grant, which
Rome had declined to renew. Then in 271 the formula changes: Vaballathus is
Augustus, Zenobia is Augusta, and Aurelian is gone from the obverse. Whether that
was the plan all along or the decision of a regime that had run out of other
options is exactly what the evidence cannot settle.

What is not disputed is the effect. Egypt fed Rome. A power holding Egypt is not
a client whatever its coins say, and the response was never going to be
negotiated.

The later literary Zenobia — chaste, learned, descended from Cleopatra,
conversing with philosophers, led in golden chains through Rome — comes mostly
from the *Historia Augusta*, which is a fourth-century work of substantial
invention, and from the eighteenth-century readers who found her useful. Smith's
Palmyra is a corrective to it in the same way Hillenbrand's Saladin is: the
figure who reaches us was assembled later, by people who needed something.
