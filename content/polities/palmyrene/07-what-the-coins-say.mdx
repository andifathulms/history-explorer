Palmyra's rulers claimed authority by taking other people's titles, and they did
something with that device which is genuinely unusual.

Taking a title is normally a claim on one dead or defeated authority — the
Ottoman *Kayser-i Rûm*, the Buyids on Sasanian kingship, the Byzantine successor
states holding on to Roman style after 1204. One predecessor, one vocabulary, one
audience.

Palmyra made two at once, in two languages, to two audiences. In Palmyrene
Aramaic, Odaenathus was *mlk mlk'*, king of kings — the Sasanian sovereign's own
title, taken by the man who had beaten his army, and legible to everyone from
the Euphrates eastward as a claim on the Iranian imperial tradition. In Latin and
Greek, on the coins and in the inscriptions, the family used Roman offices and
then Roman imperial titles, legible to everyone from Antioch westward as a claim
on Rome.

<Figure id="loculus-relief" />

Neither claim was a fiction and neither was complete. Odaenathus had genuinely
defeated the Sasanians and genuinely held a Roman commission. What Palmyra had no
vocabulary for was itself — there was no title that meant "the city of Tadmor,
which is now the largest power between two empires", because the political
language of the third century had no room for such a thing to exist. So the claim
had to be assembled out of the two empires' words.

<Figure id="wood-baalshamin" />

That is the general condition of every power in this part of the world between
Alexander and Islam. The Ptolemies were gods in Greek and pharaohs in Egyptian.
The Jafnid phylarchs of the sixth century held Roman ranks and were praised in
Arabic verse. Almost none of them could describe itself in a single language,
and any attempt to state what such a polity claimed will have to say who was
being addressed before it says what was claimed.
