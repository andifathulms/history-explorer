Zosimus, writing two centuries later from the imperial treasury in
Constantinople, gives the fullest surviving account of how Palmyra was taken,
and it is full of the kind of detail nobody invents.

After the defeat at Emesa, Zenobia's advisers told her to give up any claim to
the city — its people were friendly to Rome — and to withdraw inside Palmyra,
where they could deliberate at leisure in safety. She did. Aurelian entered
Emesa, was cordially received, found a treasure she had not been able to carry,
and marched on Palmyra, investing it on every side while his troops were
supplied from the surrounding country.

## The man on the wall

The Palmyrenes derided the besiegers, as though they thought it impossible for
the Romans to take the city, and one man in particular used very indecent terms
about the emperor's own person.

A Persian standing beside Aurelian said: if you will allow me, sir, you shall
see me kill that insolent soldier. The emperor consented. The Persian placed
himself behind some other men so as not to be seen, shot at the man as he was
leaning over the battlements, and hit him while he was still uttering his
insults, so that he fell from the wall in front of the soldiers and the emperor.

The besieged held out anyway, expecting the Romans to run out of food, and kept
it up until they themselves had none.

## The camel

Then they held a council and decided to get to the Euphrates and ask the
Persians for help. They put Zenobia on a female camel — the swiftest kind, and
much faster than a horse, Zosimus explains — and got her out of the city.

Aurelian was extremely annoyed and sent cavalry after her. They caught her
crossing the Euphrates in a boat and brought her back.

And then Zosimus records a thought that is not creditable to anybody and is
almost certainly true. Aurelian was pleased at the sight of her, and, being an
ambitious man, immediately became uneasy at the reflection that in future ages
it would not redound to his honour to have conquered a woman.

## The surrender, and the trial

The city divided. Some of the Palmyrenes resolved to fight it out; others made
submissive gestures from the walls and begged pardon. Aurelian accepted, told
them to fear nothing, and they came out with presents and sacrifices in their
hands. He paid respect to the holy things, took the gifts, and sent them away
unharmed.

The trial was at Emesa.

Zenobia came into court and pleaded strongly in her own excuse, producing a
number of people who had *seduced her as a simple woman* — and one of them was
Longinus, the Greek philosopher and critic, whose writings Zosimus pauses to
call highly beneficial to all lovers of learning.

He was found guilty and sentenced to death, and bore it with such courage that
he spent the time consoling the friends who were distressed for him.

Several others besides Longinus, Zosimus adds, suffered upon Zenobia's
accusation.

## What the plea was

It is worth being exact about what she is reported to have argued, because it
is the opposite of the legend.

The defence was that a woman could not have done it. That she had been led,
advised, imposed upon — that the empire, the coinage, the campaign into Egypt
and the title of Augusta were the work of the men around her, and that she had
gone along with them because she was not equipped to do otherwise.

It worked. She lived. The men she named did not, and the most distinguished of
them died well, consoling other people, on the strength of testimony from the
queen he had served.
