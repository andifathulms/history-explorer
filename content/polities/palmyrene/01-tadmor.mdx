Palmyra is an oasis in the Syrian desert, roughly halfway between Damascus and
the Euphrates, and its inhabitants called it Tadmor. It had water in a place
with none, and that geography is the entire explanation for the city: goods
moving between the Persian Gulf and the Mediterranean either went the long way
round through Antioch or crossed the desert, and crossing the desert required
Palmyra.

The Palmyrenes did not merely tax the traffic. They ran it. Palmyrene merchant
families financed the caravans, and Palmyrene archers and camel troops escorted
them; there are inscriptions honouring men who brought a caravan safely from
Spasinou Charax on the Gulf, set up by the merchants who had travelled with
them. There were Palmyrene trading communities at Dura-Europos, on the Gulf, and
in Egypt. This was a city whose leading families were simultaneously its
capitalists, its diplomats and its officer corps, and Smith's argument is that
this is the state formation the city's later thirteen years came out of — not a
sudden ambition, but a community that had been organising force and money
together for two centuries.

The tariff inscription of AD 137 is the best-surviving customs schedule from the
ancient world: a stone listing the duty on slaves, purple wool, olive oil,
dried goods, prostitutes and camel loads, in Palmyrene Aramaic and Greek, set up
because the old rules were unclear and merchants were arguing. It is a document
of a city that lived on rates.

Politically it was Roman from the first century, formally a colonia by the
230s, and culturally it was neither Roman nor Parthian. The inscriptions are
bilingual, the gods are Semitic with Greek epithets, the funerary portraits in
the tower tombs show men in Parthian trousers and women in Greek drapery under
Aramaic names, and nobody in Palmyra appears to have found this a difficulty.
