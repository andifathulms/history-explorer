In 260 the Roman emperor Valerian was defeated and captured alive by Shapur I at
Edessa — the only Roman emperor ever taken prisoner by a foreign power — and the
Sasanian army moved through Syria taking Antioch and everything else it wanted.
The Roman command in the east had effectively ceased to exist.

<Figure id="shapur-valerian" />

Septimius Odaenathus, the leading man of Palmyra and a Roman senator, took his
city's forces out and caught Shapur's army as it recrossed the Euphrates, heavy
with plunder and prisoners, and beat it. He then spent several years doing it
again, twice reaching the walls of Ctesiphon, and destroying the local Roman
usurpers who had emerged in the vacuum.

<Figure id="taq-kisra" />

Rome's response was to make the arrangement official. Gallienus gave him
extraordinary authority over the eastern provinces — the sources give varying
titles, *corrector totius Orientis* among them — and Odaenathus in Palmyrene
took a second title of his own: king of kings, which is what the Sasanian
sovereign he had beaten called himself.

It is important to be precise about what this was, because the popular account
runs ahead of it. Odaenathus never rebelled. He struck no coins in his own name,
he governed in the emperor's, and he spent his career fighting Rome's enemies.
What had changed was that the effective military power in the Roman east was a
Syrian city's own army, commanded by a man who held his position because he had
won with it rather than because a bureaucracy had appointed him — and that the
grant was to him personally.

In 267 he and his eldest son were murdered, at Emesa, by a relative. The sources
give motives and do not agree on them; Zenobia was accused by later writers and
the accusation is unprovable and convenient.
