Almost everything narrative that is known about Palmyra's empire comes from one
historian writing two hundred years later, and it is worth knowing what he was
arguing.

Zosimus was a pagan in a Christian empire, and he says plainly what his book is
for. Polybius, he writes, explained by what means the Romans in a short space of
time acquired a vast empire; his own purpose is to show how, by mismanagement,
they lost it in as short a time. The mismanagement he has in mind is the
abandonment of the old religion. Palmyra is one of his demonstrations, and he
breaks off the narrative of its fall to say so.

At Seleucia in Cilicia, he says, there was a temple of Apollo with an oracle in
it, and the god used to supply people troubled by locusts with a species of bird
that would fly among the swarms and destroy vast numbers of them in a moment.
Zosimus attributes this to the happiness of that age, and notes that his own
generation has not deserved such kindness from heaven. The Palmyrenes consulted
that oracle to ask whether they would ever hold the empire of the east, and were
told to get away from the temple, an accursed race whose treacherous deeds the
gods disliked. Somebody else asked about Aurelian's expedition against them, and
was told that one falcon commands many doves, and their end depends on his
pounces.

<Figure id="afqa-engraving" />

Then the pond. Between Heliopolis and Byblos is a place called Aphaca with a
temple of Aphrodite and beside it a pool like an artificial cistern, over which a
round fire like a lamp was often seen in the air, including — Zosimus says — in
his own time, whenever people gathered there on the appointed days. Worshippers
threw offerings into the water: gold, silver, linen, silk. If the goddess
accepted the gift it sank; if she refused it, it floated, and not only the cloth
but the gold and silver too.

<Figure id="afqa-grotto" />

In the year before their overthrow the Palmyrenes held their festival there and
threw in gold and silver and cloth, and all of it sank. The next year, at the
same festival, all of it floated.

<Figure id="afqa-tobin" />

None of that is evidence about Palmyra. It is evidence about the man the record
depends on, and the reason to set it out is that his account of Odaenathus,
Zenobia, the campaigns and the trial at Emesa comes from the same pen and the
same argument. Zosimus is not indifferent to the Palmyrenes: they are an instance
in a case he is making about why Rome had declined since the emperors stopped
sacrificing, and a state that was warned by oracles and ignored them is exactly
the shape of instance he needs.

He is still, for long stretches, the only narrative there is. Zenobia's defence
at her trial, the sequence of the two battles before the siege, the flight
towards the Euphrates — all of it rests on Zosimus. He was using Palmyra to argue about the gods, and he is the
reason we can say anything about it at all.
