---
title: Two borrowed vocabularies
drafted_from: smith-2013
phase: aside
---

This site codes Palmyra's legitimation as `titulature` and `conquest`, and the
first of those is worth unpacking, because Palmyra did something with titles that
no other polity in this corpus quite matches.

`titulature` in the coding rulebook means taking a *predecessor's* title as one's
own ground: the Ottoman *Kayser-i Rûm*, the Buyids on Sasanian kingship, Trebizond
and Nicaea both holding on to Roman style after 1204. It is normally a claim on
one dead or defeated authority.

Palmyra made two at once, in two languages, to two audiences. In Palmyrene
Aramaic, Odaenathus was *mlk mlk'*, king of kings — the Sasanian sovereign's own
title, taken by the man who had beaten his army, and legible to everyone from
the Euphrates eastward as a claim on the Iranian imperial tradition. In Latin and
Greek, on the coins and in the inscriptions, the family used Roman offices and
then Roman imperial titles, legible to everyone from Antioch westward as a claim
on Rome.

Neither claim was a fiction and neither was complete. Odaenathus had genuinely
defeated the Sasanians and genuinely held a Roman commission. What Palmyra had no
vocabulary for was itself — there was no title that meant "the city of Tadmor,
which is now the largest power between two empires", because the political
language of the third century had no room for such a thing to exist. So the claim
had to be assembled out of the two empires' words.

That is the general condition of everything in this region of the corpus and it
is why the region exists. The Ptolemies were gods in Greek and pharaohs in
Egyptian. The Jafnid phylarchs of the sixth century held Roman ranks and Arabic
poetry. A power in the eastern Mediterranean between Alexander and Islam was
almost never able to describe itself in one language, and the site's institutions
field, which takes a set of values and one source, cannot say that a polity was
making different claims to different people. The chapters have to.
