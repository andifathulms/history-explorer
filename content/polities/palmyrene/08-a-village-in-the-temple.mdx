What survived was a garrison town. Diocletian built a legionary camp in the ruins
twenty years later; the site was a Roman frontier post, then a Byzantine one,
then an Umayyad and Mamluk castle on the hill above, then a village inside the
temple of Bel, and then — from the eighteenth century — a ruin that European
travellers drew and argued about. The village was moved out in the 1930s so the
ruins could be excavated.

Thirteen years puts this among the shortest spans on the site — only the
Zubayrid caliphate is briefer, and Alexander's own empire is the same length —
and the reason the page exists is not the thirteen years. It is that a caravan city organised
by merchant families around the protection of trade produced, when two empires
failed simultaneously, an army capable of beating both of them — and that this
was possible because the escort and the tariff and the tribal levies had been
there for two hundred years, doing something else.

## The shape of that list

Read the sequence again slowly, because it is unusual.

Palmyra was not abandoned and it was not continuously a city. It was a site that
successive powers found useful for successive reasons, none of them the reason
it had originally existed. A caravan city stops being a caravan city when the
caravans stop, and the caravans stopped because Rome took both ends of the route
and Persia closed the other side. What was left was a good defensive position
with water, halfway between two frontiers.

<Figure id="village-tadmor" />

So it became a garrison. Then a different garrison. Then a castle. Then a
village, built inside the cella of the temple of Bel, using the temple's own
walls as the outer walls of houses — which is the most economical thing anyone
ever did with the place, and preserved the temple by inhabiting it.

## And then a ruin

The last stage is the one that made it famous, and it is the only stage in which
Palmyra was valued for having been Palmyra.

European travellers reached it in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries,
drew the colonnade, published the drawings, and argued about the inscriptions.
The bilingual texts in Greek and Palmyrene Aramaic are how the language was
recovered. The city became, in that period, a fixed image — the desert ruin, the
warrior queen, the merchant princes — and the image has almost nothing to do with
the two centuries of quiet tariff-collecting that actually built it.

<Figure id="wood-engraving" />

The village was moved out in the 1930s so the ruins could be excavated
properly, which completed the process: a place that had been continuously
inhabited for two thousand years was cleared of its inhabitants in order to be
preserved as what it had been before them.
