Nine miles south of Delhi stands an iron column twenty-three feet eight
inches high, tapering from sixteen inches across at the base to twelve at
the top, cut with an inscription that names no dynasty, gives no date, and
was already unreadable when a Muslim dynasty in Delhi took an interest in
it centuries before any Englishman saw it.

## What the inscription says

It is a Vaishnava dedication, recording that a king named Chandra raised
the pillar — called, in its own text, a *dhvaja*, a standard — to the god
Vishnu on a hill named for Vishnu's footprints. Before it gets to the
dedication, it spends four lines on why the king deserved to make it.

He is the one "on whose arm fame was inscribed by the sword" when, fighting
in the Vanga countries, he beat back with his own chest a confederacy of
enemies who had united against him. He is the one who crossed the seven
mouths of the river Sindhu in battle and conquered the Vahlikas. The breezes
of the southern ocean, the inscription says, are perfumed even still by his
prowess. And having won sole supreme sovereignty by his own arm, and held
it a very long time, he set up the standard bearing his own beautiful,
full-moon face.

## Whose pillar

Fleet, editing the text in 1888, records that nobody before him had managed
to read the king's own name correctly — early transcriptions of a badly
weathered lithograph made the inscription "quite unintelligible from
beginning to end," and it took two further re-readings, forty years apart,
before Bhau Daji established in 1875 that the name was Chandra.

Establishing the name did not establish which Chandra. Fleet lays out the
candidates without settling on one: Fergusson, struck by the Persian
influence visible in the pillar's capital, argued for one of the early
Gupta Chandraguptas and dated it to 375 or 400 AD. Fleet's own inclination,
on separate grounds, was to allot it to Chandragupta I, the first Gupta
ruler to take the title Maharajadhiraja — though he flags the one real
objection to his own guess: the inscription says nothing about the
Indo-Scythian rulers the early Guptas are known to have had to overthrow to
establish themselves at all. He does not rule out Chandragupta II either,
and neither did most of what was written after him.

## An object that outlasted the argument about it

Whichever Chandra commissioned it, the pillar itself has survived in a
condition the inscription's own readers kept remarking on: "the writing...
is in a state of excellent preservation throughout, owing, of course, to
the nature of the substance on which it is engraved." A column of wrought
iron, more than fifteen hundred years old by any of the proposed datings,
standing in the open air the whole time, with its dedication still legible
when nobody could agree whose reign it belonged to.

That is, in miniature, the shape of most Gupta history. The court that
produced the age later ages would call classical left behind pillars,
plates and coins bearing names, titles and boasts - and left the work of
sorting out which king did which conquest to everyone who came after,
arguing from the same handful of durable objects the dynasty happened to
leave standing.
