The decimal place-value system with a symbol for zero, treated as a number rather
than a blank, is attested in India in this period. Aryabhata's astronomical work
of 499 uses place-value; the Bakhshali manuscript and the inscriptional record
carry the notation; Brahmagupta in the seventh century writes the rules for
operating with zero and with negative numbers.

It travelled. Indian astronomical texts reached Baghdad in the eighth century,
al-Khwarizmi wrote on Indian calculation in the ninth, and his work reached Latin
Europe through translations in the twelfth — which is why the digits are called
Arabic in English and Indian in Arabic, and why *algorithm* is his name.

Every number anyone writes down today, on any monument, in any ledger, in any
scientific measurement, is written in it.

It is not a script, and it is not a religion, and no later state claimed to
inherit it — so it falls outside every category by which the influence of an
empire is normally reckoned. A notation is not a thing a dynasty can be credited
with in the way a conquest or a conversion is; it belongs to the mathematicians
who worked it out, several of whom lived under governments that no longer
existed by the time they wrote.

Which is roughly the point. The Guptas held less ground than the Mauryas and
ruled much of it through other people's kings. Judged on the things empires are
usually judged on, they are middling. What came out of the century they
presided over is the arithmetic of the entire world, and there is no scale on
which those two facts can be weighed against each other.
