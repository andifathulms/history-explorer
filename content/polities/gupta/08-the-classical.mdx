The Gupta period is called the classical age of India, and it is worth knowing
who called it that and when, because the label is doing work.

## Where the term came from

It is a nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century coinage, applied by scholars
working with a Greek and Roman analogy in mind: a period of political unity,
formal perfection in literature and art, and a canon that later ages refer back
to. Vincent Smith and others used it, and Indian nationalist historiography
took it up gladly, because it supplied a golden age that was indigenous, Hindu,
and demonstrably not a gift from anybody.

There is a real thing underneath the label. The Sanskrit literary canon does
take its classical shape here — Kalidasa is conventionally placed at the Gupta
court, and the *Shakuntala* is the play that Goethe wrote a quatrain about and
that made Sanskrit literature a European subject in the 1790s. Temple
architecture in dressed stone begins here. The Ajanta paintings are Vakataka
work of the same decades. Aryabhata writes in 499.

## And what it hides

Three things, and they are the reasons to be careful with the word.

It flattens the century after. The scientific tradition peaks *after* the
empire: Varahamihira, Brahmagupta, and the mathematics that mattered most all
belong to the seventh century, when there was no Gupta state at all. So does
Nalanda's greatest period, and most of what Xuanzang came to India to study.

It quietly assumes that unity produces culture. Some of the most productive
periods in Indian intellectual history had no paramount power in them, and some
of the empires had very little to show.

And it has been used politically. A golden age that is Hindu, Sanskritic and
ends when invaders arrive is an argument as much as a chronology, and it has
been deployed as one — which is not an argument against the period's
achievements but a reason to be exact about their dates.

## The thing that actually travelled

Not the literature, which stayed inside its language. Not the dynasty, which
nobody claimed to succeed.

The decimal place-value notation, with a symbol for zero treated as a number
rather than a blank. It is attested in India in this period; Brahmagupta wrote
the rules for operating with it, including with negative numbers, in 628. Indian
astronomical texts reached Baghdad in the eighth century; al-Khwarizmi wrote on
Indian calculation in the ninth; his work reached Latin Europe through
translations in the twelfth.

Which is why the digits are called Arabic in English and Indian in Arabic, and
why *algorithm* is a Persian mathematician's name.

Every date written down anywhere today is written in it.

## What that says about measuring empires

The Guptas held less ground than the Mauryas, ruled much of it through other
people's kings, and were dismantled by a combination of steppe invasion and
their own charitable endowments.

Judged on territory, duration or armies, they are a middling empire. What came
out of the century they presided over is the notation the entire world does its
arithmetic in — and there is no way to weigh those two things against each
other, because they are not the same kind of quantity.

That is not a defect in this particular case. It is what happens whenever
anybody tries to rank states: the things that can be counted and the things that
turn out to matter are only loosely related, and the second list is only
visible afterwards.
