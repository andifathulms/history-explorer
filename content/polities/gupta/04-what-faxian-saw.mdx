A Chinese monk walked across northern India at the height of the Gupta empire,
spent about a decade there, and wrote down what he saw. He was not interested in
the government, which is what makes his account of it worth having.

## The state that did not register anybody

Faxian's description of the Middle Kingdom — the Gangetic heartland — is a few
sentences long and every clause in it is a claim about administration.

> The people are numerous and happy; they have not to register their households,
> or attend to any magistrates and their rules; only those who cultivate the
> royal land have to pay a portion of the gain from it. If they want to go, they
> go; if they want to stay on, they stay.

No household registration. No compulsory attendance on officials. Revenue drawn
from crown land rather than from a general assessment. Freedom of movement.

Then the criminal law:

> The king governs without decapitation or other corporal punishments. Criminals
> are simply fined, lightly or heavily, according to the circumstances of each
> case. Even in cases of repeated attempts at wicked rebellion, they only have
> their right hands cut off.

And a detail that is easy to skip and is the whole fiscal argument: *the king's
body-guards and attendants all have salaries*.

## Why this is remarkable and what to be careful about

Set it beside the Mauryan state Megasthenes described seven hundred years
earlier — the committees registering every birth and death, the land measured
and the sluices inspected, the quarter of the produce taken — and it is the
opposite kind of government.

That is broadly what the Gupta inscriptions suggest too: an empire that held its
core directly and everything else through subordinate kings, with a lighter and
less intrusive apparatus than the Mauryas had run.

The cautions are real. Faxian was a monk moving between monasteries on
pilgrimage routes, describing the country as it looked to a traveller who was
fed and housed by religious institutions. He is comparing everything silently to
China, where household registration and corporal punishment were pervasive, so
their absence is what strikes him. And he says nothing about how the subordinate
kings taxed their own people.

But he had no reason to flatter anybody. He was not at court, he was not
petitioning, and his book was written for Chinese monks who wanted to know where
the relics were.

## The other half of the same paragraph

He follows all of that with this:

> Throughout the whole country the people do not kill any living creature, nor
> drink intoxicating liquor, nor eat onions or garlic. The only exception is
> that of the Chandalas. That is the name for those who are held to be wicked
> men, and live apart from others. When they enter the gate of a city or a
> market-place, they strike a piece of wood to make themselves known, so that
> men know and avoid them, and do not come into contact with them.

In the markets there are no butchers' shops and no dealers in drink. Only the
Chandalas fish, hunt and sell meat. Money is cowries.

So the same society that does not register its households and does not mutilate
its criminals requires a category of people to announce themselves with a
clapper so that everybody else can get out of the way.

Both sentences are in the same paragraph, by the same observer, on the same day.
A state can be light on its subjects and a social order can be merciless, and
the classical age of Indian civilisation is the place to notice that these are
different questions.

## The endowments

One more mechanism, and it is how the whole religious economy worked.

After the Buddha's *parinirvana*, Faxian says, the kings and the heads of the
Vaishyas built *viharas* for the priests and endowed them with fields, houses,
gardens and orchards, *along with the resident populations and their cattle* —
and the grants were engraved on plates of metal, so that they were handed down
from king to king *without any one daring to annul them*.

Copper-plate grants of villages, in perpetuity, transferable across dynasties.
That is a real institution and the plates survive in quantity. It is also the
beginning of something the Guptas' successors would live with: land alienated
from the revenue, permanently, by an instrument no later king felt able to
revoke.
