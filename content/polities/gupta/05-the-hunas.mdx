The Gupta empire was pushed over from outside and had already been weakened
from inside by a mechanism nobody at the time could have described as a
weakness, because it looked like piety.

## The invasions

The Hunas — the Hephthalites, or White Huns, a steppe people of the same broad
movement that produced the Huns in Europe — came through the north-west passes
from the later fifth century.

Skandagupta beat them, and said so. The Bhitari pillar inscription describes him
restoring the fallen fortunes of his family, and Junagadh records him repairing
a dam. The successful defence is real and it is also the last thing the dynasty
does that looks imperial.

Within a generation the Hunas were back and holding the Punjab and much of
western India; Toramana and his son Mihirakula ruled from Sakala, and Mihirakula
appears in the Buddhist sources as a persecutor and in the Gwalior inscription
as a great king. He was eventually beaten by a coalition of Indian rulers in the
530s, and the Guptas were only one member of it.

Beating an invader in coalition is how a paramount power discovers it is no
longer paramount.

## The plates

Underneath that, something slower.

The Gupta and post-Gupta period is when land grants to Brahmins and to religious
foundations become a normal instrument of royal patronage, recorded on copper
plates. Faxian saw them and described the terms exactly: the fields, the houses,
the orchards, *along with the resident populations and their cattle*, granted in
perpetuity and handed down from king to king *without any one daring to annul
them*.

Read that as a fiscal document rather than as an act of merit. Every grant
removes a village from the revenue permanently, and transfers with it the right
to collect from the people on it. The grantee gets the taxes; increasingly, he
gets the administrative and judicial authority as well.

Do that for two hundred years and the state has given away its own base. The
king still reigns; the villages belong to somebody else; and the intermediate
holders have their own resources, their own dependants and no particular reason
to be governed.

Some historians have called the result Indian feudalism and the label has been
argued about for fifty years. The mechanism does not depend on the label. A
government that alienates revenue in perpetuity, on tamper-proof instruments,
to a class it needs the approval of, has a diminishing amount of country left
to run.

## The dissolving

There is no date and no battle. The Gupta line continues in Magadha into the
sixth century in a reduced form; regional dynasties — the Maukharis, the
Vakatakas' successors, the Maitrakas of Valabhi — appear as independent powers
in territory that had been Gupta or Gupta-client.

By the middle of the sixth century there is no paramount authority in northern
India. Harsha assembles one again in the 600s and it lasts as long as he does.

## What the end was not

It was not a catastrophe, and this matters for how the period afterwards is
usually described.

The temples go on being built — the stone temple architecture that begins under
the Guptas develops without interruption into the regional styles of the
seventh century. The Sanskrit literary and scientific tradition continues:
Varahamihira and Brahmagupta both work in the century after the empire ends, and
Brahmagupta is the man who writes down the rules for calculating with zero.

Nalanda, the monastic university that becomes the intellectual centre of the
Buddhist world for six hundred years, is founded under the Guptas and does its
greatest work after them, sustained by exactly the kind of land grants that
were dissolving the empire.

The state came apart. Almost nothing the state had been paying for did.
