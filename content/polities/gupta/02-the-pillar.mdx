Samudragupta's account of his conquests is cut on a sandstone column at
Allahabad that already carried the edicts of Ashoka.

That is the first thing to notice about it. A fourth-century emperor recorded
his wars on a pillar a third-century-BC emperor had raised to announce that he
had given up war, and did not remove the earlier text. Both are still there, and
so is an inscription Jahangir added in the seventeenth century.

## What the inscription does

It is a *prashasti*, a formal eulogy, composed by a named court officer,
Harishena, who describes himself in it. Fleet's translation preserves the gaps
where the stone is damaged, and the surviving passages set out something more
precise than a list of victories: a taxonomy of ways of being subdued.

**Exterminated.** Of the kings of Aryavarta, the northern plain, the inscription
says he increased his majesty by *violently exterminating* them, and names nine:
Rudradeva, Matila, Nagadatta, Chandravarman, Ganapatinaga, Nagasena, Achyuta,
Nandin, Balavarman, *and many other kings*.

**Made servants.** The kings of the forest countries — the tribal territories
between the settled regions — *he made to become his servants*.

**Obeisance.** The frontier kings of Samatata, Davaka, Kamarupa, Nepala and
Kartripura, and the tribal peoples — Malavas, Arjunayanas, Yaudheyas, Madrakas,
Abhiras, Prarjunas, Sanakanikas, Kakas, Kharaparikas — gratified his commands
*by giving all kinds of taxes and obeying his orders and coming to perform
obeisance*.

**Devotion.** And then, furthest out, the Daivaputras, Shahis, Shahanushahis,
Sakas and Murundas — the Kushan and Saka rulers of the north-west and beyond —
who were won over *simply by devotion and obeisance*, offering their daughters
in marriage, giving Garuda-tokens, and asking for charters confirming them in
their own territories.

## The graded empire

Four rings, four relationships, one sentence apiece. Annihilate the neighbours,
enslave the forest, tax the frontier, accept homage from the far edge.

That is a much more honest description of what a large Indian empire was than
the word "empire" supplies. The Guptas ruled a core directly and everything else
by arrangement, and the arrangements were different at every distance. The
inscription is not hiding that; it is boasting about it, because a king who can
extract four different kinds of submission from four different kinds of people
is demonstrating range rather than admitting limits.

## And the south

The southern campaign gets its own formula, and it is the most interesting
clause in the document.

Samudragupta went down the eastern coast, defeating a series of kings whom the
inscription names one after another — it begins with Mahendra of Kosala. And
then he gave them back their kingdoms. The formula the eulogist uses is that
his glory was produced by *the favour shewn in capturing and then liberating*
them.

A raid, in other words, conducted for tribute and prestige rather than
territory, at the end of which the defeated kings resumed their thrones as
clients. Nothing in the Deccan or the far south was annexed.

Set that beside the ninth name in the extermination list and the empire's actual
shape appears. Close to home, kings are removed. Far away, kings are collected.
