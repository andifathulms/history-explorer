The Gupta dynasty's own inscriptions give its founder's grandson a title that
no other imperial house in India advertises: *Licchavi-dauhitra*, the daughter's
son of a Licchavi.

It appears in the genealogy every time. On the Allahabad pillar, on the copper
plates, on the coinage — a king describing himself, in his own formal
titulature, by reference to his mother's family.

## Why it is there

The consensus reading is that the Guptas started small and married up.

The first two names in the genealogy, Gupta and Ghatotkacha, carry only the
title *maharaja* — a substantial lord, not a paramount sovereign. The third,
Chandragupta I, is *maharajadhiraja*, supreme king of great kings, and he is the
one who married Kumaradevi of the Licchavis.

The Licchavis were an old and prestigious lineage of the Ganges basin, a
*gana-sangha* — one of the oligarchic clan-states that had existed since before
the Mauryas, and one the Buddha himself had known. Marrying into that gave a
provincial house something it could not manufacture.

Chandragupta struck coins with his own portrait on one face and his queen's on
the other, named, with the legend *Licchavayah* — the Licchavis — on the
reverse. A dynasty putting its wife's clan on the money is telling you where it
thinks its claim comes from.

## The other reading

Fleet noticed something else. The name-ending *gupta*, on the authority of the
*Vishnu Purana*, was the ending prescribed for a Vaishya — the merchant varna,
third of the four.

He is careful, and points out counterexamples: the astronomer Brahmagupta can
hardly have been anything but a Brahmin. But the suggestion he records, and
which has been argued ever since, is that the Guptas were not of high birth, and
that this is exactly why the Licchavi connection is repeated so insistently.

If that is right, then the greatest patrons of Sanskrit court culture in Indian
history — the dynasty under which the classical literary and legal canon takes
its shape — were a merchant family who had married into an old clan and never
stopped mentioning it.

That is not a discreditable origin and the inscriptions do not treat it as one.
It is simply the ordinary way a new house becomes an old one, and the Guptas
left the receipt in their own titles.

## What they started with

Magadha and the middle Ganges: the same ground the Mauryas had started from six
hundred years earlier, with the same capital at Pataliputra.

The era they used — the Gupta era, running from 319 or 320 — is conventionally
taken to date from Chandragupta I's accession, which means the dynasty
considered itself to have begun with the marriage.

Whatever the family was before, that is the year they decided to count from.
