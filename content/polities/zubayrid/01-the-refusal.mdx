---
title: The man who would not swear
drafted_from: hawting-2000
phase: formation
---

Mu'awiya's arrangement for his son required oaths, and the oaths were taken
almost everywhere. Two men in the Hijaz refused: Husayn ibn Ali, who set out
for Kufa and was killed at Karbala in 680, and Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr, who
went to Mecca and stayed there.

His standing was as good as anyone's outside the Prophet's own line. His father
was one of the earliest Companions; his mother was a daughter of Abu Bakr; he
was born in Medina in the first years of the emigration and was said to be the
first child born to the Emigrants there. What he objected to was specific and
constitutional: not that the Umayyads ruled but that the office was being
handed from father to son, when the precedent of the first four successions was
that Quraysh consulted and chose.

He did not proclaim himself caliph for three years. He sat in the sanctuary,
where killing him was politically expensive, and let the Umayyads decide how
much they wanted him. In September 683 they decided, and the army sent to
extract him besieged Mecca and wrecked the Ka'ba in the process. Then Yazid
died, the siege was lifted, and the besieging general offered to take Ibn
al-Zubayr back to Syria and have him acclaimed there. He refused that too.

Instead he took the title *amir al-mu'minin* at Mecca, and within months most
of the Islamic world had acknowledged him: Egypt, Kufa and Basra, the Yemen,
the Qaysi tribes of northern Syria, and eastward into Fars, Kerman and
Khurasan, where coins were struck in his name. For a stretch of the mid-680s
the Umayyad house held Damascus and its immediate hinterland and this man held
the rest.

That is not a rebellion. It is the shorter-lived of two caliphates, and the
next chapter is about why the language has never settled that way.
