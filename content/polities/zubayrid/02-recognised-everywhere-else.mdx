Ibn al-Zubayr never left Mecca and never commanded an army in the field, and in
684 he was very close to being caliph of the entire Islamic world. The way that
happened has nothing to do with conquest, which is why it is easy to miss.

Yazid died in 683 with the siege of Mecca still under way. His son Muawiya II
reigned briefly and died, leaving no arrangement and a ruling family that could
not agree which of its branches should succeed. In the gap, the provinces
started choosing, and they mostly chose the man in the sanctuary.

## Syria goes over

Iraq went first, and that was expected. What was not expected was Syria — the
Umayyad home province, garrisoned by the tribes the dynasty had been paying for
a generation.

Emesa declared for Ibn al-Zubayr under its governor Numan ibn Bashir. Palestine
followed under Natil ibn Qays, who took the province over as soon as the Kalbite
governor left it. Qinnasrin went the same way. In Damascus itself Dahhak ibn
Qays, holding the capital, manoeuvred between the two sides for as long as he
could and was finally forced to declare — for Ibn al-Zubayr. Even the Qaysi
tribes of the north, whose quarrel with the Kalb was really about who was paid
first, found their way to the Zubayrid side behind Zufar ibn al-Harith.

What was left to the Umayyads at that moment was one man's loyalty. Hassan ibn
Malik ibn Bahdal, Yazid's maternal uncle and the head of the Kalb, stayed with
his sister's descendants and moved from Palestine to the Jordan district to be
nearer Damascus. That was the Umayyad party: a tribal bloc and a family that
could not decide on a candidate.

## What the argument sounded like

The best evidence for how far the recognition ran is an argument recorded
between Dahhak and the Qaysites of his own camp, at the moment he was about to
go over to a conference that would have elected an Umayyad instead.

They stopped him with one sentence. Wellhausen's rendering:

> Thou hast summoned us to do homage to Ibn Zubair, the Khalifa recognised
> everywhere else, and wilt thou now follow this Kalbite to do homage to his
> nephew?

*The caliph recognised everywhere else.* Said in Damascus, by Syrians, about a
man in Mecca, against the dynasty that had built the city into a capital. It
worked; Dahhak turned round, declared openly, and pitched his camp at Marj
Rahit.

## A caliphate of oaths

This is what expansion looked like for this polity, and it explains both its
size and its fragility.

Nothing had been taken. No Zubayrid army marched into Syria, and Ibn al-Zubayr
never set foot in the province that had gone over to him. What spread was the
oath — the *bayah* — passing from province to province as each set of local
notables calculated which claimant was going to be able to pay them and punish
them.

An empire assembled that way can be assembled the other way just as fast. The
governors who declared for Mecca in 684 had nothing invested in Mecca beyond the
judgement of the moment, and the judgement of the moment was about to be
corrected in a field outside Damascus.
