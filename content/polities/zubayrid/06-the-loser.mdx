The end was quick once Syria was consolidated. Abd al-Malik dealt with the
Qaysi opposition in the north, then took Iraq: Mus'ab ibn al-Zubayr, his
brother's governor there, was defeated and killed at Maskin in 691. The
following year al-Hajjaj brought an army to Mecca and besieged it for
months — Tabari's own sources cannot agree whether it was seven or eight.

## The thunderbolts of Tihamah

Stones went into the sanctuary from the Syrian trebuchets, and then, one
morning, the sky did something the siege had not: a thunderbolt struck and
killed twelve of al-Hajjaj's men. His Syrians, who took this as an omen and
began to hold back, watched their commander pick up a trebuchet stone himself
and load it. *Men of Syria, do not consider this extraordinary. I am a native
of Tihamah: these are the thunderbolts of Tihamah. Behold, victory has come!*
The next day lightning struck several of Ibn al-Zubayr's men instead, and
al-Hajjaj had his answer ready: *You are in a state of obedience; they are in
a state of disobedience.*

## What his mother told him

Ibn al-Zubayr's men went out to the Syrians by the thousand, and among them,
with a promise of safety, were two of his own sons. When he saw how far the
defection had run, he went to his mother Asma, daughter of Abu Bakr, and asked
her advice: he had almost nobody left to fight with, and the enemy would grant
him anything he wanted of this world if he came out. She told him plainly. *If
you know you are right, persevere, for your companions have been killed while
in the right... how long is your stay in this world? Death is better.* He
kissed her hand and put on his mail coat to say goodbye. She felt it through
the embrace — she was blind, and had not seen it — and told him to take it
off: *this is not the action of someone who intends what you intend.* He did.

## The last morning

He led the dawn prayer, recited the whole of the Sura of the Pen, then told
his men to uncover their faces so he could look at them once before the
fighting. He went out reciting his own verse, that a free man bears his day
with patience where others find fault in theirs, and fought until a brick
struck him in the face. He kept fighting with the blood running into his
beard. In the end it was not a soldier who finished him but a madwoman of the
camp, who saw him fall and cried out *Alas for the Commander of the
Faithful!* — pointing him out to men who had not been able to find him in the
crowd. He was killed there, aged about seventy, on a Tuesday in 692; the
sources cannot agree which one.

When word reached al-Hajjaj he prostrated himself, then went with the officer
Tariq ibn Amr to stand over the body. *Women have borne none manlier than
he,* Tariq said. *Will you praise one who disobeyed the Commander of the
Faithful?* al-Hajjaj answered — and Tariq did not back down: *he has freed us
from blame; were it not for this, we would have no excuse. We have besieged
him seven months. He had no fortress, no stronghold, and he held his own
against us as an equal.* Abd al-Malik, told of the exchange, said Tariq was
right.

The historiographical point is the one worth carrying away. For nine years this
caliphate was acknowledged by more of the Islamic world than the Umayyads were,
struck coin in its own name from Egypt to Khurasan, and held the two holy
cities. It is nonetheless called the second fitna, and its head is called a
rebel or a counter-caliph, because the other side won and the account that
reached us was written under the dynasty that beat him and then under the
dynasty that beat them — the same filter that colours everything written about
the Umayyads themselves, operating one stage earlier.

The judgement historians do make is narrower and more interesting than
legitimacy. Ibn al-Zubayr sponsored no doctrine and no programme — unlike the
Alids, and unlike the Kharijites who were fighting their own war at the same
time — and Wellhausen's verdict that the struggle turned around him nominally
while he took little part in it has stuck. A claim to the caliphate on
procedural grounds, held by a man of impeccable standing who offered nothing
else, turned out to be a thing many people would recognise and few would fight
for.
