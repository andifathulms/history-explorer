The Zubayrid caliphate lost Syria in 684 in a battle its caliph did not attend,
did not order and probably could not have prevented. He was in Mecca, eight
hundred miles away.

## How the Umayyads found a candidate

The Umayyad recovery began with a meeting at Jabiya, where the family and the
tribes that still supported it had to settle who they were actually fighting
for. They could not agree. The Sufyanids — Muawiya's line, which had held the
caliphate throughout — were opposed by the rest of a very numerous clan, most of
which backed its old chief Marwan ibn al-Hakam.

Ibn Bahdal, who was acting for Yazid's young sons and whose Kalbite bloc was the
only real Umayyad asset, was eventually talked round. He declared for Marwan —
on terms. Khalid ibn Yazid would follow Marwan, and then Amr ibn Said, whose
family had claims of its own and had to be bought off.

A dynasty in the middle of losing a civil war stopped to negotiate a three-deep
order of succession among its own branches. That it did so and still won says
most of what needs saying about the opposition.

## The field

Marwan marched on Marj Rahit outside Damascus with the Kalb of the Jordan
district and the Sakasik, Sakun and Ghassan behind him. Dahhak was already
camped there, reinforced by the Zubayrid governors of Emesa, Qinnasrin and
Palestine.

While the two armies faced each other, a Ghassanid named Ibn Abi Nims took
possession of the city of Damascus and supplied Marwan with money and weapons.
The battle that followed lasted twenty days. At the end the Qays broke and fled
with terrible loss.

Wellhausen records who died with a precision that says what the defeat cost:
Dahhak fell, and with him eighty nobles who wore the robe of honour and drew a
pension of two thousand dirhams. That is not a casualty list, it is a payroll —
the eighty best-paid men of the Syrian establishment, killed in one place, on
the losing side.

## What it decided

Everything, and not in the way the fighting suggests.

Syria was recovered for the Umayyads in twenty days, and with Syria went the
army that had made them and would now make Abd al-Malik. Ibn al-Zubayr was still
recognised in Iraq, Arabia and Egypt, and would be for years; he lost none of
that at Marj Rahit. What he lost was the possibility of ever taking the province
that mattered, and with it the initiative.

The other legacy was longer. The battle set Qays against Kalb as a permanent
enmity rather than a quarrel, with a blood debt attached that both sides
recorded in verse and neither forgot. The Umayyad dynasty had to manage that
feud in every province it held for the next sixty years, and it never fully
managed it. Winning Syria back cost them the ability to govern it quietly.
