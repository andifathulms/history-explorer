The caliphate was decided in Iraq, and the man who lost it there was not the
caliph. Ibn al-Zubayr stayed in Mecca throughout. Iraq was held for him by his
brother Mus'ab, and Iraq meant Kufa, and Kufa was for sale.

Abd al-Malik did not begin with an army. He began with correspondence. He wrote
to the Kufan commanders in Mus'ab's camp, and to more than one of them he
promised the district of Isfahan — a Kufan assignment, administered by Kufans —
as the price of changing sides. Verses from the period survive expressing plain
apprehension about the treachery of the men of Kufa, and every leader named as
having been worked on is a Kufan.

## The unopened letter

One man brought his letter to Mus'ab with the seal unbroken.

Ibrahim ibn al-Ashtar had won the battle on the Khazir; his father had been the
formidable commander at Siffin. He handed the letter over and said what the
handing-over implied, which was that all the other commanders had presumably
received theirs too, and had kept them quiet. He advised Mus'ab to put the
traitors to death.

Mus'ab could not bring himself to do it. He was warned and he left them in
their posts.

## The battle where nobody fought

The armies met at the monastery of the Catholicus, and what happened is
intelligible without any knowledge of tactics, because there were almost none.

Ibrahim ibn al-Ashtar — the one loyal man, and by a distance the most
distinguished — fell at the very start, and his death settled the day. The
cavalry commander gave way. The rest of the chiefs simply refused to obey the
general and did not bring their tribesmen into the fight at all.

Mus'ab was left standing almost alone on the field, which is the thing the
battle is remembered for. He was thirty-six. His son, still a boy, was killed
in front of him. Then, bleeding from many arrow wounds, he was cut down by a
Kufan of the Thaqif with the shout that this was vengeance for Mukhtar — a man
Mus'ab himself had defeated and killed four years earlier, with an army largely
of these same Kufans.

Another Kufan took his head off.

## What victory looked like

Abd al-Malik entered Kufa, received the homage of the tribes, and appointed his
governors over the provinces that had just changed hands. He camped forty days
at Nukhayla, on the exact spot where Mu'awiya had camped with the Syrian army
after winning the first civil war.

Then, in the same month or the month after, he sent al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf south
with two thousand Syrians, to Mecca.

The caliph in the sanctuary had lost the war before the siege of his capital
began, and he had lost it to letters.
