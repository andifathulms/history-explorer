Al-Hajjaj did not march straight on Mecca. He went first to Ta'if, arrived in
Sha'ban and stayed several months, skirmishing with Ibn al-Zubayr's men on the
plain of Arafat and nearly always winning. Then he asked the caliph for
permission to attack the holy city itself, and for reinforcements. Both came.
The siege opened on 1 Dhu al-Qa'da 72 — 25 March 692.

Stones were thrown into the town and into the sanctuary. When a terrible storm
came on and the besiegers began to have religious scruples about what they were
doing, al-Hajjaj talked them out of it.

The defenders left. In the end they laid down their arms and were pardoned by
the Syrians, and among those who took the pardon were the caliph's own sons. He
was seventy-three. He was ashamed to do it; he took leave of his mother, went
out alone, and was killed.

## What the sources make of him

Wellhausen's judgement is unkind and hard to argue with. Ibn al-Zubayr, he
says, was in the background of the war that carries his name: the struggle
turned around him nominally, he took no part in it, and it was decided without
him. In Arabia itself, for years, the Kharijite Najda had more real influence
than the caliph did. At the end he was found in the building where he had been
keeping himself, and dragged out of it.

The intention behind the whole thing had been serious. He meant to make the
Hijaz the centre of political life again, and he could hardly have meant
otherwise given the kind of movement that had raised him. He advertised the
sanctity of his caliphate by refusing to leave the sanctuary even when the rest
of the world was open to him.

It could not work, and Wellhausen puts the reason in one line: the Hijaz had
been a lifeless province since the murder of Uthman. The government had gone
where the revenue and the armies were, thirty-six years before, and no amount
of holiness was going to bring it back.

## The word for what followed

The fall of Mecca was an epilogue. What contemporaries said had been restored
was the *jama'a*, the community — the same word used for the settlement after
the first civil war, and used here in the same way, to mean that there was one
caliph again and that arguing about it was over.

It is worth being clear about what that word was covering. Two sieges of Mecca
in ten years, the Ka'ba burnt in the first and shot at in the second, a caliph
killed at the sanctuary and his brother beheaded on a field in Iraq, a governor
left there who would spend the next twenty years holding the province down, and
a rival caliphate that had been acknowledged from Egypt to Khurasan simply
written out as a rebellion.

The community was restored in the sense that only one side was left to describe
it.
