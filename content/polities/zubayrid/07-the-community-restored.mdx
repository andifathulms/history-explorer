What al-Hajjaj was given for taking Mecca was Iraq, and Iraq did not consider
the war over. Within a few years he was fighting a Kharijite named Shabib ibn
Yazid, who held out for the better part of two years with a following that
never grew past a few hundred men — and who did something no rebel is
supposed to be able to do to the governor who has just conquered a caliphate:
he rode into al-Hajjaj's own capital, twice, and left again.

## Ghazalah's vow

The second time, Shabib brought a woman named Ghazalah with him. She had
sworn an oath that she would pray two prostrations in the great mosque of
al-Kufah itself, reciting in them the second and third suras of the
Qur'an — *al-Baqarah* and *Al Imran*, by a wide margin the two longest in the
book, chosen so that there could be no doubt she had actually stood there and
finished. She did it. Shabib had huts built for his men in the square outside
while she prayed.

Al-Hajjaj stood in that same mosque afterward and told the men of Iraq, in
front of everyone, that he had written to Damascus for Syrian reinforcements
because their own commitment to the fight could not be trusted.

## The young officer who answered back

One of the men who heard that speech pulled his chair out of the line and
asked permission to reply. Al-Hajjaj gave it. *The amir, by God, has neither
feared God nor defended the Commander of the Faithful nor shown any
commitment toward his subjects,* the man said, and sat back down. Al-Hajjaj,
furious, swung his legs off the bed where he had been resting and demanded to
know who had spoken. The man repeated it to his face. Asked what he proposed
instead, he said: that al-Hajjaj go out and fight Shabib himself.

The man was Qutaybah ibn Muslim — the same officer who would spend the next
decade taking Bukhara, Khwarazm and Samarkand for this dynasty, on the far
side of an empire nobody in that mosque could have pictured yet. He turned up
the next morning in a yellow silk robe from Herat and a red turban, his sword
slung so it looked tucked under his arm, and mounted a horse with a saddle so
oversized that a witness said he looked like a pomegranate sitting in it.

Al-Hajjaj, in armour on a mule, led the army out behind him. They held their
ground the first day, gave battle the second, and by the third had routed
Shabib's camp.

## How Shabib actually died

Not in that battle, or the next one. Shabib survived for months afterward,
withdrew as far as Kirman to recover his forces, and was finally cornered by
a Syrian commander named Sufyan ibn al-Abrad at a pontoon bridge over the
Dujayl. The two sides fought all day and into the dark, and when night
finally separated them, Shabib's own men crossed the bridge ahead of him
while he covered their retreat. As he started across last, his horse —
mid-bridge, among the lashed-together boats — lunged to mount a mare
standing in front of it, and put a hoof over the side of one of the boats.
Shabib went into the river in his armour, saying as he fell, *that God might
determine a matter that was done.* He came up once more, said a second line
— *that is the ordaining of the Almighty, the All-Knowing* — and did not
come up again. No Syrian blade ever touched him.

## What the word covered

*Jama'a* — the community — is what contemporaries said had been restored when
Mecca fell in 692, the same word used for the settlement after the first
civil war: one caliph again, and the argument over. It covered a war against
a few hundred Kharijites that ran on for years after that, fought by a
governor who had to publicly shame his own garrison and be publicly corrected
by a junior officer to keep his own men in the field. It covered a woman
finishing a vow in the conqueror's own mosque while his soldiers watched. The
caliphate that came out the other side of Ibn al-Zubayr's war did not arrive
at peace. It arrived at twenty more years of exactly the kind of fighting the
word was supposed to mean was finished.
