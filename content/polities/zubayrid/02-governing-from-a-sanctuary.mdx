---
title: Governing from a sanctuary
drafted_from: hawting-2000
phase: peak
---

For nine years the capital of most of the Islamic world was Mecca, and no other
polity in this corpus has tried that.

The choice was not administrative. Mecca is a valley town in the Hijaz with no
agriculture worth the name, no river, no manufacture and no strategic position;
it lives on the pilgrimage and on what the pilgrimage brings. Every other
caliphal centre in this site's caliphates region — Medina, Damascus, Baghdad,
Cairo, Cordoba — was chosen because it could feed an army and administer a
revenue. This one was chosen because killing a man inside the sanctuary is a
sacrilege, and because whoever holds it controls the hajj.

That second point is the one the sources dwell on and it is the machinery of the
whole caliphate. The pilgrimage was the annual assembly of the community: the
one occasion when men came from every province, heard whose name was invoked,
and went home having seen who was in charge. Ibn al-Zubayr led it. His governor
led it where he did not. The Umayyads, holding Syria, were for several years in
the position of being unable to send their own pilgrims safely, which is a
public statement about who the caliph was, made once a year, in front of
everyone.

The corollary is what the Umayyads did about it. Abd al-Malik built the Dome of
the Rock in Jerusalem during exactly these years — completed in 691, the year
before Mecca fell — and hostile later sources say plainly that he intended it
as an alternative object of pilgrimage while his rival held the real one.
Modern scholarship mostly does not accept that reading, on the grounds that the
sources saying it are Abbasid-era and hostile to the Umayyads, and that the
building's own inscriptions are addressed to Christians rather than to
pilgrims. This site does not adjudicate it. What it records is that the charge
was made, that it was made about a building put up during a war over who held
Mecca, and that both facts are in the sources.

The other consequence of Mecca is the shape of the reign. A caliph in a
sanctuary is hard to remove and hard to expand from. Ibn al-Zubayr's brother
governed Iraq for him, his agents held Egypt and Khurasan, and he himself
almost never left the Hijaz in nine years — which meant that when his
provinces began to go, he had no army in the field and no way of getting one
there.
