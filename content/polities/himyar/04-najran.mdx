Najran is an oasis town on the northern edge of Himyarite territory and it was
Christian — a substantial community with a church and a bishop, converted in the
fifth century, and connected to the Christian world across the Red Sea and up
through Syria.

In 522 or 523 Yusuf As'ar Yath'ar came to the throne in circumstances involving
an Aksumite garrison he expelled, and moved against Najran. The town was
besieged, surrendered on terms, and the terms were not kept. The leading men
were killed; the accounts describe the burning of a church with people inside
it, and mass executions in a trench.

The Christian sources are immediate and detailed. A letter of Simeon of Beth
Arsham, written within months, circulated the story through the Syriac-speaking
churches; the *Book of the Himyarites* preserves a fuller account; martyrologies
followed. They are hostile witnesses reporting an atrocity, which is the usual
epistemic position for events of this kind, and the archaeological and epigraphic
record corroborates the essentials rather than the numbers.

The Qur'an refers to it, at Surat al-Buruj: the people of the ditch, the fire
with its fuel, seated and watching what they did to the believers. The
identification with Najran is the traditional one and is not the only reading,
and this site records it as a tradition rather than as a fact about the text.

What the massacre did is the reason it is a turning point on this record and not
only an atrocity in a chapter. It made Himyar's confession a matter for
Constantinople. Justin I had every reason to want a Christian power on the Red
Sea route to India and now had a casus belli to give the Aksumite king; the
letters that reached the empire were used, and were meant to be used, to produce
an invasion.

It arrived in 525.
