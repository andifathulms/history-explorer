Somewhere around the middle of the first century AD a Greek-speaking merchant
out of Egypt wrote down what he knew about the sailing routes of the Red Sea and
the Indian Ocean — the ports in order, the distances, what sold at each, what
to bring for the local ruler, and where the anchorage was bad. He was not
writing history and he had no view about South Arabian politics. That is why his
book is worth more here than most things that were.

His account of the Arabian shore runs like this. Coming down the gulf you reach
Muza, a market-town established by law, crowded with Arab shipowners and
seafaring men, busy with commerce, sending its own ships across to the African
coast and out to Barygaza in India. Three days inland is a city called Sana, in
a region called Mapharitis, where there is a vassal-chief named Cholaebus. Nine
days further on is Saphar, the metropolis, where lives Charibael — lawful king
of two tribes, the Homerites and those living next to them, called the Sabaites;
and through continual embassies and gifts, a friend of the emperors.

Saphar is Zafar. Homerites is Himyar. Charibael is Karib'il.

## What one sentence establishes

That single line does a great deal of work.

It shows that a Himyarite king was already styling himself over both Himyar and
Saba' in the first century AD — two centuries before the titulature settles into
the formula the inscriptions record, and a good deal earlier than the point at
which the unification is conventionally dated. The claim was being made long
before it was made good.

It shows the shape of the state. Zafar is the metropolis and the king is there;
San'a is held by a subordinate chief; Muza on the coast is the commercial
engine. That is a highland capital with a client in the interior and a port,
which is exactly the arrangement that let the highland kingdom outgrow the one
at Marib.

And it shows the reach. The merchant notes further along that the East African
coast — Azania, the shore south of the Horn — is subject to Charibael and the
chief of Mapharitis, and that ships from Muza trade at Socotra. A king in the
Yemeni highlands held tributary rights across the water in Africa and had
merchants working the island trade off Cape Guardafui.

## The port that was destroyed

The strangest sentence in the passage is about somewhere else.

Down the coast past the strait lies a village the merchant calls Eudaemon
Arabia — Aden. He explains the name. It was called fortunate because in the
early days, when the voyage was not yet made from India to Egypt and nobody
dared sail across the ocean from either end, everything came together there: it
received the cargoes of both countries, the way Alexandria was then receiving
the goods of the world. It was the transfer point of the whole trade.

Then, in the translation used here: *but not long before our own time Charibael
destroyed the place.*

The name is not certain. What the manuscript actually says is that Caesar
destroyed it, which cannot be right — no Roman emperor attacked Aden in the
first century, and the author elsewhere calls his own sovereign by a different
word. Editors have proposed several repairs; the one adopted here reads
Charibael, and another candidate is the king of the frankincense country
further east. So the sentence is certain in what it reports and disputed in whom
it blames, and the polity that gained by it is not in doubt whoever swung the
hammer.

What is plain from the rest of the book is the consequence. The shipping that
had stopped at Aden now went past it to Muza, which had a bad harbour and a good
roadstead and the king's protection, and the customs revenue moved with it.
Aden's eclipse was not permanent — by the fourth century it was a working port
again, and the Arab geographers write about it in the old glowing terms — but
it lost the century in which the sea route to India was being established, and
Muza had it instead.

Two hundred years later the kings at Zafar had the whole country. This is what
that looked like on the way up: an embassy to a Roman emperor, a client chief
holding San'a, tribute from the African shore, ships of its own working the
route to India — and, down the coast, the wreck of the port that everyone used
to stop at.
