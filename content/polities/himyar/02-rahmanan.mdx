---
title: The gods stop being named
drafted_from: bowersock-2013
phase: peak

Ancient South Arabian religion is attested continuously, in tens of thousands of
inscriptions, for well over a thousand years. Almaqah at Marib, Athtar, Shams,
Wadd, Sayin at Hadramawt: named gods with temples, priesthoods, festivals,
pilgrimages and an unbroken habit of dedicating buildings and campaigns and
recoveries from illness to them.

Around 380 the royal inscriptions stop.

Not the inscriptions themselves — the same script, the same language, the same
formulas for dating and for building works continue without interruption. What
stops is the gods. In their place the texts invoke a single deity: *Rahmanan*,
the Merciful, described as lord of heaven and earth, sometimes with the addition
of "and his son" or "the lord of the Jews", with no image, no temple and no
consort.

It is one of the most abrupt religious transitions documented anywhere in
antiquity, and what makes it strange is how little else changed. There is no
conquest, no new dynasty and no foreign occupation at the point it happens. A
kingdom simply stopped naming its gods in public and started naming one, and
kept doing so for a century and a half.

The site records it as a turning point of type `conversion` — the vocabulary's
value for a change in a state's confessional order — and the corpus has recorded
several. The Bavandids accepting Islam in 842, Ghazan in 1295, Bogu Khagan and
Manichaeism in 762, Isma'il's decree in 1501. This one is unlike all of them in
being visible only through what its own documents stopped saying, and in that
the thing adopted has no name in its own texts beyond an epithet.

What the epithet meant is the next chapter, and it is the reason this page
exists.
