Around 570 the Sasanians took Yemen. The Arabic accounts have a Himyarite noble,
Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan, going to Constantinople for help against the Ethiopians,
being refused, going to Ctesiphon instead, and being given a force — reportedly
of convicts — which took the country. Whatever the details, South Arabia was a
Persian province by the 570s and remained one until the Muslim conquest.

There are therefore two dates for the end and they measure different things: 525,
when the kingdom stopped being Himyarite, and about 570, when the country
stopped being Aksumite. There is no third date on which South Arabia recovered
its independence, because it did not.

The dam went in the same period. The last great breach of the Marib works is
dated to the sixth century, and after it the irrigation was not restored; the
population that had lived on it dispersed, and the migration of South Arabian
tribes northwards into the peninsula is a large theme of early Arabic
genealogical writing. The Qur'an's account of the flood of the dam belongs to
this century and not to the kingdom at Marib, which had been gone for three
hundred years by the time the water stopped.

What South Arabia was when Muhammad was born is worth setting out plainly. A
formerly wealthy country whose
irrigation had failed, ruled by a Persian governor, with a large Jewish
population and a large Christian one and the old temples abandoned, remembering
an Ethiopian invasion and a Jewish king and a thousand years of kingdoms whose
inscriptions were still standing in the fields.

Yemen then spends three hundred years as a province of somewhere else, and the
transition into the first of those centuries is characteristic of the whole
period. There was no conquest of Yemen by the Muslim armies. There was the
surrender of a Persian governor, which is the last act of Sasanian rule in the
country and also the first act of Islamic rule in it, performed by the same man
without leaving his post.
