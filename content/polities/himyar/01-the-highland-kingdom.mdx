---
title: The kingdom that had rain
drafted_from: fisher-2015
phase: formation
---

Himyar appears in the inscriptions in the last century BC, in the highlands
south-west of Marib, with a capital at Zafar and a citadel called Raydan. It was
one of several South Arabian kingdoms and initially not the most impressive.

What it had was different ground. Saba' lived on a dam catching a flood twice a
year at the edge of a desert, and on a percentage of a caravan road. Himyar had
the Yemeni highlands: terraces on mountainsides with reliable monsoon rainfall,
which support a dense agricultural population without any hydraulic engineering
at all, and a position facing west towards the Red Sea rather than north towards
the caravan route.

In the first two centuries AD that stopped being a secondary advantage. The
Roman shipping that went past Arabia on the monsoon, described in the *Periplus
of the Erythraean Sea*, made the caravan road less necessary and the coast more
so, and the kingdom with the coast and the agriculture began winning the
competition with the kingdom that had the toll.

Three centuries of that competition are documented in the inscriptions and are
genuinely confusing — alliances, campaigns, shifting titles, Aksumite
interventions from across the Red Sea from the third century onwards. The
outcome is legible in the titulature. By the late third century the ruler at
Zafar styled himself king of Saba' and of dhu-Raydan; not long after,
Hadramawt and Yamanat were added, and South Arabia was one kingdom for the
first time.

This site codes the legitimation `titulature` on that account. The Himyarite
kings did not stop using the Sabaean royal name for two and a half centuries
after Saba' had ceased to exist, because it was the prestigious claim in the
country they now ruled — which is the same move the Ottomans made with
*Kayser-i Rum* and the Buyids with Sasanian kingship, on a smaller stage and
with the same logic.
