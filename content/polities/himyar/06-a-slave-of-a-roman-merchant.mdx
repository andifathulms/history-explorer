title: A slave of a Roman merchant

Procopius was writing in Constantinople in the 540s about the eastern war, and he
stopped to explain what had become of Himyar, because Justinian had tried to use
it and failed.

His account of the conquest is brisk. The king of the Ethiopians, a Christian and
a devoted one, found that a number of the Himyarites on the opposite mainland
were oppressing the Christians there outrageously — many of the oppressors being
Jews and many of them holding to the old faith. He collected ships and an army,
beat them, killed the king and a great many others, installed a Christian
Himyarite named Esimiphaeus in his place on condition of an annual tribute, and
went home.

Then the sentence that decides the next forty years. Many in the Ethiopian army
— slaves, and everyone readily disposed to crime — were quite unwilling to go
back with the king, and stayed behind, because they wanted the land of the
Himyarites; for it is an extremely goodly land.

Those men, with others, rose against Esimiphaeus not long after, shut him in a
fortress, and made a king of their own. His name was Abraha. Procopius, who
plainly found this remarkable enough to specify, records that Abraha was a
Christian, and a slave of a Roman citizen engaged in the shipping business at
Adulis.

The attempts to undo it went the same way as the conquest. The Ethiopian king,
angry on Esimiphaeus's behalf, sent three thousand men under a relative of his;
that army also decided it would rather stay in a goodly land, opened negotiations
with Abraha without telling its commander, and then, when the fighting started,
killed the commander and joined the other side. A second expedition was heavily
beaten and went home. After that, says Procopius, the king of the Ethiopians
became afraid and sent nothing more. Abraha eventually agreed to pay tribute to
the next Ethiopian king, and by that means made his own position secure.

What Constantinople wanted out of all this is the part worth keeping, because it
shows what Himyar looked like from a thousand miles away. Justinian sent an
ambassador, Julianus, to both courts with a proposal built on their shared
religion. The Ethiopians were to buy silk in India and sell it to the Romans,
which would make them rich and stop Roman money going to Persia. The Himyarites
were to install a fugitive named Qays as captain over the Ma'add and invade
Persia overland with their own army and the Saracens.

Both kings promised. Neither did anything.

Procopius gives the reasons and they are entirely practical. The Ethiopians could
not buy silk from the Indians because the Persian merchants lived next to the
harbours where the Indian ships first put in and were in the habit of buying
whole cargoes on arrival. And it seemed to the Himyarites a difficult thing to
cross a desert so large that the journey took a long time, in order to attack a
people much more warlike than themselves. Abraha, later, when his power was
finally secure, promised Justinian many times that he would invade Persia, and
once actually set out, and turned straight back.

That is the position of South Arabia at the end of its independence, described by
a man in the imperial chancery who had no reason to flatter it. It had been
conquered by one Christian power, governed by a runaway slave of a Roman
shipowner, and was being solicited by a third as a strategic instrument against a
fourth — and its own answer to the solicitation was that the desert was wide and
the enemy was tougher than they were. There is no more accurate summary of what
it had become, or of why the Sasanians took it a generation later without much
difficulty.
