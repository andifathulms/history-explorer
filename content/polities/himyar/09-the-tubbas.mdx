The Arabic word for the great kings of pre-Islamic Yemen is *tubba'*. It is not
a dynasty and not quite a title in the ordinary sense; it is what the Himyarite
kings had been, collectively, once nobody living remembered them. And for the
next thousand years it functioned in Yemen as the standard against which power
was measured.

Follow it forward through the chronicles and it is everywhere.

Ibn Khaldun, describing the arrival of the Abbasid officer who founded a dynasty
on the Yemeni coast in 819, writes that he took the Tihama, founded a city to
live in, was acknowledged in Hadramawt and Shihr and the country of Kinda, and
so acquired the power and dignity formerly enjoyed by the Tubbas. That is the
measure applied to a governor sent from Baghdad by a caliph: not that he was the
greatest of the caliph's officers, but that he had got hold of what the old
kings had.

At San'a in the same period the ruling family were the Banu Ya'fur, whom the
chroniclers call descendants of Himyar and one of the noblest houses to have
survived the rule of the Tubbas. They held San'a, Bayhan, Najran and Jurash for
a century and a half. When the coastal dynasty's greatest ruler was old and his
provinces were slipping, the man at San'a who read his name in the Friday prayer
and sent him nothing was a Himyarite, and the historian who records the insult
records the pedigree in the same sentence, as though it explained something.

The genealogists went further than that. The Zaydi imams at Sa'da, who were
Alids from Medina and whose whole claim rested on descent from the Prophet's
family, are also attached to the posterity of the Tubbas in one of Ibn Khaldun's
chapters — which, as his English editor drily notes, is difficult to derive from
the pedigree Ibn Khaldun himself prints elsewhere. Two incompatible pedigrees
for the same men, and the reason to want the second one is that in Yemen it
helped.

## What was actually left to see

The physical remains encouraged this. They were considerable and they were
visible.

Zafar, where the kings had reigned, was still known in the twelfth century as
the seat of empire of the Tubbas, and by then it was in ruins. At San'a there
stood the palace called Ghumdan, reduced to a high mound, of which the historian
Umara says flatly that no later king of Yemen ever built one like it, or so
lofty. Towns up and down the country were said to date from the Tubbas, and the
houses in some of them were built of their stone.

That is the ordinary condition of medieval Yemen: a landscape of famous ruins
that everybody could point at, belonging to kings whose language could no longer
be read, in a script that had gone out of use, and whose religion — a Jewish
monotheism that ran the state for a century and a half — had been so completely
replaced that the tradition remembering them barely mentions it.

## What the word was for

It is easy to read all this as nostalgia and it is not quite that.

Yemen after Islam was a country of many simultaneous authorities — a caliph's
governor on the coast, an Alid imam in the northern highlands, a Himyarite
family at San'a, chiefs holding mountain fortresses that no lowland army could
take. Nobody in it held everything, and everybody in it needed an argument for
why they should. The Tubbas were the one thing all of them could point at: a
period, safely closed, in which Yemen had been a single country ruled from
inside itself rather than a province of somewhere else.

So the word did a job. To say a man had the dignity of the Tubbas was to say he
had reassembled the country; to say a family had survived the Tubbas was to say
it was older than the conquest and owed its standing to nobody. Both claims were
about the present.

The kingdom that generated the word had been Jewish, wrote in a script that had
gone out of use, spoke a language nobody could still read, and ended by being
invaded from Africa. Almost none of that is in the tradition. What survived was
the bare proposition that Yemen had once been great and had been great on its
own — which is the most portable thing a vanished state can leave, and the
hardest to disprove.
