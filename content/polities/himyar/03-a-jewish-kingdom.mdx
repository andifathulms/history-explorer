title: A Jewish kingdom in Arabia
drafted_from: bowersock-2013

The monotheism Himyar adopted around 380 was, on the evidence, Judaism or
something very close to it, and by the early sixth century the kingdom was
explicitly and publicly Jewish.

The evidence is of several kinds and it accumulated slowly. Inscriptions that
invoke Rahmanan and refer to *Israel* and to the *people of Israel*; a synagogue
and Jewish burial inscriptions at Zafar and elsewhere; a royal formula asking
for help "for his people Israel"; Hebrew and Aramaic words appearing in Sabaic
texts. The last king, Yusuf As'ar Yath'ar, is described as a Jew by Christian
sources that hated him, by later Arabic tradition that made him a legend, and —
which is the point — by his own inscriptions.

Historians argue about how much of the population followed the court and about
whether what the royal texts describe is rabbinic Judaism, a Judaising
monotheism of local development, or something between. Bowersock's book is
partly an argument for taking the Jewish character of the sixth-century kingdom
at face value against a tradition of scholarly hedging. What is not seriously
disputed is that the state was Jewish in its public formulas for something like
the last two decades of its independence, and monotheist for a century and a
half before that.

For this corpus it is a singular entry. Sixty-odd polities, and the confessional
range runs across Sunni and Shi'i Islam in most of its schools, several
Christianities, Zoroastrianism, Buddhism, Manichaeism and the Chinese state
cults. This is the only Jewish state in it, and it is one of very few in the
period between antiquity and the twentieth century anywhere.

Two things follow that are worth stating carefully.

The first is what it was for. South Arabia in the fourth century sat between two
expanding Christian powers — Rome across the Red Sea trade and Aksum directly
across the water, which had adopted Christianity under Ezana at almost exactly
the moment Himyar's inscriptions changed. A monotheism that was neither of theirs
was a way of not being a client of either, and the choice reads as strategic as
much as devotional. The corpus has seen the move before in different clothes:
the Uyghurs taking Manichaeism, a Mesopotamian faith neither of their neighbours
held, is the closest parallel.

The second is that it did not work. The confessional choice that was meant to
keep two Christian empires at arm's length gave them, in the end, the reason to
come.
