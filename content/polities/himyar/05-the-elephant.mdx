---
title: Kaleb, Abraha, and the elephant
drafted_from: bowersock-2013
phase: contraction
---

Kaleb of Aksum crossed the Red Sea in 525 with Byzantine encouragement and,
according to the sources, Byzantine ships. Yusuf was defeated and killed —
tradition has him riding his horse into the sea — and a Christian client was
installed at Zafar. This site's Aksum record now carries the edge, which is the
first it has ever had.

Within a decade the client had been displaced by Abraha, an Aksumite general who
made himself king of Himyar and ruled it for something like thirty years, at
first in defiance of Kaleb and later in a negotiated peace. He is the best
documented ruler of South Arabia in the whole period, because he cut long
inscriptions and one of them survives at Marib.

That inscription records the repair of the dam, which is worth noting: the
hydraulic system on the Saba' record was still being maintained in the 540s, a
thousand years after it was begun, by an Ethiopian general ruling a formerly
Jewish kingdom in the name of a Christian god.

He built a church at Sana'a that the Arabic sources describe as extraordinary,
and this is where Himyar enters the tradition every Muslim reader knows. Abraha,
in that tradition, marched north on Mecca to destroy the Ka'ba and divert the
pilgrimage to his own church, with an elephant in his army; the elephant knelt
and would not advance, and birds dropped stones on the host, and it was
destroyed. The Qur'an's Surat al-Fil refers to it, the year is called the Year of
the Elephant, and it is traditionally the year of Muhammad's birth.

Historians treat the campaign as probable and the date and the details as
uncertain: an inscription of Abraha's records a northern expedition, and the
traditional dating of the Year of the Elephant to about 570 is difficult to
reconcile with the epigraphic chronology of his reign. This site is not going to
adjudicate it and does not carry a turning point for it. What belongs on the page
is narrower and is not in doubt: the last independent power in South Arabia was
an Ethiopian Christian kingdom, and the Arabic tradition that Islam grew out of
remembered it as the thing that came closest to destroying Mecca.
