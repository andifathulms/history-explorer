title: Armenia, six hundred miles away

The word Armenian appears in nine chapters of this corpus — as Byzantine
generals, as Fatimid viziers, as troops in half a dozen armies — and no
Armenian polity has had a page. This is one, and it is not in Armenia.

The Armenian highlands were annexed by Byzantium in the 1040s and overrun by
the Turks after Manzikert in 1071, and the population moved. A great many went
south-west into Cilicia, the well-watered plain between the Taurus and the
Mediterranean, where Armenian communities and Byzantine garrison commanders of
Armenian origin were already established. In 1080 a lord named Ruben took a
castle in the mountains above it and refused to acknowledge anybody, and his
descendants spent a century taking the passes, the plain and the ports.

What they built is one of the odder objects in this corpus: an Armenian
national state, with the Armenian church and the Armenian language at the
centre of it, on ground that had never been Armenia, constructed by refugees
within two generations of arriving.

Its position made it necessary to everybody. Cilicia holds the Cilician Gates,
the pass through the Taurus by which armies enter Syria from Anatolia, and its
port at Ayas was where the overland route from Tabriz reached the
Mediterranean. Crusading armies came through in 1097 and every crusade
afterwards; Byzantine emperors campaigned to recover it; Genoese and Venetian
merchants took quarters at the port.

The Rubenids played that position with considerable skill and one large cost.
In 1198 Levon was crowned king at Tarsus with the recognition of the western
emperor and the pope, and a crown sent from Constantinople as well — a refugee
lordship acknowledged as a kingdom by the Latin and Greek worlds at once. The
price was a union of the Armenian church with Rome, which the clergy and the
population largely would not accept, and the argument about it ran for the rest
of the kingdom's existence.
