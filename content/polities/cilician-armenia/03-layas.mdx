*Here the Book begins; and first it speaks of the Lesser Hermenia.*

The most famous travel book of the Middle Ages opens on this kingdom. Marco
Polo's first chapter, before Persia and before China and before anything he is
remembered for, is a page about Cilician Armenia — because that is where he
started, and because in the 1270s everybody going east started there.

His account is a merchant's, and mostly complimentary about the trade and rude
about everything else.

## What he says

There are two Armenias, the Greater and the Less. The Lesser is governed by a
king who maintains a just rule but is himself subject to the Tartar. The country
has numerous towns and villages and everything in plenty; it is excellent for
hunting; and it is, he adds, by no means a healthy region but grievously the
reverse.

Then the sentence the Armenian nobility would not have enjoyed:

> In days of old the nobles there were valiant men, and did doughty deeds of
> arms; but nowadays they are poor creatures, and good at nought, unless it be at
> boozing; they are great at that.

And then the reason he mentions the place at all:

> Howbeit, they have a city upon the sea, which is called Layas, at which there
> is a great trade. For you must know that all the spicery, and the cloths of
> silk and gold, and the other valuable wares that come from the interior, are
> brought to that city. And the merchants of Venice and Genoa, and other
> countries, come thither to sell their goods, and to buy what they lack. And
> whatsoever persons would travel to the interior of the East, merchants or
> others, they take their way by this city of Layas.

## What that was worth

Layas — Ayas, on the gulf beyond Alexandretta — was the western end of the
overland route from Tabriz, which after the Mongol conquests was the road along
which the goods of Iran, Central Asia and further east reached the
Mediterranean. Every Italian merchant handbook of the period gives the stages:
Ayas to Sivas, Sivas to Erzincan, Erzincan to Erzurum, Erzurum to Tabriz.

A small kingdom held the port at the end of that road. It taxed what came
through, it granted quarters and privileges to the Genoese and the Venetians,
and the trade is why a mountain lordship of refugees was solvent enough to hire
troops, endow monasteries and pay a million dirhams a year in tribute later on.

It is also why the kingdom could not be neutral. In 1294 a Venetian fleet
attacked Genoese vessels in the harbour and there was a sea battle at Ayas
between two Italian republics, fought in an Armenian king's port, over the
right to buy pepper from Persia.

## The other page

Polo's next chapter is on Turcomania, which is the Sultanate of Rum, and it is
worth reading beside this one because it dates the pair so precisely.

Three sorts of people, he says: Turkomans in the hills with their cattle and
their excellent horses, and Armenians and Greeks living mixed among them in the
towns doing trade and handicrafts, who weave the finest and handsomest carpets in
the world. The chief cities are Konya, Sivas and Kayseri. They are subject to
the Tartar of the Levant.

Two neighbouring states, each described in a page, each identified by what it
sold and by whose tribute it paid. Neither passage mentions a war. This is what
the eastern Mediterranean looked like to somebody who had come to buy: an
Ilkhanid customs system with kings inside it.

## The coin

Yule illustrates the chapter with a coin of King Hetum and Queen Isabel, the
two of them standing together holding a cross between them.

It is a good object to stop on. Isabel was the daughter of the Levon crowned at
Tarsus in 1198; Hetum was the constable's son who married her and then went to
Karakorum and knelt to the Great Khan. The coin shows a king and queen of a
Christian kingdom, in Armenian letters, minted in a country where the money that
mattered was Italian and the overlord was a Mongol.
