title: The crown and the church

On 6 January 1198, in the cathedral at Tarsus, Levon was crowned king of
Armenia by the Armenian catholicos, in the presence of a papal legate and an
envoy of the western emperor, with a crown that had been sent from Germany and
another that arrived from Constantinople.

That is an extraordinary sentence for a dynasty that had begun as mountain
lords a century earlier, and every element of it had a price.

The Latin recognition was the expensive one. The papacy did not crown schismatic
kings, and what was agreed was a union of the Armenian church with Rome. The
Armenian church is one of the oldest national churches in the world, it had
been out of communion with both Rome and Constantinople since the fifth century
over the definition of Christ's nature, and it was the institution that had
kept Armenian identity together through six hundred years of having no state.
Levon's clergy and his population did not accept the union. Some bishops
refused it outright; the catholicos who negotiated it was resented; and for the
following two centuries every Cilician king had to manage a court that needed
western help and a church that regarded the price as apostasy.

The kingdom was reorganised on Frankish lines at the same time and more
comfortably. Barons held fiefs, the offices took French names — the constable,
the marshal, the seneschal — and Smbat the Constable, the king's brother who
had gone to Mongolia, compiled a law code drawing on the Assizes of Antioch
alongside Armenian and Byzantine law. Cilician nobles married into the crusader
houses and the crusader houses married into them.

So the state that resulted was Armenian in language, church and self-
description, and Frankish in its institutions, Byzantine in its titles and
Mongol in its foreign policy within fifty years. It is the most thoroughly
hybrid polity in this corpus, and the hybridity was not cosmopolitanism. It was
the price of continuing to exist in a place where four larger powers met.
