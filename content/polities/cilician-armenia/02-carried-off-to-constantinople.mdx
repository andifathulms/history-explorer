Ruben's own descendants ruled as mountain lords rather than crowned
kings for more than a century after his death, and the contemporary
Armenian chronicler Matthew of Edessa - a priest writing from nearby
Edessa through the worst of it - recorded their succession as a
running story of alliance, betrayal and, twice within a single
generation, outright capture by a hostile Byzantine emperor.

## A house built one prince at a time

Ruben was succeeded by his son Kostandin, and Kostandin by his own
son Toros I, each generation extending Rubenid authority further out
of the mountains and into the Cilician plain by the same methods -
marriage alliances with the crusader states to the south, careful
distance from whichever Byzantine or Turkish power was strongest that
year, and outright war against rival Armenian and Byzantine lords
still holding fortresses in the region. Toros allied closely enough
with the crusader principality of Antioch that he fought alongside its
army against the Muslim states to the east; his own brother Levon
succeeded him and pushed Rubenid authority further still, seizing
Byzantine-held towns in the plain that no mountain lord before him
had controlled.

## An emperor who came in person

That expansion provoked the response Byzantium had been building
toward for years. In July 1137 the emperor John II Komnenos marched
into Cilicia in person, besieged Levon's own city of Anazarbus for
thirty-five days under sustained catapult fire, and took it when the
defenders judged further resistance hopeless. John carried off
everything of value to Constantinople - the treasury, an icon of the
Mother of God the Armenians particularly venerated, and Levon himself
along with his sons and wife, all transported across the sea into
Byzantine custody. Levon died there, a prisoner in the city that had
once been the crusaders' own unwitting benefactor at Nicaea forty
years before, never seeing Cilicia again.

## A recovery, and a second captivity threatened

Rubenid authority did not end with Levon's capture. His son Toros II
escaped Byzantine custody and returned to Cilicia to rebuild what his
father had lost, contesting the plain against Byzantine governors for
the rest of his own long reign and maintaining enough independent
standing that Sultan Kilij Arslan II of Rum himself traveled to
Constantinople under the reigning Manuel I Komnenos to swear a
personal oath of obedience rather than risk the same fate Levon had
suffered. The pattern set in these two generations - Rubenid princes
extending their reach into the plain, provoking a Byzantine or Turkish
response, and rebuilding after every setback - repeated for another
sixty years before a descendant of the same house was finally crowned
king in 1198.
