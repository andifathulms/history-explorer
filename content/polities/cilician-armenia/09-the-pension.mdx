The last king of Armenia spent his final years touring the courts of western
Europe asking for an army, and was given an annuity by the king of England
instead.

Yule sets it down in a footnote, in the flat register in which footnotes deal
with the ends of kingdoms. Levon VI, the ex-king — into whose mouth the
chronicler Froissart puts some extraordinary geography — had a pension of a
thousand pounds a year granted him by Richard II, and died at Paris in 1398.

He had been crowned at Sis and was a prisoner in Cairo within about a year of
it. Released, he went to Europe and worked at it — Froissart has him in the
middle of the English and French courts, and the geography Froissart attributes
to him is wild enough that Yule declines to take it seriously — and what he came
away with was a thousand pounds a year and no army. Twenty-three years of a man
with a title and no country, arguing.

The title itself went on without him. Yule records that on the extinction of the
male Rubenid line in 1342 the kingdom had passed to John de Lusignan of the
royal house of Cyprus, and it is through the Lusignans that *King of Armenia*
entered the collection of styles that European dynasties carried for centuries
after the places had gone.

## The four beasts

Polo's contemporary Marino Sanuto, writing about how a crusade might be
supplied, described the kingdom of the Pope's faithful Armenians as caught
between the teeth of four fierce beasts: the Lion Tartar, the Panther Soldan,
the Turkish Wolf, and the Corsair Serpent.

It is a better summary than most modern ones, and the useful part is the count.
Four. Cilicia was not squeezed between two powers, which is a survivable
position and the one most small states occupy. It was on the meeting point of
the Mongols, the Mamluks, the Turkmen of the interior, and the Mediterranean sea
powers who came into its harbour to fight each other — and it needed something
from all four.

## What did not fall

Yule notes, in 1903, what was left: *the chief remaining vestige of this little
monarchy is the continued existence of a Catholicos of part of the Armenian
Church at Sis, which was the royal residence.*

That is the durable thing. The kingdom lasted at most three centuries; the
catholicosate its kings had brought to Hromkla and then to Sis outlived it under
the Mamluks, then under the Ottomans, and then — after the destruction of 1915
emptied Cilicia of Armenians entirely — moved again, to Antelias in Lebanon,
where it still is. It has now existed for something over seven hundred years,
most of them without a state.

The books went too. The gospels illuminated at Hromkla in the 1250s and 1260s,
the finest medieval manuscripts made in that part of the world, were carried out
in stages by people leaving, and are now in Yerevan, Jerusalem, Venice and Los
Angeles.

A refugee state made a church and a library. Both of them survived it by
centuries, and both of them survived by being carried.
