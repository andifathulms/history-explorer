---
title: Disguised as a servant
drafted_from: smbat-bedrosian
phase: contraction
---

In 1253 the king of Armenia dressed as a servant with a pack animal, left Cilicia
with a few men, and rode to Mongolia. His elder brother, who was constable of the
kingdom and had made the same journey five years earlier, wrote it down in the
annals he kept year by year.

Smbat's account is domestic in tone and that is what makes it useful. The entry
for 1252 is the queen's death — daughter of King Lewon, mother of three sons and
five daughters, adorned with every virtue — with a note that one of the daughters
had married a Frank, the lord of Sidon, who then lost Sidon at dice, so that the
Templars bought it. The entry for 1253 is the journey.

Het'um went in disguise and in fear, crossing Cappadocia where, Smbat says, the
Ishmaelite tribes lived, guided by a monk named Barsegh who had done the route
many times. They stopped near Erzurum at the house of a prince and waited there —
because the presents for the khan had not caught up with them. The king's father
Kostandin had assembled the goods in Cilicia and sent them on after him with
trustworthy men. When they arrived Het'um went to Möngke, gave them, and was
received joyfully and granted everything he asked. The khan attached two of his
own men to the Cilician party for the journey home, and Smbat names them:
Markatea, who was one-eyed, and Pach'u.

He got back in September 1256, and his brother's sentence about it is the best
thing in the chronicle. *If, in going to him, he had moved clandestinely, in his
return he went through them like a lion.*

Within a month of reaching home he called an assembly of his brothers, relations
and gentry — a hundred thousand men, Smbat says, which is the kind of figure a
chronicle gives when it means everybody — and invaded the Seljuk territory
through the Taurus passes, and came back with cattle, sheep, horses, mules,
servants and gold. The same year he put his eldest son Lewon on a horse for the
first time, and summoned to the ceremony his son-in-law Bohemond of Antioch, the
lord of Sidon, and the Countess of Jaffa.

Set those three entries in a row — the disguise, the lion, the raid — and the
policy is entirely legible. Cilicia had spent a century as one small Christian
kingdom among Muslim neighbours who were individually stronger than it was. What
the journey bought was not protection in the abstract. It was permission: from
1256 the king of Armenia could raid the Sultanate of Rum with a hundred thousand
men and nothing would happen to him, because the people who would otherwise have
stopped him now answered to the khan he had just given presents to.

It worked for about fifty years and then the thing he had attached himself to
became Muslim, and made peace with Egypt, and stopped answering letters. Smbat
himself was killed in 1276 fighting the Mamluks. What had been the shrewdest
decision any Armenian ruler made in three centuries turned into the reason there
was nobody to appeal to when the Mamluk armies came up through the passes.
