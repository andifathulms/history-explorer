The Armenian church's see moved into this kingdom's territory, to Hromkla and
then to Sis, and stayed there for two hundred years. What that produced is the
reason the kingdom matters to more people than it has readers.

<Figure id="roslin-luke" />

Cilician manuscript painting is the high point of Armenian art. The workshops
at Hromkla in the thirteenth century, and above all T'oros Roslin working there
in the 1250s and 1260s, produced illuminated gospels of a quality that put them
among the finest medieval books made anywhere — and they are distinctively
themselves, drawing on Byzantine models and on crusader and western
manuscripts arriving through the ports, in a manner that is neither. Roslin
signed his work, which medieval illuminators mostly did not.

<Figure id="roslin-signature" />

The kingdom also produced law and history in quantity: Smbat the Constable's
code and his chronicle, Vardan Arewelts'i's world history, Het'um of Korykos
writing in French for a papal audience about how a Mongol alliance could
recover the Holy Land. The last of those is a book by an Armenian prince, in
French, arguing to Rome for a partnership with a pagan empire — which is the
kingdom's whole foreign policy compressed into an artefact.

Two things are worth drawing out.

The first is the timing. Cilicia's manuscript production peaks in the decades
after 1266 — which is to say after the Mamluk invasions had begun, while the
kingdom was paying tribute and losing towns. Roslin was working at Hromkla in
the years the plain was being raided. That pattern is common enough on this
frontier to be worth stating rather than treating as a curiosity: Rumi was
writing at Konya under Mongol suzerainty, al-Jazari built his machines for a
vassal principality at Amid, and the Ma'munids of Khwarazm kept al-Biruni for
fifteen years and lasted twenty-two. Whatever produces books, it is not
political security.

The second is what survived. The kingdom fell in 1375 and the church did not,
and the books left in people's luggage. A state that lasted at most three
centuries produced an institution and a library that have now lasted seven.
