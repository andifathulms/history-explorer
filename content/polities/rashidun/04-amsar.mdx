---
title: The garrison towns
drafted_from: donner-1981
phase: peak

Under Umar the conquering armies were not settled on the land they had taken.
They were quartered in new foundations built for the purpose — Basra and Kufa in
Iraq, Fustat in Egypt, Qayrawan later in North Africa — and paid stipends from
the tax revenue of the surrounding province, registered in a roll called the
*diwan*.

The reasoning was practical: dispersing an army into estates dissolves it, and
the revenue depended on the existing agricultural order continuing undisturbed.
The consequence was structural, and it outlasted the reasoning by centuries. It
produced a conquering class that was concentrated, urban, salaried, and idle
between campaigns, living beside a subject population it did not intermarry with
at first and did not need to convert, since the tax base depended on the subject
population staying what it was.

The *amsar* are where this site's religion-carried count for the Rashidun comes
from, and the reason it is a count of one rather than nothing. Conquest alone
does not qualify under the coding rules; conquest followed by durable religious
institutions does. The garrison towns are that follow-through — mosque,
governor's residence, tribal quarters and stipend roll, planted as a permanent
settlement rather than a camp — and they became the cities from which Islam
spread into the countryside over the following two centuries, long after the
conquests had stopped.

They were also where the conquest army did its politics. Kufa in particular
spent the next hundred years as the most reliably ungovernable city in the
caliphate, and both of the civil wars in this region's history begin with its
garrison deciding something.
