---
title: The war that made the polity
drafted_from: donner-1981

Abu Bakr ruled for a little over two years and spent almost all of it fighting
Arabs. The *ridda* — the turning back — is usually told as the suppression of
an apostasy, and the label is worth examining before the events, because the
label is the thing that was actually at issue.

Much of Arabia had come to terms with Muhammad in the two years before his
death, and a great many of those terms were agreements with him. When he died,
tribes across the peninsula stopped remitting the *zakat* to Medina. Some
followed prophets of their own — Musaylima in the Yamama, Tulayha among the
Asad, the prophetess Sajah — and some simply declined to keep paying a levy to
a town they had contracted with rather than joined. The two are not the same
refusal, and the tradition tends to file them together.

Medina's answer collapsed the distinction on purpose. To call the withholding
of *zakat* apostasy was to assert that the agreements had been made with a
permanent thing and not with a man, and that the thing had a claim on Arabian
tribes that outlived the person who negotiated it. That is a constitutional
argument, and it was settled by force. Khalid ibn al-Walid broke Tulayha at
Buzakha and then destroyed Musaylima's following in the Yamama, and the eastern
and southern risings were put down in turn over roughly a year.

What matters here is not the moral question but the machinery. To fight the
*ridda*, Medina had to raise, direct, supply and pay columns operating hundreds
of miles apart, drawn from tribes whose ordinary military behaviour was the raid
and whose ordinary political behaviour was the feud. It did so, and it worked,
and it demonstrated something that had not been demonstrable in Arabia before:
that tribal manpower could be commanded centrally rather than merely allied
with.

The armies did not disband afterwards. They were assembled, victorious, unpaid
by anything except what the campaign had produced, and pointed at Arabia — where
there was nothing left to take. Within months they were pointed north instead.
The conquests are not a separate event from the *ridda*. They are the same
instrument used again, on richer ground, and the continuity is the reason this
page treats a civil war inside Arabia as the polity's formation rather than as
a disturbance during it.
