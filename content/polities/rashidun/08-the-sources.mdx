title: What the neighbours wrote down

Every other chapter on this page rests on Arabic narrative sources that were
compiled a long time after the events they describe. Ibn Ishaq died in 767 and
survives through an editor who died in 833; al-Waqidi died in 822; al-Baladhuri
in 892; al-Tabari, whose history is the spine of almost every modern account of
these thirty years, in 923. The nearest of them stands a century and a quarter
from Saqifa, and the furthest close to three.

That is not a reason to discard them, and this site does not. It is a reason to
say out loud what kind of thing they are: material transmitted, arranged and
argued over by people with positions in later quarrels, about a period whose
outcome those quarrels were still contesting. Both the works these chapters are
drafted from spend a great deal of effort on exactly that problem.

There is an independent check, and it is the reason this chapter exists.
Christians, Jews and Zoroastrians living in the conquered provinces wrote
things down at the time, in Greek, Syriac, Armenian, Coptic and Ge'ez, and
roughly a hundred and twenty such texts survive with something to say about the
first century and a half. Thomas the Presbyter, writing in Syriac around 640,
records a battle between the Romans and the Arabs of Muhammad east of Gaza and
dates it to 634 — a contemporary, non-Muslim, near-dated notice of the
conquests beginning. The Greek *Doctrina Jacobi*, from about the same decade,
has a report of a prophet who has appeared among the Saracens. The Armenian
history attributed to Sebeos, written in the 660s, gives a narrative of the
conquests by someone who was alive for them. John of Nikiu describes the taking
of Egypt from inside it.

What they confirm is the frame: the dates roughly, the direction of the
campaigns, the speed, the scale of the Byzantine and Sasanian collapse. What
they do not do is talk about the conquerors the way the later Arabic tradition
does. They call them Hagarenes, Ishmaelites, Saracens, Arabs — hardly ever
Muslims — and they are much more interested in what the conquest is doing to
them than in who is caliph. A reader who wants a name for the office, an
account of Saqifa, or the theology of the dispute at Siffin will not find it
here, and that absence is itself information about how visible the internal
constitutional argument was from outside.

The practical consequence for this page: the four rulers, the two civil wars
and the succession vocabulary come from the Arabic tradition and from modern
scholars reading it critically, while the fact that a state of this size
appeared in the 630s and took Syria, Egypt and Iran is attested by people who
had no reason to invent it and every reason to notice.
