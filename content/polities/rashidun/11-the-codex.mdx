The traditional account is specific and it is a story about the garrison towns.
Hudhayfa ibn al-Yaman, campaigning on the northern frontier, heard troops from
the Iraqi and Syrian contingents quarrelling over how the Qur'an was properly
recited, and went to Uthman to say that the community would end up disagreeing
about its scripture the way earlier communities had. Uthman appointed a
committee under Zayd ibn Thabit, worked from a collection held by Hafsa, the
daughter of Umar, produced a standard consonantal text, sent a copy to each of
the principal centres, and ordered the competing written material destroyed.

Take the political shape of that seriously and it is an act of state rather
than an act of scholarship. A state fixed a text, chose the dialectal form it
would be fixed in, distributed it through the *amsar* — the same garrison towns
that carried the stipend roll and the provincial administration — and destroyed
the alternatives. The network that made it enforceable is the one the previous
chapter describes. Very few things this state did reached further or lasted
longer, and it did it with the apparatus it had built to pay soldiers.

The historian's position is more careful than either the traditional account or
its loudest critics. The sources do not agree closely on the date, placing the
project at various points in Uthman's twelve years. They also preserve, without
much embarrassment, the memory that respected Companions had collections that
differed from the one that survived, and that some of them objected. What the
early manuscript evidence shows is a consonantal skeleton that is remarkably
stable remarkably early, alongside at least one witness — the lower, erased
text of the Sanaa palimpsest — that departs from it in ways the standard
tradition does not record. Neither the claim that the text was fixed at a
stroke by a committee nor the claim that it was assembled generations later
survives that evidence comfortably.

That argument is not going to be settled here. What is not in doubt is the
political fact underneath it: the standardisation is the clearest surviving
case of the caliphate doing something to the religion rather than merely
professing it, and it was done with the machinery built to pay soldiers.
