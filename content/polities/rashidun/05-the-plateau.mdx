Syria fell in a campaign. Persia fell in a decade of raiding, and the difference
is the most misunderstood thing about the conquests.

Two battles broke the Sasanian state's capacity to defend itself: al-Qadisiyya
in 636, which opened Ctesiphon and the irrigated Sawad behind it, and Nihawand
in 642, where the last army the empire assembled was destroyed. After Nihawand
there was no Persian field force and no Persian government. What there was, was
a plateau of walled towns, mountain districts and provincial magnates, none of
whom had surrendered, and every one of whom had to be dealt with separately.

Baladhuri's eastern volume is a record of that work, and it is repetitive in a
way that is itself the point.

## Raid and return

The method was not occupation. Uthman ibn Abi al-As was ordered into Fars, made
his camp at Tawwaj, and operated out of it — *making raids from that point and
returning thereto*. Abu Musa was told to help him from Basra, and did the same
thing from the other direction, *returning thereto after each raid*. The armies
lived in a garrison and went out from it. Territory was not held between raids;
it was revisited.

What a raid produced was a settlement, and the settlements are recorded one by
one because centuries later the tax status of each district still depended on
which kind it had been. Shabir was taken by force after siege and battle.
Khurra of Sabur fought first and then capitulated, on condition of paying jizya
and kharaj and giving counsel to the Muslims. Kazarun and Nubandajan were
subdued. Arrajan and Shiraz capitulated on terms. A single province produced a
dozen separate legal outcomes, and Fars was one province of many.

Even the fighting was not always one-sided. At Rishahr the Persian commander
Shahrak was unhorsed and killed by Sawwar ibn Hammam, and Shahrak's son
immediately killed Sawwar; Baladhuri's informants thought the battle there
comparable in violence to Qadisiyya itself. That is a considerable claim about a
place almost nobody has heard of, and it suggests how much of the real
resistance happened after the decisive battles were over.

## The covenants that did not hold

The most revealing pattern is what happened to agreements once made.

When Nihawand was over, most contingents went home to their provinces, but the
Kufans stayed with Hudhayfa and raided Adharbayjan, and the province bought
peace for eight hundred thousand dirhams. Umar then replaced Hudhayfa with Utba
ibn Farqad — and, hearing that Utba had sent him sweetmeats wrapped in cotton
cloth from his new province, sent them back with a note: *thou eatest sweetmeats
not the fruit of thy toil nor of thy father's toil.* Utba, for his part,
remembered arriving on embassy at Medina to find the caliph eating gristly camel
flesh.

Then Uthman became caliph and appointed al-Walid ibn Uqba. *And when they broke
the covenant, al-Walid raided them in the year 25* — the twenty-fifth year of the
hijra, some four years after the peace was bought. He raided Muqan, Babar and
Taylasan too, plundering and taking captives, and when the villages of
Adharbayjan asked for peace again he granted it — on exactly the terms Hudhayfa
had given them the first time.

A province had been conquered, had paid, had repudiated the arrangement, had
been reconquered, and had ended on the same terms as before. That happened
across the plateau for years, and it is why the Sasanian empire is dated to 651
rather than to 642: the state was beaten in a season and the country was not
finished for a decade.

Umar, who could not go and see any of it, wrote to the Kufans wintering up there
about the food and the fur clothing, telling them to eat nothing not lawfully
slaughtered and wear nothing ceremonially unclean. A caliph in Medina
legislating on the garments of soldiers in the Caucasus foothills is a fair
picture of how far the thing had already outrun the men running it.
