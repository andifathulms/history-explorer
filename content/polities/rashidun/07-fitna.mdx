The third caliph, Uthman, was besieged in his own house at Medina by mutineers
from the Egyptian and Iraqi garrisons and killed in 656. The grievance was
distribution: who received stipends, who received governorships, and whether the
caliph's Umayyad kinsmen were receiving both.

Ali was acclaimed at Medina in the aftermath and immediately inherited the
accusation that he had benefited from a murder he had not prevented. He never
cleared it. He fought and beat a coalition of prominent Companions at the Battle
of the Camel outside Basra, then faced Mu'awiya, Uthman's kinsman and the
governor of Syria for twenty years, at Siffin in 657. Siffin ended not in a
result but in an agreement to arbitrate — and the agreement itself split Ali's
army, since a faction held that submitting God's judgement to human arbitrators
was itself the sin. Those who left became the Kharijites, and one of them killed
Ali at Kufa in 661.

Ali's son Hasan came to terms with Mu'awiya rather than continue, and the
caliphate passed to the Umayyad house.

Two things end here and it is worth separating them. The office continues — this
is the same caliphate, and Mu'awiya took the same titles. What ends is the
method: after 661 no caliph is chosen by acclamation at Medina, and the
succession becomes dynastic in practice long before anyone defends it in
principle. The site records that as a `dynastic replacement`, which is the closed
vocabulary's way of saying the institution survived the people.
