Egypt is the one theatre where the conquered side wrote an account that
survives, and it does not read like the others.

John was bishop of Nikiu, a town on a Delta island that Amr ibn al-As took on
his way to Alexandria. He was writing within living memory of the events, in a
country that had just changed hands, for readers who had lost. His chronicle
reaches us through an Ethiopic translation of an Arabic translation of a Coptic
or Greek original, which is a long chain: the dates are unreliable and the
place-names are damaged. What comes through undamaged is the texture.

## Collapse, not defeat

There is no great battle in John's Egypt. There is a command structure coming
apart.

The Roman general John, sent to hold the Fayyum, is drowned in the river, and
Theodore the commander-in-chief drags the body out in a net and sends it up the
chain to Heraclius. Guards are posted at the rock of Lahun to watch for the
enemy; the Arabs go around through the desert instead, lifting sheep and goats
from the high ground without anyone noticing. When a relief officer named
Leontius finally arrives, John records that he was *obese in person, quite
without energy and unacquainted with warlike affairs*, and that he took half the
available troops back to Babylon almost at once to report.

The Roman command in Egypt spends the war calculating. They want to attack
before the Nile rises and the sowing begins, after which nobody can campaign
without destroying the crop. They are still waiting when the fortress of Babylon
falls.

And at Kebrias the general Domentianus, learning the Arabs were near, boarded a
ship and left. His soldiers, seeing their commander gone, *cast away their arms
and threw themselves into the river in the presence of their enemies*, and were
killed in the water; one man, John says, got out alive.

## The sack

The version of the conquests in which armies changed hands and the countryside
went on as before is not what the bishop of Nikiu describes.

At Nikiu itself, finding no soldiers left to resist, the Arabs *proceeded to put
to the sword all whom they found in the streets and in the churches, men, women,
and infants, and they showed mercy to none*. Then they went on to other places
and did the same. John gives the day — a Sunday in the month of Genbot — and
then stops, saying it is impossible to recount what was done there.

This is a partisan telling by a man whose own town was sacked, and it should be
read as one. But it is the only contemporary voice from inside, and what it
insists on is that the transfer of Egypt was violent in a way the administrative
sources do not record, because the administrative sources are interested in what
was agreed rather than in what was done.

## Why the Romans lost it

John's most striking passage is not about the fighting at all. It is about
whose side the Egyptians were on.

Lower Egypt, he writes, split in two: one party held with the Roman general
Theodore, and the other *wished to join the Moslem*. Egypt had spent a decade
under Cyrus, the Chalcedonian patriarch imposed by Heraclius, who had persecuted
the Coptic church and driven its patriarch Benjamin into hiding. When Benjamin
came back to Alexandria in the thirteenth year after his flight and went round
inspecting the churches, John reports what everyone was saying:

> This expulsion of the Romans and victory of the Moslem is due to the
> wickedness of the emperor Heraclius and his persecution of the Orthodox
> through the patriarch Cyrus.

The Egyptians, in John's account, explained their own conquest as a judgement on
Constantinople.

## The tax

Then the arithmetic starts, and the bishop's tone changes again.

Amr *exacted the taxes which had been determined upon, but he took none of the
property of the Churches, and he committed no act of spoliation or plunder*. He
had fixed Alexandria's assessment at twenty-two thousand gold dinars. His
appointed prefect Menas collected thirty-two thousand and fifty-seven. Amr
deposed him for it and put in John of Damietta, a man the bishop says gave away
his own property to the poor and wept over their condition.

That is where the chronicle leaves Egypt: not conquered and settled, but paying.
People hid because they could not raise the sum, and John says they gave up
their children against the monthly payments. Some converted; he names a
Chalcedonian monk of Sinai who took Islam, put down the habit, took up a sword
and turned on the Christians. To the bishop this was the worst of it, worse than
the killing, because the killing ended.
