---
title: A generation, four directions
drafted_from: donner-1981
phase: expansion
---

Between 633 and 651 Arab armies took Syria from the Byzantines, Iraq and then
the whole Iranian plateau from the Sasanians, and Egypt from Byzantine
administration. The Sasanian empire ceased to exist. The Byzantine empire lost
its richest provinces and never recovered them.

The explanation that survives scrutiny is not religious enthusiasm, and not
Byzantine and Sasanian exhaustion after their own long war, though both are
real. It is organisational. The Medinan state could direct tribal manpower that
had previously been unavailable to anyone — not because it converted it, but
because it could pay it, feed it, and give it somewhere to go. The *ridda*
campaigns had already demonstrated that force could be assembled centrally. The
conquests were the same capability pointed outward, at states whose frontier
defences assumed raids rather than annexation.

The chapter has so far named no battles, which understates how few there were.
Three engagements carry most of the weight in the sources. At the Yarmuk in 636
the Byzantine field army in Syria was destroyed, and Heraclius withdrew behind
the Taurus and did not seriously contest the province again. At al-Qadisiyya,
in the same year or close to it, the Sasanian field army broke and Ctesiphon
was open, and with it the Sawad — the irrigated alluvium of lower Iraq, the
richest tax province either empire had, and the revenue the next twenty years
of caliphal politics were conducted over. At Nihawand in 642 the last assembled Sasanian force was
beaten, after which the plateau was reduced province by province over nine
years rather than won in the field.

Two empires lost their armies in about six years and neither raised a
replacement in time. That is the whole military history of the conquests, and
the brevity of it is why the explanations that reach for enthusiasm or for
providence have so little to work with: the decisive fighting was rare and the
consolidation was long.

What the conquests were not was a migration. The armies moved as armies and the
countryside was left substantially as it was: existing landholders, existing tax
registers, existing officials, and in Syria and Egypt existing Greek-language
administration that went on functioning for two more generations. The conquerors
took the revenue and left the machinery.

This is why the maps mislead. A polity of six million square kilometres in 655
is an accurate figure for the extent of a claim to tribute. It is not a
statement about how much of that area anyone in Medina could see.
