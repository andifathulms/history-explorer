Between 633 and 651 Arab armies took Syria from the Byzantines, Iraq and then
the whole Iranian plateau from the Sasanians, and Egypt from Byzantine
administration. The Sasanian empire ceased to exist. The Byzantine empire lost
its richest provinces and never recovered them.

The explanation that survives scrutiny is not religious enthusiasm, and not
Byzantine and Sasanian exhaustion after their own long war, though both are
real. It is organisational. The Medinan state could direct tribal manpower that
had previously been unavailable to anyone — not because it converted it, but
because it could pay it, feed it, and give it somewhere to go. The *ridda*
campaigns had already demonstrated that force could be assembled centrally. The
conquests were the same capability pointed outward, at states whose frontier
defences assumed raids rather than annexation.

## Three battles and a decade

The decisive fighting was remarkably brief. At the Yarmuk in 636 the Byzantine
field army in Syria was destroyed and Heraclius withdrew behind the Taurus. At
al-Qadisiyya, in the same year or close to it, the Sasanian field army broke and
Ctesiphon was open, and with it the Sawad — the irrigated alluvium of lower
Iraq, the richest tax province either empire had, and the revenue the next
twenty years of caliphal politics were conducted over. At Nihawand in 642 the
last assembled Sasanian force was beaten.

Two empires lost their armies in about six years and neither raised a
replacement in time. That is the whole military history of the conquests at the
level of set-piece battle, and the brevity of it is why explanations reaching
for enthusiasm or for providence have so little to work with.

What followed was much longer and much less tidy, and it went differently in
each direction. Syria was a campaign against a field army and a negotiation with
Arabs already in Roman pay. The Iranian plateau was a decade of raiding out of
garrison towns, district by district, with agreements repudiated and re-imposed.
Egypt was a provincial administration that came apart faster than anyone
attacking it expected, in a country whose own people were divided about whether
they wanted it defended. Three chapters follow, one for each.

## Not a migration

What the conquests were not was a movement of peoples. The armies moved as
armies and the countryside was left substantially as it was: existing
landholders, existing tax registers, existing officials, and in Syria and Egypt
existing Greek-language administration that went on functioning for two more
generations. The conquerors took the revenue and left the machinery.

That is true as a description of what the new rulers built, and it is worth
holding alongside what the bishop of Nikiu says was done to his own town. Both
happened. An administration can be inherited intact by men who sacked the place
first.

This is also why the maps mislead. A polity of six million square kilometres in
655 is an accurate figure for the extent of a claim to tribute. It is not a
statement about how much of that area anyone in Medina could see.
