The conquest was not one event. It was several hundred negotiations, across
every theatre it reached, and the terms of each of them mattered for centuries
afterwards, because the legal status of a town's land, its churches and its
inhabitants depended on how it had come under Muslim rule. A place taken by
storm was at the conqueror's disposal. A place that capitulated held whatever
its agreement said it held.

That distinction is why so much of the earliest surviving material about the
conquests reads less like a chronicle than like a bundle of deeds — in Syria,
on the Iranian plateau, and in Egypt alike, though the paper meant something
different in each.

## Damascus, both ways at once

Damascus was besieged for four months. In the end, at sunrise, one Muslim force
broke in at one gate — and at the same time the bishop hurried to Khalid ibn
al-Walid at another and capitulated, and then opened his gate and walked in
beside him holding the written terms Khalid had given him.

So the city fell by assault and by treaty simultaneously, and the two
commanders met in the middle, in the oil-sellers' market.

The Muslims argued about it on the spot, and the objection they raised is
revealing: Khalid was not the commander, so how could terms he wrote bind
anybody? The reply of Abu Ubayda, who was, has become one of the most quoted
sentences of the conquest period — that even the lowest of the Muslims can make
terms binding on all of them. He signed Khalid's document and disregarded the
fact that part of the city had been taken by force. All of Damascus was
therefore treated as having capitulated, and he wrote to Umar to say so.

## What a capitulation actually contained

At Hims the terms guaranteed the inhabitants their lives, their property, their
city wall, their churches and their wells — with one exception written into the
grant, a quarter of the church of St John, which became a mosque. Those who did
not become Muslims were to pay *kharaj*.

Then the practical business: the city was divided into lots, one marked for
each Muslim to build a house on, and the newcomers were settled in every
property whose owners had left and every yard standing empty.

That is the conquest in miniature. A guarantee, a carve-out, a tax, and a
housing allocation — and the last of those is where the Arab presence in the
Syrian cities actually came from, since a great many people had fled north to
Heraclius at Antioch and their houses were standing empty.

## The arguments about the paperwork

The compilers were not naive about their documents, and their disagreements are
preserved rather than smoothed away.

One authority claimed that the people of Damascus had agreed to give up half
their houses and half their churches. Al-Waqidi answered that he had read the
statement Khalid issued to the Damascenes and there was nothing of the kind in
it, and that he had no idea where the story came from; what actually happened
was that many inhabitants fled and the vacant dwellings were occupied.

The dating is disputed too, and the explanation offered for the discrepancy is
the sort of thing nobody invents. Khalid's document, it is said, carried no
date when he wrote it. Before the Muslims marched to the Yarmuk the bishop came
back and asked him to renew it and add Abu Ubayda and other Muslims as
witnesses, in case Khalid did not return. Khalid did so, and dated it the day
he renewed it — which is why the conquest and the surviving instrument are a
year apart.

A besieged bishop asking for a second copy with more signatures, because he had
noticed that the man who gave him the first might be killed in the next battle,
is a better guide to what the conquest felt like from underneath than any
account of the fighting.

## Herat, on paper

Farther east the same habit produced documents even where the plateau's usual
method — raid, return to garrison, raid again — left no single siege to
narrate. When Abdallah ibn Amir sent a column against Herat in Khurasan, its
mayor came to terms before the city was stormed, and ibn Amir had the agreement
written down rather than left as a spoken understanding a later governor could
dispute or deny:

> In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful. This is what Abdallah
> ibn Amir commands the mayor of Herat, Bushanj and Badaghis: he bids him fear
> God and be true to the Muslims, and to keep in peace the lands under his
> hand. And he has made a treaty with him in the name of Herat, plain and
> mountain, on condition that he pay the jizyah to which he had agreed,
> dividing this fairly over the lands. He who refuses what is laid upon him
> has no covenant and no protection. Written by Rabi ibn Nahshal and sealed by
> ibn Amir.

Five sentences: a warning to keep faith, a tax obligation, an instruction that
the burden be apportioned fairly rather than dumped on whichever village a
collector reached first, and a penalty clause that answers itself — break the
terms and the terms are void, which was the whole of what the document had
promised in the first place. Nothing in it touches religion or garrisons or
conversion. Baladhuri's eastern volume records the same shape again and again
across the plateau, at town after town: a conqueror negotiating a fixed,
written payment with local notables who kept their own standing in exchange
for it — produced far from Damascus by a different governor, out of the same
institutional habit rather than a copied form.

## Alexandria, and the covenant Amr did not keep

Egypt's capitulation was not the sack John of Nikiu describes. Alexandria
itself never stood a second siege. Cyrus, the same Chalcedonian patriarch the
Egyptians blamed for driving them toward the Muslims in the first place, went
to Amr himself at Babylon and negotiated while the city behind him was still
walled, garrisoned and unbeaten.

The terms John records read like Hims translated into a different vocabulary
of concession: a fixed tribute; an eleven-month truce before any of it took
effect; safe passage by sea for whichever Roman troops wanted to leave with
their goods, and a toll on any who chose the overland route instead; and a
clause that mattered as much as the money — Rome would stop warring on the
Muslims, the Muslims would stop seizing churches, neither side would meddle in
the other's affairs, and the Jews of Alexandria were named specifically as
free to stay.

Cyrus brought the terms home and the garrison accepted them without a fight.
The ordinary population did not, and gathered to stone their own patriarch for
making peace before anyone had asked them. He talked them down — *I have made
this treaty in order to save you and your children* — and they gave him gold
to add to the tribute rather than see it broken.

It did not survive its author. John's verdict on what followed is unsparing:
Amr, he writes, *had no mercy on the Egyptians, and did not observe the
covenant they had made with him, for he was of a barbaric race.* The taxes
were tripled. A treaty this formal — negotiated by name
between a patriarch and a general, reported clause by clause — still depended
entirely on the winning side choosing to go on keeping it, which is the fact
Damascus's dating dispute and Herat's penalty clause both gesture at and
Alexandria states outright.

## What the paper was actually worth

Three theatres, three negotiators, three different fates for the document
afterward. Khalid's survived because a besieged bishop had it re-witnessed
before the one battle that might have killed its only signatory. Ibn Amir's
survived because the winning side wrote it, with its own enforcement built
into the wording. Cyrus's did not survive at all, because the losing side had
written it, and the winning side decided before long that it no longer needed
the transaction it had signed. The conquest looked nearly identical on
paper in every province it reached. What differed — and what the compilers on
every side of it kept arguing about for two centuries afterward — was which
piece of paper anyone still had to honour once the fighting had actually
stopped.
