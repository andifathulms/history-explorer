Under Umar the conquering armies were not settled on the land they had taken.
They were quartered in new foundations built for the purpose — Basra and Kufa
in Iraq, Fustat in Egypt, Qayrawan later in North Africa — and paid stipends
from the tax revenue of the surrounding province, registered in a roll called
the *diwan*.

<Figure id="amr-mosque" />

The reasoning was practical: dispersing an army into estates dissolves it, and
the revenue depended on the existing agricultural order continuing undisturbed.
The consequence was structural, and it outlasted the reasoning by centuries. It
produced a conquering class that was concentrated, urban, salaried, and idle
between campaigns, living beside a subject population it did not intermarry
with at first and did not need to convert, since the tax base depended on the
subject population staying what it was.

The *amsar* are also how Islam actually travelled. Conquest by itself converts
nobody; what converts a province is an institution somebody can walk into. Each
garrison town was a permanent settlement rather than a camp — mosque,
governor's residence, tribal quarters, stipend roll — and these became the
cities from which Islam spread into the surrounding countryside over the
following two centuries, long after the armies had stopped moving.

They were also where the conquest army did its politics. Kufa in particular
spent the next hundred years as the most reliably ungovernable city in the
caliphate, and both of the civil wars in this region's history begin with its
garrison deciding something.
