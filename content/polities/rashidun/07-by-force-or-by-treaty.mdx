title: By force or by treaty

The conquest was not one event. It was several hundred negotiations, and the
terms of each of them mattered for centuries afterwards, because the legal
status of a town's land, its churches and its inhabitants depended on how it
had come under Muslim rule. A place taken by storm was at the conqueror's
disposal. A place that capitulated held whatever its agreement said it held.

That distinction is why so much of the earliest surviving material about the
conquests reads less like a chronicle than like a bundle of deeds.

## Damascus, both ways at once

Damascus was besieged for four months. In the end, at sunrise, one Muslim force
broke in at one gate — and at the same time the bishop hurried to Khalid ibn
al-Walid at another and capitulated, and then opened his gate and walked in
beside him holding the written terms Khalid had given him.

So the city fell by assault and by treaty simultaneously, and the two
commanders met in the middle, in the oil-sellers' market.

The Muslims argued about it on the spot, and the objection they raised is
revealing: Khalid was not the commander, so how could terms he wrote bind
anybody? The reply of Abu Ubayda, who was, has become one of the most quoted
sentences of the conquest period — that even the lowest of the Muslims can make
terms binding on all of them. He signed Khalid's document and disregarded the
fact that part of the city had been taken by force. All of Damascus was
therefore treated as having capitulated, and he wrote to Umar to say so.

## What a capitulation actually contained

At Hims the terms guaranteed the inhabitants their lives, their property, their
city wall, their churches and their wells — with one exception written into the
grant, a quarter of the church of St John, which became a mosque. Those who did
not become Muslims were to pay *kharaj*.

Then the practical business: the city was divided into lots, one marked for
each Muslim to build a house on, and the newcomers were settled in every
property whose owners had left and every yard standing empty.

That is the conquest in miniature. A guarantee, a carve-out, a tax, and a
housing allocation — and the last of those is where the Arab presence in the
Syrian cities actually came from, since a great many people had fled north to
Heraclius at Antioch and their houses were standing empty.

## The arguments about the paperwork

The compilers were not naive about their documents, and their disagreements are
preserved rather than smoothed away.

One authority claimed that the people of Damascus had agreed to give up half
their houses and half their churches. Al-Waqidi answered that he had read the
statement Khalid issued to the Damascenes and there was nothing of the kind in
it, and that he had no idea where the story came from; what actually happened
was that many inhabitants fled and the vacant dwellings were occupied.

The dating is disputed too, and the explanation offered for the discrepancy is
the sort of thing nobody invents. Khalid's document, it is said, carried no
date when he wrote it. Before the Muslims marched to the Yarmuk the bishop came
back and asked him to renew it and add Abu Ubayda and other Muslims as
witnesses, in case Khalid did not return. Khalid did so, and dated it the day
he renewed it — which is why the conquest and the surviving instrument are a
year apart.

A besieged bishop asking for a second copy with more signatures, because he had
noticed that the man who gave him the first might be killed in the next battle,
is a better guide to what the conquest felt like from underneath than any
account of the fighting.
