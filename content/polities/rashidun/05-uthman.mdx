---
title: Twelve years, and who got the surplus
drafted_from: hinds-1972
phase: peak
---

Uthman ibn Affan ruled for twelve years, longer than Abu Bakr and Umar
together, and until now this site recorded him only as a corpse in the
following chapter. That is the standard shape of the story and it is worth
resisting, because the quarrel that killed him is the quarrel the caliphate
spent its next century having.

The conquest did not stop under Uthman. Iran was finished — Yazdegerd III was
killed at Merv in 651, which is the year this site's Sasanian page ends — the
frontier reached the lower Indus, Cyprus was raided and taken in 649, and an
Arab fleet met a Byzantine one at sea in the mid-650s, which for a power that
had not existed thirty years earlier is a remarkable sentence. But the rate of
new revenue fell, and a state whose stipend roll had been written on the
assumption of continuous conquest found itself arguing about a fixed pot.

Hinds reads the crisis as distributional, and the argument is unsentimental.
Three things moved together. Uthman asserted the centre's claim on the *sawafi*,
the crown lands taken from the Sasanian royal domain, and on provincial surplus
generally — money the garrisons had been consuming where it was raised.
He appointed kinsmen: Mu'awiya was already in Syria, Abdallah ibn Sa'd held
Egypt, Marwan ran the chancery, and the pattern was visible enough to be
described by his enemies as a family taking possession of a public office.
And precedence in Islam — how early one had come to the movement — was the
basis of stipend rank, which meant that the men arriving into the garrisons in
the 640s were permanently junior to men whose claim was that they had been at
Badr.

The opposition assembled out of that. The *qurra'*, the reciters and
early-comers whose standing was religious, supplied what Hinds calls a
rallying-cry and a cloak for the material grievances of the tribesmen — and the
grievances were real whether or not the cloak fitted. Kufa and Basra produced
their own factional versions of it. Egypt produced the party that went to
Medina.

He would not resign. Besieged in his own house and offered the option, he
refused to disown an office he held to be God's to withdraw, and in June 656
the besiegers came in over the back wall and killed him. He was about eighty
and had been reading.

The consequence is the whole of the next chapter, and it is not really about
Uthman. Once a caliph could be killed by Muslims over the distribution of
revenue, the question of who was entitled to decide such things was open, and
nobody closed it again for a very long time.
