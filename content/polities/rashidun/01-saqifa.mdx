Muhammad died at Medina in 632 without a designated successor and without an
institution to produce one. What happened next happened in an afternoon, in a
roofed enclosure belonging to the Banu Sa'ida, while the burial was still being
arranged.

The Medinan Ansar met to choose a leader from among themselves. A small group
of Meccan Emigrants arrived, argued that the community would not accept a
leader from outside the Prophet's own tribe of Quraysh, and secured the
acclamation of Abu Bakr. The argument that carried the room was not scriptural.
It was that the Arabs would obey Quraysh and nobody else.

<Figure id="nabawi-1908" />

That improvisation set the shape of the office. The successor — *khalifa* —
took no prophetic authority, only the political and military leadership of the
community, and he took it by acclamation rather than by descent or appointment.
Every later dispute over the succession, including the one that ends this
period, is a dispute about which of those two things should have decided the
question.

Abu Bakr then spent almost his entire two years fighting Arabs who took the
same view of the agreements that he did not — that they had been made with a
man, and had ended with him. That war is the next chapter, and it is where the
office acquired the thing acclamation had not given it.
