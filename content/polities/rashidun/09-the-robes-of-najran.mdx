title: The robes of Najran
drafted_from: baladhuri-futuh

The Christians of Najran, in the Yemen, made terms with Muhammad himself. They
would deliver two thousand robes a year, a thousand in Safar and a thousand in
Rajab, each worth an ounce of silver, with any surplus or shortfall in value
reckoned up; weapons, horses, camels or goods could be substituted at the same
valuation. They would lodge his messengers for up to a month, never longer. In
case of war in the Yemen they would lend thirty coats of mail, thirty mares and
thirty camels, and anything that died would be made good. In exchange they were
not to be pressed to change their religion or their standing in it, not called
up for military service, and not charged the tithe.

That is the whole of it: a payment schedule, a billeting clause, an equipment
loan, and a guarantee. Nothing in it is theological.

The document went on being the most valuable thing they owned for two hundred
years.

## Expelled, and still holding the paper

Umar expelled them from Arabia, invoking a saying attributed to the Prophet in
his last illness that two religions should not remain in the peninsula. He
bought their properties, and they were resettled at a place near Kufa that took
their name. His letter ordered that whoever in Syria or Iraq they passed among
should clear land for them to till.

They regretted it and asked to be reinstated. Umar refused. When Ali became
caliph they came to him and pleaded on the strength of his own closeness to the
Prophet, and he answered that Umar had been a man of sound judgement and he
disliked to act differently.

So the treaty survived the loss of the country it had been made about. What
they carried into exile was the assessment.

## Two centuries of appeals

Under Uthman they presented the Prophet's written statement and Umar's
recommendation. An inquiry found their situation had become injurious to
landowners whose land they were occupying, and the caliph reduced the
assessment by two hundred robes — "for the sake of Allah, and in place of their
old lands" — and wrote to his governor at Kufa commending them as people under
protection.

Under Mu'awiya they complained that they were scattered, that some had died and
some had become Muslims, and produced Uthman's reduction. He took off another
two hundred.

Then al-Hajjaj, governing Iraq, decided they had sided with the rebellion of
Ibn al-Ash'ath. He put the figure back up to eighteen hundred, and specified
that the robes were to be of the figured kind.

Umar II had a census taken. It showed them reduced to a tenth of their former
number, and his ruling went to the words of the agreement: the tax it imposed
was on their heads, not on their land, so the poll-tax of the dead and of those
who had become Muslims was cancelled. He fixed the liability at two hundred
robes, valued at eight thousand dirhams.

Yusuf ibn Umar, governing Iraq a generation later and a partisan of al-Hajjaj's
memory, restored the original figure.

## Myrtle in the road

When the first Abbasid caliph rode into Kufa, the Najranis met him on the road,
strewed myrtle branches in front of him, and threw some over him as he came
home from the mosque. He was delighted. Later they brought their case: how few
of them there now were, what Umar II had granted and Yusuf ibn Umar had taken
away, and — a nice touch — that they were somehow related to his mother's
family. Witnesses spoke for them. He put them back to two hundred robes.

When Harun al-Rashid passed through Kufa on his way to the pilgrimage they came
again, complaining of the tax-collectors. He had a statement written fixing the
number at two hundred and ordering that they deal with no collectors at all but
pay directly into the treasury. Baladhuri's informant adds four words that are
the point of the whole story: *the statement I myself saw*.

## What actually outlasted the caliphate

The state that made the original agreement lasted thirty years and ended in
civil war. The men who confirmed it were murdered, one after another.

The agreement was still being produced, argued from, revised and re-issued by
the fourth dynasty to hold the office, a hundred and seventy years after the
Prophet dictated it, on behalf of a shrinking Christian community living beside
Kufa in a country it had been expelled from and had never got back.
