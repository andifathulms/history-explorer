Syria was the first province taken and the one that decided what the caliphate
would become, because the man who governed it for twenty years afterwards took
the empire with it.

The campaign was not a single march. Khalid ibn al-Walid came up to the Ghuta,
the orchard belt outside Damascus, and stood an hour at the pass with the black
banner spread; the bishop of Damascus came out to him with gifts and asked for
a covenant, and Khalid gave one. Then he left and went south to Busra, which
came to terms — lives, property and children safe, and a poll-tax, with one
account putting it at a dinar and a measure of wheat for each adult. Ma'ab in
the Balqa' surrendered on the same terms as Busra. The pattern was already
established before the battles that are remembered: towns treated with, and the
terms written down.

## The Ghassanids

The Arabs of Syria were already there, and they were on the other side.

Heraclius assembled Greeks, Syrians, Mesopotamians and Armenians, and sent as
his vanguard Jabala ibn al-Ayham of Ghassan at the head of the Arabs of Lakhm
and Judham and the rest — Arab tribes long settled in Roman Syria and long paid
by Rome to hold its desert edge. The conquest of Syria was not Arabs arriving in
a country without them. It was one Arab political order displacing another that
had been in Roman service for generations.

Jabala's own story is the whole ambiguity in one man. After the fighting he came
over, telling the Ansar "ye are our brethren and the sons of our fathers", and
professed Islam. Then, when Umar came up to Syria, he struck a man of Muzayna in a dispute and
knocked out his eye, and Umar ruled that he should suffer the same. *Is his eye like
mine?* Jabala said. *Never, by Allah, shall I abide in a town where I am under
authority.* He renounced it all and went back to the Greeks with thirty thousand
men. Umar sent an expedition after him four years on with instructions to treat him
gently and appeal to the blood between them, offering him his own faith and a
fixed payment; Jabala refused and stayed. One of Umar's own companions thought
the caliph had mishandled it, and said so: had he taken the payment and been
friendly, the man would have stayed a Muslim.

A king of Ghassan preferred exile in Constantinople to equality before a
Medinan judge. That is a sharper statement of what the new order demanded than
any battle provides.

## The Yarmuk

The armies met at the Yarmuk, the river the Greeks called the Hieromax, in 636.
Baladhuri says twenty-four thousand Muslims fought, that the Greeks and their
auxiliaries chained themselves together so that no man could hope in flight, and
that some seventy thousand of them died. The figures come to him at third hand
and he does not vouch for them; what is not in doubt is that the survivors
scattered as far as Palestine, Antioch, Aleppo, Mesopotamia and Armenia, and
that no replacement army came south.

Abu Sufyan had travelled up as a volunteer to see his sons and brought his wife;
Hind bint Utba, who had led the women of Mecca against Muhammad's army at Uhud,
was in the line calling on the men to cut the enemy down. The families that had
fought the Prophet longest were now fighting for the state he left, and their
sons would inherit it.

Heraclius was at Antioch when the news reached him. He went north to
Constantinople, and Baladhuri has him turn at the pass and say goodbye:

> Peace unto thee, O Syria, and what an excellent country this is for the enemy!

He never came back. The emperor who had broken the Sasanian war effort a few
years before conceded the richest provinces of his empire in a sentence about
pasture.
