---
title: The man who wanted the crown back
drafted_from: chi-4
phase: formation
---

Mardavij ibn Ziyar came out of Gilan, the Caspian country next to the Daylam
that produced the Buyids, and in about four years he took Isfahan, Hamadan,
Rayy and the Jibal, and stood a march away from Baghdad. What he intended to do
there is the reason anybody remembers him.

He was not proposing to take the caliphate, or to hold it as a Shi'i amir with a
caliph in his keeping, which is what his own officers would settle for a decade
later. He proposed to abolish it. The sources
report a golden throne made for him, a jewelled crown in the Sasanian manner, a
plan to be crowned at Ctesiphon in the ruined palace of the Sasanian kings, and
the public celebration of Sadeh, the old Iranian midwinter fire festival, at
Isfahan. The programme was the restoration of the Persian monarchy, three
centuries after it ended, by a Caspian highlander with an army.

It did not survive contact with his own troops. In 935, at Isfahan, his Turkish
slave soldiers killed him in the bathhouse. The reported provocation is small —
a slight, a withheld reward — and the structural cause is the commonest one in
the military history of the region: a purchased guard with no stake in its
master's project, at the point where the project stops paying.

What followed matters more than what he intended. His brother Vushmgir kept the
Caspian provinces and lost the rest. His officers took the rest — and among
those officers were Ali, Hasan and Ahmad, the sons of Buya, who had come down
from the same mountains as mercenary captains and were now employers. Within
ten years they had Baghdad.

So the most ambitious political idea in tenth-century Iran ended in a bathhouse
and was inherited by three men who did not share it, and who took the city
Mardavij had been marching on and left the caliph exactly where he was.
