Two objects survive this dynasty and they are both unusually good.

The first is standing. In 1006 Qabus had a tower built on a mound outside his
capital: a shaft of unornamented fired brick, about fifty metres tall, ten
flanges running its full height like a star in plan, with a conical cap and no
decoration at all except a band of Kufic inscription. It is the earliest dated
tomb tower in Iran and it is one of the great buildings of the Islamic world by
almost universal agreement, and what it is remarkable for is severity. There is
no tile, no colour, no carving. There is a geometry, executed in brick, at a
scale that carries across empty country.

<Figure id="gonbad-e-qabus" />

The inscription is the part that belongs in a history rather than in an
architectural catalogue. It gives the date twice: once by the lunar hijri
calendar, and once by the Yazdgirdi era — the solar reckoning counted from the
accession of the last Sasanian king. Seventy years after Mardavij proposed to
be crowned at Ctesiphon, his grand-nephew dated his own tomb by the Persian
kings as well as by the Prophet's emigration, on a monument built to be seen
from a long way off.

The second is a book. Around 1082, Kay Ka'us ibn Iskandar — Qabus's
great-grandson, ruling almost nothing — wrote the *Qabus-nama* for his son
Gilanshah. It is one of the earliest substantial works of Persian prose and one
of the most likeable books of its century. Formally it is a mirror for princes,
the genre the Karakhanids and the Seljuks were both commissioning at the same
time, and it is nothing like either of theirs. Alongside the chapters on kingship and justice there are chapters on
how to buy a horse and how to buy a slave, on playing chess, on the etiquette
of the bath, on how much to drink, on being a guest, on writing poetry without
being tedious, and on what to do if you must take up a trade. He tells stories
against himself. The advice assumes the reader will not be a great king.

<Figure id="qabus-nama-folio" />

He was right. Gilanshah is the last Ziyarid, and the dynasty ended in about 1090
when the Nizari Isma'ilis took its territory — the same Daylam and Elburz
country the Ziyarids and the Buyids had both come out of, changing hands once
more to somebody with a new idea.

A dynasty that opened by proposing to restore the Persian empire closed with a
retired prince explaining to his son how to behave at dinner. Both documents
are Persian, and the second one is the one people still read.
