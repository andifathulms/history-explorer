title: The signet ring

Qabus was at Janashk, taking the air, when his own nobles and officers decided
to be rid of him. He knew nothing about it until the night they surrounded his
camp, attacked it and plundered his baggage; his retainers drove them off. They
went instead to Gurgan, took the city, and sent to Tabaristan for his son
Minuchihr, whom they told that unless he deposed his father and accepted the
crown from their hands they would find someone else and be done with the family
altogether. Minuchihr could not oppose them and agreed.

Qabus withdrew to Bistam with his household to see what would happen, and his
son was made to march against him. Ibn Isfandiyar's account of the meeting is
the best thing in the Ziyarid record. Minuchihr came into his father's presence
with every mark of respect and in tears, said how the events had forced him into
the appearance of opposing him, and offered — if Qabus wanted it — to declare
openly for him instead. Qabus, who could see his own position clearly, gave his
son his blessing, handed over his signet ring and the keys of his treasuries,
and went back to Janashk to spend what was left of his life in prayer. The men
who had deposed him could not feel safe while he was alive, and before long they
found an opportunity to kill him quietly. He was buried under a dome outside
Gurgan, on the road to Khurasan. Minuchihr mourned three days in the Daylamite
fashion and took the throne, and Ibn Isfandiyar notes that his father was soon
forgotten.

What the new ruler then had to do is the measure of how far the family had come
down. The caliph al-Qadir sent condolences and a title, Falak al-Ma'ali. That
was the easy part. The serious business was Mahmud of Ghazna, and Minuchihr
bought him with presents and professions of loyalty until Mahmud recognised him
in Gurgan, Tabaristan and Qumis and sent an ambassador with a robe of honour. In
exchange Minuchihr paid fifty thousand dinars a year and supplied a thousand
picked Daylamite soldiers for the campaign against Nardin. Then he sent the
greatest noble in Gurgan to Ghazna to ask for one of Mahmud's daughters, and
sent him a second time with the qadi of Gurgan to draw up the contract and bring
the bride home.

Only after all of that did he dare avenge his father. He hunted down the men who
had killed Qabus and put nearly all of them to death; one, the son of Khargash,
got away into exile and was never heard of again. The commander of the old
king's army evaded capture for a long time and finally took refuge with the
sultan at Nishapur, who handed him over.

A ruler who has to marry into a foreign dynasty, pay it an annual tribute and
lend it his infantry before he can execute his own father's murderers is not
manoeuvring between two powers any more. He is a subject of one of them. That is
the arrangement the Ziyarids lived under for the rest of their existence.
