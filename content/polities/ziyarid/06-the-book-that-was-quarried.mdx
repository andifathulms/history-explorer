The dynasty produced two things worth having, and only one of them travelled.
The tower stayed where it was. The book went into general circulation, and what
happened to it there is the Ziyarid afterlife.

## What Qabus was

Take the measure of the man first, because it makes the ending funnier and
sadder.

Qabus b. Vushmgir, styled Shams al-Ma'ali, came from a house that traced itself
— by al-Biruni's reckoning, and al-Biruni lived at his court — to the Sasanian
king Kavadh, through the Qarinvands, one of the seven great families of
Sasanian Persia. Ibn Khallikan says that the number of poets who came to him and
praised him in splendid qasidas exceeded that of any other court. He wrote
Arabic well enough for his letters to be collected as a model of style, and a
treatise on the astrolabe that a competent judge approved; he corresponded with
the Sahib Ibn Abbad; Ibn Isfandiyar praises his eloquence, his horsemanship, and
his knowledge of philosophy, astronomy and astrology.

And then, Browne says, having listed all that: unfortunately, with all these
gifts of mind, birth and character, he was stern, harsh, suspicious and at times
bloodthirsty. He executed a chamberlain on suspicion of embezzlement, and it was
that execution which drove his nobles into revolt, deposed him, and had him put
to death.

## The book

Seventy years later his great-grandson Kay Ka'us, ruling almost nothing, wrote
the *Qabus-nama* for his son — and put the account of Qabus's deposition and
murder in it, along with everything else he thought a young man ought to know.

It is a strange and generous book. Browne, who catalogues its anecdotes one by
one, notes that it holds stories about a rich pilgrim's brutal answer to a poor
one, about how a caliph's favourite slave was saved from drowning, about Plato's
distress at being praised by a fool, and about the physician al-Razi being
alarmed because a madman smiled at him. It is a mirror for princes written by a
man with no principality, for a son who was going to lose what was left.

## And then everybody stole from it

Here is what became of it.

Dawlatshah, compiling his celebrated biographies of the poets four centuries
later, mentions the *Qabus-nama* exactly once. Browne demonstrates that he is
in fact indebted to it throughout, and gives the instances: Dawlatshah's account
of the deposition and murder of Qabus b. Vushmgir is taken from it; so is his
story of how the Sayyida, mother of Majd al-Dawla, talked Mahmud of Ghazna out
of attacking Rayy.

Some of the borrowing goes further than borrowing. The *Qabus-nama* has a rule
that nobody might strike a man the king had already struck, out of respect for
the royal chastisement, and attributes it to the Greeks; Dawlatshah attributes
the same practice to the court of Mahmud of Ghazna. The book has the celebrated
story of a threatening letter answered with the three letters A.L.M.; in the
*Qabus-nama* the answer comes from the caliph and the riddle is solved by one
Abu Bakr Kuhistani, who is promoted for it, and Firdawsi is not in the story at
all. By Dawlatshah's time it is a Firdawsi story.

## What kind of survival that is

So the last Ziyarid's book outlived the dynasty by being taken apart for
material by writers who did not credit it, whose versions of its stories became
the famous ones, and who attached its anecdotes to more famous men.

That is not a bad description of what happened to the family as well. A house
that had proposed to restore the Persian monarchy ended as a small Caspian
principality; its greatest figure was murdered by his own officers; and the one
piece of it that went on circulating did so under other people's names.

The tower, meanwhile, is still standing in the same field.
