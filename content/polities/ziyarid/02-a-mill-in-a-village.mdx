---
title: A mill in a village
drafted_from: miskawayh-margoliouth

Mardavij began as somebody else's captain. The man he served was Asfar ibn
Shirawayh, who had taken Rayy and then Qazwin, and who governed Qazwin by
imposing an enormous levy on it, handing his Daylamites authority over the lives
and property of the inhabitants, stripping the townspeople of any right they
could appeal to, and torturing the local officials on a scale that Miskawayh
says astonished Asfar himself. The people of Qazwin, men, women and children
together, walked out to the oratory and spent an entire day praying to be
delivered from him. When this was reported to Asfar he thought it funny.

The next day Mardavij revolted, attacked him and put him to flight. Asfar rode
hard for a day and was not caught. On the second day he was hungry, and stopped
at a mill in a village to ask for food; the miller gave him bread and milk.
Mardavij, following, found that the hoofprints stopped, looked around, took hold
of a labourer and asked about Asfar. The man said he did not know Asfar but had
seen a rider go into the mill. Mardavij broke in, found Asfar eating, and cut
off his head. Then he went back to Qazwin, calmed the town, redressed the
grievances, stopped the extortion, made a number of handsome promises and left.
Miskawayh's comment is one sentence: so their prayer was answered.

What he did next is the part that matters, because he did to Isfahan exactly
what Asfar had done to Qazwin. He took Rayy and then Isfahan, and at Isfahan he
seized property, had women taken, and governed as a tyrant. He sat on a throne
of gold with a second throne of silver below it for whoever he chose to honour
that day, and on audience days he had the army drawn up in ranks at a distance
from his person. He told people that he was Solomon the son of David and his
soldiers were the demons.

That is the same programme the first chapter describes in its dignified form —
the crown, the Sasanian ceremonial, the coronation planned at Ctesiphon — seen
from inside the province paying for it. The two accounts are not in conflict.
A restoration of the Persian monarchy, attempted in four years by a man with no
revenue base and an army to keep, had to be financed out of the towns he passed
through, and the ceremonial that announced it was the same ceremonial that made
his own soldiers stand at a distance and take orders from a man claiming to
command demons.

Miskawayh adds the detail that finishes it. He humiliated the Turks, who became
disaffected and started looking for their chance.
