Qutb-ud-din Ahmad Shah, the ruler who briefly followed Ahmad Shah's own
son, died suddenly in 1458 after a bout of hard drinking, young enough
and healthy enough beforehand that the court suspected poison — a
suspicion that fell on his own wife and ended, once the accusations
finished working their way through the family, with her being cut to
pieces by jealous co-wives. His successor, an uncle, was deposed after
twenty-seven days of open misrule, and the throne passed on 25 May 1458
to a youth barely out of boyhood: Abu'l-Fath Mahmud, who would rule for
the next fifty-three years and become known to history as Mahmud Begada.

## A child who outwitted his own conspirators

His first test came almost immediately. Courtiers persuaded the new king
that his own minister was plotting to depose him in favour of an infant
puppet, and Mahmud, still new to the court's intrigues, let them arrest
and imprison the man. Warned overnight of the deception, he went in
person to the gate where his minister was held, talked his way past the
guards by sheer force of bearing, and restored him to his post before the
conspirators could act — foiling, before he was even fully grown, a plot
built on exploiting his own inexperience.

## Two great fortresses and a new name

The military reputation that followed was built on real conquests: the
Rajput strongholds of Girnar and, in 1484 after a Rajput garrison chose
mass self-immolation over surrender, Champaner — a victory that left
Gujarat's kingdom running unbroken from the borders of Malwa to the sea.
It was after Champaner's fall that Mahmud first acquired the title by
which he is remembered, Begarha — "two forts" — for the pair of legendary
citadels he had taken.

## The poison legend and the appetite behind it

His elder brother had died, by every appearance, of poison, and the
stories that grew up around Mahmud's own survival were stranger than the
suspicion that produced them. He was said to have absorbed poisons into
his own body gradually from childhood until a fly landing on his hand
would die on contact, immune to anything an assassin might put in his
food — a legend durable enough that the English poet Samuel Butler was
still invoking "the prince of Cambay's daily food" of "asp and basilisk
and toad" in *Hudibras*, published in 1664, more than a century and a
half after Mahmud's death. The reality behind the legend was more
mundane and, in its own way, no less remarkable: a recorded daily intake
of twenty to thirty pounds of food, a cup each of honey and butter and up
to a hundred and fifty bananas on waking, and rice left at both sides of
his bed each night so that he would find something to eat whichever way
he turned.

Tall, robust, bearded to his waist, mild to his own servants and ferocious
toward the Hindu kingdoms he fought, Mahmud died at Ahmadabad on 23
November 1511, summoning his designated heir Muzaffar from Baroda only in
time to help carry his coffin to its tomb — the greatest of Gujarat's
sultans, by his own historians' judgement, and the one whose fifty-three
years on the throne left the kingdom at its widest extent.
