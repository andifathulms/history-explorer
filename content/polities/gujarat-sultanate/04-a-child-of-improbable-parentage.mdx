Two puppet kings came and went in the confusion after Bahadur Shah's
death before the throne settled, in 1554, on a boy of eleven, Ahmad Shah
II, whose regent I'timad Khan kept him a virtual prisoner for the whole
of his short reign. When Ahmad, old enough at last to resent his own
captivity, fled to a rival noble's protection, I'timad Khan crushed the
faction that had sheltered him and dragged the young king back to
Ahmadabad in chains in all but name.

## A boy's plots and a regent's fear

Confinement did not make Ahmad harmless in I'timad Khan's eyes. The boy
amused himself hatching schemes for his gaoler's assassination with
whatever officers could reach him, even play-acting the killing by
slicing a plantain stem in two and boasting that he could do the same to
his regent — games I'timad Khan knew were empty, but feared some
ambitious officer might one day take seriously enough to act on. In July
1562 he settled the risk permanently: Ahmad was murdered, and his body
thrown into the open ground below the citadel, with a cover story
blaming an anonymous rival for a supposed nighttime tryst.

## An heir invented rather than found

Ahmad's death left no acknowledged heir, and I'timad Khan supplied one:
a child named Nathu, whom he swore under oath was a secret son of the
previous king Mahmud III, hidden from birth after the king had first
ordered the pregnancy aborted. The story convinced no one who examined it
closely, but no noble in Gujarat was willing to accept a rival of equal
rank on the throne instead, and the fabricated child was crowned Muzaffar
III — a kingship built, from its very first day, on an oath its own
author would later recant whenever it suited him.

## Ten years of nobles at war with each other

What followed was ten years, by Haig's own description, of little more
than open warfare between great nobles who had carved the kingdom into
private fiefs and answered to no one above them. Afghan chieftains
expelled each other from whole provinces; a noble's own kinsman was
beheaded by his rivals on the way to a game of polo; I'timad Khan himself
was driven out, recalled, and driven out again as the factions that had
put him back in power turned on him within months. By the time any of
them thought to look outward, the kingdom they were fighting over had
almost nothing left to defend it with.
