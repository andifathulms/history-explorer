Zafar Khan was the son of a Rajput convert to Islam, Wajih-ul-Mulk of
Didwana, and in 1391 the Delhi sultan Muhammad Shah sent him to govern a
province whose existing administration had already stopped answering to
the capital in anything but name. His predecessor Farhat-ul-Mulk, long
since a law unto himself, met him in the field at Gambhir in January 1392
and was defeated and mortally wounded — the first act of a governorship
that spent the next several years dragging Gujarat's disorderly nobles
back under one authority.

## A pretext, not yet a proclamation

Delhi gave him his opening without meaning to. In 1396 two rival kings,
Mahmud Shah and Nusrat Shah, were both claiming the Delhi throne at once,
and with no way to tell which one deserved his allegiance, Zafar Khan
simply stopped rendering any — the moment historians mark as the true
founding of an independent Gujarat, though he had been ruling the
province as though it already were one for years.

## A son's coup and a brother's poison

Old age slowed him rather than sons' patience. His son Tatar Khan, eager
to march on a Delhi thrown into chaos by Timur's invasion, grew tired of
waiting, seized his own father, imprisoned him, and had himself crowned
Nasir-ud-din Muhammad Shah. Zafar Khan answered from captivity: he
persuaded his own brother, whom the new king had just appointed regent,
to follow Tatar Khan's camp and poison his wine. The regent did exactly
that, freed his imprisoned brother, and restored him to the throne he had
been forced off — and Zafar Khan, in 1407, ascended once more, this time
under the title Sultan Muzaffar, a father who had outlived his own son's
rebellion by arranging his murder.

## What his grandson built on the ruins

Muzaffar died in June 1411, and it was his grandson Ahmad who gave the
kingdom the seat it would keep for the rest of its existence. Putting
down a rebellion of his own uncles without bloodshed and fighting off a
fresh invasion from Malwa, Ahmad Shah built a new capital, Ahmadabad, on
the site of the old town of Asawal — a city that would outlast every
dynasty that ever ruled from it, standing today as Gujarat's largest.
