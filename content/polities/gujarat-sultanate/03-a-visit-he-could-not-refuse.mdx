title: A visit he could not refuse

Bahadur Shah, who took the throne in July 1526, ruled a kingdom whose
reach eventually stretched, on his own historian's count, from Gujarat and
Khandesh through Malwa, Ajmer, Chitor and a dozen lesser conquests to the
Deccan itself. His decisive campaign came on 17 March 1531, when he
captured Mandu and extinguished the Khalji dynasty of Malwa outright —
absorbing, at a stroke, the neighbouring kingdom whose own wars with
Gujarat stretched back over a century.

## An empire's worth of enemies at once

Success on that scale drew in an opponent Bahadur could not out-manoeuvre.
Humayun, alarmed by a Gujarati sultan strong enough to absorb an entire
neighbouring kingdom, marched against him, forced him into a humiliating
flight from his own entrenched camp at Mandasor, and pursued him as far as
Diu on the coast, where Bahadur had already, out of desperation, invited
the one power capable of checking the Mughals: Portugal.

## An ally he meant to betray

The alliance with the Portuguese was never meant to last. Bahadur granted
them a fortress at Diu in exchange for naval support against Humayun,
regained his kingdom once the Mughal threat had passed, and then began
quietly planning to remove the ally he had needed only months before —
a plan the Portuguese commander at Diu was well aware of before it was
ever attempted.

## The negotiation that became an ambush

Late in 1536 Bahadur pressed the Portuguese governor to meet him in
person, and when repeated excuses failed to put him off, the king was
finally persuaded, against every one of his own counsellors' advice, to
visit the governor's ship on 13 February 1537. What began as a courtesy
call turned into a trap the moment he tried to leave: Portuguese boats
closed around his barge, swords were drawn, and in the fighting that
followed Bahadur leapt into the water rather than be taken. He drowned
within sight of his own coast, along with every officer who had
accompanied him aboard.

## A judgement history was still willing to make in his favour

His own court historian, weighing his one real strategic blunder at
Mandasor against a reign of real conquest, called him liberal, generous
and valiant, with a loftier ambition than any king of his line before him
— and even Haig, writing centuries later with the Portuguese trap laid
bare, allows that the judgement was not undeserved. What followed his
death bore out the historian's harsher verdict on everything after him:
Bahadur left no son, and the three kings who nominally succeeded him
would rule, in Haig's own words, as puppets in the hands of a nobility no
longer answerable to anyone above them.
