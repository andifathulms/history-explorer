By 1572 the regent I'timad Khan had run out of Gujarati allies willing to
back him and did what a decade of factional war had made almost routine
among the kingdom's own nobles: he invited a foreign army in against his
rivals, writing to the Mughal emperor Akbar and asking him to invade.

## An empire that barely had to fight

Akbar needed little persuading. His army reached Patan while the noble
besieging Ahmadabad was still in the field, and the mere approach of an
imperial force was enough to send Gujarat's contending factions
scattering in every direction, Muzaffar III among them. Found in hiding
near Akbar's own camp that November, the fabricated king who had reigned
for a decade over nothing more than his nobles' quarrels was brought
before the emperor, detained as a political prisoner, and Gujarat was
formally annexed to the Mughal empire without a single significant
battle having been fought for it.

## A prisoner's second chance, and what he did with it

Akbar did not keep Muzaffar confined indefinitely. He was eventually
allowed to live out his life quietly in Kathiawar — a mercy the deposed
king did not accept as final. In 1583, more than a decade after his
capture, a fresh rebellion against Mughal rule offered what looked like a
path back to the throne, and Muzaffar joined it.

<Figure id="gj-coins" />

## Ten years a fugitive, then a blade

The rebellion gave him nothing but ten more years as a hunted man, the
greater part of them spent in flight from one refuge to the next. In
1593 imperial troops finally ran him to ground. Rather than be taken
alive a second time, Muzaffar III — a king whose very claim to royal
blood had been invented by the regent who crowned him — cut his own
throat, closing, on his own terms if nothing else, a reign that had
opened with somebody else's oath.
