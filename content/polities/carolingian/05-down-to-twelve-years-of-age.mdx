In 802, two years after the coronation, Charlemagne issued a long capitulary
setting out what his inspectors were to do and what his subjects had sworn. It
survives, and it is the clearest statement anywhere of how a government with no
salaried officials, no tax and no capital proposed to govern.

It opens with the inspectors. The emperor chose from among his nobles the most
prudent and wisest men — archbishops, bishops, venerable abbots and pious laymen —
and sent them over the whole kingdom, so that men should live according to law
and right. And where anything in the law is otherwise than according to right and
justice, they are to inquire into it most diligently and make it known to him, and
he, God granting, hopes to better it.

Then a sentence that tells you who the emperor thought the enemy was. Let no one,
through his cleverness or astuteness — *as many are accustomed to do* — dare to
oppose the written law, or the sentence passed upon him, or prevail against the
churches of God, or the poor, or widows, or minors, or any Christian man.

## The oath, and what it was discovered to mean

The second section is the important one, and the reason it is important is that
it is a correction.

Everyone in the kingdom who had sworn fealty to Charlemagne as king was now to
swear it again to him as emperor, and everyone who had not sworn was to do so —
*all, down to those under twelve years of age*. And it was to be publicly
explained to all of them, so that each one should understand it, how many things
that oath contains.

Because, the document says, many had hitherto believed it meant only fidelity as
regards the emperor's life, not bringing an enemy into the kingdom, and not
consenting to or keeping silent about another man's disloyalty. It means more
than that. And the list follows:

> Firstly, that every one of his own accord should strive, according to his
> intelligence and strength, wholly to keep himself in the holy service of God
> according to the precept of God and to his own promise — inasmuch as the
> emperor can not exhibit the necessary care and discipline to each man singly.

That clause is the Carolingian state's whole theory of itself. The emperor cannot
supervise everybody, so everybody is put under oath to supervise himself, and the
oath is given a religious content so that breaking it is a sin as well as a crime.
A government that cannot reach into a village has made every adult and every
twelve-year-old in it a sworn agent of its purposes.

The rest of the list is more ordinary: nobody is to conceal a runaway imperial
slave, or plunder churches, widows, orphans or strangers, or dodge the summons to
arms, or let a count release a man from military service for a bribe or out of
regard for kinship. And then a clause against professional pleaders — nobody is to
make a practice of arguing another man's case in court out of cupidity, or to
impede a just judgment by cleverness; each man is to plead his own, unless he is
infirm or does not know how, in which case the inspectors or the judge shall plead
for him.

## What the government was worried about

Read as a list of anxieties rather than as a legal code, the capitulary is candid.

Counts are not to conceal thieves, robbers, murderers, adulterers, magicians or
witches under any pretext, induced by reward or flattery. Judges are to judge
justly, *according to the written law and not according to their own judgment*.
Bishops and abbots are to have bailiffs and judges who are lovers of justice,
because harmful or greedy ones produce the greatest evils. Abbots are to live
where the monks are, wholly with the monks. Priests and deacons keeping women in
their houses lose their position and their inheritance. Judges, counts and envoys
are not to extort payment of a fine from a destitute man after the emperor has
forgiven it.

And two clauses that are not about control at all. No one throughout the realm
may deny hospitality to the rich, to the poor, or to pilgrims: nobody shall refuse
shelter and fire and water to anyone travelling in God's service. And anyone who
comes to the emperor's presence seeking alms on account of indigence or hunger,
Christian or pagan, is not to be constrained to service, or taken possession of,
or sold — and whoever transgresses that shall atone for it with his life.

## The feud

The longest single clause is about murder, and its argument is worth following
because it shows a government trying to legislate its way out of something it
could not police.

It begins with theology — how can any man hope to be pleasing to God who has slain
His son, and where can one flee God, to whom all secrets are manifest? — and then
arrives at the practical instruction. When a murder has happened, the guilty man
is to hasten at once to compound with the dead man's relatives. And then the
clause that is really the point:

The relatives of the dead man shall by no means dare to carry their enmity
further, or refuse to make peace with him who seeks it; they shall pledge their
faith and make a lasting peace, and the killer shall pay the wergeld without
delay.

Compensation for killing was old and universal. What this adds is compulsion on
the victim's side: the injured family is forbidden to decline the money. An empire
held together by oaths and a few hundred interrelated families could survive a
great many murders and could not survive the feuds that followed them, and so a
capitulary issued in the emperor's name orders grieving relatives, under the royal
ban, to accept a payment and shake hands.
