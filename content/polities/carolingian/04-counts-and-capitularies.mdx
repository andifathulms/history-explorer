There was no capital until Charlemagne settled at Aachen in his sixties, and
Aachen was a palace with hot springs rather than a city. There was no
bureaucracy, no salaried civil service, no archive of the kind this corpus's
Byzantine and Abbasid records assume, and no tax.

What there was: counts, capitularies and inspectors.

A count held a district, dispensed justice, raised the host and took a third of
the judicial fines. He was appointed by the king from the aristocracy, and could
be moved — which is the whole difference between this and what came after, and
the subject of this record's third turning point. Bishops and abbots held
comparable authority and were appointed the same way.

Capitularies were the king's written instructions, issued in chapters, on
everything from the conduct of counts to the management of royal estates to the
correct singing of the liturgy. Several hundred survive. Whether they were
enforced is the central argument in the field; McKitterick's position, and the
reason this record's institutions are drafted from her, is that the number of
surviving manuscript copies made in the provinces is evidence that people at
least thought they mattered.

And the *missi dominici*, the lord's envoys: pairs of inspectors, one lay and one
clerical, sent out in circuits to hear complaints against counts, administer the
oath of loyalty and report back. It is a serious attempt at supervision by a
government with no standing presence anywhere.

## What held it together

Oaths and the aristocracy, mostly.

Every free man was required to swear loyalty to the emperor personally, renewed
at intervals and administered by the missi. The great families that supplied the
counts were interrelated, held land across the whole empire rather than in one
region, and had an interest in the empire staying one thing because their
property did not fit inside any single part of it.

That last point is worth holding onto for the fifth chapter, because it is what
changed. As long as a magnate's estates were scattered from the Loire to the
Rhine he needed one emperor. Once the empire had been divided three times and the
frontier ran through his holdings, he had to choose a king, and after that he
was a regional figure whose interests were regional.

## And the books

The court gathered Alcuin from Northumbria, Paul the Deacon from Lombardy,
Theodulf from Spain, and set the monasteries copying. That programme is the
subject of this record's last chapter and it is the reason the empire is
remembered at all outside a specialist literature.

It is also the clearest case on this site of a coded field understating a polity,
and the last chapter says so. The three influence counts measure successor
claims, religions carried and descendant scripts. The Carolingian achievement is
that the great majority of surviving classical Latin literature reaches us in
ninth-century Frankish copies, and that the letterforms of this sentence are
theirs, and neither is any of those three things.
