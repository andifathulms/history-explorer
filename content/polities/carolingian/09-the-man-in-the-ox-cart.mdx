Nearly every familiar image of Charlemagne comes from one short book written by
a courtier who had served him for over twenty years, on the model of Suetonius,
in the 820s, after the man had been dead for some time and the family's grip on
the empire had started to slip.

Einhard's *Life* is the reason we think we know what Charlemagne looked like,
what he ate, that he swam, that he kept writing tablets under his pillow and
practised making letters and could never manage it. It is also a defence brief,
and it is at its most transparent in the first two pages, before Charlemagne has
appeared at all.

## The kings the family displaced

The book opens on the Merovingians. Einhard is explaining why it was legitimate
for his employer's grandfather to take a crown that belonged to somebody else,
and his method is to make the somebody else ridiculous.

The Merovingian line, he says, had long since been devoid of vital strength and
conspicuous only for bearing the empty title of king. Real power lay with the
mayor of the palace. There was nothing left for the king to do but be content
with his name, his flowing hair and his long beard, sit on his throne and play
the ruler, and receive ambassadors and dismiss them in words that had been
suggested to him or imposed on him outright. He owned nothing except the title
and one country estate with a small house and enough servants to run it.

And then the detail everybody remembers:

> When he had to go abroad, he used to ride in a cart, drawn by a yoke of oxen,
> driven, peasant-fashion, by a ploughman; he rode in this way to the palace and
> to the general assembly of the people, that met once a year for the welfare of
> the kingdom, and he returned home in like manner.

It is devastating, and it is probably not quite true. The long hair was the
badge of Merovingian royalty and of free status; the ox-cart appears to have
been an ancient royal custom rather than a humiliation, which is why the king
used it to travel to the annual assembly of his own people. Einhard has taken
two pieces of sacral kingship and presented them as the props of a fool.

Then, three sentences later, he records that the last of them was deposed,
*shaved*, and put into a monastery. Cutting the hair off was the deposition. If
the hair had been the empty vanity Einhard has just called it, there would have
been no need to cut it.

## Why it matters

A courtier writing about a dynasty he serves is not a neutral witness, and this
one is unusually easy to catch because he tells you what he is doing. He
declines to describe the crossing of the Alps on the grounds that his subject is
the manner of the emperor's life rather than the incidents of his wars — having
already described the trackless ridges, the heaven-aspiring cliffs and the
ragged peaks in the act of refusing. He says it would be folly to write about
Charlemagne's birth and boyhood because nothing was ever written on the subject
and nobody now alive can supply it, which is an honest admission and also a
convenient way past the years when the family's claim was weakest.

The *Life* is where almost everything vivid about this empire comes from, and
the vividness was manufactured by somebody with a position to protect. Both of
those things are true at once, and the second one does not cancel the first. It
does mean that the picture of the last Merovingian kings — long-haired, useless,
trundling to the assembly behind a pair of oxen — is a portrait painted by the
people who took their kingdom, and the only one anybody has.
