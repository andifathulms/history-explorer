Charles the Fat had the whole empire back, briefly, from 884 — not by conquest
but because every other adult male Carolingian had died. He held it for three
years, failed to relieve Paris from a Viking siege and paid the besiegers to go
away instead, and was deposed by an assembly of magnates in November 887. He
died two months later.

<Figure id="viking-ship" />

Nobody replaced him. The Annals of Fulda say that the kingdoms which had obeyed
him fell apart into pieces and, not waiting for their natural lord, each made a
king out of its own guts.

Five or six kingdoms emerged in a year, and only one of the men who took them
was a Carolingian in the male line. That is where the empire stops.

## Nobody conquered it

That is worth stating flatly, because it is unusual. Empires normally end
because somebody ends them, and where no conqueror is named it is usually
because no source survives to name one rather than because there was none.

Here it is positively established that there was no external agent. The
magnates of each region chose a king from among themselves. The empire had been
divided by inheritance until the pieces were the units that mattered, and the
offices had become hereditary until the men holding them were the ones who
decided.

The machinery that took the empire apart was the machinery that had been
running it all along.

## What was left standing

The kingdoms. West Francia and East Francia had been created at Verdun
forty-five years earlier and they did not go away; the men who held them in 888
are the beginning of the French and German royal successions.

The church. Bishoprics and monasteries had been endowed with land and
privileges throughout, and they were the institutions that survived the ninth
century intact, with their libraries, their scriptoria and their claim to
appoint their own heads that the eleventh century fought about.

And the title. Otto I — a Saxon, from the people Charlemagne had conquered and
baptised at spearpoint — was crowned emperor at Rome in 962, seventy-four years
after the last Carolingian emperor was deposed, in explicit revival of
Charlemagne's empire. The office went on being claimed for another eight
centuries by men who had never held a foot of Frankish ground.

What the empire actually left, though, is not political at all.
