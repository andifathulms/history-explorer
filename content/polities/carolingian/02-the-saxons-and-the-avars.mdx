Two campaigns on the eastern side of the empire did more to determine what kind
of state it was than the coronation did, and neither is remembered.

## Saxony

The Saxon wars ran from 772 to 804. There was no Saxon king to defeat and no
capital to take: the country was a set of regional groupings that could submit,
disperse and reassemble, and the Frankish method was to invade every year,
extract oaths, build fortresses, and come back when the oaths were broken.

The instruments were baptism and deportation. Submission meant accepting
Christianity; the capitulary Charlemagne issued for Saxony in the 780s prescribes
death for refusing baptism, for cremating the dead, for eating meat in Lent. Tens
of thousands of Saxons were moved west and Franks settled east. It took a
generation and it worked, in the sense that Saxony was Christian and Frankish
afterwards and produced the dynasty that revived the empire a century and a half
later.

It is worth being exact about what that means. Latin Christianity was carried
into Saxony, and the instrument was an army. Missionaries followed the
capitularies rather than preceding them, and the durable ecclesiastical
province that resulted — bishoprics, monasteries, tithes — was built on ground
cleared by thirty years of annual invasion. Calling that the spread of a
religion is accurate. Calling it anything gentler is not.

## The Avar ring

The Avars had held the Hungarian plain for two hundred and fifty years and had
spent most of that period extracting subsidies from Constantinople, which paid
rather than fight them. The proceeds sat in a fortified encampment the Frankish
sources call the ring.

In 795 and 796 Frankish armies took it. The Royal Frankish Annals describe
fifteen wagons of gold and silver, each drawn by four oxen, going west. Einhard
says that the Franks had been poor and became rich, and lists the gifts
Charlemagne sent out of it to the pope, to his followers and to churches across
the empire.

## What a state without taxation does

That is why the treasure matters more here than a heap of loot usually does.

Wickham's argument is that the single largest
difference between the Roman world and the early medieval west is the
disappearance of the land tax. A Carolingian king had no assessment, no census,
no cadastre and no revenue officials. What he had was his own estates — which
were vast — plus tolls, gifts, judicial fines, and whatever the annual campaign
produced.

An empire funded that way has to keep campaigning, and its capacity is limited by
what the king personally owns. It cannot pay a standing army, so it fights with
an obligation on landholders. It cannot pay officials, so it rewards them with
land, which they then own.

Two and a half centuries of Byzantine subsidy, paid to the Avars to keep them
quiet, moved west in fifteen ox-drawn wagons and made the difference between a
poor Frankish reign and a rich one. Einhard says plainly that the Franks had
been poor and became rich. That is a description of a
state whose finances were an event rather than a system, and the arithmetic of
it eventually ran out.
