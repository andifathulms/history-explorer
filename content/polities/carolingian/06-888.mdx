Charles the Fat had the whole empire back, briefly, from 884 — not by conquest
but because every other adult male Carolingian had died. He held it for three
years, failed to relieve Paris from a Viking siege and paid the besiegers to go
away instead, and was deposed by an assembly of magnates in November 887. He died
two months later.

Nobody replaced him. The Annals of Fulda say that the kingdoms which had obeyed
him fell apart into pieces and, not waiting for their natural lord, each made a
king out of its own guts.

Five or six kingdoms emerged in a year, and only one of the men who took them was
a Carolingian in the male line. That is where this record stops.

## `fragmentation`, and `by` is empty

The field is exactly right for once, which is worth noting on a site that spends
a good deal of time arguing that its closed vocabularies are the least wrong of
six options.

Nobody conquered the Carolingian empire. There is no external agent to name in
`by`, and unlike the Hittite and Middle Assyrian records — where a conqueror
probably existed and no source names one — here it is positively established that
there was not one. The magnates of each region chose a king from among
themselves, because the empire had been divided by inheritance until the pieces
were the units that mattered and the offices had become hereditary until the men
holding them were the ones who decided.

The mechanism is in this record's `succession_rule` field, and it is the only
record in this corpus where reading the four institution fields in order gives
you the ending.

## What was left standing

The kingdoms. West Francia and East Francia had been created at Verdun forty-five
years earlier and they did not go away; the men who held them in 888 are the
beginning of the French and German royal successions.

The church. Bishoprics and monasteries had been endowed with land and privileges
throughout, and they were the institutions that survived the ninth century
intact, with their libraries, their scriptoria and their claim to appoint their
own heads that the eleventh century fought about.

And the title, which had seventy-four years of history and eight hundred and
forty-four still to run. Otto I, a Saxon — from the people this record's second
chapter describes being conquered and baptised at spearpoint — was crowned
emperor at Rome in 962 in explicit revival of Charlemagne's empire, and this
record's `successor_claims` count of 1 is that.

The last chapter is about what the empire actually left, which is not political
and does not appear in any field on this page.
