On Christmas Day 800 the pope crowned Charlemagne emperor in Rome. There had not
been an emperor in the west since 476, and there was already one in the east, who
regarded the coronation as a usurpation — the more so because the eastern throne
was at that moment occupied by a woman, Irene, which the Frankish position held
to mean it was vacant.

<Figure id="aachen-chapel" />

The empire itself was Frankish expansion given a Roman title: Gaul, most of
Germany after thirty years of Saxon wars, northern Italy, and a march across the
Pyrenees. It was administered through counts and royal envoys rather than a
bureaucracy, and its most durable product was not political but cultural — a
programme of manuscript copying and script reform in the monasteries that
preserved the bulk of the Latin literature that survives, in a new hand,
Carolingian minuscule, that is the ancestor of the lower case in this sentence.

## The elephant

The best evidence that this was one connected world rather than several is an
animal.

Charlemagne and Harun al-Rashid exchanged embassies, and Harun sent an elephant
named Abul-Abbas, which walked from Baghdad to Aachen and lived there for eight
years. Both rulers had a reason. Harun's rival was the Umayyad emirate at
Cordoba, in Spain, on Charlemagne's southern frontier. Charlemagne's rival was
Byzantium, on Harun's northern one. Each was the other's enemy's enemy, and both
courts understood the geography well enough to act on it.

An emperor in Aachen and a caliph in Baghdad, neither of whom could reach the
other with an army, worked out that they had the same two problems and could do
something about it. The elephant is the part everyone remembers; the strategic
calculation behind it is the part that says what the ninth century was actually
like.

The empire itself was divided among Louis the Pious's sons at Verdun in 843 and
had effectively dissolved by 888 — by inheritance practice rather than by
defeat.
