Louis the Pious inherited the whole empire in 814 because his two elder brothers
had died, which is the only reason it passed intact. He then spent twenty-six
years trying to arrange that it would happen again on purpose.

The Ordinatio Imperii of 817 is a document trying to hold two incompatible
things. The empire is to remain one and
indivisible, because it is Roman and an emperor is singular. And it is to be
divided among the sons, because that is what Frankish inheritance does. Louis's
solution was one emperor with two subordinate kings, all three brothers, the
younger two owing the elder obedience.

Then a fourth son was born to a second wife, the settlement was reopened to
provide for him, and the three elder sons rebelled against their father — twice,
deposing him once and restoring him, in a sequence involving a public penance, an
assembly at a place called the Field of Lies, and papal intervention on both
sides.

## Verdun

Louis died in 840 and his sons fought for three years. The Treaty of Verdun in
843 divided the empire into three, and the method is the most revealing detail
in the whole business: surveyors were sent out to assess the territories so that
the three shares would be equal in revenue.

That is not how a Roman empire is divided. It is how an estate is divided
between heirs, and it is the same instinct that broke up the Seljuk and Ayyubid
realms in the following centuries: a kingdom treated as family property to be
shared out rather than as an office that only one man can hold.

West Francia and East Francia became France and Germany. The middle share,
Lotharingia, ran from the North Sea to Rome, had the imperial title and the two
capitals, no natural frontier on either side, and was fought over until 1945.

## Quierzy

In 877 Charles the Bald, about to leave for Italy, issued a capitulary providing
that if a count died while he was away, the count's son should hold the office
until he returned to decide.

It was a provision for one absence. It was read afterwards as a concession of
what was already happening: that offices held at the king's pleasure,
transferable, supervised by travelling inspectors, were becoming hereditary
family property.

The arithmetic behind it is simple. A state with no tax rewards
its servants with land. Land given is land gone. The Carolingians had given away
a great deal of the royal estate over three generations, in benefices that were
supposed to revert and did not, and by the 870s a king had less to give and less
leverage over the men holding what had already been given.

## The Vikings

They arrive in the middle of all this and are usually given the credit.

The raids began in the 790s, became annual and then permanent from the 840s, and
by the 880s Viking armies were wintering in the Seine and Loire valleys and
besieging Paris. The response — local defence by whoever was there, fortified
bridges, payments to make them go away — was necessarily local, and it made the
regional commander more important than the distant king.

That is real and it is an accelerant rather than a cause. The empire was already
being divided by inheritance and its offices were already becoming hereditary
before the great raids started. What the Vikings did was make the case for a
regional strongman unanswerable.
