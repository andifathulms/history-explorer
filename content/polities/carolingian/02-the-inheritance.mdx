The empire lasted eighty-eight years and its political achievements did not
survive the ninth century. Almost everything durable about it is textual.

The Carolingian court gathered scholars from Northumbria, Italy, Spain and
Ireland, and set the monasteries to copying. The great majority of surviving
classical Latin literature reaches us through Carolingian manuscripts: the
oldest copy of most Roman authors is ninth-century, made in a Frankish
scriptorium, from a late antique exemplar that then perished. Without that
programme the western classical canon would be a fraction of its size.

They also reformed the script. Carolingian minuscule — clear, separated words,
distinguished letterforms, a functioning lower case — replaced a set of regional
hands that had become nearly illegible to anyone outside the region that wrote
them. Italian humanists rediscovered it in the fifteenth century, took it for
Roman, and cut their type to match; every roman typeface since descends from
that mistake.

This site does not count that as a descendant script. Under the coding rules
minuscule is a change of hand within the Latin alphabet, not a new writing
system or an adaptation to a new language, so the count on this page is null.
That is the rules working correctly and producing a page that understates the
polity, which is the trade the rules were chosen for: they can be applied
uniformly, and uniform application sometimes costs something.
