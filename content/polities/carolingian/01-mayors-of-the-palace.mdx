The family that took the imperial title in 800 had been running the Frankish
kingdoms for four generations without holding a throne.

The office was mayor of the palace, originally a household steward and by the
seventh century the effective head of government, with the Merovingian kings
reduced to a ceremonial function that Einhard later described with contempt and
that was probably less abject than he made it. Charles Martel held it, beat an
Arab raiding force near Poitiers in 732 and campaigned against everybody else,
and did not take the crown.

His son Pepin did, in 751, and how he did it is the beginning of everything on
this record. He sent to the pope to ask who ought to be called king — the man who
had the power or the man who had the title — received the answer he had
commissioned, deposed the last Merovingian into a monastery, and had himself
anointed with holy oil in a ceremony borrowed from the Old Testament that
Frankish kingship had not previously used.

## What anointing bought

It made the king a sacred person. That is why this record's `legitimation` field
carries `divine-sanction` and not merely `descent`: a Merovingian was king by
being of the blood of Merovech, and a Carolingian was king because God, through
the church, had made him one, which is a claim available to a family that had no
blood claim at all.

It also created a debt. Pepin crossed the Alps twice on the papacy's behalf
against the Lombards and gave the pope the territories that became the papal
state. The relationship that produced the coronation of 800 — and the later papal
argument that an empire conferred could be withdrawn — starts here, with a
usurpation that needed a churchman's opinion.

## Charlemagne's wars

He reigned forty-six years and campaigned in most of them, which is a fiscal
statement as much as a military one and the fourth chapter is about why.

The Lombard kingdom in northern Italy was taken in 774 and Charlemagne added its
crown to his own. Bavaria was absorbed in 788. The Spanish march was pushed
across the Pyrenees, in a campaign whose rearguard was destroyed at Roncesvalles
in 778 by Basques and became, three centuries later, the Song of Roland.

And the Saxons. Thirty-two years of war east of the Rhine against a people with
no state to surrender, fought by annual invasion, forced baptism, mass
deportation and, at Verden in 782, the reported execution of four thousand five
hundred prisoners in a day. It ended with Saxony Christian and Frankish, and it
is the campaign this record's `religions_carried` count is made from.

By 800 the Frankish realm ran from the Ebro to the Elbe and from the North Sea to
Rome, which had not been assembled by anybody since the western empire on this
site's Roman record stopped existing. The next chapter is about what to call it.
