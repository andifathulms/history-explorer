In 883 Charles the Fat spent three days at the monastery of St Gall and asked a
monk there to write about his great-grandfather. The book that came of it
addresses him throughout as a descendant — *your great-grandfather Charles*,
*your most glorious father Lewis*, *your most religious grandfather Lewis called
the pious*. The emperor it was written for held the whole empire at that moment,
for the last time, and was deposed four years later.

Notker is not Einhard. He had not known Charlemagne, he had few documents, and he
takes unusual trouble to say where his material comes from: an old monk of his own
house, and that monk's father Adalbert, who had followed the queen's brother
through the wars against the Avars, the Saxons and the Slavs. The picture Notker
gives of the arrangement is of an old soldier anxious to tell and a young monk
unwilling to listen — and the stories were repeated often enough that not only the
facts but the words stuck. What he produced is a collection of anecdotes, and its
historical value is not what it reports but what it shows: seventy years after the
man died, with people who had marched behind him still alive to be asked, the
legend was already finished.

## Pavia

The set piece is the approach to Pavia in 774, and it is not written like prose.

The Lombard king Desiderius is on a tower with an exiled Frankish noble named
Otker, watching the army come. The baggage waggons appear: *Is Charles in that
vast army?* Not yet. The gathered forces of all the nations: *Surely Charles moves
in pride among those forces.* Not yet, not yet. The personal attendants: *There is
Charles.* Not yet, not yet. The bishops and abbots and the clerks of the chapel.
At that Desiderius wants to hide himself in the earth. And Otker, who had once had
thorough knowledge of the preparations of the unconquerable Charles, tells him
what to watch for:

> When you see an iron harvest bristling in the fields; and the Po and the Ticino
> pouring against the walls of the city like the waves of the sea, gleaming black
> with glint of iron, then know that Charles is at hand.

Then the emperor arrives, and the word is repeated twenty times in a paragraph. An
iron helmet, iron gauntlets, an iron breastplate, an iron spear, an iron falchion,
iron plates on the thighs, iron greaves, an iron shield, a horse iron-coloured and
iron-hearted, everyone around him copying him as closely as possible, the fields
filled with iron, the sun's rays thrown back from it. *A people harder than iron
paid universal honour to the hardness of iron.* And from the walls, the cry: *Oh
the iron! Woe for the iron!*

Otker looks at all this and says, *There is the Charles that you so much desired
to see*, and falls to the ground half dead.

Nobody stood on a tower and said that. The refrain, the threefold denial, the
watchword, the collapse — it is the shape of a song, written out as prose by a man
who had heard it sung.

## Why it is worth having

Because of when it was made.

The empire the iron army had built lasted eighty-eight years. Notker was writing
in its last decade, for the last man to hold all of it, at a court that would
shortly pay a Viking army to leave Paris because it could not beat it. The Charlemagne in his
pages — supernaturally shrewd, protector of Jerusalem, terrifying at a distance —
is not a memory of a government. It is what a government's descendants tell each
other when the government is failing.

And the anecdote he puts nearest this one gives the trick away. Recalling the
reign of Charlemagne's grandson, Notker says that Germany was made to pay a penny
an acre towards ransoming Christian captives in the Holy Land, and that the appeal
was made *in the name of the dominion anciently held over that land by your
great-grandfather Charles*. Charlemagne never held the Holy Land. Somebody was
already raising money on the claim that he had.

That is the afterlife of this empire beginning while it is still going. Two
centuries later the Crusaders believed they were following Charlemagne's road to
Jerusalem; in 1165 an emperor had him canonised; by the Renaissance he was the
dim centre of a cycle of poems about knights who never existed. The process starts
here, in a monastery in Switzerland, in a book written to flatter a man who was
about to lose everything his great-grandfather had assembled.
