The empire lasted eighty-eight years and its political achievements did not
survive the ninth century. Almost everything durable about it is textual.

The Carolingian court gathered scholars from Northumbria, Italy, Spain and
Ireland, and set the monasteries to copying. The great majority of surviving
classical Latin literature reaches us through Carolingian manuscripts: for most
Roman authors the oldest copy in existence is ninth-century, made in a Frankish
scriptorium, from a late antique exemplar that afterwards perished. Take that
programme out of the ninth century and the western classical canon is a fraction
of its size — not because the works would have been condemned, but because
parchment rots and nobody else was copying.

## The letters

They also reformed the script, and the reform is the reason this is legible.

The hands in use across the post-Roman west had drifted apart into regional
varieties that had become nearly unreadable to anybody outside the region that
wrote them, run together, heavily abbreviated, and inconsistent from one
scriptorium to the next. Carolingian minuscule replaced them with something
designed to be read by a stranger: clear letterforms distinguished from one
another, words separated by spaces, a functioning lower case, capitals used for
a reason.

That is a piece of infrastructure rather than an ornament. A text is only as
durable as the next person's ability to read it, and an empire that wanted its
capitularies obeyed in Aquitaine and its liturgy sung correctly in Bavaria had a
practical stake in a hand that travelled.

Then it travelled further than intended. Italian humanists in the fifteenth
century found ninth-century manuscripts of classical authors, took the hand they
were written in for the hand of the Romans themselves, and cut their type to
match it. Every roman typeface since descends from that mistake. The letters in
front of you are a Renaissance reproduction of a Frankish reform of a late Roman
cursive, adopted because somebody misdated a manuscript.

## What it is not

None of that is a conquest, a conversion or a dynasty, and it is worth being
clear that the empire's most durable product is one nobody at the time would
have listed among its achievements.

Charlemagne's own reputation rested on Saxony, Italy, the Avars and the imperial
title. Two of those were undone within a century of his death and a third passed
to a Saxon. The scriptoria were a means to an end — correct texts for a correct
liturgy, run by a king who thought a botched psalm was a governance problem.

What survived is the means. The empire fell apart because Frankish inheritance
custom took it apart and there was no tax revenue to hold it together; the
libraries it endowed were the one asset that could not be divided among three
brothers, granted away as a benefice, or lost at Verdun. Bishops and abbots kept
them, and went on copying after there was no emperor to ask them to.

Eleven hundred years later the political settlement is a subject for
specialists, and the classical literature and the alphabet are common property.
