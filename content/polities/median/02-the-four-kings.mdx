There is exactly one narrative of how the Medes became a power, and Herodotus
wrote it. It runs to four kings and about a hundred and fifty years, and it is
worth setting out at length before anyone starts arguing with it, because
everything else ever said about Median history is either drawn from this
account or written against it.

## Deioces builds a wall and disappears behind it

Deioces was chosen to rule by Medes who wanted their disputes settled, and the
first thing he asked for was a bodyguard and a palace. The second was a city.
He compelled the Medes to make one fortified city and to pay chief attention to
it, and there he built the walls the Greeks called Agbatana — seven of them,
standing in circles one within the other, each ring higher than the next by the
height of its battlements, with the palace and the treasure-houses inside the
last. The battlements of the first circle were white, of the second black, of
the third crimson, of the fourth blue, of the fifth red; the two innermost were
overlaid with silver and with gold.

Then he made himself invisible.

He established the rule, Herodotus says, which he was the first to establish:
that nobody should enter the presence of the king, that all business should be
done through messengers, and that the king should be seen by no one. To laugh
or to spit in his presence was forbidden, and that last prohibition applied to
everybody without exception, the king included.

The reason given is not mystical and is worth quoting for its bluntness. He
surrounded himself with this state so that his fellows, who had been brought up
with him and were of no meaner family nor behind him in manly virtue, might not
be grieved by seeing him and make plots against him, but that being unseen by
them he might be thought to be of different mould.

That is a working description of how a first among equals stops being one. The
men most dangerous to Deioces were the men who knew what he had been, and the
remedy was architecture. Meanwhile the people wrote down their causes and sent
them in; he judged them and sent them out again; and he kept watchers and
listeners over all the land he ruled.

## Phraortes attacks the wrong city

Deioces reigned fifty-three years and united only the Medes. His son Phraortes
was not satisfied with that. He marched first against the Persians and made
them subject, and then, ruling two strong nations instead of one, went from
people to people through Asia until he came to the Assyrians at Nineveh — who
by then, Herodotus notes, had been left without support because their allies
had revolted, though at home they were prosperous enough.

He was killed there, in the twenty-second year of his reign, and most of his
army with him.

## Cyaxares sorts the army into kinds

His son Cyaxares was, in Herodotus' judgement, much more warlike than his
forefathers, and the reason given is administrative rather than temperamental.
He was the first to band the men of Asia into separate divisions: he arrayed
the spearmen and the archers and the horsemen apart from one another, where
before that they had all been mingled together without distinction.

An army sorted into arms can be given orders by arm. That single sentence is
the closest thing in the Median record to an account of an institution, and
what it describes is the difference between a levy and a machine.

He fought the Lydians in the war that ended when the day turned to night, he
brought everything above the river Halys under his rule, and he went back to
Nineveh to avenge his father. He beat the Assyrians in the field and sat down
before the city.

## Twenty-eight years, ended at dinner

And then a great army of Scythians came upon him, under Madyas son of
Protothyes, who had driven the Cimmerians out of Europe and followed them the
long way round with the Caucasus on their right. The Medes fought them, were
beaten, and lost their power.

For twenty-eight years the Scythians ruled Asia. Herodotus says they exacted
the tribute they had laid on each people and then, quite separately from the
tribute, rode about and took whatever else they wanted, and that by this
combination of unruliness and recklessness everything was ruined.

What ended it was not a battle. Cyaxares invited the greater number of them to
a banquet, made them drunk, and killed them.

The Medes then recovered their power and their subjects, took Nineveh, and made
the Assyrians their subjects — all except the land of Babylon. Cyaxares died
having reigned forty years, counting the years the Scythians held, and Astyages
his son received the kingdom from him.

That is the story. Every element of it is arresting and none of it is
corroborated, and the next chapters are about the one part that is.
