title: An army that changed sides

Cyrus of Anshan revolted against Astyages around 553 and defeated him around
549. This site's Achaemenid record opens with the event and with the detail that
matters: the Median king's own army is said to have handed him over.

That story is in Herodotus, in Ctesias, and — importantly — in the Babylonian
Nabonidus Chronicle, which is contemporary and says that Astyages' troops
rebelled against him and delivered him to Cyrus. It is the second of the two
things about the Medes that a cuneiform document confirms.

What Cyrus did afterwards is the subject of this record's fourth chapter and is
the reason the Median question is hard. He did not treat Media as a conquered
country. Ecbatana became one of the Achaemenid royal capitals; Medes held high
office and appear beside Persians in the Persepolis reliefs and in the
administrative tablets; and the Achaemenid genealogy made Cyrus a grandson of
Astyages through his mother, which converts a usurpation into an inheritance.
Greek writers thereafter used Mede and Persian almost interchangeably — the
Persian wars are the Median wars in Greek, and *medizing* is the word for
collaborating with the enemy.

That treatment is either evidence that the Medes had been an empire worth
inheriting, or the reason we believe they were.

This record's edge to the Achaemenid page is marked `contested` for exactly that
circularity, and it is one of very few contested edges in the corpus. The site
carries the record because something was overthrown in 549 and because a cited
extent figure exists for it. It carries four null institutions because it is not
established that there was a state to have them. And it carries two turning
points, both dated by documents nobody Median wrote.

The Medes are the polity this corpus is least sure about, and moving the page
here — to the head of Iran before Islam, where the Achaemenid record's own first
chapter points — puts the uncertainty at the beginning of the sequence rather
than filed away in a different region. Twelve centuries of Iranian empire begin
with a question mark, and it is better to say so on the first page than to have
the second page assume it.
