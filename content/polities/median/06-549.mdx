Cyrus of Anshan revolted against Astyages around 553 and defeated him around
549, and the detail that matters is that the Median king's own army is said to
have handed him over.

That story is in Herodotus, in Ctesias, and — importantly — in the Babylonian
Nabonidus Chronicle, which is contemporary and says that Astyages' troops
rebelled against him and delivered him to Cyrus. It is the second of the two
things about the Medes that a cuneiform document confirms.

<Figure id="nabonidus-chronicle" />

What Cyrus did afterwards is the reason the Median question is hard. He did not
treat Media as a conquered country. Ecbatana became one of the Achaemenid royal
capitals; Medes held high office and appear beside Persians in the Persepolis
reliefs and in the administrative tablets; and the Achaemenid genealogy made
Cyrus a grandson of Astyages through his mother, which converts a usurpation
into an inheritance. Greek writers thereafter used Mede and Persian almost
interchangeably — the Persian wars are the Median wars in Greek, and *medizing*
is the word for collaborating with the enemy.

That treatment is either evidence that the Medes had been an empire worth
inheriting, or the reason we believe they were.

The succession from Media to Persia is disputed for exactly that circularity.
What is not disputed is that something was overthrown in 549. What cannot be
established is what it was: nothing survives to show how it was governed, and
the two events that can be dated are dated by documents no Mede wrote.

The Medes are the state everybody is least sure about, and they stand at the
head of twelve centuries of Iranian empire. That is worth saying at the start
rather than assuming past: the sequence that runs to the Sasanians begins with
a question mark, and the question is not a small one.
