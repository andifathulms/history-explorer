title: What the Assyrians wrote down

The Medes enter the written record in the ninth century BC, in Assyrian royal
inscriptions, and they enter it in the plural.

Assyrian kings campaigning east into the Zagros from Shalmaneser III onwards
record receiving tribute from the land of the Medes, and what they describe is
a country of *bel ali* — city-lords — many of them, named individually, holding
fortified settlements and horses. Assyria wanted the horses particularly; the
Zagros was where cavalry mounts came from, and the campaigns are largely about
extracting them. Sargon II lists dozens of Median chiefs. Esarhaddon made
treaties with several of them at once, and the tablets survive.

At no point in three centuries of Assyrian documentation is there a king of the
Medes. There are Median chiefs, plural, who pay, revolt, are punished, and pay
again.

That is where the evidence starts, and it sets the shape of everything after
it. Most states of this period can be introduced with a founder, a capital and
a date. The Medes have to be introduced with a plural noun in somebody else's
tribute list.

Herodotus supplies the rest — four kings, a capital at Ecbatana with seven
concentric walls in seven colours, an empire from the Halys to eastern Iran —
writing a century after it had ended, from Persian informants, in a work whose
Median section has the shape of a folk tale. The next chapter sets that account
out in full, and the one after it is about whether to believe him.

What is not disputed is that something out of western Iran destroyed Assyria in
612 BC, and that Cyrus took something over in 549. What was in between is the
argument.
