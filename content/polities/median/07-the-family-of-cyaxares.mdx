Twenty-seven years after Cyrus took Ecbatana, the name of a Median king was
still worth enough to raise an army with.

In 522 BC Darius seized the Persian throne and the empire came apart in his
hands. He spent something over a year putting it back together, and when he had
finished he had the whole account carved on a cliff at Behistun, above the road
from Babylon to Ecbatana, in three languages. It lists nine rebels. Two of them
claimed to be Medes of the same house.

## Phraortes

The first is named on the rock as Fravartish, whom the Greeks would call
Phraortes. Darius' own text puts the claim in his mouth in a single sentence:

> A certain Median named Phraortes revolted in Media, and he said unto the
> people: "I am Khshathrita, of the family of Cyaxares."

And the sentence that follows is the one worth stopping on. *Then did the
Medians who were in the palace revolt from me and go over unto that Phraortes;
he became king in Media.*

Not the tribes, not the hill country. The Medes at court — the ones inside the
Persian administration, who had presumably done well out of it — left and went
to a man on the strength of a great-grandfather's name.

The revolt was serious. Darius sent Hydarnes, who beat the Medes at Marush and
then could not finish the job and sat down to wait for the king. Parthia and
Hyrcania went over to Phraortes as well, and had to be put down separately by
Darius' own father. Only when Babylon was settled could Darius march into Media
himself. He caught Phraortes at Kundurush; the pretender fled to Raga with a few
horsemen and was taken.

What was done to him is recorded because Darius wanted it recorded. His nose and
ears were cut off, his eyes put out, his tongue cut out; he was chained and put
on display at the gate of the palace in Ecbatana, and then crucified.

## And the second one

There was another. Citrantakhma raised Sagartia, and the inscription says he
too gave himself out to be of the family of Cyaxares. Darius sent Takhmaspada —
a Mede — to deal with him, and had him mutilated in the same way and crucified
at Arbela.

Two men, in two provinces, in the same year, reaching for the same dead
dynasty. Whatever the Median kingdom had actually been, the memory of it was
still a live political instrument a generation after it stopped existing, and
Darius thought it dangerous enough to answer with a mutilation performed at the
gate of the Median capital and then published on a mountainside in three
scripts.

## The quieter inheritance

The rest of it went by absorption rather than revolt.

Ecbatana stayed a royal residence and became one of the Achaemenid capitals.
The Median tribes are still being listed as tribes. And Herodotus, describing
Persian habits a century later, remarks almost in passing that the Persians
wear the Median dress, judging it more comely than their own — the conquerors
dressed as the conquered, at court, as a matter of taste.

Herodotus records something else about the Median order that the Persians kept:
the nations under it governed one another in a graded chain, the Medes ruling
the whole and in particular their nearest neighbours, those ruling the peoples
who bordered on them, and those again the ones beyond. Rule passed outward by
stages rather than radiating from a centre. He says the Persians observed the
same rule in giving honour, which is to say they inherited the shape of the
thing along with the clothes.

A kingdom whose existence modern scholarship still argues about left behind a
court dress, a way of ranking subject peoples, and two men who were tortured to
death for saying they belonged to its royal house.
