The Neo-Assyrian empire on this site's Ancient Near East record was the most
formidable military state the world had seen, and in the space of about fifteen
years it stopped existing. The Babylonians revolted under Nabopolassar in 626;
the Medes came down out of the Zagros; and in 612 the two of them together
besieged and took Nineveh.

The Babylonian Chronicle records it. That matters more than it sounds, because
the Chronicle is a contemporary cuneiform document written by people with no
stake in a Median empire, and it is effectively the only such document about the
Medes doing anything. It names them as participants — using the term
Umman-manda, which is an old Mesopotamian word for barbarian northerners rather
than an ethnic label — and it describes an army and a king called Cyaxares. It
does not describe provinces, an administration, or an empire.

After Nineveh the Medes and Babylonians divided what Assyria had held, roughly:
Babylonia took Mesopotamia and the west, the Medes took the north and the
Iranian side. Then, according to Herodotus, Cyaxares pushed west into Anatolia
and fought Lydia for five years.

That war produced the one precisely dated event in Median history, and the date
comes from astronomy rather than from any record. In the middle of a battle on
the river Halys a total solar eclipse fell; both armies stopped, and a peace was
made and sealed with a marriage. Herodotus says Thales of Miletus had predicted
the eclipse, which he almost certainly could not have done in the way described.
The eclipse itself is real and can be computed: 28 May 585 BC.

The frontier settled on the Halys and stayed there for thirty-six years, until a
Persian crossed it.

So the peak of this record is two events, one attested by a Babylonian chronicle
and one by the sky, with sixty years of asserted empire between them that no
contemporary document describes.
