Ali Barid Shah, the first of his family to take the royal title
outright, ruled a kingdom that had shrunk considerably by the time he
inherited it — reduced at one point by Ahmadnagar's own king to a
territory worth barely four hundred thousand hoons in revenue after a
rapid campaign took Owsa, Kandhar and Udgir back out of Barid hands.
Ali Barid survived a second Ahmadnagar siege of his own capital only by
handing over two of his own courtiers as a bribe to secure Bijapur's
military support — a humiliation the two men felt so keenly that one
of them later murdered the Bijapur king he had been given to. Ali Barid
himself reigned forty-five years despite these pressures, dying in
1562, the longest tenure any king of Bidar would hold.

## A dynasty smaller than its neighbors' patience

Bidar's own later kings held their small territory through a rapid
sequence of short reigns — seven years, then three, then a child-king
deposed by his own relative — a pattern of instability common to the
smaller Deccan sultanates once their larger neighbors had grown
confident enough to treat them as prizes rather than rivals worth
fearing outright.

## A gap the chronicler asked readers to fill

Firishta closes his account of Bidar with an unusually candid
admission: unlike his sources for Bijapur and Ahmadnagar, he could find
no written histories at all for the Qutb Shahy, Imad Shahy or Barid
Shahy dynasties, and was forced to reconstruct their chronology from
oral tradition gathered among well-informed people of his own time. He
asks explicitly that later readers correct the record if better sources
ever came to light. His narrative for Bidar ends with Amir Barid Shah
II still reigning as of 1609 — the last year the source names — without
any account of how or when that reign, or the dynasty itself,
eventually came to a close.
