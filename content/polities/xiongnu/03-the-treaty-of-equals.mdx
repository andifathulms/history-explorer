In 200 BC the founding emperor of the Han led an army north against the Xiongnu
and was surrounded at Baideng for seven days before being let out. The
settlement that followed is the thing this confederation is actually for.

## The heqin terms

The agreement — *heqin*, harmonious kinship — had four parts. A Han princess of
the imperial house went to the chanyu as a wife. A fixed annual consignment of
silk, grain, wine and other goods went north. The two states were declared
brothers of equal standing. And the frontier was fixed at the Great Wall, with
markets opened along it.

The payments ran for about sixty years, and were increased repeatedly.

That is a settled empire treating a steppe confederation as a diplomatic equal
and paying it an annuity — which is not how the Han described its relations with
anybody else, and is why the arrangement was so unpopular at court that it had
to be argued about for four generations.

## Why the chanyu needed it

The usual explanation for steppe pressure on China is scarcity: nomads lack
grain and cloth, so they raid.

Di Cosmo's argument is that the Xiongnu state was built on the tribute rather than driven to it by hunger. The confederation was
an assembly of peoples with their own chiefs, and what held it together was
distribution. The chanyu who could hand out Chinese silk, grain and manufactured
goods to subordinate chiefs, in front of their own followers, was a chanyu who
was obeyed; the one who could not was a chief with a title.

That inverts the usual reading. The raiding is not an alternative to the
tribute — it is what happens when the tribute is interrupted, because the centre
has to find something to distribute or come apart. And a settled empire that
pays is not being extorted so much as underwriting a neighbour's political
system, which is precisely what the court critics who wanted to stop paying
kept saying.

It also means the two states made each other. Han expansion under Wudi was as
much a cause of steppe centralisation as a response to it: a large, aggressive,
unified neighbour is exactly the condition under which scattered pastoral
peoples benefit from combining, and the confederation grew in response to
pressure as much as it applied it.

## What the archaeology adds

The Chinese portrait is of a people with nothing, taking. The ground shows
something else.

Excavation in Mongolia and southern Siberia has produced substantial settlements
with rammed-earth walls, agriculture including millet cultivation, iron and
bronze working on a large scale, and elite burials at Noin-Ula and elsewhere
containing imported Chinese lacquer and silk alongside local goldwork of very
high quality and textiles from as far away as Bactria and the Greek world.

That is a stratified society with its own production and its own long-distance
trade, not a horde living off a frontier. The silk in the burials came from
China; the carpets did not.

## What was actually being sold

Read together, the treaty and the graves describe a transaction rather than a
tribute.

The Han was buying a frontier. What it got for its silk was sixty years in which
the northern border was a market rather than a war, at a cost the court could
calculate and complained about but kept paying, because the alternative — which
Wudi eventually chose — was campaigns into the Gobi that emptied the treasury,
lost tens of thousands of horses, and did not settle anything either.
