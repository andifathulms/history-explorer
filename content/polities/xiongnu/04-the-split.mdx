The confederation was not destroyed. It divided, and then each half was
absorbed by something else, which is how most steppe empires end and is much
harder to date than a battle.

## What Wudi's wars did

From about 133 BC the Han abandoned the payments and went over to campaigning.
The wars ran on and off for decades and they were enormously expensive: armies
of a hundred thousand and more sent into the Gobi, horse losses that ruined the
imperial studs, and a fiscal crisis that produced state monopolies on salt and
iron to pay for it.

They did not conquer the steppe and were not designed to. What they did achieve
was strategic: the Han took the Hexi corridor, which separated the Xiongnu from
the Qiang to their south-west and opened the road to the Tarim, and then spent
a century contesting the Tarim oases — because the oases were the Xiongnu's
other source of the goods a chanyu had to distribute.

That is the mechanism by which a steppe confederation is actually broken. Not by
defeating its army, which can ride away, but by cutting off the things it needs
to pay its own chiefs with.

## 60 to 36 BC

The strain came out where such strain always comes out, at the succession.

In the 50s BC a disputed succession produced rival chanyus — at one point five
at once — and the confederation split. The southern claimant, Huhanye,
submitted to the Han in 51 BC, went to court, and was settled on the frontier as
a subordinate; his brother Zhizhi went west and was killed by a Han expedition
in 36 BC at his fortified base on the Talas.

Huhanye's submission is worth being precise about. He did not become a Han
subject population. He received a title, a subsidy and a frontier position, and
his people remained a distinct polity — which is to say the Han had finally
found a way of paying that came with a hierarchy attached, and the price of the
subsidy was now a formal acknowledgement of inferiority.

## AD 48

The final division is a repeat, a century later, of the same fracture.

Another succession dispute split the confederation into a Southern Xiongnu,
which submitted and was settled inside the frontier as a federate people, and a
Northern Xiongnu, which held the Mongolian steppe.

The northern half was then broken between about 89 and 91 by a coalition of Han
armies and the Xianbei, a rising power on their eastern flank — the ordinary
fate of a weakened steppe centre, finished off by a subordinate people that had
been waiting.

Where the northern remnant went is the great open question of the field. A
population moving west out of Mongolia in the second century and the appearance
of the Huns on the Volga in the fourth is a tempting line to draw, and the
evidence for drawing it is thin and much argued over. It should be treated as
unresolved.

## What became of the southern half

They stayed inside the frontier for two hundred and fifty years as a federate
military population, supplying cavalry, and gradually became one of the several
peoples of the northern Chinese borderland.

In AD 304 one of their leaders, Liu Yuan — descended from the chanyus, educated
in the Chinese classics, and claiming the Han imperial surname on the strength
of the marriage alliances his ancestors had made — declared himself king of Han
and began the destruction of the Jin dynasty.

That is the last act of the Xiongnu name in China, and it is a precise
inversion of Modu: not a steppe confederation extracting tribute from an empire,
but a man of Xiongnu descent claiming the empire itself, in its own vocabulary,
on the strength of a princess sent north four hundred years earlier.
