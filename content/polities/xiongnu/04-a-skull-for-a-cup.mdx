Modu broke the Yuezhi and drove them west. It was his son Lao-shang,
chanyu from 174 BC, who finished them - and the way he did it decided
where an empire would eventually rise two thousand miles from the
steppe that produced the war.

## The old and elevated one

Lao-shang - the name the Chinese sources use for him means something
like "old and elevated," whether a genuine title or simply a
translation of one - inherited the heqin treaty his father had
extracted from the Han along with the throne, and inherited the
unfinished business with the Yuezhi as well. The Yuezhi had already
lost their original pastures between Dunhuang and the Qilian range to
Modu's own campaigns and had regrouped further west, still confident
enough in their hundred thousand trained bowmen to think lightly of
the Xiongnu who had already beaten them once.

Lao-shang did not give them the chance to recover further. He attacked
again, killed the Yuezhi king outright, and - in the detail every
version of the story keeps - had his skull made into a drinking cup.

## Where the survivors went

The blow this time was final. The bulk of the Yuezhi did not merely
retreat; they fled west in earnest, passing the oasis kingdom of
Dayuan in Ferghana and continuing on to conquer Daxia - Bactria -
where they settled among a population the Han sources describe as
having no strong central ruler to resist them. A remnant too weak to
join the migration broke off and took refuge among the Qiang tribes to
the south, remembered afterward as the Lesser Yuezhi to distinguish
them from the kingdom their kinsmen built in the west.

That kingdom in Bactria did not stay a refugee settlement. Within a
few generations its own successor states began the process that would
produce the Kushan Empire - a Central and South Asian power whose
entire origin traces back to one chanyu's decision to finish a war his
father had started, on a steppe more than a thousand miles from where
its consequences finally settled.

## What is not recorded

Lao-shang held the throne until 160 BC. Beyond the Yuezhi campaign and
the routine of heqin gifts and frontier raiding the treaty had already
made a fixed rhythm, little more survives about his own reign in
detail. He was succeeded by Junchen, chanyu through the 150s and 140s
BC and into the opening years of Emperor Wu's reign, whose own break
with the whole heqin arrangement - and the wars that followed - is the
confederation's next turning point.
