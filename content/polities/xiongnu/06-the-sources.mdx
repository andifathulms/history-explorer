The Xiongnu left no writing. Everything narrative about them comes from Chinese
histories, principally Sima Qian's *Shiji*, composed by a court historian of the
empire they were extracting tribute from, roughly a century after Modu.

<Figure id="xn-simaqian" />

Di Cosmo's argument is that the resulting portrait — nomads as a natural,
unchanging predatory force, driven by scarcity to raid the settled world — is a
Chinese political construction rather than an observation, and that it was
serving an argument at court about whether to appease the steppe or campaign
against it. The archaeology gives something less convenient: substantial
settlements, agriculture, metalworking, and burials indicating a stratified
society with considerable wealth that was not all taken from China.

<Figure id="xn-plaque" />

This matters beyond the Xiongnu, because the portrait was applied to every steppe
people afterwards by the literate societies they bordered — in the Chinese
histories of the Türks and the Khitan, in the Persian and Arabic writing on the
Turks, and in the European accounts of the Mongols, which reach for the same
vocabulary with no knowledge of the Chinese original. The steppe is almost
entirely documented by its enemies, and the enemies were mostly arguing with
each other about frontier policy.

The same caution applies to the figures. The reach commonly given for this
confederation — nine million square kilometres — is one of the largest anybody
attaches to a pre-modern state, and it describes a polity whose internal
structure is inferred rather than recorded. It is a published estimate rather
than an invention, and it should be read as an area over which tribute was
plausibly collected rather than a territory anyone administered.
