The Xiongnu are the first steppe empire that can be described in detail, and
what they left is not a state, a script or a religion. They left a shape that
recurred for seventeen centuries.

## The shape

A pastoral confederation forms on the Mongolian steppe under a single supreme
title. Its cohesion depends on the ruler's ability to distribute goods he does
not produce, so it needs access to a settled economy — by treaty, by trade, by
tribute or by force, and usually by all four in rotation. The settled empire
alternates between paying and campaigning, and finds that both are expensive.
The confederation eventually splits at a succession, one part submits and is
settled on the frontier, and a subordinate people that had been waiting takes
the centre.

Every element of that is visible in the Xiongnu between 209 BC and AD 91.

Then: the Xianbei, the Rouran, the Türks, the Uyghurs, the Khitan, the Jurchen,
the Mongols. Not one of them is a repetition — the Uyghurs built a city, the
Khitan ran two administrations at once, the Mongols took the settled world
instead of taxing it — but every one of them is recognisably working the same
problem, and several of them said so.

The Orkhon inscriptions, carved eight centuries later, are a Türk qaghan
telling his people that Chinese generosity is a weapon and that a steppe empire
that settles near the market dies of it. That is the Xiongnu problem, stated
from the inside, by a man whose own state was living on the same silk.

## What it cost the Xiongnu to be first

Being the template has a price, and the Xiongnu paid it: they became the
definition rather than a case.

Because Sima Qian wrote them up first and wrote them up well, his portrait —
nomads as a permanent, unchanging predatory force, driven by scarcity toward the
settled world — became the frame through which every later steppe people was
described by everyone who bordered them. It is in the Chinese histories of the
Türks and the Khitan. It is in the Persian and Arabic writing on the Turks. It
is in the European accounts of the Mongols, which reach for the same vocabulary
without any knowledge of the Chinese original.

The frame was a political argument at the Han court about frontier policy before
it was an ethnography, and it has been in service ever since.

## The other name

There is one more afterlife and it is a philological argument rather than a
fact.

*Xiongnu* in the Chinese sources, *Hun* in the Latin ones, and the two are
similar enough that they have been identified with each other since the
eighteenth century. Sogdian letters found near Dunhuang, written about 313, use
a word for the people who had just sacked Luoyang that is the same word later
used for the Huns, which is the strongest single piece of evidence for a
connection.

Whether that is descent, a migration, or a prestigious name picked up by
unrelated people on the western steppe is not settled and may not be settleable.

It is a fitting last item. A confederation that left no writing of its own is
known through its enemies, has given its portrait to every steppe people since,
and may or may not have given its name to the one that finished the Roman
frontier — and the best evidence either way is a merchant's letter, in a third
language, complaining about a sacked city.
