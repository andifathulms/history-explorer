Around the year 1670, by the tradition Bryant collected from the
clan's own elders, a man named Malandela led his wives and children
south out of the Ngoni homeland, driving his cattle before him through
a mid-summer downpour. He crossed the Mpembeni and Mkhumbane
streams - ground his own descendants would one day call their
motherland - then the Mthonjaneni heights, and down into the Mfule
valley beyond, making for the coast for reasons no tradition
preserves.

## A river that split a family

The crossing of the Mfule split Malandela's own party for good. He
and the foremost of his people forded the swollen river before it
became impassable; the stragglers behind, trapped by the flood, spent
the delay foraging a field of wild melons and, when the waters
dropped, refused Malandela's call to break camp and march - they had,
they said, better work to do. "Stay where you are, then, with your
dirty old melons," Malandela is said to have shouted back, and they
did: building huts, taking wives, and becoming in time an independent
clan of their own, the people of the Water-melons.

## A tradition the historian himself distrusts in its details

Bryant is candid that only the broad outline of this story can be
trusted - that Malandela's party did trek down from the interior by
way of the Mfule valley and settle on the Mhlatuze is, he judges,
about as certain as anything in oral tradition gets; the color and
incident wrapped around it may or may not have happened as told. What
is not in doubt is the outcome: from this same Malandela the people
who would one day call themselves Zulu - "the Heavens" - traced their
own beginning as a distinct clan among the many scattered across the
Natal hill country.
