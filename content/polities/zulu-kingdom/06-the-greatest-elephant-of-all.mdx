Shaka never risked taking a wife, fearing sons who might one day
contest his own throne - but he loved his mother without
qualification, and in October 1827, while hunting elephants sixty
miles from his capital at Bulawayo, he was told Nandi lay dying.
Despite the lateness of the hour he ordered the whole hunting party to
march through the night, covering the distance by noon the next day.
The trader Henry Francis Fynn, present throughout, left a direct
eyewitness account of what followed - one of the few moments of
Shaka's private life recorded by a European who actually saw it happen
rather than heard it secondhand.

## Two hours of silence, then a cry

Fynn found Nandi's hut full of mourning women and smoke thick enough
that he had to ask them to leave so he could breathe; her illness, he
told Shaka directly, was hopeless, and she would not survive the day.
Shaka had his regiments withdrawn to their barracks and sat in
complete silence for two hours, saying nothing, before rising the
instant word came that she had died. He appeared before her hut in
full war dress, stood with his head bowed on his shield for twenty
minutes with tears visibly falling, and then broke into the wailing
cry that was the signal for public mourning to begin.

## Sixty thousand mourners, and a massacre that outran any order

What followed, in Fynn's own account, went far beyond ordinary
mourning. Tens of thousands converged on the kraal over the following
day and night, wailing without rest, until Shaka ordered a first
round of executions and the crowd - as if determined to prove its own
grief to him - broke into a general, self-perpetuating massacre,
neighbors killing neighbors under cover of communal frenzy, the dying
and the merely exhausted cut down alike by those "mad with
excitement." Fynn estimated seven thousand dead by the afternoon of
the first day alone, in mourning that continued for days afterward -
a spasm of violence that even Shaka's own killings, the source notes,
had never before required a direct order to sustain.
