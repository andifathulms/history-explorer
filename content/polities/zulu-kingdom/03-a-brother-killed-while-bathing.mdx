Senzangakhona died in 1816 without naming an heir - there was no
right of primogeniture among the Nguni, Bryant notes, only the reigning
chief's own choice, and Senzangakhona made none before he died.
Dingiswayo, recognized as the Zulu clan's overlord, claimed the right
to choose in his place, and his choice was Shaka, the exiled son
serving in his own army. Word reached Dingiswayo's court of two things
at once: the old chief's death, and that a favored son, Sigujana, had
already assumed the chieftainship at the Zulu capital in his stead.

## A half-brother recruited to murder

Shaka moved against the usurper without confronting him directly. He
turned to a younger half-brother, Ngwadi - Nandi's son by her second
marriage - who had grown close to Sigujana despite his own
illegitimate standing, close enough to join him bathing at the river.
On the appointed day Ngwadi hid two accomplices in the grass along the
bank; as Sigujana sat in the water, two spears took him from behind
and pinned him where he sat, and the killers fled for the coast.
Bryant judges it unlikely Dingiswayo knew nothing of the plot - the
outcome suited him too well - and Dingiswayo promptly arranged
Shaka's own escort home: his trusted headman Ngomane, a Mthethwa
nobleman as companion, and a company of Shaka's former comrades from
the iziCwe regiment.

## A throne taken without a fight

With that armed and imposing company behind him, Shaka met no
resistance at all on reaching the Zulu capital, and settled in at once
as the clan's lawfully established chief. He never saw his patron
Dingiswayo again. Behind him he left the two people who had shaped
him - the mother who had carried him through exile and the chief who
had given a destitute boy a home - and ahead of him lay a small clan
he now meant to turn, by whatever means proved necessary, into
something no one in the country had yet seen.
