The massacres following Nandi's death turned private grief into open
revolt. Mkabayi, Senzangakhona's own sister and one of the last
surviving elders of the royal house, was convinced Shaka had
deliberately killed his own mother, and carried that conviction to
his half-brothers Dingane and Mhlangana - "How long shall your mother
lie unavenged?" - and from them to Mbopa, Shaka's own trusted
head-servant, without whose access to the king no plot could succeed.
By the time Shaka ordered the entire adult male population marched
north on campaign, leaving himself effectively alone at his Dukuza
capital surrounded only by women, the conspiracy was already formed
around him.

## A dream confided to the wrong ear

Shaka's own instincts seem to have warned him something was wrong. He
dreamed he was already dead and that Mbopa served another king, and
confided the dream to a woman of his household who repeated it to
Mbopa within the hour. If the warning registered, it came too late to
matter. Dingane and Mhlangana, ordered north with the army, doubled
back on the pretext of illness, and on 22 September 1828 approached
Shaka's kraal along with Mbopa while he sat receiving a report from
messengers just returned from Mpondoland.

## Killed by the men closest to him

Mbopa created the opening, storming in with a weapon in each hand and
driving off Shaka's attendants under cover of feigned loyalty. In the
confusion Mhlangana struck first, his spear catching Shaka's arm
rather than his side; Dingane's thrust followed. Shaka turned to find
himself face to face with his own brothers' weapons and fled toward
the gate, pleading, "Ye children of my father, what is the wrong?" -
and received no answer. He fell a few yards outside the kraal, begged
for his life, and was finished off by Mbopa. Bryant is careful to note
that no European witnessed the killing directly, and that surviving
Zulu accounts differ on nearly every detail - who struck first, where
exactly Shaka stood or sat, even whether Dingane took any direct part
at all - leaving only the outcome itself beyond real dispute: the boy
once despised by his own mother's clan, who had built the most
formidable kingdom southern Africa had yet seen, was dead at
forty-one, killed by the two brothers and the servant he had trusted
most.
