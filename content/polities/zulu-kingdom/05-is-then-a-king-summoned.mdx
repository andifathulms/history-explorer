Shaka's own rise to unrivaled power in the region owed as much to his
former patron's death as to his own campaigns. Dingiswayo, the
Mthethwa chief who had sheltered Shaka's family and backed his claim
to the Zulu throne, marched against the rival Ndwandwe chief Zwide in
1818 and was captured after wandering, by one account already
befuddled with drink, into an enemy patrol. Brought to Zwide's own
capital, he was received at first with the courtesy due a royal
prisoner - a beast slaughtered for his entertainment, beer served in
proper state - before the true purpose of his captivity began.

## A summons a king would not answer

Zwide's own mother, Ntombazi, described by the source in Shakespeare's
own words as "a tiger's heart wrapped in a woman's hide," pressed her
son not to let the prisoner go. When Zwide's men came to summon
Dingiswayo with deliberate contempt, mangling his name in the
address, the Mthethwa chief answered simply, "Is, then, a king
summoned?" - defiance that cost him his life. His bodyguard of armed
women fought to keep his captors from the hut where he sat and died
alongside him rather than leave; Dingiswayo himself was killed on
Zwide's order, his head kept by Ntombazi as a trophy and the rest of
his body dismembered for parts believed to carry power.

## An empire built on the space his death left open

Dingiswayo's death removed the one authority in the region Shaka had
still formally answered to, and left the Mthethwa federation he had
built leaderless at the exact moment Shaka commanded the region's
most disciplined army. Shaka moved into the vacuum directly, absorbing
Mthethwa territory and turning the military machine Dingiswayo had
given him room to build against every neighboring clan in reach -
Ndwandwe included, in a war of retribution for his patron's death that
would run for years and leave the whole eastern seaboard of southern
Africa reordered under Zulu rule by the time it was over.
