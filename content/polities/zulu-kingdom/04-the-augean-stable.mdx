With a new army built and loyal to him alone, Shaka turned first on
his own household - uncles, nephews, anyone against whom he held a
grievance real or invented, dispatched without trial or delay into
graves three feet deep dug for the purpose. Only once his own kraal
was, in his own reckoning, cleaned did he turn outward, and the first
target he chose was personal: the eLangeni clan, his own mother's
people, among whom he and Nandi had spent the hardest years of their
exile two decades before.

## A debt paid to the people who once mocked him

Shaka marched the umBelebele regiment twenty-five miles over the
Mthonjaneni heights to the same country that had once treated him with
such open contempt, surrounded the eLangeni capital, and had every
man against whom he held an old grudge impaled alive on the palisade
around the cattle enclosure and burned. Makhedama, the eLangeni
chief who had once, by tradition, ruled over the household that
scorned him, was spared outright and even permitted to rebuild
elsewhere - though Shaka had him killed later regardless. Near the
site of the massacre he built a kraal of his own and named it, with
grim satisfaction, "the place where he swallowed his own spittle" - a
monument, in his own telling, to a debt finally settled in full.

## Justice by the standard of the court, not the missionary

Bryant, writing for a European readership a century later, does not
flinch from calling the killings awful - but pairs the judgment with a
reminder that impalement and burning were the tools of statecraft in
"highly civilized" ancient Assyria and Egypt too, not some special
mark of Zulu savagery. What mattered for Shaka's own kingdom was the
lesson every surviving clan took from it: the boy once despised as a
nobody now commanded an army capable of reaching any clan in the
country, and meant to be believed the first time he gave an order.
