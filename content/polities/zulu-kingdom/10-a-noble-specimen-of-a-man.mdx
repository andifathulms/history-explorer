Isandhlwana bought the kingdom months, not survival. A reinforced
British column resumed the advance on Ulundi, Cetshwayo's own royal
capital, through the following spring, and on the 4th of July, 1879,
the two armies met on open ground outside the town. The British force
- some four thousand Europeans and a thousand native troops, with
guns and Gatlings - formed a hollow square and let the Zulu attack,
estimated at fifteen to twenty thousand men, break against it.
Artillery and volley fire held the advance at a distance the assegai
could never close; within half an hour the attack wavered, and the
cavalry rode out through the broken ranks to complete the rout. Ulundi
itself was set alight before the day was over, and with it burned the
last massed Zulu army the kingdom would ever field.

## Found in the forest

Cetshwayo did not surrender. He fled north into the Ngome forest with
a small party of attendants, and it took a mounted patrol under Major
Marter more than a month to run him to ground, on the 28th of August,
in a stockaded kraal deep in the trees. Warned that resistance would
mean the kraal burned over his head, the king came out at last and
stood before his captor - "quite the king," in the account of the men
who took him - and offered the closest thing to an explanation he had:
"You would not have taken me, but I never thought troops could come
down the mountain through the forest."

<Figure id="z-sohn" />

He was marched back to the ruins of his own capital, given barely
three hours among them, and put aboard a coasting vessel bound, though
he was not told so at first, for exile rather than for the
Pietermaritzburg he expected. When the road proved wrong he grew
withdrawn, telling his escort more than once that it would have been
better to be killed than sent over the sea. He was landed at Cape
Town on the 4th of September and held there under guard - the last
king of an independent Zulu kingdom, a man his own captor described,
without apparent irony, as a noble specimen, entirely a king in
manner even in the hour of his capture.
