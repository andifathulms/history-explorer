By 1856 the Zulu throne belonged, in name, to Mpande - the half-brother
who had turned against Dingane a generation before with Boer backing,
and who had ruled since without ever naming which of his own many sons
would succeed him. Two of them settled the question themselves. Cetshwayo
and his brother Mbulazi each gathered a following among the kingdom's
regiments and fought for the succession outright; Mbulazi's party was
broken in battle and its leader killed, with great slaughter among his
followers and a stream of refugees fleeing across the border into
Natal.

What followed the victory was, by the standards of the dynasty, restrained.
Cetshwayo carried the nation with him and held every practical lever of
power from that year forward, but he never moved against his father's
life or formally seized his throne. Mpande lived on for sixteen more
years, reigning in little but name - his own great size, by the end,
too much for him to walk unassisted - while his son governed in fact.
It was, the record of the time notes, a strikingly restrained
arrangement for a dynasty whose own founder had died at his brothers'
hands within twelve years of taking power.

## A coronation with strings attached

Mpande died of natural causes in 1872, and Cetshwayo - already king in
every sense but the title - was proclaimed to rule outright. The
following year, colonial authorities from Natal staged what was
officially styled a friendly expedition to mark the occasion, sending
Theophilus Shepstone to preside over a coronation ceremony in
Zululand itself. The gesture carried a deliberate second meaning: it
was used to establish, on the colonial side at least, that Cetshwayo
held his crown by the neighbouring colony's own sanction rather than
by right - a claim of authority over the kingdom's own succession that
the coronation's hosts intended to make use of again before the decade
was finished.
