An ultimatum delivered to Cetshwayo in December 1878 demanded, among
other terms, the effective dismantling of the Zulu military system
itself within thirty days - a condition no Zulu king could accept
and remain king in anything but name. When the deadline passed
unanswered on the 11th of January, 1879, British columns crossed into
Zululand from several directions at once, the central column advancing
under Lord Chelmsford toward a camp it made no effort to fortify,
beneath a hill called Isandhlwana.

## Ten thousand men over the hills

On the morning of the 22nd, with Chelmsford and half his force away
scouting for the Zulu army he believed still distant, that army - some
ten thousand strong by the defenders' own estimate - came over the
hills to the camp's left in a wave the pickets had no time to give
proper warning of. The defending line, a few companies of British
infantry with native levies and a handful of artillery, held for a
time; the guns threw shellfire into the packed ranks and the massed
volleys of the 24th Regiment told heavily enough on the front of the
Zulu advance that it wavered and, for some minutes, lay down in the
grass rather than close the distance.

<Figure id="z-fripp" />

It was the flanks, not the front, that broke the defense. The Zulu
right worked around behind the camp under cover of the broken ground,
and when the native contingent covering that side of the line gave
way and fled, the whole position was opened from the rear. The camp
was overrun; the retreat toward the Buffalo River became a running
slaughter, with the assegai doing its work among fugitives both
mounted and on foot the whole distance to the water, and the river
itself claiming more of the wounded and the drowning than any single
volley had. No one left in the camp when the position finally broke
survived to tell of it directly - the full scale of the defeat was
only pieced together from what could be read on the ground itself,
months afterward.

<Figure id="z-cairn" />

It was the heaviest defeat inflicted on a British army by any
colonized people in the whole of the nineteenth century, and it did
what the ultimatum's own thirty-day deadline had not: it stopped the
invasion of Zululand in its tracks, for a time.
