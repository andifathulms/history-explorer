Nebuchadnezzar died in 562 and the throne changed hands four times in six years.
His son Amel-Marduk reigned two years and was killed by his brother-in-law
Neriglissar, who reigned four and died in his bed; Neriglissar's son
Labashi-Marduk reigned some weeks and was killed by a conspiracy of officials,
who put forward one of their own. Nabonidus was an elderly courtier of Assyrian
provincial background, probably from Harran, with no connection to Nabopolassar's
family whatever.

This is the sequence the `succession_rule` field on this record is not describing.
It reads `nomination`, because that was the rule: the king designated a son as
crown prince, who held an establishment and appears in the accounts as such.
Coding rule 7 says the field takes the rule and not the outcome, and the two are
further apart here than anywhere else in the corpus. Three of the five
transitions in eighty-seven years were coups, and the last one broke the dynasty
entirely.

## Ten years at an oasis

In about 553 Nabonidus left Babylon. He campaigned into northern Arabia, took the
oasis of Tayma, and stayed there for roughly a decade, leaving his son Belshazzar
in Babylonia with the army and the administration. He returned in 543 or 542.

Beaulieu's monograph is the reason this site can date the departure and the
return at all, and it is also the reason the page does not present the episode as
religious mania. The older reading made Nabonidus a heretic who abandoned Marduk
for the moon god Sin of Harran and was driven out by outraged priests. Beaulieu's
case is that the moon-god devotion is real and documented and was not the cause:
Tayma sat on the incense routes, the Babylonian economy had a serious interest in
the Arabian trade, and the king was doing something with a strategic logic that a
hostile source later made unintelligible.

This record codes the move as a `capital-move` turning point and marks it
`contested: true`. No source calls Tayma a capital. What can be said is that the
king and the court were there and the government of Babylonia was conducted from
elsewhere for ten years, which is what the field is for; the contested flag
carries the fact that this is the site's reading and not a citation.

## The festival that did not happen

The consequence that mattered was liturgical. The akitu festival each spring
required the king to take the hands of Bel in Babylon, and while the king was in
Arabia it could not be held. The chronicle records this year after year in the
same flat formula — the king did not come to Babylon, Nabu did not come, Bel did
not go out, the festival did not take place — which is one of the strangest
documents in Mesopotamian literature: an official annual record of an absence.

This record's `legitimation` field carries `divine-sanction` and nothing else.
For ten years the annual public act that constituted it was not performed.

## The Verse Account

After the conquest, a Babylonian text was produced setting out Nabonidus's
crimes: he built a temple to a god nobody wanted, he mocked the priests, he
abandoned the city, he was mad. It is savage and it is well written and it was
composed under Persian rule by people with an interest in the new arrangement
looking like a rescue.

It is worth naming what that means for the last chapter. Almost everything hostile
about Nabonidus comes from sources written after he lost, including the Cyrus
Cylinder, which is a document produced by the conqueror explaining why Marduk had
wanted him to win. This is not a reason to disbelieve them, and it is a reason to
notice that the fall of Babylon is one of the very few events in this corpus for
which the winner's propaganda survives in the loser's own language and script,
addressed to the loser's own gods.
