Nebuchadnezzar died in 562 and the throne changed hands four times in six
years. His son Amel-Marduk reigned two years and was killed by his
brother-in-law Neriglissar, who reigned four and died in his bed; Neriglissar's
son Labashi-Marduk reigned some weeks and was killed by a conspiracy of
officials, who put forward one of their own. Nabonidus was an elderly courtier
of Assyrian provincial background, probably from Harran, with no connection to
Nabopolassar's family whatever.

There was a rule, and it had nothing to do with any of that. The king
designated a son as crown prince, who held an establishment of his own and
appears in the accounts as such. Three of the five transitions in eighty-seven
years were coups anyway, and the last of them ended the dynasty.

## Ten years at an oasis

In about 553 Nabonidus left Babylon. He campaigned into northern Arabia, took
the oasis of Tayma, and stayed there for roughly a decade, leaving his son
Belshazzar in Babylonia with the army and the administration. He returned in
543 or 542.

Beaulieu's monograph is the reason the departure and the return can be dated at
all, and it is also the reason the episode is no longer told as religious
mania. The older reading made Nabonidus a heretic who abandoned Marduk for the
moon god Sin of Harran and was driven out by outraged priests. Beaulieu's case
is that the moon-god devotion is real and documented and was not the cause:
Tayma sat on the incense routes, the Babylonian economy had a serious interest
in the Arabian trade, and the king was doing something with a strategic logic
that a hostile source later made unintelligible.

Whether Tayma should be called a capital is disputable and no source calls it
one. What can be said is that the king and the court were there and Babylonia
was governed from somewhere else for ten years. The uncertainty is real rather
than a formality: nothing in the sources actually names the place a capital,
and calling it one anyway is an inference from what the king and his
administration were doing there, not a citation of anyone saying so.

## The prayer from Qumran

The stay left one more trace, centuries later and far from Babylonia: a
fragmentary Aramaic text found at Qumran and known as the Prayer of Nabonidus,
copied by a scribe in the first century BC from a story that had by then been
circulating for four hundred years.

In it Nabonidus, not Nebuchadnezzar, speaks in his own name: *"I, Nabonidus,
was afflicted with an evil ulcer for seven years, and far from men I was
driven,"* until a Jewish exorcist — one of the exiles from Judah, the very
population this empire's own conquest of Jerusalem had carried to Babylonia —
pardoned his sin and told him to write the experience down. Before that, the
text has him praying uselessly to *"gods of silver and gold, bronze and iron,
wood, stone and lime."*

Seven years of affliction sitting almost exactly on top of ten years away
from Babylon is not a coincidence worth dismissing, and neither is the
detail that heals him: a Judean exile, the population this same king's
predecessors had deported, restoring a Babylonian king to health in a story
Babylonians themselves did not write down. What the text will not do is
settle the historical question. It has no interest in trade routes, and
Beaulieu's strategic reading has no room for a curse and an exorcist. The two
accounts are not describing the same king from different angles so much as
using the same absence for two entirely different purposes — one an
administrative decision defended after the fact, the other a morality tale
about a foreign king brought low and forgiven by somebody his own family had
made a captive.

## The festival that did not happen

The consequence that mattered was liturgical. The akitu festival each spring
required the king to take the hands of Bel in Babylon, and while the king was
in Arabia it could not be held. The chronicle records this year after year in
the same flat formula — the king did not come to Babylon, Nabu did not come,
Bel did not go out, the festival did not take place — which is one of the
strangest documents in Mesopotamian literature: an official annual record of an
absence.

This was a kingship that rested on the god's choice and on nothing else. For
ten years the annual public act that constituted it was not performed.

## The Verse Account

After the conquest, a Babylonian text was produced setting out Nabonidus's
crimes: he built a temple to a god nobody wanted, he mocked the priests, he
abandoned the city, he was mad. It is savage and it is well written and it was
composed under Persian rule by people with an interest in the new arrangement
looking like a rescue.

It is worth naming what that means for the last chapter. Almost everything
hostile about Nabonidus comes from sources written after he lost, including the
Cyrus Cylinder, which is a document produced by the conqueror explaining why
Marduk had wanted him to win. This is not a reason to disbelieve them, and it
is a reason to notice that the fall of Babylon is one of the very few events in
antiquity for which the winner's propaganda survives in the loser's own
language and script, addressed to the loser's own gods.
