In October 539 Cyrus defeated the Babylonian army at Opis on the Tigris. Sippar
was taken without a battle two days later. On the sixteenth of Tashritu, the
Chronicle says, Ugbaru the governor of Gutium and the army of Cyrus entered
Babylon without a fight; Nabonidus was arrested when he returned to the city;
Cyrus himself entered seventeen days after that. Belshazzar was probably killed
at Opis.

The whole thing took about three weeks, which is quicker than the fall of Assyria
that this record's first chapter opens with — and the comparison needs making
carefully, because the two are not the same kind of event at all.

## What did not end

Assyria ended and the cities were not reoccupied, the language of the court
stopped being written, and within two centuries Greeks passing the ruins were
told they had been built by somebody else. None of that happened here. The
temples went on functioning. The Esagila archive goes on. The private business
houses go on — the Egibi firm of Babylon has documents on both sides of 539 and
does not appear to have noticed the difference. Contracts continue to be drafted
in Akkadian in the same formulae, in the same script, on the same clay, dated now
by the regnal year of Cyrus king of lands.

Astronomical diaries were still being written in cuneiform in Babylon in the
first century AD, five hundred years after the last Babylonian king. That is why
Beaulieu's book runs to AD 75 and why this record's `span` stops in 539: the
state ended and the civilisation did not, and this site measures states.

## The claims

The `successor_claims` count on this record is 2, and both entries are titulature
in coding rule 1's paradigm sense.

Cyrus had a clay cylinder deposited in the city wall, in Akkadian, in the
traditional Babylonian royal form, in which Marduk searched the lands for a just
king, chose Cyrus, and had him take Babylon without a battle because Nabonidus
had wronged the city. It styles him king of Babylon, king of Sumer and Akkad,
king of the four quarters. Two hundred and seventy years later Antiochus I
rebuilt the temple Ezida at Borsippa and recorded it on a cylinder of his own, in
Akkadian, in the same form, calling himself king of Babylon. Neither man was
Babylonian and both wanted the office.

The Achaemenid claim also has an edge in this site's data, because a conquest
joins the two. The Seleucid one does not, because nothing joins them but a
document. Coding rule 1 keeps the count and the edges separate on purpose, and
this page is what that looks like in practice: a claim with no edge is not a
missing edge.

## What this record fixed

Two things on this site were wrong for as long as this page did not exist, and
both are worth recording here rather than in a commit message.

The Assyrian record's `ended.by` named the Medes alone. Nineveh was taken by two
powers and the field could only name one of them, because the other had no page
to point at. It now names both, in the order the Chronicle puts them in.

And the region drew no thread. The Ancient Near East had exactly one internal
edge, `neo-assyrian -> median`, and when the Median record moved to Iran before
Islam the edge crossed a regional boundary and the thread had to come down. The
edge added with this record — `neo-assyrian -> neo-babylonian`, the same
coalition, the same siege, the same year — has both ends inside the region, and
the thread is back on one edge.

That is a thin thread and it should stay thin. Five of this region's six records
do not succeed each other. They were contemporaries who wrote to each other,
fought each other and married each other's daughters, and none of those is a
succession. Hard rule 7 exists so that a region full of that is a finished
region and not an incomplete one.
