In October 539 Cyrus defeated the Babylonian army at Opis on the Tigris. Sippar
was taken without a battle two days later. On the sixteenth of Tashritu, the
Chronicle says, Ugbaru the governor of Gutium and the army of Cyrus entered
Babylon without a fight; Nabonidus was arrested when he returned to the city;
Cyrus himself entered seventeen days after that. Belshazzar was probably killed
at Opis.

The whole thing took about three weeks, which is quicker than the fall of
Assyria that this empire had been built on — and the comparison needs making
carefully, because the two are not the same kind of event at all.

## What did not end

Assyria ended and the cities were not reoccupied, the language of the court
stopped being written, and within two centuries Greeks passing the ruins were
told they had been built by somebody else. None of that happened here. The
temples went on functioning. The Esagila archive goes on. The private business
houses go on — the Egibi firm of Babylon has documents on both sides of 539 and
does not appear to have noticed the difference. Contracts continue to be
drafted in Akkadian in the same formulae, in the same script, on the same clay,
dated now by the regnal year of Cyrus king of lands.

Astronomical diaries were still being written in cuneiform in Babylon in the
first century AD, five hundred years after the last Babylonian king. That is
why Beaulieu's history of Babylon runs to AD 75 while the kingdom stops in 539.
The state ended and the civilisation did not.

## The claims

Two later rulers claimed the office outright, and both did it in writing, in
Babylonian.

Cyrus had a clay cylinder deposited in the city wall, in Akkadian, in the
traditional Babylonian royal form, in which Marduk searched the lands for a
just king, chose Cyrus, and had him take Babylon without a battle because
Nabonidus had wronged the city. It styles him king of Babylon, king of Sumer
and Akkad, king of the four quarters. Two hundred and seventy years later
Antiochus I rebuilt the temple Ezida at Borsippa and recorded it on a cylinder
of his own, in Akkadian, in the same form, calling himself king of Babylon.
Neither man was Babylonian and both wanted the office.

The difference between the two is instructive. Cyrus took the city; Antiochus
inherited a claim to it through three intervening dynasties and rebuilt a
temple to press it. Neither was Babylonian, and both understood that the office
was worth having in the form the Babylonians recognised — which is why a
Macedonian king, nearly three centuries after the last Babylonian one, was
still having cylinders written in Akkadian and buried in walls.
