Judah revolted twice. The first time, in 597, Nebuchadnezzar took the city after
a short siege, deposed the young king Jehoiachin, deported him and several
thousand of the ruling and skilled classes to Babylonia, and installed his uncle
Zedekiah. The second time, in 589, Zedekiah revolted in turn, and the answer was
an eighteen-month siege ending in 587 with the walls breached, the temple burned,
the palace burned, and the remainder of the governing class taken to Babylonia.
Judah stopped being a kingdom.

The Babylonian side of this is thin and entirely ordinary. A vassal broke his
oath, was besieged, and was destroyed; the Chronicle covers the first siege in
four lines and the tablet for the years of the second does not survive. Ration
lists excavated in Babylon record barley and oil issued to *Ya'ukinu, king of the
land of Yahudu* and his five sons, which is Jehoiachin, alive and on the payroll,
and that is roughly the extent of the imperial documentation.

## Why it is a turning point anyway

Because the deportees wrote.

This site's Assyrian record makes a version of this argument about Samaria in
722, and it is worth being precise about how the two differ, because they are not
the same observation twice. Samaria's deportees did not leave a literature;
what happened is that the *survivors in the south* — Judah — wrote about
Assyria, and the reputation of Sennacherib and Sargon reached the modern world
through a neighbour's account of being terrorised.

Here it is the deportees themselves. A substantial part of the Hebrew Bible was
written, edited or given its final shape in and after the Babylonian exile, by
people who were in Babylonia because of the events in this chapter. The result
is that this empire's afterlife is out of all proportion to its administrative
footprint. It supplies the Tower of Babel, the whore of Babylon, the writing on
the wall, the willows and the harps, and a king who goes mad and eats grass —
and that last one is not even about the right king.

## The wrong king

The book of Daniel puts Nebuchadnezzar through seven years of madness in the
wilderness and then has a Belshazzar, called his son, lose the kingdom on the
night of a feast. Neither is Nebuchadnezzar's. The king who absented himself for
years in the desert was Nabonidus, forty years later, and Belshazzar was
Nabonidus's son, not his. A fragmentary text from Qumran, the Prayer of Nabonidus,
tells the madness story with the right king's name in it.

So the most widely known single anecdote about this empire is a real event,
correctly remembered, attached to the wrong man — and the page that carries the
real version is the fifth chapter of this record. It is a good demonstration of
what it means to say that the political history here is Mesopotamian and the
reputation is Judaean. The two are working from different archives and they do
not reconcile.
