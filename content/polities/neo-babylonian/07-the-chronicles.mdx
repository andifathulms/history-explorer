Almost every date in the preceding six chapters comes from one class of
document, and it is worth describing, because nothing else in the ancient world
offers anything quite like it.

The Babylonian Chronicles are a series of tablets, compiled in Babylonia and
copied and recopied for centuries, recording events year by year in a plain
annalistic register: in the *n*th year of king X, in the month Y, the king
marched to Z, took it, and returned. They are not royal inscriptions. They do
not praise anybody. They record defeats. The tablet covering the fighting with
Egypt in 601 says the two sides inflicted heavy losses on each other and that
Nebuchadnezzar went home and spent the next year rebuilding his chariotry,
which is not a sentence any Assyrian royal inscription would contain about its
own king.

## Why that matters

For most of the Bronze Age Near East, the chronology rests on synchronisms,
king lists and reconstructed regnal totals, and the absolute dates move by
decades depending on which framework is adopted. Hittite dates are given in one
of three competing chronologies; Mitanni's can hardly be given at all.

None of that applies here. 626, 612, 605, 597, 587, 539 are fixed. The reason
is that the Chronicle series is anchored to astronomical observation —
Babylonian scribes recorded eclipses and planetary positions alongside the
regnal years, and those can be computed backwards and matched exactly. The Late
Bronze Age has no such anchor and the Iron Age does.

So the shape of this region's evidence changes sharply somewhere in the eighth
century, and it changes for reasons that have nothing to do with how important
the states were or how well they governed. Mitanni was a great power and can
barely be dated. This was a smaller empire that lasted eighty-seven years, and
it has an economy reconstructed from twenty-one thousand tablets and a calendar
correct to the day.

That asymmetry is the single most important thing to know about the evidence
for this part of the world, and it has nothing to do with the states
themselves.
