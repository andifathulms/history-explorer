Almost every date in the preceding six chapters comes from one class of document,
and it is worth describing it, because nothing else in this corpus before the
Islamic period offers anything comparable.

The Babylonian Chronicles are a series of tablets, compiled in Babylonia and
copied and recopied for centuries, recording events year by year in a plain
annalistic register: in the *n*th year of king X, in the month Y, the king
marched to Z, took it, and returned. They are not royal inscriptions. They do not
praise anybody. They record defeats. The tablet covering the fighting with Egypt
in 601 says the two sides inflicted heavy losses on each other and that
Nebuchadnezzar went home and spent the next year rebuilding his chariotry, which
is not a sentence any Assyrian royal inscription would contain about its own
king.

## Why that matters for this site

Two of this region's four older records — Mitanni and the Egyptian New Kingdom —
have chapters explaining that the chronology rests on synchronisms, king lists
and reconstructed regnal totals, and that the absolute dates move by decades
depending on which frame you adopt. The Hittite record entering alongside this
one has a note on its `span` saying that its dates are Bryce's middle chronology
and that a `{ min, max }` would misrepresent the problem, because the
disagreement is about the frame rather than about the figure.

This record has none of that. 626, 612, 605, 597, 587, 539 are fixed. The reason
is that the Chronicle series is anchored to astronomical observation — Babylonian
scribes recorded eclipses and planetary positions alongside the regnal years, and
those can be computed backwards and matched exactly. The Late Bronze Age has no
such anchor and the Iron Age does.

So the shape of this region's evidence changes sharply somewhere in the eighth
century, and it changes for reasons that have nothing to do with how important
the states were or how well they governed. Mitanni is a great power with one
coded institution and no secure dates. This one is a smaller empire that lasted
eighty-seven years and it has four coded institutions, an economy reconstructed
from twenty-one thousand tablets, and a calendar that is correct to the day.

That asymmetry is not a defect in the earlier pages. It is the single most
important thing a reader can know about this region, and the honest way to
present it is to let the gaps stand where they are and say why they are there.
