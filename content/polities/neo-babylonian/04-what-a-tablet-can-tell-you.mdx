Every other record in this region is coded from a narrative. The Assyrian
institutions on this site come from royal inscriptions read against an
archaeology of provincial administration; the Egyptian ones come from Kemp
reconstructing a state from its material remains; the Mitanni record has one
coded field out of four because there is no archive at all.

This one is coded from accounts. Jursa's study rests on more than twenty-one
thousand dated tablets from Babylon, Borsippa, Nippur, Sippar and Uruk —
temple books, private business archives, loan contracts, rent agreements, wage
receipts, price notations — running continuously through the seventh, sixth and
fifth centuries. It is not a better class of chronicle. It is a different kind of
evidence, and it means that for this eighty-seven-year state we can say things
that cannot be said about any other polity in this corpus for another five
hundred years.

## Bow-land

The `military_basis` field here reads `land-grant, mercenary`, and the first
value is not an inference from how armies usually worked. Land in Babylonia was
held from the crown or from a temple in return for producing an equipped man,
and the parcels are named in the documents for the equipment they owed: bow-land,
horse-land, chariot-land. A holder who could not go himself paid a substitute,
and the substitution shows up in the accounts as a cash line. Jursa can follow
individual parcels through changes of tenant across decades.

The second value has an odd corroboration. Greek and Carian professionals served
in the Babylonian army, and one of them was Antimenidas, the brother of the poet
Alcaeus of Mytilene, who came home with a decorated sword and had a poem written
about him. A lyric fragment from Lesbos and a ration tablet from Babylon are
describing the same institution from opposite ends of the Mediterranean world.

## What is not here

`revenue_basis` reads `land-tax, tribute`, and the absence of `plunder` is the
most substantive coding decision on this page.

The Assyrian record immediately above it in this region carries three values,
the third being `plunder` in coding rule 6's strict sense: Assyria campaigned
annually, the campaign returned movable wealth and populations, and the royal
inscriptions account for what it brought as a standard section. That is a fiscal
institution.

This state stopped campaigning seriously after the mid-580s. The thirteen-year
siege of Tyre is the last major western operation and it ended in a negotiated
submission; after that the reign is building work. Jursa's accounts show an
economy running on irrigated agriculture, temple estates, an increasingly
monetised silver market and long-distance trade, with tribute from the Levant
alongside. Nothing in them treats campaign proceeds as structural income, and
coding rule 6 asks for exactly that before the value goes in.

So the two neighbouring records in this region — the empire and its successor —
differ on a coded field in a way that reflects a real difference in how they were
paid for, and one holds a third of the other's territory while being, by every
measure the tablets support, richer.

## The building

What the money went on is still partly standing. Nebuchadnezzar rebuilt Babylon
on a scale that made it the largest city in the world: the double walls, the
Processional Way, the Ishtar Gate with its moulded dragons and bulls in glazed
brick, the ziggurat Etemenanki that the Tower of Babel story is about. His
inscriptions are almost entirely building inscriptions. For a king remembered as
a destroyer, the documentary record he left of himself is a construction ledger.

One famous thing is missing from it. No Babylonian text mentions a hanging
garden. The gardens are entirely a Greek tradition, first appearing centuries
later, and the most serious modern proposal relocates them to Nineveh and to
Sennacherib. This site does not carry them as a measure or a turning point,
because there is nothing to cite.
