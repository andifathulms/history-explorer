Alongside the walls and the fall, Herodotus reports how he was told Babylonians
lived. It is the earliest description of Mesopotamian society by an outsider, it
is largely wrong, and the shape of the wrongness is worth having.

## The auction

He calls it the wisest custom they have.

Once a year in each village the girls who had reached marriageable age were
brought together in one place with a ring of men around them, and a crier sold
them one at a time — the most beautiful first, who fetched a large sum, then the
next, and so on. Wealthy men bid against each other for these. Then, when the
comely ones were gone, the crier stood up the least shapely, and any who were
crippled, and asked who would take them for the smallest sum; each went to the
man willing to accept least.

And the money came from the first half of the sale. The dowries of the women
nobody would bid for were funded by the prices paid for the women everybody
wanted.

The safeguards are the part that makes it sound like a real institution rather
than a traveller's story. A man could not simply give his own daughter to
whomever he liked. A buyer could not take a woman away without providing
sureties that he would actually marry her. If the two did not get on, the law
required him to pay the money back. Men from other villages were allowed to come
and bid.

Herodotus then says it has stopped. Since the conquest, he writes, the people
have been oppressed and ruined, and the poor now prostitute their daughters for
want of a living.

## The sick in the marketplace

The second-wisest custom, he says, is that they carry the sick out into the
market square, because *of physicians they make no use*. Passers-by come up and
advise, if they have had the same complaint or seen somebody who has. Walking
past a sick man without speaking is not permitted; you must at least ask what is
wrong with him.

This one is demonstrably false, and the refutation is a Babylonian document. The
laws of Hammurabi, twelve centuries before Herodotus, fix the surgeon's fee for
opening an abscess in the eye at ten shekels for a citizen, five for a
dependant, two for a slave, and specify that a surgeon who blinds a citizen has
his fingers cut off. A society that schedules operating fees and malpractice
penalties has physicians.

## What he was actually collecting

The third custom he calls the most shameful: that every woman must once in her
life sit in the precinct of the goddess he names Mylitta and go with a stranger
who throws a silver coin into her lap. He describes the enclosure with its cords
and its straight passages, the rich arriving in covered carriages with
attendants, the coin that may be of any value and cannot be refused because the
act has made it sacred — and the women who are not beautiful waiting three or
four years to discharge the obligation.

Read the three together and a pattern appears. Each is a story about Babylonian
women — sold, or obliged, or ruined — and each is framed as a custom the Greeks
should judge: the wisest, the next wisest, the most shameful.

He is not reporting Babylon. He is reporting what a fifth-century Greek audience
found it useful to believe about the largest and oldest city in the world: that
it was fabulously rich, that it organised sex and marriage by public transaction,
and that it had been brought low. The walls in the other chapter are ninety
metres high for the same reason these customs are extreme. Babylon was the place
against which everything was measured, and a measure has to be conspicuous.

The one line in the whole passage that is probably straight reporting is the sad
one — that since the conquest the poor sell their daughters. That is what happens
to cities that lose wars, and it required no informant with a story to tell.
