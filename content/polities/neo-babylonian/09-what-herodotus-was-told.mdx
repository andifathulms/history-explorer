About a century after Cyrus took Babylon, a Greek came to look at it and wrote
down what he was told. By then it was a provincial capital of the Persian
empire and had already revolted against it twice.

Herodotus is not evidence for what the Neo-Babylonian empire was. He is the
best evidence there is for what the world had decided about it, and the
difference is the whole point of the chapter.

## The city

He gives it as a square, each side a hundred and twenty furlongs, four hundred
and eighty in circuit, standing in a great plain — a city, he says, of a
magnificence greater than any other of which we have knowledge. Round it a deep
broad trench full of water, and then a wall fifty royal cubits thick and two
hundred high, the royal cubit being three fingers longer than the common one.

The figures are impossible. The wall he describes would be about ninety metres
tall. What the numbers record is not the city but its reputation: by the fifth
century Babylon had become the standard against which the size of cities was
measured, and the way to say a place was enormous was to give it Babylonian
dimensions.

## The fall

His account of the conquest is the part everybody knows.

Cyrus, he says, put half his army where the Euphrates entered the city and half
where it left, and told them to go in by the riverbed when the water became
passable. Then he took the rest of his men upstream and turned the river into
an old swamp. The stream sank to the middle of a man's thigh and the Persians
walked in along the channel.

And then the detail that has kept the story alive for two and a half thousand
years. The city was so large, Herodotus says — and he attributes this to the
people who lived there — that after the outskirts had fallen the Babylonians in
the middle did not know it. They were holding a festival at the time, and went
on dancing and rejoicing until they learned the truth only too well.

He adds, drily, that if they had understood what was happening they could have
shut the river gates, lined the embankments, and caught the Persians in the
channel like fish in a trap.

## Against the cuneiform

The Babylonian sources say something else. The Nabonidus Chronicle has the army
defeated at Opis, Sippar taken without a battle, and Babylon entered without a
fight; the cylinder Cyrus had deposited in the city wall, described two
chapters earlier, tells the same handover as a god's own choice rather than a
conquest.

Those two are propaganda too, produced by or for the winner. But they are
contemporary, they are in the language of the place, and they describe an
administrative handover rather than a siege.

So the most famous thing about the fall of Babylon — the diverted river, the
soldiers in the riverbed, the dancers who did not know — is a story that grew
in the hundred years afterwards, in a city that had learned to explain to
visitors how it had been taken.

## What that is an afterlife of

The empire lasted eighty-seven years and left twenty-one thousand tablets, an
economy that can be reconstructed to the price of barley, and a chronicle
series accurate to the day.

What travelled was none of that. What travelled was the walls, the gardens
nobody can locate, the feast on the night of the fall, and the name — used ever
afterwards, by people who had never been there, as the word for a great and
doomed city.
