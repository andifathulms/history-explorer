In November 626 BC a man named Nabopolassar was made king in Babylon. Nothing
certain is known about where he came from. He is usually taken for a Chaldean —
one of the tribal groups that had been settled in the southern marshes for three
centuries and had produced Assyria's most persistent Babylonian enemies — but he
never says so, and in one of his own inscriptions he calls himself *mār lā
mammāna*, the son of a nobody.

That phrase is worth pausing on, because it is not an insult recorded by an
enemy. It is a formula the king chose for his own building inscription. He had
no dynasty behind him and no claim of descent to make, and rather than invent
one he made the absence part of the argument: Marduk had raised up a man who was
nothing. It is the reason this record's `legitimation` field carries
`divine-sanction` alone with no `descent` beside it, which is unusual in this
corpus and is not a gap.

## Fourteen years

Babylon in 626 was an Assyrian province that had been in revolt, on and off, for
a hundred and fifty years. This site's Assyrian record carries Sennacherib's
destruction of the city in 689 as a turning point, and the estimate there — that
it poisoned the relationship that eventually destroyed the empire — is what the
next fourteen years look like from this side.

Nabopolassar spent them fighting for Babylonia and nothing else. He took Uruk
and Nippur slowly, was pushed back repeatedly, and did not cross into Assyria
proper until 616. Assyria at this point had been through a civil war between two
of Esarhaddon's grandsons, had lost Egypt, and was fighting on three fronts, but
it was not obviously dying. A war that took fourteen years to reach the enemy's
own territory is not the war of a state that was pushing over a corpse.

## The alliance

What changed it was Cyaxares. Something out of western Iran — this corpus's
Median record is candid that we do not know what — joined the Babylonians, and
between them they took Ashur in 614 and Nineveh in 612. Nabopolassar's chronicle
says the two kings met at the ruins of Ashur and made a treaty of friendship.
The Assyrian remnant under Ashur-uballit II held out at Harran with Egyptian
help until 609, and then there was no Assyria.

The division of the spoils is the part that matters for what follows. The Medes
took the northern and eastern uplands; Babylon took the whole of the Assyrian
west — Syria, the Levant, the routes to Egypt. In seven years a provincial
insurgency had become the successor to the largest empire the world had held.

Nabopolassar was about seventy. He gave the western command to his son.
