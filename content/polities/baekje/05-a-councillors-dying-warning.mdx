By the mid-seventh century Baekje's court had grown, in the source's own
judgment, "utterly corrupt," ruled by a king it calls incompetent even
as fighting with Silla continued on and off across the peninsula. One of
the king's own best councillors was imprisoned and starved to death for
rebuking his excesses, but sent a final message from his cell before he
died: guard the two chokepoints of Baekje's own defense, "Charcoal Pass"
and the Baek River, and surprise would be impossible. The warning went
unheeded.

## A defector opens the door

A Silla captive who had escaped Baekje's capital was persuaded to return
as an informant, and found Baekje's own Prime Minister willing to sell
his country's defenses for a price. From that point, the source says,
Baekje's downfall properly began. Meanwhile Silla secured what Baekje
could not: sustained Chinese military backing, formalized in 659 when
the Tang Emperor ordered Gen. So Chŏng-bang to carry a hundred and
thirty thousand men by sea to Baekje's own coast and coordinate with a
Silla army converging by land.

## Omens, a council divided, and a pass surrendered

Tradition records a season of portents heralding the kingdom's fall —
frogs swarming trees, phantom bellowing over the palace, a buried
tortoise shell reading "Baekje is at full moon; Silla is at half moon."
A council of war split over whether to meet the Chinese fleet or the
Silla army first, and while the court debated, Baekje's own troops
reached Hwangsan Pass to find Gen. Kim Yu-sin's Silla army already
streaming through it. Baekje's general Kyebaek, who had killed his own
family before the campaign so grief could not make him waver in battle,
held his ground and fell there with his men.

## A king made to pour the victors' wine

With the pass lost, nothing stood between Silla's army and Baekje's own
capital. The king fled north; the palace women, left behind, are said to
have thrown themselves from a cliff above the harbor rather than face
capture, a site still known as Nak-wha-am, the "Precipice of the Falling
Flowers." Days later the king returned from hiding and surrendered
voluntarily along with his Crown Prince. China offered to split the
conquered territory with Silla, but Silla's own commander declined any
share, saying revenge for old wrongs was all Silla had sought. At the
victory feast that followed, Baekje's king was made to pour wine for his
conquerors — and was then carried to China with four sons, eighty-eight
officials and nearly thirteen thousand of his own people, closing a
kingdom the source credits with six hundred and seventy-eight years on
the throne under thirty kings. He died in China the following year.

## A pretender's kingdom that did not outlast him

Resistance did not end with the king's capture. A former official named
Pok Sin raised a rebellion in Baekje's name, proclaimed a son of an
earlier king as monarch, and came close enough to alarming the Chinese
emperor that he summoned Silla troops to put it down. Combined Silla and
Chinese forces broke the movement's main stronghold, but pockets of
resistance held out for years afterward across a countryside that had,
in the end, not been reconciled to Baekje's fall so much as exhausted by
it.
