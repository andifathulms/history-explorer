Li Yuanhao inherited a state his grandfather had spent a reign consolidating
and his father had spent a reign defending, and he did not intend to spend
his own reign accepting the terms either of them had settled for. He drilled
his soldiers daily "after the fashion of the Chinese manual," built up stores
of arms, and watched the Song army opposite him grow soft on the same long
peace that was letting him prepare. In 1034 he turned those preparations into
open war, using a border governor's incursion into his own territory as
pretext for an offensive that needed very little pretext to begin with.

## Encouraged by his success

The early fighting went his way, and Boulger records what that success did
to his ambitions directly: "encouraged by his success, Chao Yuan resolved to
assume a higher style than that of Prince of Hia" — the patent his family
had held from Song and, before Song, from Tang. His justification rested on
descent rather than conquest alone: he came, he argued, of a family that had
in the past borne the imperial dignity itself, and a prince descended from
emperors owed no throne the deference a patent implied. He took the title of
Emperor, returned the letters patent Song rulers had sent his predecessors
over the years, and answered the resulting insult with an army already in
the field.

## Sanchuan and Yang Moulong

The war that followed went badly for Song almost throughout. At Sanchuan the
two armies fought for three days before the advantage settled with Xia. The
decisive engagement came near the town of Yang Moulong, where Li Yuanhao
commanded in
person and out-manoeuvred Song's field commanders badly enough to surround
their army entirely. The fighting that followed was, by Boulger's account,
never in doubt once the encirclement closed: Song's generals died one after
another rather than abandon the field, and by nightfall the flower of the
Song army lay dead on the plain. One commander, urged by his own soldier to
retreat from a lost battle, refused outright — "I withdraw! the general of
this army. The battle is indeed lost, but I can and ought to die" — and was
found afterward among the thickest of the slain.

## What the war bought

Song did not recover quickly from the defeat, and further trouble followed
almost immediately: Liao, watching a weakened neighbour, seized the moment to
occupy ten Song border cities under the terms of an entirely separate
grievance. Negotiations eventually brought Xia and Song to peace in 1042 on
terms that left Li Yuanhao's imperial title untouched in practice, whatever
face-saving language the treaty used for Chinese consumption — an annual
payment in silver, silk and tea flowing north in exchange for the return of
formal deference that had cost Song several armies to fail at collecting by
force.
