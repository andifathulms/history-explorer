Parker calls it, in passing, "a well-organized state" — a description he
does not extend lightly to most of the polities on China's northern and
western frontier. It was founded in the Ordos region by a family descended
from the Toba branch of the Xianbei, the same steppe lineage that had ruled
northern China centuries earlier as the Northern Wei, and it governed there
"almost independently" from the year 890 onward, long before anyone in it
claimed the title of emperor.

## Two dynasties, two surnames

The family's Chinese surname changed with whichever dynasty happened to be
granting it favour. The Tang court gave it the name Li, in the way a
struggling imperial centre rewarded a frontier family for military service
it could not otherwise compel. When the Song reunified China in 960, the
compliment was renewed in the currency of the new court: the family took the
surname Zhao — the House of Song's own name — "presented as a compliment in
turn to the early 'Emperors' of Hia." Parker's scare quotes around
"Emperors" mark exactly the ambiguity the family lived inside for a century
and a half: nominally a Chinese governor's house holding a Chinese-granted
name, in practice a hereditary power in the Ordos answering to nobody's
day-to-day command.

## What the histories agree on

Parker is explicit that this was not an obscure or marginal state, whatever
its position between larger neighbours might suggest. "The histories of the
Cathayan, Niichen, and Sung dynasties all contain lengthy descriptions of
this country" — Liao, Jin and Song each found it worth a sustained chapter of
their own record, which is a different thing from being a footnote to any of
the three. It had, by the time its rulers stopped being satisfied with a
princely patent, an administration capable of fielding a drilled army and
inscriptions in a script of its own — the same script Chinese and Western
scholarship would still find largely undeciphered eight centuries later.

## The patent nobody in the family found sufficient

For most of this first century and a half the family's rulers accepted the
patent of Prince of Hia from whichever Chinese court was issuing one, paid
the outward respect the arrangement required, and used the interval to build
the state that patent nominally subordinated. It was not, on the evidence of
what came next, an arrangement any of them regarded as a settled description
of what they actually were.
