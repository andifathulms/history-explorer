Xia's rulers spent the eleventh century playing Song and Liao against each
other with a skill neither larger neighbour ever quite matched in return.
Parker's narrative of the period reads less like the history of a client
state than like the history of a small power that had worked out, well
before anyone had a word for it, that two rival patrons will always
outbid each other for a border ally's loyalty — provided the ally never
lets either one feel secure in it.

## The 1028 opening

In 1028 the Xia ruler captured the Uyghur city of Ganzhou, a conquest that
extended his territory west but also, more usefully to him, gave Liao a
fresh grievance to raise against Song at exactly the moment Liao wanted
leverage of its own. The Liao court massed troops in the Beijing plain,
reopened a decades-old dispute over ten border districts, and wrote to the
Song emperor — addressed, with deliberate condescension, as "elder brother"
— complaining as much about frontier river-works as about Xia's expansion.
Song's envoys saw through the manoeuvre immediately: the ostensible
territorial claim was cover for what Liao actually wanted, which was a
larger subsidy. Song's own position on Xia was blunt and confident — "China
is quite competent to deal with that power satisfactorily" — a confidence
the following decade would not vindicate.

## The war Song lost and the war Liao won

That confidence broke first at Sanchuan and Yang Moulong, where Li Yuanhao
handled Song's armies as badly as Song had claimed no border state could.
Liao read the same defeat as an opportunity and used it to extract an
increased annual payment from Song without having to fight for it at all —
diplomacy riding on the back of a war it had contributed nothing to.

Liao's turn against Xia came a few years later. In 1044 the Liao court
declared war on Xia over its habit of harbouring Liao deserters, announced
the fact formally to the Song court as though seeking its endorsement, and
won. In 1049 Liao announced a second victory. Xia, which had just spent a
decade proving itself more than a match for Song's armies, discovered in the
same decade that it was not remotely a match for Liao's.

## What the arrangement was actually for

None of the three powers trusted the other two, and all three kept dealing
with each other anyway, because the alternative — a war on two fronts against
allied neighbours — was worse for each of them than an uneasy triangle in
which loyalties shifted with the balance of recent battles. Song insisted, in
its official correspondence, on being addressed as "Great Song" rather than
merely "the Court of the South," a formality that cost Liao nothing to grant
and Song a great deal to feel it had preserved. Xia took no side permanently and every side
temporarily, and outlasted Liao by a full century on exactly those terms —
still standing, more or less on its own, when the neighbour it had fought,
allied with and outmanoeuvred by turns finally fell to a conqueror neither of
them had yet had reason to fear.
