Genghis Khan attacked Xia three times before he finished with it, and each
campaign taught him something the next one used. The first, in 1207, was a
raid rather than a conquest — he captured one fortress, collected the
tribute the victory brought him from a section of the Kirghiz, and withdrew.
Two years later he came back to finish the job and found it harder than
expected: Xia's king put his full strength into the field, the campaign
dragged into a river crossing and a failed attempt to flood the country, and
it was only when Genghis threatened complete overthrow that
the Xia king sued for peace. Genghis accepted, married the king's daughter,
and folded Xia's considerable military resources into his own — depriving
Jin of a potential ally on his own western flank in the same stroke. The
war with Jin that had been building for years broke out the following year.

## An alliance that did not survive Genghis's absence

Xia's forces fought alongside the Mongols against Jin more than once in the
years that followed, an alliance neither side entered from affection. When
Genghis left for four years to break the Muslim states of Central Asia, from
Kashgar to the Jaxartes and the Indus, the Xia court concluded that an
absent conqueror and a dead general — Muqali, his lieutenant in China, died
the same year as the old Xia king — added up to an opportunity. The young
ruler who inherited the Xia throne judged, correctly as far as it went, that
his father's deference to the Mongols had cost more than it had bought, and
refused to continue it. What he had not judged correctly was how fast
Genghis could return from four years of campaigning eight thousand miles
away.

## The campaign that ended two lives

Genghis came back in 1223, resumed personal command against Jin, and spent
1224 in preparation for a reckoning with Xia specifically — the largest army
he had yet assembled for any of his Chinese campaigns. The Xia king, who
boasted he could field half a million men, discovered in the event that the
boast did not matter. The Mongols took the principal cities and fortresses
in succession, and in a battle fought over the frozen Yellow River — Boulger
draws the comparison to Napoleon's artillery on the ice at Austerlitz — the
Xia army was all but destroyed. The king who had broken with the Mongols
died, Boulger writes, "of the grief caused by his numerous misfortunes"
before the war was even finished; his successor gave formal submission in
1227, the year the conquest was completed.

## Whose last campaign it actually was

Genghis did not live to see Jin finished, though he and Muqali between them
had already broken its military power beyond real recovery. He is recorded
to have refused, as one of his last acts, a peace offer from the Jin ruler
whose earlier protestations of friendship he had never trusted. He died in
his camp on the Xia frontier on the twenty-seventh of August, 1227 — by the
most probable account a natural death, though several less probable ones
were told afterward. Boulger states it plainly: the conquest of Xia was the
last military undertaking of Genghis Khan's life, closing out fifty years of
campaigns with the one that had, in the end, taken the two years he did not
have left to give it.
