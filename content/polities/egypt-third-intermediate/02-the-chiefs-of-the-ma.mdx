The kings who eventually reunified the two houses the split at Thebes had
created were not descended from either of them. They came from a population
Egyptian kings had been settling on land in the Delta and Middle Egypt for
generations, as prisoners of war, mercenaries and eventually hereditary
landholders: Libyan military families organised under their own chieftains,
titled Great Chief of the Ma, who by the late New Kingdom already commanded
enough men to matter to whichever Egyptian throne wanted their service.

## From hired swords to the throne itself

For roughly a century and a half after Herihor's assumption of Theban
kingship, the arrangement at Tanis held: a Ramesside-descended throne in the
north, marrying into and negotiating with the high-priestly house in the
south, neither side strong enough to absorb the other and neither willing to
give up its own half. What ended the standoff was not a war between the two
existing houses. It was a third party already inside the country, with its
own armed following, deciding to stop lending its spears to other men's
kingships and take the throne outright.

Shoshenq I, a Great Chief of the Ma whose family had held that title for
generations, became king of Egypt at Tanis and did something neither the
Ramessides nor the Theban high priests had managed in a century: he placed
his own son in the high priesthood of Amun. For the first time since the
split, one family held the throne in the north and the religious
establishment in the south at once — not by defeating the Theban
priesthood, but by installing a relative at the head of it.

## What "reunification" actually meant

Egypt was not, by this, restored to what it had been under the New Kingdom.
Real regional power remained with the Libyan chieftains who commanded local
followings across the Delta and Middle Egypt, many of them Shoshenq's own
kinsmen granted authority over particular towns and garrisons in what
amounted to a family confederation wearing the outward form of a unified
Egyptian kingship. It looked, from Tanis and from Thebes both, like the
country was one again. What it actually was, was a single family that had
married and installed its way into controlling both of the pieces the
previous century had left standing — with a great many armed cousins still
holding pieces of their own underneath it.
