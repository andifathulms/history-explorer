---
title: What Piye left undone

Piye went home to Napata having received every rival king's submission, and
his own stela treats that as the end of the story: a mighty ruler returning
having conquered Lower Egypt, the Delta kinglets reduced to tributaries but
left on their own thrones. It was not the end of the story. It was the
opening of the one his family and his rivals both went on to write.

## The dynasty that stopped commuting

Piye's successors did not keep ruling Egypt from four hundred miles away.
Within a generation, Shabaka took the throne and did what Piye himself had
declined to: he held Egypt directly, as a resident king rather than a
distant overlord collecting submission from Napata. The Kushite line became
Egypt's own Twenty-Fifth Dynasty in fact as well as in the king-lists,
restoring temples, reviving Old and Middle Kingdom artistic forms
deliberately, and ruling as pharaohs who happened to have come from the
south rather than as Nubian kings who happened to hold Egypt on loan.

That closer grip is also what made the dynasty someone else's problem to
remove rather than merely resent. Assyria, expanding west under Esarhaddon,
took Memphis in 671; Ashurbanipal sacked Thebes in 663. Both times the
Kushite kings fought for the country in person rather than abandoning it
from a safe distance, and both times they lost. The Twenty-Fifth Dynasty
withdrew to Nubia and did not come back — not because Piye's model of
distant overlordship had failed, but because his successors had chosen a
different model, one that put an army in Egypt for an Assyrian army to
fight and beat.

## Whose family actually won

The line that inherits Egypt after the Kushites are pushed out is not a new
one. It is Tefnakht's.

The Saite house at Sais, whose ambition toward Thebes was the immediate
provocation for Piye's entire campaign a century earlier, survived its own
king's submission, survived a Kushite century as a subordinate Delta
dynasty, and was still there — reduced but intact — when Assyria's own grip
on Egypt loosened in turn. A descendant of that same line, Psamtik I, threw
off Assyrian vassalage in the 650s and reunified the country his own
ancestor had once tried and failed to bring under Saite control by force.

Nobody in either fight got the outcome the sources credit them with wanting.
Piye's stela claims a conquest and settles for tribute. Tefnakht's ambition
triggers the invasion that humbles him personally and is answered, three
generations later, by his own family actually holding the whole country —
a claim made good only after the dynasty that had defeated Tefnakht in the
first place was itself driven out by a third power neither he nor Piye had
any reason to expect.
