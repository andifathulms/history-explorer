Piye, king of Kush at Napata, moved north with an army and, over a campaign
that took him the length of the Nile valley, received the submission of
every rival king ruling in Egypt. His own account of it, carved on a
granite stela set up at Gebel Barkal, is the fullest surviving description
of the country's condition at the moment it stopped being divided —
because Piye had every one of the men he defeated named, individually,
coming to him.

## Kings on their bellies

Namlot of Hermopolis, one of the first to submit, is recorded prostrating
himself: "Then he threw himself on his belly in the presence of His
Majesty." Peftjauawybast of Herakleopolis does the same, greeting Piye as
"Horus, mighty King, bull who attacks bulls." Iuput of Leontopolis arrives
with his own chief officials, "bearing their tribute." Osorkon of Tanis,
the diminished heir of the dynasty Shoshenq I had founded two centuries
earlier, comes not under compulsion but, in the stela's own account,
because he "came expressly to behold the beauty of His Majesty" — a court
formula for submission dressed as an honour.

Tefnakht of Sais, the one king whose ambition had actually provoked the
invasion, is the only one who does not appear in person. His letter of
capitulation is quoted instead: "Peace be with you! I cannot look upon your
face in days of shame... I cannot stand before your fiery blast, for I am
terrified of your grandeur." Piye accepted the submission without forcing
the meeting.

## What actually made him angry

The stela's most quoted passage is not about any of this. It is about
horses. Touring Namlot's stables at Hermopolis after the surrender, Piye
found the animals starved and said so, in words the inscription preserves
directly:

> As I live, as Re loves me, as my nose is rejuvenated with life, how much
> more painful it is in my heart that my horses have been starved than any
> other crime that you have committed at your discretion.

Set beside the war itself — a campaign to reunify a country split among
rival kings for three and a half centuries — a line about mistreated horses
reads almost as comic relief. It is also exactly the kind of detail a
purely triumphal inscription would have no reason to include, and its
presence is part of what has convinced generations of readers that Piye's
stela is describing what actually happened rather than only what a king
wanted remembered.

## What the reunification actually was

Piye did not stay in Egypt to rule it directly. Having received every
rival king's submission, he returned to Napata, and the stela's own closing
acclamation frames the campaign as complete in itself: "O mighty ruler, O
mighty ruler! Piye, O mighty ruler! You return having conquered Lower
Egypt." The Delta kinglets he had just defeated mostly kept their local
thrones, now as his tributaries rather than as independent claimants to a
title none of them had ever been able to make good on the whole country.
What ended at Gebel Barkal was not the existence of these small Egyptian
courts. It was any question of who, among all the kings ruling pieces of
Egypt, actually held the country.
