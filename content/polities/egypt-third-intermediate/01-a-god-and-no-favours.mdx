When the New Kingdom's central authority gave out, Egypt did not have a
war of succession. It had a quiet administrative divorce: a Ramesside line
kept the throne and the old capital's name at Tanis in the Delta, and the
High Priest of Amun at Thebes kept the south, the temple's own estates, and
— within a generation — the trappings of kingship itself. Herihor, the
general who held the high priesthood at the moment of the split, began
writing his own name in a royal cartouche and dating documents by his own
regnal years, the first Theban priest to claim in writing what the office
had in practice already become.

## What that divorce looked like from the outside

The clearest evidence for what this arrangement actually meant, day to day,
for the people meant to enforce it comes from a document written in the
first person by a Theban temple official named Wenamun, sent north to
Byblos on the Levantine coast to buy cedar for the ceremonial barge of
Amun — the kind of routine procurement mission Egyptian temples had managed
for a thousand years, backed for most of that time by a state whose power
nobody along that coast had to be persuaded of.

Wenamun's own account records instead a mission gone wrong at every stage.
Robbed of his silver and gold before he even reached Byblos — "a man of my
ship fled after stealing one vessel of gold worth 5 deben, four jars of
silver worth 20 deben, and a bag with 11 deben of silver" — he arrives with
nothing to offer and is kept waiting, ignored, before the prince of Byblos
will even receive him. When Zakar-Baal finally does, he tells the Egyptian
envoy exactly what has changed:

> If the ruler of Egypt were the lord of what is mine and I were his
> servant, he would not have sent silver and gold to say: "Carry out the
> business of Amun."

He is not refusing to sell cedar. He is refusing to accept an Egyptian
commission as a command rather than a purchase — a distinction that would
not have needed making a century earlier, when the New Kingdom's own kings
held Levantine princes as tributaries rather than trading partners.

## What the god's own priest could not fix

Wenamun's report ends, in the surviving fragment, without confirming
whether he made it home. Whatever became of him personally, the mission's
failure is not a story about one unlucky envoy. It is what happens to a
sacred commission — the god's own business, invoked by name — when the
throne behind it no longer has an army anyone abroad needs to fear. A
kingship split between Tanis and Thebes had kept the forms of Egyptian
religious authority fully intact. What it had lost, somewhere in the years
since the empire that once ran to the Euphrates came apart, was the
authority to make anyone outside Egypt's own borders pretend to care.
