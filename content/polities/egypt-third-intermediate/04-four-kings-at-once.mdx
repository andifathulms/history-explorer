The unity Shoshenq I had assembled by marriage and appointment did not
outlast his family's own grip on the chieftaincies underneath it. Within a
few generations of his reign, rival lines of the same Libyan military
aristocracy that had put him on the throne began doing exactly what he had
done: converting local, inherited command over land and followers into a
royal title of their own, without waiting for the Tanite house to either
grant it or be strong enough to refuse it.

## A crown that stopped being singular

By the eighth century, Egypt had not one king claiming the old Twenty-second
Dynasty throne but several, ruling simultaneously from different Delta and
Middle Egyptian centres, most of them kin to each other and none
acknowledging a common overlord. A line at Leontopolis in the eastern
Delta held its own throne name; a line at Hermopolis in Middle Egypt did
the same; the old Tanite house continued, reduced, at Tanis itself; and at
Sais in the western Delta, a chieftain named Tefnakht built a following
strong enough that Manetho's later tradition credits him with founding a
new dynasty of his own — the only one of the rival houses that took its
name from a place with no prior claim to any earlier Egyptian throne at
all.

What each of these kings actually ruled in practice was rarely more than
the town he held and whatever territory his own soldiers could enforce
obedience in. The royal titulary each of them used — the same five-name
formula, the same divine sonship, the same claim to rule the whole of Upper
and Lower Egypt — makes the fragmentation harder to see in the written
record than it was on the ground: on paper, Egypt in this period has an
embarrassment of legitimate pharaohs. In practice, it had none with the
power the title had always implied.

## What finally provoked an answer

Tefnakht of Sais is the figure the record credits with actually testing how
far this arrangement could be pushed. Not content with the western Delta,
he moved south along the river toward Middle Egypt and Thebes itself,
threatening to bring the temple estates of Amun — the one institution every
rival Delta house still nominally deferred to — under a Saite king's direct
control. It was that move, more than any of the quieter decades of
parallel kingship before it, that finally drew a response from outside
Egypt's own fractured elite entirely: a Kushite king at Napata, watching
events on his own northern frontier, decided that a threat to Thebes was a
threat he could not simply observe.
