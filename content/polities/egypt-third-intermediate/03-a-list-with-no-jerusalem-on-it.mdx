Shoshenq I is the only king of this period most people outside Egyptology
have heard of, and almost entirely for one campaign, remembered under a
different name entirely: the Bible's Book of Kings records that in the
fifth year of Rehoboam of Judah, "Shishak king of Egypt" marched against
Jerusalem and carried off the treasures of Solomon's temple. Nearly every
Egyptologist accepts the identification of Shishak with Shoshenq — the
names correspond closely enough, and the timing fits. What the Egyptian
side of the same event actually records is harder to line up with the
biblical story than the identification itself suggests it should be.

## What the wall at Karnak says

Shoshenq commemorated the campaign on the Bubastite Portal at Karnak with a
relief of the king smiting a mass of captives, accompanied by a topographic
list of roughly a hundred and fifty place names — towns and regions in the
Levant the campaign claims to have reached or conquered. It is exactly the
kind of triumphal record a New Kingdom pharaoh three centuries earlier would
have commissioned for a comparable campaign, in the same genre and the same
register of formulaic conquest.

Jerusalem is not on it. Whether that is because the list is damaged in
precisely the place a mention would have stood, because Shoshenq's forces
bypassed the city after receiving tribute rather than besieging it, or
because the Egyptian version of events did not consider a payoff worth the
same commemorative weight as a captured town, cannot be settled from the
relief alone — but the absence is real, on a monument that names roughly a
hundred and fifty lesser places in the same region.

## Two records of the same year, not agreeing on what mattered

Neither account is obviously the more reliable one read alone. The biblical
record is written to explain why Solomon's temple was stripped of treasure
under his son's reign, and Shoshenq's own monument is written to present a
Libyan military family's second king as heir to the New Kingdom's own
conquering pharaohs — each account emphasising the version of the same
campaign that served the purpose it was written for. What the discrepancy
actually shows is less about which side exaggerated and more about how
differently the same event could matter to the two courts that recorded it:
a treasury emptied was worth writing down in Jerusalem; a temple's worth of
plunder collected without a siege was, for Shoshenq's own purposes, close
enough to a conquest to carve in stone as one.
