Judaea in the second century BC had two hereditary titles that mattered and the
Hasmoneans held neither.

Kingship belonged to the house of David, and there had not been a king in
Jerusalem since the Babylonians took it in 587 — an event this corpus's
Neo-Babylonian record carries as a turning point, and whose consequences are
still running four hundred and fifty years later on this page. The high
priesthood belonged to the line of Zadok, which the Seleucids had been selling
to the highest bidder for a generation, but which was at least a line.

The Hasmoneans were a priestly family from Modein, of the course of Jehoiarib,
with no connection to either. What they had was that they had won.

## The decree

In 140 BC an assembly described as a great gathering of priests, people, rulers
of the nation and elders of the country made Simon leader and high priest — the
Greek word is *ethnarch* — and commander of the army, and decreed that the
offices should be his and his sons' after him. It forbade anyone, priest or
layman, to contest the arrangement or to convene an assembly without him. It was
engraved on bronze tablets and set up in the Temple precinct, and the text is
quoted in the fourteenth chapter of 1 Maccabees.

One clause in it is doing a great deal of work: Simon holds the offices *until a
trustworthy prophet shall arise*. That is the assembly conceding that it does not
have the authority to settle the question permanently and settling it anyway.

## Why the succession field says `election`

This is one of a small number of records in this corpus where the
`succession_rule` field is filled from a surviving document rather than inferred
from what happened, and the others are worth naming because the comparison is
instructive. The Hittite record has Telipinu's Edict of about 1500 BC. The
Neo-Assyrian record has Esarhaddon's succession oath of 672.

The Urartian record in this corpus closes on an observation drawn from those
two: that a succession rule is what a state writes after being badly hurt by not
having one — Telipinu legislated after a century of murdered kings, Esarhaddon
after a civil war caused by his father's assassination — while four dynasties
whose successions worked perfectly never wrote anything down.

This record is a third reason, and it refines rather than contradicts the
finding. The Hasmoneans wrote their rule down at the outset, before anything had
gone wrong, because they had no lineage and a document was the only thing they
could put in its place. A state can legislate a succession out of injury or out
of illegitimacy, and this is the second kind.

What the decree does not do is name an order. Coding rule 7 takes the rule and
forbids supplying what a source does not say, so the field reads `election` and
not `primogeniture`, and the century that followed ran son, son, brother, widow,
and a civil war between two brothers that handed the country to Rome.

## What they built instead

Regev's argument, and the reason this record's ideological fields are coded from
him rather than from the books of Maccabees, is that the Hasmoneans knew exactly
what they were missing and spent a hundred years compensating.

The coinage is legible in that light: paleo-Hebrew letters revived from a script
five centuries out of use, no human image, the legend naming the high priest and
the *hever* — the assembly — of the Jews, in a deliberate archaism that looks
past the Seleucids to something older. The palaces at Jericho are Hellenistic
buildings with ritual baths in them. Hanukkah, as a festival, is a Hasmonean
institution about the Temple they had recovered.

A dynasty that has to keep explaining why it rules is a dynasty that leaves an
unusual amount of evidence about what it thought ruling was.
