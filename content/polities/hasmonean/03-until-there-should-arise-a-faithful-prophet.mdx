In 140 BC an assembly at Jerusalem passed a decree making Simon high priest,
commander and ethnarch, and had it cut on bronze tablets and set up in the
sanctuary. The text is quoted in full in 1 Maccabees, and it is the founding
constitutional document of the Hasmonean state — which is why the single
qualifying clause in it matters so much.

The decree recites the family's services and then makes the grant. The Jews and
the priests were well pleased that Simon should be their governor and high priest
**for ever, until there should arise a faithful prophet**. He was to be their
commander; to have charge of the sanctuary; to appoint officers over the works,
the country, the armour and the fortresses; to be obeyed by every man; to have
all documents in the country drawn in his name; and to be clothed in purple and
wear gold. Nobody among the people or the priests was to annul any of it or
contradict him, or convene an assembly in the country without him, on pain of
punishment. The assembly commanded that the writing be put on tablets of bronze
and set up in a conspicuous place within the sanctuary precinct, and that copies
be deposited in the treasury, so that Simon and his sons might have them.

Four things are worth taking out of it.

The first is that this is an elective monarchy in all but name, and knows it. The
Hasmoneans were priests of an undistinguished course, not of the high-priestly
line and certainly not of the house of David. They had no claim of the kind the
office required. So the office was conferred, by a decree, by the people and the
priests — and the decree says so, at length, with the reasons.

The second is *and his sons*. The copies go to the treasury for Simon and his
sons to hold; the decree opens by asking what thanks shall be given to Simon and
his sons. A hereditary succession is being established in a document whose whole
argument is that the man deserved it personally.

The third is the bronze. The Romans had made bronze tablets familiar — 1
Maccabees itself describes the Roman treaty being written on them — and the
Jerusalem assembly now used the same medium for its own constitution. The
Hasmoneans had sent embassies to Rome and been received as friends and
confederates, and the decree cites that recognition as one of the grounds for the
grant. They were building a Hellenistic monarchy out of a revolt against
Hellenisation, and even the metal was borrowed.

The fourth is the clause. *Until there should arise a faithful prophet.* It is
not a formality; the same phrase occurs earlier in the book, at the point where
the defiled altar stones are stored away because nobody knows what to do with
them and the decision is deferred to a prophet who has not come. Here it is doing
constitutional work. The grant is permanent and revocable at once — valid until
somebody with genuine authority turns up to say otherwise.

That sentence is the Hasmonean state's own statement of what was wrong with it,
inscribed on bronze at its foundation and set where everyone could read it. Every
later quarrel in the dynasty's history — the Pharisees' objection to Jannaeus
holding the priesthood, the two brothers taking their claims to a Roman general —
is an argument about a title that had been issued with an expiry condition and no
expiry date.
