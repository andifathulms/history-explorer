John Hyrcanus succeeded his father Simon in 135 and ruled thirty-one years, in
which the Seleucid empire — this corpus's record for it is about a state coming
apart in exactly this period — stopped being able to interfere. He took Samaria
and destroyed the temple on Mount Gerizim. He took Idumaea. His son Aristobulus I
took Ituraea in the north. His son Alexander Jannaeus took the coastal cities
and most of the Transjordan.

In a little over forty years a temple-state around one city became a territorial
kingdom running from the Golan to the Egyptian frontier, larger than anything
ruled from Jerusalem since Solomon.

## 104, and a word

Aristobulus I put on a diadem. Josephus says he was the first of the family to
do it, and the significance is not vanity: a diadem in the Hellenistic east is a
specific claim to a specific kind of office, and it made the high priest of the
Temple simultaneously a Greek-style monarch with a Greek regnal name.

Regev's case is that this was not simple Hellenisation. The Hasmonean state, on
his reading, is a national monarchy rather than a conventional Hellenistic one —
closer in type to Macedon than to the Seleucids, ruling a people rather than a
collection of cities, and using Greek royal forms to say something that was not
Greek. The coins keep the Hebrew and add the Greek. The palaces have the baths.

What is not in doubt is that combining the two offices in one person created the
central quarrel of the next century. Josephus has the Pharisees ask Hyrcanus to
give up the high priesthood and keep the civil rule. He refused, and his son went
further and took a crown as well, and by Jannaeus's reign the argument was a
civil war in which the king crucified eight hundred of his own subjects while
dining with his concubines, having imported Pisidian and Cilician mercenaries to
fight Judaeans.

That is this record's `military_basis` field in one sentence. `conscript,
mercenary`, and the second value is the grievance.

## Idumaea

The conquest that matters most for this site is the smallest of them.

Hyrcanus took Idumaea, the country south of Judaea, and — Josephus says —
required its inhabitants to be circumcised and to live under Jewish law or leave.
Modern readings soften that in various ways and the coercion is genuinely
argued about; this record marks the turning point `contested: true` for that
reason. What nobody disputes is the result. Idumaea was Jewish afterwards, and
stayed Jewish.

This record's `religions_carried` count is 1 and the item is Judaism, which is a
count coding rule 2 allows for exactly this shape: conquest followed by durable
religious institutions, where a source connects the two. It is worth saying
plainly that the instrument was an army. The rule counts propagation, and
propagation by annexation is still propagation; a corpus that only counted
missions would produce a tidier and less honest number.

The consequence is the record that follows this one. Herod's family were
Idumaean and were Jews because of this campaign, and the objection his opponents
made to him for fifty years — that he was a half-Jew, that his Judaism did not
count — is an objection to what Hyrcanus did in 112.

The dynasty's single entry on the influence axis is the reason the dynasty that
destroyed it existed.
