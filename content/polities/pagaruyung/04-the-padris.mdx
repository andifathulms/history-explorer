The crisis that ended Pagaruyung grew out of the same trade that had sustained
it. By the later eighteenth century the highland economy ran increasingly on
coffee grown for the world market rather than on gold, and the profits of
that trade financed a religious reform movement that had no use for the
kingship at all.

## Reform and its financing

The Padri movement — named, by one account, for pilgrims returning through
the port of Pedir on their way home from Mecca — argued for a stricter
observance of Islamic law against practices the reformers considered
un-Islamic: cockfighting, gambling, the chewing of betel, and the matrilineal
inheritance system itself, which the strictest reformers held could not be
reconciled with Quranic rules of inheritance. Dobbin's account traces the
movement's financing directly to the coffee boom: the same highland villages
producing a cash crop for export produced the surplus that let Padri leaders
raise followings, arm them, and build fortified settlements independent of
both the adat chiefs and the Pagaruyung court.

## A war on every side

What followed, from the first years of the nineteenth century, was a civil
war fought on more than one front at once: Padri fighters against the
customary chiefs whose adat authority they rejected, Padri fighters against
the royal house whose kingship rested on exactly the customary order under
attack, and eventually all of them against the Dutch, who arrived to back
the side that would cede them the most.

<Figure id="benting" />

Pagaruyung itself was burned during the fighting. The royal family fled the
highlands, and for a period the kingship existed only as a claim held by
people no longer resident in their own capital — a state of affairs the
sources describe candidly rather than passing over.

## What the kingship had left to offer

By the time the surviving royal house re-established a presence and looked
for help against the Padris, it had almost nothing left to bargain with
except the one thing it had always had: the claim to be the legitimate
authority the Padris were displacing. That claim was worth something to a
Dutch administration looking for a local partner and worth increasingly
little to a Minangkabau population that had spent a generation watching the
kingship fail to protect either its own capital or the customary order it
existed to arbitrate.
