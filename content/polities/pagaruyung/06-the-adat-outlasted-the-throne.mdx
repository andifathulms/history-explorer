---
title: The adat outlasted the throne

The kingship ended in 1833. The system it had spent five centuries
arbitrating did not.

Josselin de Jong's study, written more than a century after Sultan Alam
Bagagarsyah's arrest, describes a Minangkabau adat still functioning as a
living system of matrilineal descent, inheritance and customary law — not a
memory of one, but a structure people in the highlands and in the Minangkabau
diaspora were still operating inside. Dutch colonial administration governed
the highlands after 1833 without needing a Pagaruyung king to legitimate it,
but it did not, and by most accounts could not, simply abolish the adat
system underneath its administration. Colonial officials spent the following
century negotiating with customary authority rather than replacing it,
because the alternative was administering several million people's land and
inheritance disputes directly.

## What travelled

The same migratory habit that had once carried Pagaruyung's reputation the
length of the gold and pepper coasts — Minangkabau settlers carrying their
adat with them to new territory rather than leaving it behind — is what let
the system outlast the kingdom that had once stood at its centre. Josselin de Jong's own comparative subject, the Negeri Sembilan
states of the Malay peninsula, is a case of exactly that: a Minangkabau
migrant population that had left Sumatra centuries earlier and kept the
matrilineal structure running under an entirely different, and much later,
political roof.

Pagaruyung itself, by the time colonial rule was established, was a district
of Dutch Sumatra with a royal history rather than a royal government. What
had not stopped was the thing the kingship had only ever administered at one
remove: several million people organising inheritance, land and lineage
through their mothers rather than their fathers, in villages the throne had
never taxed and could never have commanded.

## What the office actually was, in the end

A kingship with no army, no revenue worth the name, and no fixed territory
looks, from the outside, like one of the thinnest states in the region's
whole history. Measured by what depended on it, that reading has it
backwards. Adityawarman
planted an office in 1347 that a matrilineal society with no other referee
found genuinely useful for five centuries, and the office disappeared in
1833 leaving behind exactly what it had been arbitrating rather than
commanding: an adat system that needed no throne to keep working, because
the throne had never been where the system's real authority lived.
