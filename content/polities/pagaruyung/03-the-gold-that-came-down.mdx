Every account of Pagaruyung from the sixteenth century onward mentions the
same thing before it mentions anything else about the kingdom: gold.

The rivers draining the highlands carried alluvial gold, and the Minangkabau
interior was, by report, one of the richest gold-producing regions in the
archipelago. Portuguese and later Dutch and English traders on the west coast
dealt with highland intermediaries for it without ever needing to reach the
court itself, and the metal's reputation reached further than any political
claim the Pagaruyung kings made. A kingdom that struck no coinage anyone has
found nonetheless had a name that functioned, in the trade of the whole
region, as a byword for the metal that came out of its rivers.

## Recognition without administration

What the gold trade did for the kingship was indirect. Pagaruyung did not tax
the mines directly in any arrangement the sources describe, and it did not
administer the coastal ports where the gold was actually sold to foreign
traders. What it retained was the thing described in the previous chapter:
the standing to confirm a local ruler's title.

Minangkabau colonists had, over centuries, settled the river systems running
down to both coasts of Sumatra, carrying the adat system and the idea of
Pagaruyung's precedence with them. A raja on the coast, dealing directly with
Dutch or English factors and holding no highland territory at all, might
still describe his authority as resting on a grant or a recognition from
Pagaruyung — a claim the Europeans generally could not verify and mostly did
not try to. It cost the coastal ruler nothing to make and cost Pagaruyung
nothing to grant, and it supplied both of them with something: local
legitimacy for one, a wider reputation for the other.

## What that made the kingdom

Measured by territory held, revenue collected or force commanded, Pagaruyung
in this period is close to nothing. Measured by the number of other rulers
who found it useful to be able to say they were recognised by it, the
kingdom's reach ran the length of the gold and pepper coasts of central
Sumatra.

That is not a contradiction and it should not be smoothed into one. A
kingship that supplies legitimacy rather than force is doing real political
work even when it is doing nothing else, and the fact that outsiders — Dutch
company officials among them — kept treating a courtesy title from an
army-less highland court as worth recording is itself the evidence that the
courtesy mattered to the people using it.
