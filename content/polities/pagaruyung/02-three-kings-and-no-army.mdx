By the time the record resumes, the kingship at Pagaruyung had become an
unusual arrangement: an office of three people, holding no territory of its
own, and commanding no soldiers, whose authority the surrounding lineages
nonetheless treated as real.

## The Kings of the Three Seats

Later tradition and colonial-era reports describe the office as split three
ways: a Raja Alam, king of the realm; a Raja Adat, king of custom; and a Raja
Ibadat, king of religious observance. All three traced descent from
Adityawarman's line, and all three sat, in principle, at Pagaruyung. Josselin
de Jong's study treats the arrangement as the political expression of a
society organised around a different problem than the one a conventional
kingship solves.

That society was, and largely still is, matrilineal. Property, clan
membership and the right to hold a lineage's titles descended through women;
a man held authority in his sister's household and his sister's children's
affairs rather than in his wife's. What that arrangement does not supply is a
single point of decision when lineages disagree with each other, because a
system organised around dozens of separate matrilineal houses has no
built-in referee between them.

## What the kingship was actually for

The Pagaruyung kings supplied that referee function without supplying an
army to enforce it. Disputes over land, over the boundaries between adat
territories, over who held a disputed title, could be brought to Pagaruyung,
and a ruling from there carried weight precisely because the kingship stood
outside the matrilineal lineages that were doing the disputing. It was
authority by virtue of not belonging to any of the parties, which is a
different kind of legitimacy from the one a conqueror's descendants usually
claim.

The kingship's reach was consequently uneven and largely a matter of
reputation. Villages far from the highland court might invoke a Pagaruyung
title to settle a local matter without ever having sent tribute there or
seen a royal official. Rulers of Minangkabau colonies on the east coast, far
from the highlands and dealing in pepper and gold with Malay and European
traders, continued for centuries to seek recognition or a royal letter from
Pagaruyung even when the highland court could not have compelled them to do
anything at all.

## Why that lasted

A kingship with no soldiers is ordinarily a kingship that gets replaced by
whoever does have some. Pagaruyung's did not, for exactly as long as nobody
found it worth the trouble of replacing: the office claimed no revenue worth
extracting, held no territory worth conquering, and settled disputes other
powers would otherwise have had to settle themselves. It survived by being
too useful, and too empty of anything to seize, to be worth ending.
