The Minangkabau highlands of central Sumatra had no king before 1347, and the
man who became one did not come from them.

Adityawarman was connected by descent and marriage to the old Malayu court of
Sumatra and to the Javanese court of Majapahit, where an inscription names him
among the great officers of the realm. He was a follower of Tantric Buddhism,
and the statue made in his likeness — a fearsome, multi-armed image found near
the site of his capital — is carved with a dedication identifying him and
dating it. He had himself installed in the highlands around Pagaruyung, in the
gold-bearing interior behind the Sumatran west coast, and had the fact recorded
in stone in a language and a script that owed everything to the Indianised
courts he came from and nothing to the highlands he now ruled.

<Figure id="adityawarman" />

Why the highlands accepted him is not recorded. What his inscriptions record is
what mattered to him: his own genealogy, his titles, and — repeatedly — his
control of the gold that came down from the rivers of the interior.
A prince
with connections to two larger courts and an interest in a metal neither of
them mined themselves is not a difficult motive to construct, but it remains a
construction. Nothing datable in the record explains, from the highland side,
why a Minangkabau population that had never had a raja before agreed to have
one now.

<Figure id="prasasti" />

His own inscriptions run from 1347 to 1375. After that there is nothing for a
century and a half. When the record picks up again, in Portuguese accounts of
the early sixteenth century, it describes a kingship that no longer looks like
a Javanese governor's outpost at all: not one king but three, and an authority
that worked by consent rather than command. What happened in the gap produced
that kingship, and none of it is dated.
