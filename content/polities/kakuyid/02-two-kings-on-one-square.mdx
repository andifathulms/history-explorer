---
title: Two kings on one square
drafted_from: elliot-dowson-4

In 1029 Ala al-Dawla watched what happened to the man whose family had given him
Isfahan, and appears to have drawn the correct conclusion from it.

Mahmud of Ghazna came west that year to take Jibal. On the way, Majd al-Dawla —
the Buyid at Rayy, the son of the regent Sayyida Shirin, and the cousin through
whom the Kakuyids held anything at all — sent a messenger to Ghazna complaining
about his own commanders and asking for help against them. Mahmud sent troops
towards Rayy. Majd al-Dawla joined them, and they arrested him.

Khwandamir preserves the interview. Mahmud had the prisoner brought into his
assembly and asked him whether he had read the *Shahnama* and whether he knew
Tabari's history. Yes. Had he ever played chess? Yes. Then, said Mahmud: in
those books, have you ever seen it written that two kings can rule in one
country — and on the board, have you ever seen two kings on one square? No, said
Majd al-Dawla. Then how does it come about, said Mahmud, that you handed the
reins of your power to a man stronger than yourself? He sent the Buyid and his
son to Ghazna under guard and gave the province to his own son Mas'ud.

Two years later Mahmud was dead, Mas'ud had blinded his brother and taken the
throne, and one of the first acts of the new reign was a *farman* handing the
governorship of Isfahan to Ala al-Dawla ibn Kakuya. Khwandamir puts the
transaction in a single dry sentence and then explains the family name — in the
language of Daylam a maternal uncle is called *kakuya* — and adds the whole of
the dynasty's political history in a second sentence: this Kakuya was at first
the viceroy of Iraq, and afterwards he arrogated to himself absolute dominion.

The arrogating was done by watching where the sultan was. Mas'ud spent his
reign campaigning in India, which Khwandamir treats as the error that lost him
everything else, and while he was on the Indus, Ala al-Dawla rebelled and drove
the Ghaznavid governor of Rayy out of the city. Mas'ud came back in 1036 or
1037 to find the Seljuks established in Khurasan and his Iranian provinces
gone, and by Khwandamir's account regretted the Indian expedition for the rest
of his short life. A poet sent him verses on the subject: your enemies were
ants and have grown into serpents; kill the ants that have become serpents,
give them no rest and delay no longer, for if they are given time the serpents
will become dragons.

The verses were about the Seljuks. They describe the Kakuyids exactly as well.
Ala al-Dawla never won a battle that mattered and never held anything for long
against anybody who came for it in force. What he did instead, for thirty
years, was accept the governorship from whoever was strongest, wait for that
man to be somewhere else, and take the city back — which is why the answer to
Mahmud's question turns out to be that two kings can share a square perfectly
well, provided one of them is prepared to be the smaller piece for as long as
necessary.
