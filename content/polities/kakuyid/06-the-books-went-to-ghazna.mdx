Ibn Sina died in his patron's service in 1037 and the Kakuyid state outlived
him by fourteen years. What happened to his library is the better story, because
it runs through three dynasties and ends in a fire.

## Why he was there

Ibn al-Athir is blunt about the arrangement, and Browne repeats him without
softening it: there is no doubt as to the unsoundness of Ala al-Dawla's
religious views, and it was on that account that Avicenna attached himself to
his court, so that he might be unmolested in the composition of his own
heretical works.

That is a transaction, and both sides understood it. A minor Daylamite prince
with a reputation for not caring what a man believed acquired the most famous
mind of the age, who in return produced the *Canon*, the *Danishnama* in Persian
for his patron to read, and a quantity of philosophy that would have been
awkward almost anywhere else.

The price of a heterodox court is that it has no friends.

## 1034

In 1034 Ala al-Dawla was defeated by the troops of Ghazna.

They plundered Isfahan, and among the plunder they took Ibn Sina's books. The
whole library was carried east and deposited in one of the libraries of Ghazna
— the city of the sultan who had demanded him from Khwarazm twenty years
earlier, had a portrait of him circulated when he ran, and never got him.

Mahmud got the books in the end. He had been dead four years.

## And then Jahansuz

They stayed at Ghazna for a hundred and sixteen years.

In 1151 Ala al-Din Husayn of Ghur took the city and burned it for seven days,
and was called *Jahansuz*, the World-burner, ever afterwards. Browne records
what went with it: the books remained in that library until they were destroyed
in the fire, in the sack of Ghazna by the King of Ghur, appropriately called
the World-burner.

So the working library of Ibn Sina — the annotated copies, the drafts, whatever
he had not published — was taken as loot from a small Iranian principality by a
Turkish empire, held for over a century in an Afghan mountain town, and burned
by a mountain chief from Ghur avenging his brothers.

## What survived instead

Everything that survives of him survives because it had already been copied out
and sent elsewhere.

The *Canon of Medicine* went to Baghdad and Cairo and eventually into Latin, and
was taught in European faculties into the seventeenth century. The *Danishnama*
survived as the first substantial philosophy in Persian. The correspondence with
al-Biruni survived because two young men had argued about Aristotle in writing
and other people kept the letters.

None of that was in the library. The library was the part that was his, and it
is the part that is gone.

A dynasty that is a footnote in every political history of Iran paid for the
conditions in which those books were written, and could not protect them for a
single year after it lost a battle.
