---
title: What they built at Yazd
drafted_from: chi-4
phase: aside
---

In 1036 or 1037, two military governors serving Ala al-Dawla built a domed
tomb-shrine at Yazd, on the edge of the desert, and it is still standing. It is
called the Davazdah Imam, the Twelve Imams, and it is the oldest dated building
in the city.

Its importance is structural and it is a genuinely large point in the history
of building. The problem it solves is the ancient one of putting a round dome
on a square room. The Iranian answer was the squinch — an arch thrown across
each corner to turn the square into an octagon — and what the Davazdah Imam
contains is one of the earliest surviving dated examples of a squinch broken
into a trefoil: three small arched cells instead of one plain arch. Multiply
that subdivision, and repeat it upward in tiers, and you have *muqarnas*, the
honeycomb vaulting that covers half the significant architecture of the Islamic
world for the next eight hundred years — Seljuk portals, the Alhambra, the
Safavid mosques of Isfahan.

The building is small, in a provincial town, put up by two officers of a minor
dynasty in the year before their patron's philosopher died.

That last sentence is the point. The Kakuyids held Isfahan for forty-three
years, ruled Yazd as somebody else's fief-holders for ninety more, and vanished
at a battle fought for a Seljuk sultan against Khitans from north China. By any
measure of power they are a footnote.

What they left is a settled decade that produced the *Canon of Medicine*, the
first substantial philosophical writing in Persian, and a corner of a dome at
Yazd on which the next eight centuries of Islamic architecture is built.

There is no way of ranking states that captures that, and there probably should
not be. A squinch broken into three cells is not a quantity. It is a solution to
a problem, arrived at in a provincial town by somebody whose name is not
recorded, and copied for eight hundred years by people who never heard of the
dynasty that paid for it.
