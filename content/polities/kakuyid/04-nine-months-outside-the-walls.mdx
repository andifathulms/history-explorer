title: Nine months outside the walls

Bar Hebraeus tells the fall of Isfahan without mentioning the family that held
it, which is the most accurate thing about his account.

Tughril Beg sat down in front of the city and stayed there. Bar Hebraeus gives
nine months and puts it in a year he cross-dates two different ways and gets
wrong both times, so the figure and not the date is what to keep. The people
inside, worn down by a siege he describes as an imprisonment, did not appeal to
their own ruler or to any neighbouring power. They sent to Baghdad and asked the
caliph to intercede with the sultan on their behalf.

The caliph refused. Tughril had been asking him for some time to be honoured
with titles befitting his kingship, and al-Qa'im had not been willing. The
Isfahanis had now given him something he could exchange for it. He agreed, wrote
to Tughril, addressed him as Lawful King, Asylum of the Muslims, Rukn al-Din
Sultan Tughril Beg, and at the end of the letter asked him to be merciful to the
people of Isfahan.

Tughril, Bar Hebraeus says, had been expecting exactly this. He accepted the
caliph's intercession and sent back twenty thousand dinars to the caliphal
treasury and two thousand for the officials who administered it. And from that
time he began to have the figure of a bow drawn at the head of his seal with the
titles written inside it — the *tughra*, and the clerk licensed to draw it the
*tughra'i*.

Every party to that transaction got what it wanted except the one being fought
over. The townspeople got relief; the caliph got twenty-two thousand dinars and
a client who had been made to ask twice; the sultan got a name with *al-Din* in
it, a seal design that outlived his dynasty by eight centuries, and the city.
Nobody involved thought it necessary to consult the man who was supposed to be
ruling Isfahan, and no account of the episode records what he did during the
nine months.

That is what the end of a middle-sized principality looks like from outside. It
is not conquered so much as handed over in the course of an unrelated
negotiation between two larger parties, as the consideration in a bargain about
titles.
