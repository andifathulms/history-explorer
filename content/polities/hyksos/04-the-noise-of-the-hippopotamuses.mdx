By Apepi's reign, Thebes was a tributary of Avaris in everything but name —
a rump Egyptian kingship in the south, paying deference north, while a third
power, the kingdom of Kerma in Nubia, watched from the other direction. The
literary tradition preserves one story about how that arrangement came
apart, and whether or not its details are exact, its shape matches what
happens next closely enough to be worth taking seriously as evidence of the
relationship it describes.

## A complaint from four hundred miles away

Apepi, in the story, sends a messenger to Seqenenre, the Theban king, with a
demand: that the hippopotamus pool kept at Thebes be done away with,
because the animals' bellowing keeps him awake in Avaris — a journey of
several hundred miles away, well beyond any distance at which the animals
could plausibly be heard. The complaint is transparently not about noise. It
is a test of whether a tributary king will act on an order from the throne
he answers to, issued for no better reason than that the throne can issue
it. The surviving copy of the text breaks off before showing Seqenenre's
reply.

## What his body shows instead

Seqenenre Tao did not survive whatever followed. His mummified body,
examined most recently by computed tomography, carries at least five
distinct wounds to the head and face — an axe blow across the cheek that
exposed the teeth and fractured the jaw, a wound above one eye, and others
— inflicted, on the pattern of the injuries, by more than one weapon and
more than one attacker, with no wounds anywhere on his arms of the kind a
man raises to block a blow. The current reading is an execution rather than
a death in open combat: a king captured and killed by an enemy who no
longer needed him alive, not a man cut down still fighting.

## What his sons did with it

Whatever actually happened at whatever encounter killed him, his son Kamose
treated it as an opening rather than a defeat to be absorbed. Kamose's own
account — carved on stelae at Karnak in his own words rather than preserved
through a later retelling — frames the war he then launched not as
retaliation for an insult but as a plain grievance about the country's own
condition: that a king could not be secure in Egypt while sharing it with
an Asiatic and a Nubian, each holding his own slice of it. Whatever the
literary hippopotamus story exaggerates or invents, it is describing exactly
the kind of provocation that account of Kamose's own war says started it.
