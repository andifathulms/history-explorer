The name by which this dynasty is known was never what it called itself. It
is a Greek rendering, transmitted by the third-century BC Egyptian priest
Manetho and preserved only in quotations by later writers, of an Egyptian
phrase — *heka khasut*, rulers of foreign lands — that Egyptian scribes had
long used more generally, for any chieftain from beyond the eastern frontier.
Manetho's own account, as the Jewish historian Josephus quotes it centuries
later, describes an invading horde of obscure race storming Egypt without a
battle, burning cities, and enslaving the population.

Nothing found at the site that was actually their capital supports that
story.

## What the ground shows

Avaris, in the eastern Delta, was not stormed from outside during this
period. Excavation there shows a Levantine population — Semitic-speaking, on
the evidence of names and material culture close to contemporary Syria and
Palestine — already settled and growing across several generations before
any of it ruled Egypt as a dynasty at all: Levantine-style houses, burials,
and pottery appearing in the town's stratigraphy well before the political
takeover Manetho places at a single dramatic moment.

By the time a line of local rulers at Avaris begins taking the full Egyptian
royal titulary — Horus name, Golden Horus name, throne name and birth name,
the whole formula a legitimate pharaoh used — the Fifteenth Dynasty is best
understood not as a foreign army that broke in, but as a resident foreign
population whose own leadership stepped into an authority a collapsing
Thirteenth Dynasty could no longer project into the Delta at all.

## What the claim to titulary actually did

Whatever the process that got them there, the decision that follows it is
unambiguous. Ruling as pharaohs, in the traditional five-part style, rather
than as tributary chieftains or a separate foreign enclave answering to
nobody, put this dynasty's kings inside the same institution of divine
kingship that Thebes and, eventually, every earlier Egyptian record consulted
here claims for itself. It is also exactly the claim later Egyptian sources
never forgive them for. Every hostile account written after the dynasty
fell attacks it as false kingship rather than as foreign rule pure and
simple — a distinction that only makes sense if the claim to real kingship is
what actually landed.
