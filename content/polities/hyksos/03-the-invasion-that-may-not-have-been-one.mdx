---
title: The invasion that may not have been one
drafted_from: shaw-2000

---

Two things about this dynasty are repeated in almost every popular account
of it: that it seized Egypt by sudden foreign invasion, and that it
introduced the horse, the war chariot and the composite bow to a country
that had lacked them. The archaeological picture that has accumulated since
systematic excavation of Avaris began complicates both claims without
simply reversing either one.

## The invasion

Manetho's account, written some fourteen centuries after the events it
describes and preserved only through later excerpts, is the ultimate source
of the sudden-invasion story: an obscure race, he says, overran the land
without a battle and burned the cities. What the excavated stratigraphy at
Avaris actually shows is a resident Levantine population growing across
generations before any political takeover, and a dynasty of local rulers
emerging from a settlement already there rather than arriving as a foreign
army passing through it. The transition from a weak, distracted Thirteenth
Dynasty to an independent Delta kingship looks, on this evidence, far more
like a population already present asserting home rule than like a conquest
narrative can accommodate — closer to the Thirteenth Dynasty's own kings
simply losing their grip on the Delta than to Manetho's story of it.

## The chariot

The technology question survives the revision better than the invasion
story does. Horses, composite bows and horse-drawn chariots do appear in
the Egyptian archaeological and textual record for the first time in this
general period, and the association with populations connected to this
dynasty's own Levantine background is real — these were established
military technologies across contemporary Syria-Palestine before they show
up in Egypt at all. What is harder to state precisely is a single moment of
transfer credited to one king's decision, since the same period is exactly
when Egypt's contact with the Levant, hostile and otherwise, was already
increasing on multiple fronts. The technology's origin outside Egypt is not
in serious doubt; a clean story of this dynasty as the sole and deliberate
agent of its introduction goes further than the evidence, read carefully,
actually supports.

## Why the harder story is worth keeping

Both revisions point the same direction: a dynasty remembered by its
enemies as sudden, foreign and destructive looks, on the ground its own
capital sat on, more like an already-resident population that inherited
power gradually and kept running an Egyptian administration once it had it.
That does not make the account that survives it wrong about how later
Egyptians felt. It makes it a document of how the losers of a war two
generations later chose to describe the side they had beaten — which is a
real fact about the war of liberation, just not the fact it presents itself
as being about its cause.
