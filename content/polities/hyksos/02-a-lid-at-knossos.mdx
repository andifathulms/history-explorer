Khyan, one of the dynasty's earlier kings and one of the harder to date
precisely, is attested on objects scattered further from Egypt than any
other Hyksos ruler's name: an alabaster jar lid carved with his cartouche,
excavated in the ruins of the palace at Knossos on Crete; a granite lion
bearing his name, bought on the antiquities market in Baghdad, of unknown
original findspot but plausibly reflecting contact reaching toward
Mesopotamia; and objects associated with his name as far as Hattusa, the
Hittite capital in central Anatolia.

None of this is a conquest record. It is the debris of gift exchange,
trade, or diplomatic courtesy between royal courts — the ordinary evidence
by which the ancient eastern Mediterranean's ruling houses are shown to have
known of each other at all. A Delta kingdom usually described, in the
Egyptian sources that survive it, purely as an occupying force turns out
also to have been a node in a network of courts that reached from Crete to
central Anatolia.

## A capital built for it

Avaris itself, excavated across decades by Manfred Bietak's expeditions, is
consistent with the objects: a harbour town with warehousing on a scale that
implies organised long-distance trade, and — in one garden precinct — the
fragmentary remains of wall paintings executed in an Aegean, Minoan-influenced
style, of a kind otherwise associated with Crete and its trading partners.
Whether the painters came from Crete or simply worked in a style Crete had
made fashionable across the region cannot be settled from the fragments
alone; either answer makes Avaris a place the wider Mediterranean world
reached, not a barracks sealed off from it.

## What continued under the longest reign

Apepi, the best-attested king of the line and, at up to forty years by the
Turin Canon's own figure, the longest-reigning, presided over a Delta
administration that kept using Egyptian scribal practice without evident
disruption. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus — a scribal exercise copying and
extending an older Middle Kingdom text on arithmetic, fractions and geometry
— carries a colophon dating its copying to Apepi's own thirty-third regnal
year, written in his name as the reigning king. A foreign dynasty's scribes
were, by this evidence, still training on the same mathematical curriculum
Egyptian scribes had used for centuries, under a king whose own name is what
they used to date it.
