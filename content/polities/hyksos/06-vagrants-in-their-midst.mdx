Ahmose's capture of Avaris ended the dynasty. It did not end what the dynasty
was used for.

## What the conquerors built out of it

The clearest afterlife is institutional, and it belongs to the Theban house
that did the conquering. A permanent standing army, continuous campaigning
beyond Egypt's old frontiers, Nubia annexed and administered directly rather
than merely raided, the Levant held through vassal princes — and the
equipment all of that ran on, the horse and light chariot, the composite bow,
better bronze and better armour, all Hyksos-period introductions that the
army which destroyed Avaris turned around and used against everyone else.
Egypt conquered a foreign dynasty and kept its army. The New Kingdom is what
that theft became, in the hands of the family that did the stealing.

## What one queen a century later did with the memory

The other afterlife is not about equipment. It is about what a ruler with a
shaky claim to the throne could still do with the memory of a defeat nobody
living had witnessed.

Roughly a century after Ahmose took Avaris, Hatshepsut — a woman ruling as
pharaoh in her own right, a position with no comfortable precedent — had an
inscription cut at the rock-cut temple of Speos Artemidos that reaches back
past her own father's generation to invoke the Hyksos directly:

> I have raised up what was dismembered, beginning from the time when the
> Asiatics were in the midst of the Delta, in Avaris, with vagrants in their
> midst, toppling what had been made.

She goes further, casting her own building programme as reversing not just
ruin but privilege: *while I restored what had decayed, I annulled the former
privileges that existed since the Asiatics were in the region of Avaris of
Lower Egypt.* Whatever had actually survived a century of Theban and then
New Kingdom rule to still count as unrestored by Hatshepsut's own reign, the
inscription does not need it to be literally true. It needs a ruined past to
have personally repaired, and a defeated foreign dynasty a century gone was a
past nobody was left to defend.

## A claim built the same way twice

The move is not unique to Egypt or to this period. A conqueror or a
successor invoking a defeated predecessor's ruin, or a predecessor's
legitimacy, to secure a contested claim of their own recurs wherever a throne
needs propping up with something older than the ruler's own reign — a
Cyrus explaining in writing why a wronged Marduk had wanted Babylon to change
hands, an Antiochus rebuilding a Babylonian temple three centuries after the
last native king to press exactly the same kind of claim. Hatshepsut's
version runs the trick in reverse: not a claim to inherit what an ended
dynasty had held, but a claim to have finished undoing the damage it did.
Either way, the dynasty is more useful defeated and half-forgotten than it
ever was standing, and it is still being used for something a hundred years
after the last of its kings went to Palestine and never came back.
