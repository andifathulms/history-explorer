---
title: Three generations of ambassadors
drafted_from: photius-freese
phase: contraction
---

The Byzantine empire employed the same family as its ambassadors to the Arabs
for three generations, and the last of them wrote a book about it. The book is
lost; a ninth-century patriarch's summary of it survives, and it is the fullest
outside account of what the Kindite chieftaincy was worth to an empire.

The line runs like this. Nonnosus's grandfather was sent to Arethas — al-Harith
the Kindite — under the emperor Anastasius, to conclude a treaty of peace.
Nonnosus's father Abramos was sent under Justin to al-Mundhir of Hira, and got
two captured Roman generals released. Then Justinian sent Abramos to Qays the
Kindite, and a treaty was concluded on condition that Qays's son Mu'awiya be
taken to Constantinople as a hostage. Then Nonnosus himself was sent to the same
Qays.

Photius identifies him in a way the Arabic tradition would recognise: Qays was
chief of two of the most illustrious Saracen tribes, the Kindites and the Ma'add.
That is the Hujrid position stated from outside — not a kingdom, but the
leadership of two confederations, held by one man and negotiable with.

Nonnosus's instructions were threefold: to persuade Qays to come to the emperor
in person, and then to go on to Kaleb king of Aksum and to the Himyarites. He got
through treacherous tribesmen, wild animals and a great deal of difficulty, and
came home safely.

What Qays did is the end of the story, and Photius gives it in two sentences.
After Abramos had been sent to him a second time, Qays set out for
Constantinople, having first divided his chieftaincy between his brothers Amr and
Yazid. He took a large number of his people with him. And the emperor appointed
him administrator of Palestine.

A man who led the tribes of central Arabia partitioned his following among his
brothers, emigrated with a substantial part of it, and accepted a provincial
office inside the Roman empire. Whatever the house had been, that is what it was
worth by then: a career, transferable, and better exercised somewhere else.

Photius keeps one other thing from the lost book, and it is the most valuable
observation in it about the society the Hujrids led. Most of the Saracens, says
Nonnosus, have a sacred meeting-place consecrated to one of the gods, where they
assemble twice a year. One assembly lasts a whole month, ending about the middle
of spring when the sun enters Taurus; the other lasts two months and is held
after the summer solstice. During these meetings complete peace prevails, not
only among themselves but with everyone else in the country — and, he adds, even
the animals are at peace with each other and with people.

That is a description of the Arabian sacred months, recorded by a Greek envoy
about a century before Islam, as an institution a diplomat needed to know about:
there are periods in which nobody in Arabia is at war, they are fixed
astronomically, and they belong to a shrine.

Photius then notes that other strange and more or less fabulous information is
also given, and declines to repeat it — which is how most of Nonnosus was lost.
