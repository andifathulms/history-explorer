---
title: The third family
drafted_from: fisher-2011
phase: formation

Three Arab dynasties were on retainer in late antiquity, and it is worth being
careful about their names. Ghassan, Lakhm and Kinda are confederacies —
groupings of tribes. The Jafnids, the Nasrids and the Hujrids are the ruling
families inside them, and they are what the empires actually dealt with; later
Arabic tradition merged the two so that a family with a following became a
nation with a royal house.

The pattern is the same in all three cases and the paymasters are different.
Rome paid the Jafnids to hold the Syrian steppe. Sasanian Iran paid the Nasrids
at al-Hira to hold the Euphrates frontier. And Himyar — the South Arabian
kingdom at the height of its power in the fifth century — paid the Hujrids to
hold central Arabia.

That third relationship is the one usually left out, because Himyar is usually
left out. A reader who knows the Jafnids and the Nasrids as the Arab clients of
the two great empires has been given a picture with a third empire missing from
it: South Arabia in this period was a substantial state with an interest in the
tribes to its north, and it managed them the way Rome and Iran managed theirs.

What all three families held, they held on the same terms. Their money came from
somebody else's treasury, and their right to lead came from somebody else's
grant — which is a different kind of authority from either conquest or descent,
and it is the same kind whether the grantor was the Roman emperor or a king in
the Yemeni highlands. It works for exactly as long as the grantor can pay and
wants to.
