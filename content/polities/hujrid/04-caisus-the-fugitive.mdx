Around 531 the emperor Justinian sent an ambassador named Julianus down the Red
Sea with two requests, and the second of them is the only contemporary outside
notice of what had become of the Hujrids.

The first request went to the Ethiopians: buy silk from India and sell it to
the Romans, so that Roman money stops going to Persia. The second went to the
king of Himyar, and it was this. Install **Caisus** as captain over the
Maddeni, and then invade Persia with a great army of your own people and of the
Maddene Saracens.

Maddeni is Ma'add — the tribes of central Arabia. Caisus is Qays. And the way
Procopius describes him tells you the whole position of the family:

> This Caisus was by birth of the captain's rank and an exceptionally able
> warrior, but he had killed one of the relatives of Esimiphaeus and was a
> fugitive in a land which is utterly destitute of human habitation.

A man of the ruling stratum, personally formidable, wanted for a killing at the
Himyarite court, living in an empty desert — and being discussed by the Roman
emperor as an asset that the Himyarite king might usefully reactivate.

## What the request assumes

Two things, and both are informative.

It assumes that the leadership of the central Arabian tribes was in the gift of
the king of Himyar. Not a matter of who the tribes followed, but of whom Zafar
appointed. That is exactly the relationship the Hujrid position had always
rested on, and here it is stated as a plain fact by an outside power making
plans on the strength of it.

And it assumes the office was vacant, or vacant enough to be filled by
proclamation. Nobody in Constantinople thought there was a functioning
confederation in central Arabia with a leader who would have to be dealt with.
They thought there was a job, and an exile who could be put back into it.

That is the condition of the house a few years after its chief was killed
recovering al-Hira. Not destroyed, not conquered, and not there either: a
qualification without a position, held by a man who could not go home.

## Nothing happened

Neither king did what he had promised.

The Ethiopians could not buy Indian silk, because Persian merchants sat at the
harbours where the Indian ships first came in — they lived next door — and
bought whole cargoes before anyone else could bid.

And the Himyarites found that crossing the desert to reach Persia was harder
than it had sounded in Constantinople: a country empty of people, extending so
far that the march would take a very long time, at the end of which they would
arrive among men far more warlike than themselves.

Later, Procopius adds, once Abraha had made his own position in Himyar
completely secure, he repeatedly promised Justinian that he would invade
Persia — and started once, and turned straight back.

So a Roman emperor spent years planning a war in which Arab client tribes under
a restored Hujrid would open a second front against Persia, and the plan died of
distance. The dynasty's last appearance in the record of the empires is as an
instrument that was considered, agreed to, and never picked up.
