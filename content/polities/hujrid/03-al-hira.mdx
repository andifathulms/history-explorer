Around 525 al-Harith ibn Amr, the Hujrid chief, took al-Hira.

Al-Hira was the seat of the Nasrids, the Arab dynasty that held the Euphrates
frontier for Sasanian Iran — the eastern counterpart of the Jafnids, whose own
chief would destroy the Nasrid king in battle in 554. It
was a real town with churches and a court and a literary reputation, and it was
Persia's instrument in Arabia.

<Figure id="lakhmid-map" />

A chief from central Arabia holding it, however briefly, is the most striking
thing the Hujrids ever did. It is the only occasion on which one of the three
client dynasties displaced another, and for a few years it made this family
something more than the object of somebody's Arab policy: Constantinople and
Ctesiphon both had to deal with them as a party rather than manage them as an
asset.

<Figure id="hira" />

It did not hold. Al-Mundhir of al-Hira recovered his capital, and in 528 he
defeated al-Harith and killed him, along with — the accounts say — a large part
of his family. The Persians backed the recovery, and the whole episode reads
from Ctesiphon as a client being restored rather than a war being won.

<Figure id="khawarnaq" />

The Hujrid position in central Arabia did not survive it. The confederation broke
into its constituent tribal groupings, which spent the following decades fighting
each other and were still doing so within living memory of the rise of Islam. The
sons and grandsons of the house became claimants to something that no longer
existed, and one of them wrote about it.
