Central Arabia is not a place a state governs. It is a place tribes move through
— a plateau of pasture and oases where a confederation can be assembled and a
population cannot be taxed, and where the significant question for any outside
power is not who holds it but who can guarantee that a caravan crosses it.

That is what the Hujrids were for. Himyar, at the height of its power in the
fifth century and interested in the routes north towards the Hejaz and the Roman
frontier, installed and backed a family of the Kinda to exercise authority over
the tribes of the Najd. The family traced itself to Hujr, called Akil al-Murar,
and their position rested on Himyarite recognition, Himyarite money, and their
own ability to hold a coalition together.

At its widest the Hujrid position extended over a great deal of central and
northern Arabia and reached towards both imperial frontiers, and by the early
sixth century Roman and Persian diplomacy had to take account of it. Roman
embassies dealt with the Hujrids directly; a Kindite chief appears in the
Byzantine sources negotiating over the tribes of the desert frontier, which is a
thing Constantinople did not do with people it considered marginal.

Qaryat al-Faw, on the western edge of the Empty Quarter, is the archaeological
site most associated with Kinda: a substantial town with markets, houses,
temples, tombs and inscriptions, excavated since the 1970s and one of the
richest pre-Islamic sites in the peninsula. This record's `capitals` field is
nonetheless empty, because no consulted source calls it a capital of this
dynasty, and the site does not fill a field with a plausible guess.

What the Hujrids never acquired was anything that would outlast the subsidy and
the man. There was no city they governed from, no administration, no revenue of
their own. This is the standing condition of the three client dynasties and it is
why all three ended the way they did: they were arrangements, and an arrangement
can be cancelled.
