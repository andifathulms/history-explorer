---
title: The prince who went looking for a kingdom
drafted_from: fisher-2011
phase: aside
---

Imru' al-Qays is the first poet of the Arabic canon. His *mu'allaqa* — one of
the seven odes tradition says were hung at the Ka'ba — opens with the line every
Arabic-speaking schoolchild knows, about halting at an abandoned campsite to
weep over the remembered beloved, and the convention it established governed
Arabic poetry for a thousand years afterwards.

He was a Hujrid. His father Hujr ibn al-Harith was the last Kindite king over
the Banu Asad, who killed him; and the tradition has Imru' al-Qays receiving the
news at a drinking party and saying that today is for wine and tomorrow for
serious matters, and then spending the rest of his life trying to recover his
father's kingdom. He went to the tribes, and to the Jafnids, and finally to
Constantinople to ask Justinian for an army — and died on the way home, in
Anatolia, of a poisoned robe in the legend and of something else in fact.

Almost none of that can be verified. The biography comes from Arabic sources
written three centuries later, in a tradition that assembled poets' lives out of
their own verses, and Fisher's book is careful about the difference between what
the pre-Islamic poetry can be used to establish and what it cannot. What is
reasonably secure is that a Kindite prince of that name existed, that the
dynasty had lost its position, and that the poetry which became the foundation
of Arabic literature was composed in and about that world.

Political weight and cultural output come apart constantly, and the Hujrid case
is about as extreme as the separation gets. A client dynasty that held nothing,
built nothing, lasted perhaps eighty years and left no institution is remembered
because the man who lost it wrote well about having lost it.

What it transmitted was not a religion, a script or a claim that anyone
inherited. It was a way of beginning a poem — the halt at the abandoned
campsite, the weeping over the traces — and every Arabic poet for the next
thousand years began that way, including the ones writing in Cordoba and
Isfahan who had never seen a desert.
