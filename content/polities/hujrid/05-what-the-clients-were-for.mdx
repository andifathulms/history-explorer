By the middle of the sixth century the Hujrid confederation had dissolved, and
this record ends there with `fragmentation` and an empty `by`, because nobody
conquered it and there was nothing to conquer.

That is the third of three endings and they are worth setting beside each other,
because between them they are the state of Arab politics on the eve of Islam.

The Hujrids ended in the 530s and 540s, broken by a Persian client and then by
their own tribes, with Himyar — the power that had backed them — collapsing at
the same time under an Aksumite invasion. The Nasrids of al-Hira ended in about
602, when the Sasanian king executed the last of them and put a Persian governor
in his place. The Jafnids ended in 582, when the Roman emperor arrested the
supreme phylarch and divided his following among fifteen chiefs.

Within about seventy years, all three empires dismantled their own Arab
frontier arrangements, for reasons that were sound in each case and unrelated to
each other. Rome's client had acquired an independent religious constituency.
Persia's had become unreliable. Himyar's patron ceased to exist.

Then, in the 630s, armies came out of Arabia and took the Sasanian empire
entirely and the Roman east permanently.

The connection between those two facts is one of the oldest arguments in the
field and this site is not going to settle it. The Jafnid record states the
caution and it holds here: the Roman east in the 630s had just survived
twenty-six years of Persian war and plague, there is more than one thing wrong
with it, and an argument that runs from a decision in 582 to a battle in 636 has
to explain fifty-four years.

What can be said is narrower and is not in dispute. Three empires spent two
centuries building Arab political authority on their frontiers — courts,
subsidies, titles, churches, a poetry — and then ended all of it within a
lifetime. The people those arrangements had organised did not stop existing, and
the next thing they organised themselves into was not somebody's client.
