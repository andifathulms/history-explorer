Everything the Hujrids left is poetry, and the poetry did not survive by being
written down at the time. It survived by being memorised, professionally, for
two hundred years.

Pre-Islamic Arabia had an institution for this. Every professed poet had a
*rawi* — a reciter — to whom he gave his poems as he composed them and who
transmitted them onward. Many rawis were poets themselves, and many poets had
served as somebody else's rawi first. Imru' al-Qays, the Hujrid prince, was the
rawi of a poet of al-Hira before he was anything else.

That is the whole apparatus. No archive, no library, no manuscript: a chain of
named men, each holding a body of verse in his head and passing it to the next.

## The collectors

The chain reaches paper in the eighth century, in Iraq, under the Umayyads and
the early Abbasids, when scholars began hunting down what survived of the old
poetry. Four names matter, and the two that matter most for this dynasty are
uncomfortable ones.

Hammad al-Rawiya — a man of Persian descent, son of a Shapur — is the source of
the first redaction of Imru' al-Qays's poems, and it was Hammad who assembled
the selection that became the seven *Mu'allaqat*. Everything the canon holds
about the Hujrid prince passed through him.

Hammad is *suspected* of having put forward compositions of his own as ancient
poems. His contemporary Khalaf al-Ahmar, another foreigner of enormous
learning, is *known* to have done it.

That is the transmission of the pre-Islamic canon stated honestly: a two-century
oral chain, terminating in eighth-century Iraq with a small number of collectors
of non-Arab origin, at least one of whom forged, and the most important of whom
is under suspicion.

## The suspension

The tradition that gave the poems their name says they were so admired that
Arabs had them written out in gold on fine white Egyptian cloth and hung inside
the Ka'ba, which is why the seven are also called the Golden.

It is a story about status rather than a report of an event, and it is doing
something precise. The odes are being placed, retrospectively, in the one
building that everybody afterwards agreed mattered — before Islam, in a shrine
Islam kept.

## What the dynasty came to

Put the two things together and the Hujrid afterlife is stranger than the
ordinary case of a state remembered through its literature.

A client confederation in central Arabia held a position granted by a South
Arabian kingdom for perhaps eighty years, lost it, and left nothing — no city,
no institution, no successor, no inscription of its own. Its last prince's
poetry became the foundation of the literary language of a civilisation that had
not yet begun.

And it did so by being carried in the memories of professional reciters through
the rise of Islam and the conquests, into the hands of Persian and Turkish
scholars in Iraq who were compiling a canon of Arab antiquity for an empire that
needed one — some of whom were writing their own contributions to it.

The Hujrids survive. What survives of them was selected, arranged and possibly in
part invented by people from outside Arabia, two hundred years after the last of
them died.
