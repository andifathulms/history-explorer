Everything modern scholarship knows about the Zhangzhung language
traces back to a cache of manuscripts sealed inside a cave complex at
Dunhuang, on the Silk Road in what is now western China, and rediscovered
in the early twentieth century. Among the thousands of Tibetan-language
documents in that hoard, a handful were written in an unfamiliar tongue
using Tibetan letters - texts nobody at the time could read, in a
language nobody could immediately identify.

## A scholar's hypothesis, decades ahead of proof

It was F. W. Thomas who first proposed, working through the strange
vocabulary and grammar of these fragments, that they represented two
distinct unknown languages, one of which he identified as Zhangzhung.
The hypothesis rested on painstaking analysis of word forms in
documents that gave no direct statement of what language they were
written in - an argument built entirely from internal linguistic
evidence, decades before later scholars could test it against other
material.

## A dictionary preserved by the religion that outlived the kingdom

The clearest confirmation came from an unexpected direction: a
Tibetan-Zhangzhung dictionary published in Delhi in 1965, compiled
from vocabulary preserved in Bon monastic texts and edited by a monk
serving as chief priest of a Bon monastery in Kathmandu. That later
scholars could analyze this dictionary and find a genuine, internally
consistent West Tibetan language behind it - not merely religious
jargon invented to sound old - depended entirely on Bon communities
having kept using Zhangzhung vocabulary in their own scriptures for
nearly a thousand years after the kingdom itself had stopped existing
as a political entity. The kingdom left almost no state records
behind; what it left instead was a religious tradition careful enough
about its own inherited language to keep a dictionary's worth of it
alive.
