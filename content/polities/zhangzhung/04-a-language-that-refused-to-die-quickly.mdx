title: A language that refused to die quickly

A political kingdom's absorption did not mean its language went
silent. For roughly four centuries after Zhangzhung's territory
became part of the Tibetan Empire, Zhangzhung continued to be spoken
and, within Bon religious communities specifically, continued to be
written. It was not until around the eleventh century that Tibetan
finally displaced it completely, a slow erosion rather than a sudden
extinction.

## A tribe's religion as an accidental archive

The reason any of this is recoverable at all comes down to one
group's own institutional persistence. The dMu tribe's Bon
communities kept using Zhangzhung vocabulary in their scriptures long
after ordinary political and administrative use of the language had
ended - not as a deliberate act of linguistic preservation, but
because a religious tradition's own core texts do not get casually
rewritten in a new language. Scholars examining this material
centuries later have debated how much of it represents a genuinely
old, spoken language versus later invention dressed up to look
ancient, but the more careful linguistic analyses - looking past the
specifically religious vocabulary that invites that suspicion, to the
ordinary grammar and basic word-stock underneath it - support the
conclusion that a real, distinct language stands behind the texts.

## What a kingdom becomes when only its liturgy survives

Zhangzhung is, in the end, a case study in how differently political
and cultural survival can play out. The kingdom's own government,
army, and ruling family left no continuous record recoverable by
later historians. Its language, carried inside a religious tradition
with every reason to keep repeating the same sacred words generation
after generation, outlasted the state that once spoke it by close to
half a millennium.
