Zhangzhung's independence ended the way a great deal of early
Tibetan diplomacy did: through marriage rather than open war, at
least at first. Songtsen Gampo, consolidating the power that would
become the Tibetan Empire, married a Zhangzhung princess - a match
that bound the two courts together and gave the rising power around
Lhasa a direct claim on Zhangzhung's own royal house.

<Figure id="zz-statue" />

## An absorption without a battle on record

No source consulted here preserves an account of a conquest, a
defeated Zhangzhung army, or a captured king. What survives instead
is the marriage alliance itself and the plain fact of the outcome:
during or shortly after Songtsen Gampo's own reign, Zhangzhung's
territory and political structures were absorbed into the empire he
was building. Whether this happened through negotiation, through a
slower administrative takeover, or through violence that simply went
unrecorded is not something the surviving sources can settle.

## The last independent power on Tibet's own frontier

Zhangzhung's absorption mattered beyond the territory itself. It
removed the one substantial rival power inside what would become
ethnically and linguistically Tibetan territory, leaving Songtsen
Gampo's own dynasty as the unchallenged center of the plateau's
political life. Every kingdom the empire would go on to fight -
Tang China, the Turks, the Arab caliphate - lay beyond Tibet's own
borders. Zhangzhung was the last one inside them.
