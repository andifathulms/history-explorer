Before there was a Tibetan Empire, there was a power in western Tibet
that the empire's own founders had to reckon with. Zhangzhung
controlled the high country around what is now the Mount Kailash
region, exerting real political influence over its neighbors long
before Songtsen Gampo's own dynasty rose to prominence around Lhasa.
Later Tibetan sources take its existence for granted - it appears in
the record already established, already a going concern, with no
surviving account of how it came to be that way.

## A language that outlasted the politics

What survives of Zhangzhung is not a king list or a chronicle but a
language - one now classified as Tibeto-Burman, once spoken across
the kingdom's own territory before Tibetan gradually displaced it. The
language did not vanish the moment the kingdom lost its independence;
it continued to be spoken, and evidently written, for centuries
afterward, only dying out completely around the eleventh century. A
political kingdom and a linguistic community turned out to have very
different lifespans, and only the second one left much of a trace.

## A people identified with a religion, imprecisely

Zhangzhung is often described simply as the homeland of the Bon
religion, but the more precise picture is narrower: the language
itself is specifically associated with the dMu tribe within
Zhangzhung, whose members were Bon believers, and it is through that
one tribe's own religious texts that the association between
Zhangzhung and Bon took hold in later tradition. The larger kingdom
almost certainly held a more varied population than the popular
shorthand suggests, but it is the dMu tribe's own religious
preservation of the language that ensured any of it survived to be
studied at all.
