Borobudur and Prambanan stand about twenty kilometres apart, on the same
volcanic plain in central Java, built within roughly a century of each other.
Borobudur is a Mahayana Buddhist stupa-mountain of some two million blocks with
five kilometres of narrative relief around its galleries. Prambanan is a
Shaivite complex whose central tower stands forty-seven metres and whose
reliefs carry the Ramayana. Between and around them are Kalasan, Sewu, Plaosan,
Mendut, Sambisari and forty or so others.

One small kingdom on one plain built the lot, in about a hundred and fifty
years.

## Buddhist and Hindu, twenty kilometres apart

The obvious question about that plain is how one small kingdom produced the two
monuments at once, and the older answer was that it did not: that there were
two dynasties, the Buddhist Sailendras and the Shaivite line of Sanjaya, ruling
in rivalry or in alternation.

That reading has been argued down over the last half-century toward something
messier: a single ruling group with both patronages inside it, intermarrying,
in which the labels describe what a particular king endowed rather than which
house he belonged to. Nobody has settled it, and a page like this is not the
place to try.

What is worth saying is that the kingdom carried neither religion anywhere.
Shaivism and Mahayana Buddhism arrived in Java by the same channels as
everything else Indic, from India by sea, and Medang's contribution was not to
spread them but to build more of them, in stone, than anywhere outside India.
Pagan did the same thing in Burma three centuries later with ten thousand
temples and equally little missionary effect.

Enormous devotion and no propagation at all is a combination that turns up more
often than the usual story about religions suggests.
