Around 929 Mpu Sindok moved the court from central Java to the Brantas valley in
the east. Central Java was never again the seat of a Javanese kingdom.

Nothing in the record explains it. There is no defeat, no invasion, no
succession crisis; the inscriptions simply stop being issued on one plain and
start being issued four hundred kilometres away. Merapi erupting, an epidemic,
a Srivijayan raid and a deliberate move toward the rice surplus and the trade of
the Brantas have all been proposed, and none of them is attested. The turning
point on this record is marked contested for that reason.

What the move left is the reason this record exists. The largest concentration of
eighth- and ninth-century monumental architecture in Southeast Asia stands on a
plain that a state walked away from, and it stood there through everything that
followed — Kediri, Singhasari, Majapahit, Demak, the sultanates, the Dutch —
without anybody ruling from it again. Borobudur was under volcanic ash and
vegetation when Raffles's men were shown it in 1814.

## The other reason this record matters to the region

The move east is where Java's dynastic chain starts running through the Brantas.
This corpus now has four consecutive Javanese records on that river system:
Kediri from 1042, Singhasari from 1222, second-Kediri for a year, Majapahit from
1293. All of them are downstream of a decision taken in 929 by a king this site
did not carry until now.

It also puts this record in the same landscape as the one the Melaka page keeps
pointing at. Melaka, with no farmland, bought its rice largely from Java; Demak,
on the north coast, sold it. The granary both of those records depend on is the
Brantas and the plains around it, and 929 is when the Javanese state moved onto
it.
