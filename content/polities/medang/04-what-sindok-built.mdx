Mpu Sindok did not simply relocate a court. He spent his reign turning a
displacement into a functioning kingdom, and within a decade of the move
east he had to prove it could survive a direct attempt to undo it.

## An attack that was answered rather than absorbed

An inscription dated 937 records that around 928-929 an army from
Srivijaya's Malayu base landed on Java's coast and pushed inland as far as
Nganjuk before Sindok's forces defeated it. The stone that records the
victory credits Sindok personally with saving the kingdom, which is worth
noticing: the same Srivijayan reach that would later be blamed for helping
end the dynasty in 1016 had already tried once, within a few years of the
capital's move, and had been beaten back rather than accommodated.

## A kingdom built on water rather than on a battlefield

What actually filled the ninety years between the move and the pralaya was
not warfare but plumbing. Between 900 and 1060, royal intervention founded
more than forty new communities in the developing Brantas River delta, and
the surviving inscriptions of this period are overwhelmingly about
irrigation rather than conquest: dams, dykes and canals built to bring a
turbulent river under control, each one recording a king's own involvement
in commissioning it and instructing local elders on maintaining it
afterward. The Brantas flooded its banks often enough that unmanaged
farmland could not be trusted to feed a court; a managed one could, and
every east Javanese king after Sindok kept building the systems that made
that true.

## What that investment was worth

The scale of the payoff shows up precisely when the infrastructure failed.
When the river burst its dikes in 1033 under Airlangga - by then ruling
what had been rebuilt after 1016 - the resulting flood was remembered as
a genuine crisis: trading vessels could no longer reach the royal port,
and the maharaja's own income, along with his subjects', collapsed until
two new dams restored the old course. That a single breach could be
recorded as a catastrophe is itself the measure of how much an intact
system had been worth. Sindok's own successors had spent the ninth
century's end and the tenth building exactly the kind of centralised
water management whose sudden absence, decades later, was disaster enough
to write down.
