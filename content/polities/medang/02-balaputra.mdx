This site used to have one Maritime Asia region and it had two halves that barely
touched. Sumatra and the strait on one side — Srivijaya, then Melaka, Pasai,
Aceh, Johor. Java on the other — this record, Kediri, Singhasari, Majapahit,
Demak. The trade routes connected them and the records mostly did not, and the
one edge that ever crossed, Srivijaya to Singhasari in 1275, is a conquest three
centuries after the fact.

They are two regions now, and this paragraph is most of the argument for it. That
single crossing edge is the only thing the split cost.

The exception is a man.

Around 856 Rakai Pikatan consecrated the Shiva complex at Prambanan and, on the
reading of the Shivagrha inscription this record follows, defeated Balaputradeva
— the last Sailendra claimant in Java. Balaputra left.

A few years later a copper-plate charter from Nalanda in Bengal records a
monastery endowed there for the benefit of a king of Suvarnadvipa named
Balaputradeva, who gives his descent from the Sailendras of Java.

Suvarnadvipa is Sumatra. He had become king of Srivijaya.

## What the schema can do with that

Nothing, and this is the second record in two passes where the interesting
relationship has no field.

It is not an edge: Srivijaya and Medang are contemporaries, and this site's
edges run one way in time between polities where one comes after the other.
This corpus's Ternate record makes the same complaint about Tidore two
kilometres away, and its fourth chapter is entirely about a blank that means the
polity's defining relationship ran sideways.

It is not a successor claim in either direction: Balaputra did not claim Java
from Sumatra and Srivijaya did not become Medang. It is not an influence count of
any kind.

What it is, is one man walking from a defeat in one record to a throne in
another, taking a dynastic name with him, and endowing a monastery in a third
country that happens to have written it down. The site can hold that in a
turning point, marked contested because the Shivagrha reading is argued over, and
in this paragraph.

The turning point is also where this record's `peak` sits — which is a claim
worth stating plainly, since it puts the kingdom's height at a civil war rather
than at Borobudur. The reason is that Rakai Pikatan's reign is the one the
inscriptions let you see most clearly: a settled succession dispute, a monument
finished, and a defeated rival who is traceable afterwards.
