One man connects the two halves of the archipelago, and he did it by losing.

Around 856 Rakai Pikatan consecrated the Shiva complex at Prambanan and, on one
reading of the Shivagrha inscription, defeated Balaputradeva — the last
Sailendra claimant in Java. Balaputra left.

<Figure id="ramayana" />

A few years later a copper-plate charter from Nalanda in Bengal records a
monastery endowed there for the benefit of a king of Suvarnadvipa named
Balaputradeva, who gives his descent from the Sailendras of Java.

Suvarnadvipa is Sumatra. He had become king of Srivijaya.

## What that is and is not

It is not a conquest, an inheritance or a claim. Balaputra did not assert Java
from Sumatra, and Srivijaya did not become Medang; the two kingdoms were
contemporaries and stayed contemporaries.

What it is, is one man walking away from a defeat on one island, arriving on
another as its king, carrying a dynastic name with him, and endowing a
monastery in a third country that happened to write it down. Java and Sumatra
are otherwise two separate worlds in this period — different rivers, different
crops, different sources, almost no direct traffic in the record — and this is
the one place where a single individual is visible crossing between them.

The Shivagrha reading is argued over, so the whole chain is provisional. But if
it holds, the greatest maritime power in the region acquired its most famous
king because a Javanese succession dispute went the other way.

Putting the kingdom's height here rather than at Borobudur is a deliberate
choice. Rakai Pikatan's reign is simply the one the inscriptions let you see
most clearly: a succession settled, a monument finished, and a defeated rival
who can be traced afterwards to a throne four hundred miles away.
