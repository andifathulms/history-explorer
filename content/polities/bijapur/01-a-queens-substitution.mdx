Tradition holds that Yusuf Adil Shah was born a prince of the Ottoman
house, a younger son of Sultan Murad II, and that when his brother
Mehmed II ordered every rival male of the family put to death to
prevent future succession disputes, the infant's own mother contrived
to save him. She purchased a Circassian slave boy who resembled her son
closely enough to pass inspection, handed the substitute over to be
strangled in the prince's place, and entrusted the real Yusuf to a
Persian merchant with money enough to carry him far beyond the
executioners' reach. Whatever the truth behind so dramatic a story, a
man calling himself Yusuf Adil Khan later arrived in the Bahmani Deccan
from Persia and rose swiftly through royal service to command the
sultanate's foreign troops.

## An independence declared alongside four others

When the execution of Mahmud Gawan fractured Bahmani authority beyond
repair, Yusuf Adil Khan joined the same wave of governors who broke
from Gulbarga's control entirely — Ahmadnagar, Golconda, Bidar and
Berar all asserting their own sovereignty within the same years. Yusuf
took the royal title for himself and made Bijapur his capital, building
its citadel in stone and inviting scholars, officers and artists from
Persia, Turkestan and Anatolia to his court.

## A declaration that nearly cost him his throne

Around the time Shah Ismail of Persia established Shia Islam throughout
his own dominions, Yusuf Adil Shah, already a follower of that
persuasion, resolved to declare it publicly in Bijapur as well — the
first ruler in India to do so. He had the Friday prayers read in Shia
form at the grand mosque, a declaration bold enough that it briefly
threatened to cost him his throne outright, but he tempered the change
with enough restraint — forbidding public insults against the
companions the Shia and Sunni traditions dispute — that scholars of
rival schools continued worshipping side by side in the kingdom's
mosques without open conflict. Yusuf reigned twenty-one years,
recapturing Goa from the Portuguese shortly before his death, and died
of illness at seventy-five, remembered by his own court chroniclers as
a poet, musician and patron in equal measure with his skill as a
soldier.
