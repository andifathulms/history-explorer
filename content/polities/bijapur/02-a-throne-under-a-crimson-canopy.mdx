By the time Ali Adil Shah came to the throne, Vijayanagara's regent
Rama Raya had grown powerful enough, and arrogant enough, that all four
Deccan Muslim sultanates found common cause against him for the first
time in generations of mutual rivalry. Ali Adil Shah provoked the final
break deliberately, sending an ambassador to demand the return of
territory Rama Raya had taken from Bijapur over the years, confident
the demand would be refused with the contempt that gave the assembled
sultans their pretext for war.

## An army that marched where the enemy expected

The four allied kings — Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda and Bidar —
mustered their combined forces on the plains of Bijapur in December
1564 and marched toward the Krishna river, where Rama Raya had already
fortified every known crossing with cannon and earthworks. Rather than
force a defended ford, the allies feigned a march to a different
crossing point for three consecutive days, luring Rama Raya's own
forces away from their positions, then reversed course overnight and
crossed the undefended original ford before he could react.

## A regent who would not leave his throne

Battle was joined near the village that gave the engagement its name,
Talikota, on 23 January 1565. Ali Adil Shah commanded the coalition's
right wing. Rama Raya, warned repeatedly to fight from horseback rather
than from an exposed litter, refused, and when the battle turned
against him descended instead onto a jeweled throne beneath a crimson
canopy, distributing gold to encourage his own soldiers even as the
allied line closed around his position. He was captured and beheaded on
the spot, and his army, seeing their leader's head raised on a spear,
broke entirely.

## A victory whose spoils outlasted the war

The rout that followed destroyed Vijayanagara as a serious military
power for good, and Bijapur's own share of the resulting prestige and
territory marked the high point of Ali Adil Shah's reign — though
disputes over how to divide the conquered ground, particularly the
jealousy of Ahmadnagar's king, meant Bijapur never fully converted
its battlefield triumph into the outright territorial gains its role in
the campaign might otherwise have justified.
