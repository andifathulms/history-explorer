Ibrahim Adil Shah II, who came to the throne as a minor under a
succession of regents including his own aunt Chand Bibi before she
returned to her native Ahmadnagar, ruled long enough to see the
Mughal empire become a direct presence on Bijapur's own northern
border. When factional disputes inside Ahmadnagar's government invited
the Mughal prince Murad to intervene there directly, Ibrahim Adil Shah
sent his own general, Suhail Khan, north with an army to relieve the
besieged Nizam Shahy government rather than let a fellow Deccan
sultanate fall to Mughal arms unopposed — aid that arrived in time to
see the Mughal army withdraw before actually engaging it.

## A relic received with reverence

Diplomacy with more distant Muslim courts mattered as much to Ibrahim
as war closer to home. An embassy exchange brought him two hairs
attributed to the Prophet Muhammad, which he had mounted in a
jewel-set golden shrine and visited personally every Friday night and
holy day thereafter — a gesture of piety the source records alongside
his patronage of learned men and his own considerable interest in
music and architecture.

## An account that ends without an ending

Firishta's own narrative of Bijapur's history stops here, in the
middle of Ibrahim Adil Shah II's reign, with no account of how it
resolved and no closing summary of the king's own death or successors.
The source's English translator suggests Firishta meant to finish
Bijapur's history last of all the Deccan sultanates he covered, and
that his own death — probably sudden, since nothing in his surviving
work suggests he expected to stop — left the section unfinished exactly
where the manuscript breaks off. Bijapur itself endured for nearly a
century after this point, a fact this source simply has no occasion to
record.
