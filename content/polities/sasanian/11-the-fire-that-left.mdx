A state religion does not end when the state does. It becomes a minority
religion, which is a much harder thing to be, and the best-documented part of
what happened to Zoroastrianism afterwards is the story of a group who decided
not to stay and find out.

The account of it comes from one text, and its limitations have to be set out
first. The *Qissa-i Sanjan* was composed in 1600 by a Zoroastrian priest,
Bahman Kaikobad Sanjana, living at Navsari in Gujarat — which is to say between
eight and nine hundred years after the events it describes. Karaka, writing in
1884, prints it as the only substantial source there is and then spends a
footnote demonstrating that its own chronology does not work: the Hindu and
Parsi dates given for the landing do not coincide in 716, where tradition puts
it, but in 936, and he canvasses the argument that the day and month have been
transposed. His conclusion is that the accounts of the first and later
emigrations have been confused with one another.

So: a community memory, written down nine centuries late, whose dates are
demonstrably tangled. It is also all we have, and the shape of it is
corroborated by the fact that the people are there.

## Diu, and then a storm

The refugees came by sea from the Gulf and made land first at Diu, an island off
the Kathiawar coast, where the text says they stayed nineteen years — long
enough, Karaka notes, to learn the language, religion and customs of the Hindus,
which turned out to matter.

Then an aged dastur, reading the tablets of the stars, made an augury that they
should go, and they sailed for Gujarat and ran into a storm. In it they made a
vow: bring us to shore and we will kindle a fire in your honour.

They landed at Sanjan, twenty-five miles south of Daman, in a territory ruled by
a chief the text calls Jadi Rana, who saw a boatload of hard-looking armed men
and asked, reasonably, what they were. The high priest answered with sixteen
verses summarising the religion — we worship Ahura Mazda and the sun and the
five elements; we keep silence while bathing, praying, making offerings to fire,
and eating; we use incense, perfumes and flowers in our ceremonies — and the
nineteen years at Diu are what made that answer legible to a Hindu prince.

They were allowed to stay. They asked permission to build a fire temple, and got
it easily, because the people they were asking also revered fire. The temple was
finished around 721 and the fire was consecrated in it.

## The itinerary of a flame

Three hundred years of peace, the text says, and the community spread — Cambay,
Ankleshwar, Variav, Vankaner and Surat to the north, Thana and Chaul to the
south. A Pahlavi inscription in the Kanheri caves records
that a party of Parsis visited them on 2 December 999.

Then Sanjan was taken. The Parsis fought for their Hindu ruler and lost; their
general was killed by a dart; the Hindu prince fell in the same battle. What
follows is the part worth having, and it is an itinerary.

They took the fire eight miles east into the mountains of Bahrot and kept it in
a cave there for twelve years. Then to Bansda, north-east of Navsari, for
fourteen. Then to Navsari itself. In 1733, with Pindari raiders about, it was
moved to Surat; three years later back to Navsari; in 1741, after a quarrel
among the priests, to Balsar; and on 28 October 1742 to Udvada.

It is still burning at Udvada.

Sanjan has nothing. When a Bombay missionary visited in 1839 he found one or
two Parsi families; by Karaka's time there were none, and the only monument to
the settlement was the ruin of a tower of silence.

## The other direction

Some went east instead, and the traces are scattered through the Arabic
geographers rather than through any single narrative. A son of Yazdegerd is
reported to have gone to China in the seventh century. In 758 the Persians and
Arabs at Canton were numerous enough to raise a riot and plunder the city. In
845 there is a notice of mobeds at Canton, and half a century later Mas'udi
records that there were many fire temples in China.

## What this is an afterlife of

Ardashir's dynasty made Zoroastrianism the religion of a state for the first and
only time in its history: a chief priest who was an officer of government, an
endowed priesthood, fires founded by kings, and Kartir's inscription boasting of
striking at everybody else.

Four hundred years of that ended in 651. What the religion had afterwards was a
tolerated status inside somebody else's empire, a community that got smaller
every century, and a group of people who put a fire on a boat.

The empire is the reason anybody remembers the religion. The boat is the reason
the religion is still going.
