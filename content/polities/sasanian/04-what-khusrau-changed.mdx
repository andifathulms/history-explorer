---
title: The survey
drafted_from: chi-3
phase: peak
---

The Sasanian empire inherited its structural problem from the empire it
replaced: great noble houses with their own land, their own armed followings and
a decisive voice in who was king. Ardashir had come from exactly such a
house. The dynasty spent four centuries trying to reduce them and the
reforms of the sixth century are the serious attempt.

The circumstances were a crisis. Peroz had been killed with his army by the
Hephthalites in 484 and the empire paid them tribute for a generation; the
Mazdakite movement — a religious and social programme that appears in the
sources as an attack on property and on the aristocracy, and which Kavad
supported for a time — convulsed the country; Kavad was deposed and restored.
What came out of it under his son Khusrau I was a reconstruction of the state.

The fiscal half was a cadastral survey. Land was measured, crops and trees
counted, and a fixed assessment in cash imposed in place of a proportional levy
on each harvest — which made revenue predictable, made it collectable without
an official standing in every field, and made it possible to budget. A poll tax
on non-Zoroastrian subjects, graded by wealth and exempting the poor, the old,
the young and the clergy, was regularised beside it.

The military half followed from the money. The crown began paying, equipping and
mounting cavalry directly instead of depending on magnates to bring their own,
and the single army command was divided into four regional ones under *spahbeds*
so that no one commander held the whole force. The result was two armies at
once: the dehqan gentry holding land and turning out armoured on it, and a paid
professional corps beside them that answered to the treasury.

The reforms worked well enough that Khusrau I is the height of the dynasty, and
he is remembered in the later Persian and Arabic tradition as the model of a
just king — Anushirvan, of the immortal soul — which is a reputation constructed
partly by an Islamic bureaucracy that admired the system it had inherited.

Because that is what happened to it. When the Arabs took Mesopotamia and Iran a
century later they took the survey, the assessment, the tax registers and the
officials who kept them, and the *kharaj* and *jizya* of every Islamic polity on
the Islamic world afterwards are the Sasanian *kharag* and *gizyat*, under the
same names. The caliphates ran Khusrau's system for centuries, and in a good
many places they ran it with Khusrau's clerks.
