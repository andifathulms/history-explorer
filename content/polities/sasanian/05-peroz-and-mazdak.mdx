The fifth century is where the Roman frontier stops being the story. The
empire's worst defeat in its whole history came from the opposite direction,
and the crisis that followed it did more to shape the state than any of the
wars with Rome.

## Peroz and the Hephthalites

The Hephthalites were a steppe confederation that had established itself on
the north-eastern frontier, in the old Kushan territory beyond the Oxus, and
Peroz spent much of his reign fighting them. He lost more than once. Captured
on one occasion, he bought his release and left his son as a hostage against
the ransom; the empire had to strip its treasury and its temples of gold to
pay it.

He went back a third time in 484, against advice, and was destroyed with his
army in a battle the sources locate somewhere near Herat. The king of kings
was killed in the field, his body was never recovered, and the Sasanian army
that met the Hephthalites ceased to exist as a fighting force. It is the
single worst military defeat in the dynasty's history, worse than anything
Rome ever inflicted, and it happened on a frontier the Roman material makes
easy to forget the empire even had.

What followed was tribute. For roughly a generation the treasury paid the
Hephthalites to stay on their own side of the Oxus, and a state that had been
solvent found itself unable to fund an army, a court or a building programme
without deficit financing it could not sustain.

## Kavad and Mazdak

Into that fiscal wreckage came a religious and social movement associated
with a priest named Mazdak, who argued — in the surviving hostile accounts,
which is all there is — that property and women should be held in common and
that hoarding by the wealthy was itself the source of social disorder. What
Mazdak actually taught is contested, because everything written about him
was written by the aristocracy and the orthodox clergy who destroyed the
movement and had every reason to make it sound as alarming as possible.

Kavad I, king of kings and facing an empty treasury and a nobility that had
just watched his predecessor die bankrupting the state to pay foreigners,
supported Mazdak for a period. Whatever the movement's actual content, the
effect was an attack on the great landed houses at exactly the moment a king
needed money and leverage against them. The magnates and the priesthood
deposed Kavad in 496 and put his brother on the throne.

Kavad did not stay deposed. He escaped to the Hephthalites — the same power
that had just spent a generation collecting Sasanian tribute — borrowed an
army from them, and retook his throne in 498 or 499. He then turned on the
movement that had helped bring him down the aristocracy's wrath in the first
place: Mazdak and a large number of his followers were killed in a purge,
by most accounts with Kavad's own son Khusrau taking a leading part in it.

## What the crisis actually was

A king humiliated and killed by steppe nomads; a generation of tribute that
emptied the treasury; a social movement that used the resulting crisis to
attack the nobility directly; a second king restored to his own throne by the
same foreign power that had just bankrupted his predecessor; and a purge that
ended the movement while leaving the underlying problem — a crown too poor
and too weak to control its own magnates — exactly where it had been.

Nothing about that resembles decline dressed up as a Roman-facing narrative
would tell it. It is the specific, dated sequence of failures that made the
reforms of the following reign necessary rather than optional, and the man
who inherited the problem was Kavad's own son.
