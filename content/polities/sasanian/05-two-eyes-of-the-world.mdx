---
title: The two eyes of the world
drafted_from: chi-3
phase: peak
---

For four centuries the Sasanians and the Romans fought over the same corridor —
Armenia, upper Mesopotamia, the Syrian frontier — in wars that neither could win
and neither could stop. Counting the Parthian record before this one, it is six
hundred years of the same war, and it is the longest sustained rivalry between
two states in the ancient world.

The most important thing about it is that it was not a war of extermination and
neither side pretended otherwise. Sasanian and Roman diplomacy had a protocol, a
correspondence, ambassadors with defined status, and a shared formula — the two
eyes of the world, two empires whose brightness lit everything else. A
sixth-century Persian letter to Constantinople and a Roman one to Ctesiphon
address a brother. When Khusrau I took Antioch in 540, he deported its
population and built them a new city outside Ctesiphon laid out on the same plan,
with the same amenities, called Weh-Antiok-Khusrau — Khusrau's Better Antioch —
and let them keep their own bishop and their own baths.

The wars had a rhythm accordingly. Campaigns for cities and fortresses; a
frontier that moved tens of kilometres and returned; enormous payments described
by each side as subsidies for the joint defence of the Caucasus passes and by
the other as tribute; and periodic Eternal Peaces, one of which lasted eight
years.

There is a corpus point in this that the fields cannot carry. Two empires that
recognise each other as peers behave differently from empires that do not, and
almost nothing else in this site's fifteen hundred years has a counterpart it
regards that way. The Ottomans and the Safavids fought a confessional war in
which each obtained rulings that the other's subjects could be killed. The
Mamluks and the Ilkhanids fought for sixty years before signing anything. Rome
and Iran had an argument about a frontier and a diplomatic vocabulary for
conducting it, and they kept both for six hundred years.

It is also why the ending is so abrupt. A rivalry conducted at that pitch by two
states that each assumed the other was permanent produced, in 602, a war in
which one of them tried to end the other — and the next two chapters are about
what that cost them both.
