---
title: Khusrau's reforms, and Rome
drafted_from: chi-3
phase: peak
---

For four centuries the Sasanians and the Romans fought over the same corridor —
Armenia, upper Mesopotamia, the Syrian frontier — in wars that neither could win
and neither could stop. It is the longest sustained rivalry between two states
in the ancient world, and its most important feature is that both sides
recognised the other as a legitimate empire. Sasanian and Roman diplomacy has a
protocol, a vocabulary, and a shared assumption that there are two eyes of the
world.

Khusrau I's reign is conventionally the height. He reformed the land tax onto a
fixed assessment by survey rather than a proportion of each harvest, which made
revenue predictable and made the treasury independent of the great families. He
built a salaried cavalry — the *dehqan* gentry — that answered to the crown
rather than to magnates. He took Antioch in 540 and deported its population to a
new city built for them near Ctesiphon.

The reforms are why the empire could fight the war of 602–628 at the scale it
did, and the war of 602–628 is why the reforms did not save it.
