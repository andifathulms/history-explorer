The last war against Rome, described above, cost both empires everything they
had and ended with Khusrau II deposed and killed by his own nobility in 628.
What followed was four years of civil war and roughly ten claimants, in an
empire whose treasury was empty, whose army had been in the field for a
quarter of a century, and whose Mesopotamian irrigation had been wrecked by
flooding nobody had the resources to repair.

Yazdegerd III was a child when he was raised to the throne in 632, the year
Muhammad died. The Arab armies broke the Sasanian field army at al-Qadisiyya in
636 and took Ctesiphon; the empire was not defeated again so much as unwound,
province by province, over fifteen years, as garrisons surrendered and the
*dehqan* gentry made terms locally with the conquerors. Yazdegerd was murdered
by a miller at Merv in 651, having spent his reign retreating east.

<Figure id="ss-yazdegerd" />

There is a temptation to read this as decadence meeting vigour. The Sasanian
state that faced the Arabs was not decadent — it had almost destroyed Rome six
years earlier. It was bankrupt, leaderless and exhausted, which is a different
thing, and it had spent everything winning a war it then lost.

Nobody restored the line. But three centuries later the Buyids, ruling Baghdad
and holding the caliph in their hands, revived the title *shahanshah* — which
was an assertion that the Iranian monarchy the Sasanians had embodied was still
a thing a man could claim to be. It is the only such claim anybody made.
