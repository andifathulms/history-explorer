---
title: A state religion, and the church it made possible
drafted_from: daryaee-2009
phase: aside
---

The Sasanians are the Zoroastrian empire in a way the Achaemenids and Parthians
were not. They founded fire temples on a large scale, endowed a priesthood, made
the chief priest an officer of state, and produced under royal patronage the
written form of the Avesta and its Middle Persian commentary.

The inscriptions of Kartir, the chief priest under four kings in the third
century, are the clearest statement of the programme, and they are not
comfortable reading. He records establishing fires and priests across the empire
and then striking at Jews, Buddhists, Brahmins, Nazarenes, Christians, Baptisers
and Manichaeans — a list of everything else in the empire — and Mani himself, who
had preached with royal permission under Shapur, was imprisoned and died in
chains around 274.

So a state church, with the ordinary consequences. What makes the Sasanian case
worth a chapter is what happened next, which is the opposite of what a reader
would predict.

The Christians of the empire — many of them descended from the Roman deportees
of Shapur's campaigns — were persecuted heavily in the fourth century,
particularly after Constantine made Christianity Roman, because a Christian in
Iran was now a potential partisan of the enemy. And then in 410 the Church of the
East held a council at Ctesiphon under royal authorisation, organised itself into
a hierarchy under a catholicos, and became a legally recognised community of the
Sasanian empire. In 424 it declared itself independent of the Western
patriarchates altogether.

The reason is precisely the one that had made it suspect. A church that answered
to nobody in Roman territory was safe to have; and when the Roman empire
condemned Nestorius and his followers, the Church of the East's adoption of that
Christology made it permanently distinct from the church of its enemy, which
suited Ctesiphon exactly.

What followed is one of the largest facts in this corpus that almost never
appears on a map. The Church of the East, a legal minority in a Zoroastrian
empire, sent missions along the routes the empire taxed — to Arabia, to India,
to Central Asia, and to China, where a stele erected at Chang'an in 781 records
a century and a half of Christian presence under the Tang. This site's Tang and
Uyghur records are downstream of that, and none of it happens without a Persian
king deciding that a church cut off from Rome was an asset.
