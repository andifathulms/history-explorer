Shapur I, Ardashir's son, ruled for about thirty years from the 240s and fought
three Roman emperors. He defeated Gordian III, who died on campaign; he took a
large payment from Philip the Arab; and in 260, near Edessa, he defeated and
captured Valerian alive.

No Roman emperor had ever been taken by a foreign power and none was again.

What Shapur did with it is the part that mattered afterwards. He had the
victories
carved into the cliff at Naqsh-e Rustam, below the tombs of the Achaemenid
kings, in a series of reliefs showing a mounted Sasanian king and a Roman emperor
kneeling or held by the wrist. And he had an inscription cut on the Ka'ba-ye
Zartosht there in three languages — Middle Persian, Parthian and Greek — listing
his titles, his provinces, his campaigns and his victories, and the fire temples
he founded for the souls of his family.

That inscription, the *Res Gestae Divi Saporis*, is the single most important
Sasanian document, and the contrast with what went before is total. The
Parthians ruled Iran for four and a half centuries and left almost nothing
written about themselves. The Sasanians, in their first generation, were cutting
monumental self-description in three languages into a cliff chosen for its
Achaemenid associations — and they are consequently the best documented Iranian
dynasty before Islam by a wide margin, because they decided in advance to be.

The provinces the inscription lists reach from Syria to the Indus, and the title
it claims is the one the dynasty kept: king of kings of Eran and Aneran — of
Iran and of not-Iran — whose lineage is from the gods.

Shapur also deported the populations of the cities he took, which was the
standard practice of the region from the Assyrians to Timur, and settled
them in new foundations in Khuzistan and Fars. Among the deportees from Roman
Syria were large numbers of Christians, which is how a Christian community that
would eventually have its own church, its own patriarch and its own missions to
India and China came to be inside the Sasanian empire. That is the next chapter.
