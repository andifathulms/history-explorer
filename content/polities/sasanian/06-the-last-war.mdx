title: The last war

Rome and Iran had fought each other for six hundred years by 602 without either
being able to finish the other, in a rhythm this corpus records on both sides:
campaigns, sieges, a sacked capital occasionally, and a frontier that moved
tens of kilometres and returned. The war that began in 602 was different because
one side tried to win it.

Khusrau II had been restored to his throne in 591 by Roman troops. When the
emperor who had restored him was murdered by a usurper, he invaded — with a
better pretext than usual — and then did not stop.

Sasanian armies took Mesopotamia, then Syria, then Antioch. In 614 they took
Jerusalem and carried the relic of the True Cross to Ctesiphon. By 619 they held
Egypt, which had been Roman for six and a half centuries and supplied
Constantinople's grain. Asia Minor was overrun to the Bosphorus. In 626 a
Sasanian army stood on the Asian shore while an Avar host assaulted the walls of
Constantinople from Europe, and the city held because the Persians had no fleet
and could not cross.

That is the high-water mark of Iranian power in this corpus's fifteen hundred
years, and it lasted about two years.

Heraclius had already done the thing that reversed it. Rather than defend, he
took an army by sea to the Black Sea coast, campaigned through Armenia and the
Caucasus with Turkic allies, and struck at the Iranian heartland from behind the
Persian armies in the west. In 627 he beat a Sasanian force at Nineveh and moved
towards Ctesiphon.

Khusrau II was deposed by his own nobles and generals in early 628 and executed.
The conquests were returned; the True Cross went back to Jerusalem; the frontier
went back to where it had been in 602.

Both empires had spent twenty-six years and everything they had. The next
chapter is about what arrived eight years later.
