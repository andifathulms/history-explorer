In 363 a Roman emperor invaded Iran with the largest army Rome had sent east in
a century, reached Ctesiphon, could not take it, turned back, and was killed in a
skirmish during the retreat. His successor bought the army's escape with the
eastern frontier.

Ammianus was on that campaign too.

## What Shapur asked for

Julian was dead and Jovian, hastily elevated, was in Persian territory with a
hungry army and no way home that did not run through Shapur's forces.

Shapur's opening demand was framed historically: the restoration of territories
taken from him by Maximian, seventy years earlier. As the negotiation went on,
Ammianus says, what he actually wanted as the price of the Romans' redemption
was:

> five provinces on the other side of the Tigris — Arzanene, Moxoene, Zabdicene,
> Rehimene and Corduene — with fifteen fortresses, besides Nisibis, and Singara,
> and the important fortress called the camp of the Moors.

Nisibis was the anchor of the whole Roman east. Shapur had besieged it three
times in his life and failed every time.

## How it was agreed

Ammianus's verdict is unambiguous — *it would have been better to fight ten
battles than to give up one of them* — and so is his account of why it was not
fought over.

A set of flatterers worked on the emperor by repeating the name of Procopius, a
relative of Julian's commanding fresh troops elsewhere, and insisting that unless
the army recrossed the river quickly he would hear of Julian's death and raise a
revolution nobody could resist.

Jovian, worn down by this, gave up everything demanded. He obtained two
concessions with difficulty: that the *inhabitants* of Nisibis and Singara should
not be handed over along with the cities, and that Roman garrisons in the
surrendered forts could withdraw to Roman ground.

And one further clause was added, which Ammianus calls mischievous and unfair:
Rome was not to assist Arsaces of Armenia against Persia, even if he asked —
though he had always been a friend and a trusty ally. Shapur wanted him
punishable, and Armenia invadable.

## What it meant

Shapur had spent forty years failing to take Nisibis by siege. He got it, and
four other provinces and fifteen forts, in a treaty signed by an emperor who had
been in office a few days and wanted to go home.

The inhabitants were allowed to leave, and they did — Nisibis was emptied of its
Roman population and resettled. A frontier that Rome had held since Diocletian
moved west in a single document, and stayed moved.

This is the mirror image of Amida. There, Shapur won a siege and gained nothing
permanent. Here he lost a war — Julian had burned his way to the walls of
Ctesiphon — and gained the most valuable strip of territory the Sasanians ever
took from Rome, because the other side's succession had failed at the wrong
moment.

Four centuries of this frontier turn on that repeatedly. Battles moved almost
nothing. What moved provinces was somebody's dynasty coming apart while an enemy
army happened to be standing nearby.
