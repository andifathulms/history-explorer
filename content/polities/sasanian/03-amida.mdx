In 359 Shapur II besieged the Roman fortress city of Amida on the upper Tigris
for seventy-three days and took it. The fullest account of the siege was written
by a Roman staff officer who was inside the walls for all of it and got out the
night the city fell.

Ammianus Marcellinus almost never says *I*. He says *we*, and the pronoun is the
reason this is the best description of a Sasanian army at work that survives from
anywhere.

## The morning they arrived

> At daybreak, everything, as far as we could see, glittered with shining arms;
> and an iron cavalry filled the plains and the hills.

And at the head of it, the king himself: mounted, taller than the men around him,
leading the whole army — and wearing, instead of a crown, *a golden figure of a
ram's head inlaid with jewels*. Behind him came a retinue of high-ranking men of
several different nations.

The detail about the ram's head is the kind of thing only an eyewitness supplies,
and it is corroborated by Sasanian coins and reliefs: the king's headgear was the
statement of who he was, and Shapur had brought his to a Roman wall to be looked
at.

Ammianus is clear that Shapur had not intended a siege at all. He meant to test
the garrison with a parley and move on to another objective. What changed his mind
was being shot at, and then, in Ammianus's reading, an elation so extravagant that
he came to believe the walls would open at the sight of him.

## Seventy-three days

What followed was the whole repertoire of ancient siegecraft used at once.

Mines driven underground. Towers and engines brought up to the walls and burned by
the defenders. Sorties by the Gallic legions that did the Persians great harm.
And, in the end, the method that worked: enormous mounds raised against the walls
until the attackers could cross on the level and enter the city.

Inside, the thing that decided it was not the engines. Too many bodies lay
unburied with nobody to perform the rites over them, and plague broke out.
Ammianus, who had read his Thucydides and says so, digresses for several pages on
the causes of pestilence in general before returning to the fact that his own
city was dying of one.

## The escape

Amida fell in October. Ammianus got out at night, alone, and made his way to
Antioch.

He tells it without any self-dramatisation at all — it is one clause among the
military dispositions — and then goes straight on to what happened to the Roman
commanders: some executed, others kept as prisoners.

And then the story he clearly thought was the most interesting thing about the
whole campaign, which is not about the siege. Craugasius of Nisibis, a notable of
that city, had a wife who had been taken prisoner by the Persians. He went out to
a country villa eight miles from the city, ostensibly to arrange his own wedding
feast with a high-born virgin, mounted a horse, and rode at full speed to a
Persian raiding party he had heard was nearby. He gave himself up, was passed to
the king, and got his wife back — along with his household and all his property.

She died a few months later.

## What the campaign was worth

Shapur took Amida and lost so much of his army doing it that the season ended
there. The city had been a strongpoint in a frontier zone that both empires had
been contesting for a century, and taking it changed the line not at all.

That is the shape of Roman-Sasanian war for four hundred years, and Amida is the
clearest single instance of it: two states that could each hurt the other
enormously and neither of which could keep what it took. The fortress was
rebuilt. It was besieged again in 502, and again after that.
