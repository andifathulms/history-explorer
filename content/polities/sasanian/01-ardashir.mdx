The Sasanians began as hereditary custodians of a fire temple at Istakhr in
Fars, vassals of the Parthian Arsacids. Ardashir, son of Papak, spent the 210s
absorbing his neighbours in Fars, was declared a rebel, and in 224 killed the
Parthian king Artabanus IV in battle on the plain of Hormizdgan.

<Figure id="firuzabad-relief" />

What he built afterwards was not a restored Parthia. The Arsacid state had been
a confederation in which the king was first among great houses and the great
houses kept their armies. Ardashir and his son Shapur I built something more
centralised: royal cities founded by decree, a standing administration, and a
title — *šāhān šāh ērān ud anērān*, king of kings of Iran and non-Iran — that
claimed universal authority rather than primacy among peers.

The other innovation was religious. The Sasanians attached themselves to a
Zoroastrian priesthood and gave it institutional form, a hierarchy and endowed
fire temples, in a way no previous Iranian dynasty had. This is what makes the
empire legible as a confessional state four centuries before it faced one, and
it is why its fall is remembered as the fall of a religion's political home and
not merely of a dynasty.
