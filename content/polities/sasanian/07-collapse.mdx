title: Twenty-six years, then eight

Khusrau II invaded the Roman empire in 602 and very nearly finished it. Sasanian
armies took Syria, Palestine and Egypt, carried the True Cross out of Jerusalem
in 614, and stood on the Bosphorus opposite Constantinople in 626. It was the
furthest west an Iranian empire had reached since the Achaemenids.

Heraclius answered by striking at the Iranian heartland rather than defending
the provinces, campaigning through Armenia and into Mesopotamia and destroying a
Sasanian army at Nineveh in 627. Khusrau II was deposed and killed by his own
nobility in 628. What followed was four years of civil war and roughly ten
claimants, in an empire whose treasury was empty, whose army had been in the
field for a quarter of a century, and whose Mesopotamian irrigation had been
wrecked by flooding nobody had the resources to repair.

Yazdegerd III was a child when he was raised to the throne in 632, the year
Muhammad died. The Arab armies broke the Sasanian field army at al-Qadisiyya in
636 and took Ctesiphon; the empire was not defeated again so much as unwound,
province by province, over fifteen years, as garrisons surrendered and the
*dehqan* gentry made terms locally with the conquerors. Yazdegerd was murdered
by a miller at Merv in 651, having spent his reign retreating east.

There is a temptation to read this as decadence meeting vigour. The Sasanian
state that faced the Arabs was not decadent — it had almost destroyed Rome six
years earlier. It was bankrupt, leaderless and exhausted, which is a different
thing, and it had spent everything winning a war it then lost.

The line did not claim itself back. But three centuries later the Buyids, ruling
Baghdad and holding the caliph, revived the title *shahanshah* — an assertion
that the Iranian monarchy this dynasty embodied was a thing one could still
claim. That is the one successor claim counted on this page.
