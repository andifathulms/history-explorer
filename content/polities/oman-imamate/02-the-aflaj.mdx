title: Water, and who owns it

The Omani interior is not a country that supports a state by accident. Rainfall
is low and unreliable, the summer is brutal, and cultivation depends almost
entirely on the *aflaj* — underground channels, some of them kilometres long,
cut on a gradient from an aquifer at the foot of the mountains to the date
gardens of a settlement, with vertical access shafts along the length for
maintenance. The technology is old, related to the Iranian qanat, and building
one is a capital project measured in generations.

What matters politically is that a falaj is owned in shares. The water arrives
continuously and is divided by time — a household's right is a measured
interval of flow, on a rotation, and those intervals are bought, sold,
inherited, endowed and litigated. Maintaining the channel is a collective
obligation. Wilkinson's work on the aflaj is the reason this site can say what
follows: a settlement on a falaj is a corporation with a written constitution,
a body of case law, and a permanent requirement to arbitrate between its
members.

That is the society the Ibadi imamate governed, and it explains a good deal
about the shape of the office.

It explains why the imam's function was so heavily judicial. He was invited in
897 in Yemen and in 750 here to settle disputes, and the disputes were largely
about water and land. It explains why the scholars mattered — an entire legal
literature exists on falaj shares, and the men who knew it were the men who
elected and could depose the imam. And it explains why an imamate could be
constituted, destroyed and constituted again in the same mountains for a
thousand years without needing a dynasty: the thing being governed was already
organised, already litigious, and already used to arbitration from outside.

The revenue field on this record says `land-tax`. What it means is a tithe on
dates grown with water somebody's great-grandfather dug for.
