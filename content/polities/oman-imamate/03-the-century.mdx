title: The century it worked

The imamate reconstituted in 793 lasted a hundred years, and the middle of it is
the period Omani tradition treats as the model against which every later
imamate was measured.

Under al-Warith ibn Ka'b and then Ghassan ibn Abdullah and Abd al-Malik ibn
Humayd and al-Muhanna ibn Jayfar, the imamate governed the interior from Nizwa
with a judiciary, a treasury and a body of scholars producing the law it
governed by. It took the coast: Sohar was in its hands for long stretches, and
Sohar in the ninth century was one of the busiest ports in the Indian Ocean —
Omani ships were trading directly with China, and the merchant of the Sindbad
stories is a Sohari.

Ghassan ibn Abdullah is recorded as having built a fleet against the Indian
pirates who were preying on that traffic, which is a striking thing for an
elective imamate in a mountain valley to have done and is a reminder that the
interior and the coast were not separate economies even when they were separate
polities. The imamate's income was dates and the tithe; its access to money was
Sohar.

The literature is the other output and it is the more durable one. Ibadi
jurisprudence — the *madhhab* that survives today in Oman, in the Mzab of
Algeria, on Djerba and in Zanzibar — was substantially written in this century
and the one after it, by men who were participants in the events they were
legislating about. This site's Rustamid page carries the western half of the
same tradition; the two communities were in contact, and the Omani scholars are
the ones whose work continued after Tahert fell.

The imamate's own account of the period is that it was the time when the
community got it right: an imam who was learned and could be argued with, a
council of scholars who could depose him, and an interior that governed itself
by law. That account was written afterwards, by people arguing about what had
gone wrong, which is the subject of the next chapter.
