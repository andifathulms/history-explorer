---
title: The question an elective office has to answer
drafted_from: wilkinson-1987
phase: contraction
---

An office that can be taken away has to have rules about when. Ibadism held
that an imam who failed in his duties could be deposed by the community that
elected him, and for a century nobody had to work out exactly what that meant.
In 886 they did.

The imam al-Salt ibn Malik was old. A faction of the scholars, led by Musa ibn
Musa, deposed him on grounds of incapacity and elected Rashid ibn al-Nazr in
his place. Al-Salt went quietly. And then the Ibadi community divided over
whether what had just happened was lawful — whether the deposition had followed
the required procedure, whether incapacity was sufficient grounds, whether the
scholars who did it had standing, and whether the imam they installed was
therefore an imam at all.

The two positions became schools: the Rustaq party, which held the deposition
invalid and its authors in the wrong, and the Nizwa party, which held it valid.
The argument was conducted in law, at length, and it has never entirely closed —
Ibadi jurists were still writing about al-Salt's deposition centuries later, and
the division is a live category in the tradition.

It is the price of the doctrine, and this site should say so on the page that
codes both `succession_rule` and `legitimation` as `election`. A hereditary
office produces succession wars between men; an elective one produces schisms
between arguments, and the arguments do not die with the claimants. The Omani
imamate spent seven years after 886 with two parties who each considered the
other's imam illegitimate.

In 893 the losing side did what losing factions do. Muhammad ibn Nur, the
Abbasid governor of Bahrayn, was invited in, came, took Tu'am, then al-Sirr,
then Nizwa, killed the imam Azzan ibn Tamim, and sent heads to Baghdad. The
first Omani imamate ended in a foreign occupation that one of its own factions
had arranged.
