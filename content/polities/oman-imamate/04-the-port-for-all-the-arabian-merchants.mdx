title: The port for all the Arabian merchants
drafted_from: akhbar-renaudot

The century in which Oman had an elected imam was also the century in which
Omani ships sailed to China, and the two facts are connected by money.

The sailing directions survive. A ninth-century Arabic account of the route,
written by men who had used it, explains that the China ships load at Siraf
rather than Basra — goods coming down from Basra and across from Oman are
transhipped there — because the Gulf has frequent storms and shoal water. From
Basra to Siraf is a hundred and twenty leagues. From Siraf the ships run about
two hundred leagues to Muscat, at the far end of the province of Oman, passing a
place called Nabd Bani al-Safaq and an island, and rocks called Oman, and a
narrow strait between two rocks called Dordur which small vessels will attempt
and the China ships will not, and two more rocks that barely break the surface.
At Muscat they water from wells and take on Omani cattle. From there it is a
month's sail with the wind aft to the Malabar coast, and then on east.

That is the longest regular commercial sea route in the world at the time, and
the Omani imamate sat on the stretch of it where every ship had to stop for water.

The other end of the route is described in the same text and it is a city called
Khanfu, which is Guangzhou: the port for all the Arabian merchants, standing on a
great river some days above the sea, so that the water there is fresh. Foreign
merchants of four religions lived in it under their own arrangements, and the
Chinese authorities kept exact registers of them.

In 878 a rebel army came down on it. The citizens shut the gates, the siege
lasted a long time, and when the city fell the inhabitants were put to the sword.
People who knew China well told the compiler that besides the Chinese killed,
a hundred and twenty thousand Muslims, Jews, Christians and Parsees died there,
on account of trade — and that the number is exactly known, because the Chinese
are extremely precise in the accounts they keep of such people.

Then the detail that matters more than the number. The rebel cut down the
mulberry trees along with almost every other kind, and the mulberry is what the
silkworms are fed on. That destruction, says the text, is why silk failed and why
the trade in it in the countries under the Arabs came to a standstill.

And the consequence for Oman, in one clause: the merchants returned in crowds to
Siraf and Oman.

The direct voyage did not resume. Goods went on moving, but in stages, handed
between intermediaries at Kalah in the Malay peninsula and elsewhere, with the
long-haul profit divided among more people. Within fifteen years of the sack of
Khanfu the Abbasids landed in Oman and ended the imamate.

The two events are not cause and effect, and it would be neat and wrong to say
so. What can be said is narrower and harder to dismiss: a polity that had
financed an unusually independent constitution out of the traffic on one sea
route lost the far end of that route and its own government in the same fifteen
years, and did not get either of them back on the same terms.
