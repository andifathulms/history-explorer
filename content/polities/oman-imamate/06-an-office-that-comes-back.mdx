893 is where this account ends, and it is worth being explicit about why it ends
there rather than in the twentieth century.

The Zaydi imamate of Yemen is conventionally dated 897 to 1962 as a single
stretch, because its own historiography treats it as one institution with a
continuous line of imams and long interruptions. Omani historiography does the
opposite: it counts imamates, in the plural, as distinct constitutions of the
same office separated by periods of dynastic or foreign rule — the Nabhani
kings, the Buyids, the Portuguese on the coast, the Ya'ariba imams of the
seventeenth century who took Mombasa and Zanzibar, the Al Bu Sa'id sultanate,
and an imamate revived in the interior as late as the twentieth century against
a sultan in Muscat.

So the two Arabian imamates get bounded differently, and the difference is
deference rather than inconsistency: each follows the periodisation its own
sources use. Yemen's thousand-year figure measures an institution rather than a
state. Oman's hundred and forty-three years have the opposite problem — they
understate an office that kept being reconstituted in the same mountains for
another millennium.

Both figures together are the honest position, and neither of them fits the
longevity axis comfortably. That is a fact about the axis.
