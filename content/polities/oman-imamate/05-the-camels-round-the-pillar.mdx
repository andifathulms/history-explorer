The Omani chronicle records one administrative arrangement from the imamate's
best century that is worth more than a page of description, because it shows
exactly what kind of authority the office actually exercised.

Under the imam al-Muhanna, who held the office for four decades from about 810,
the province of Mahra to the south-west was tributary to Oman. The way the
tribute worked was this. Once a year the people of Mahra brought their camels to
Nizwa and walked them round a pillar that the imam had had erected there for the
purpose.

The chronicle's editor sets out both reasons the ordinance existed, and they are
not the same reason. First, so that the owners publicly acknowledged their
dependence. Second, so that the cattle-tithe due from them could be correctly
estimated.

An imamate whose whole claim was that it collected a religious obligation under
rules the scholars enforced needed to know how many camels there were, and it
found a way of counting them that was simultaneously a ceremony of submission.
The herd walks past; the assessors watch; everybody has seen it happen.

## What else the century held

The rest of the record for that period is of a functioning state doing
unremarkable things well.

An imam named Ghassan is remembered for putting an end to a formidable band of
pirates who infested those seas in vessels called *bawarij*, operating out of
the mouths of the Indus. That is a navy, or at least a naval campaign, run by an
elected imam of the Omani interior against raiders from Sind.

When one imam became too infirm to carry on the administration a mutiny broke
out among the soldiery and was resolved by appointing a regent — which is to say
the office had an administration substantial enough to have soldiers who could
mutiny, and a procedure for a ruler who could no longer function.

And Harun al-Rashid tried to reconquer Oman, and failed. That is the second
Abbasid attempt in half a century; the first had killed the first imam in 751.
The caliphate at the height of its power could not hold the country behind the
Hajar mountains, and after Harun it stopped trying for eighty years.

## How the office was actually filled

The chronicle also gives, for this period, the first account of how an election
was conducted, and it is not flattering.

The learned elders assembled at Nizwa under a president. The nobles had a
candidate. The president, by his own personal machinations, set their choice
aside and conferred the imamate on somebody else.

That is the mechanism working in the sense that it produced an imam, and not
working in the sense that everyone could see who had really decided. An elective
office in which one man's influence over the assembly is decisive has a
succession problem waiting for it — and the chronicle records that problem
arriving, at length, in the crisis that broke the imamate half a century later.
