---
title: Elected, and removable
drafted_from: wilkinson-1987

This site already has an Ibadi imamate. The Rustamids held Tahert in the
central Maghreb from about 776, on the doctrine that the leader of the
community should be the most qualified Muslim regardless of descent and could
be deposed if he failed. Oman is where the same doctrine came from and where it
lasted.

Ibadism is the surviving moderate branch of the Kharijite movement — the party
that broke from Ali at Siffin over the arbitration, which this site's Rashidun
page records as a turning point. What the Kharijites held, and what Ibadism
kept after the militant wings had been destroyed, is that leadership is a
qualification and not an inheritance: no Qurashi requirement, no Alid
requirement, no designation. The community's scholars elect the man best fitted
to the office, and if he stops being fitted they can remove him.

In 750, with the Umayyads gone and the Abbasids not yet established in the
Gulf, Omani Ibadi scholars elected al-Julanda ibn Mas'ud imam at Nizwa. It
lasted about two years. An Abbasid expedition came down and killed him, and
Oman was governed from Baghdad for the next four decades. In 793 the governors
were expelled and an imam was elected again, and that imamate ran for a
century.

The geography did most of the work. The Omani interior sits behind the Hajar
mountains, reachable from the coast through a small number of passes and
defensible at all of them, and it supported itself on the *aflaj* — the
underground channels that bring mountain water to the date gardens, which are
communal property maintained by the villages that use them. A society organised
around shared water rights and tribal arbitration, in country an imperial army
has to want badly, is fertile ground for an elective imamate and poor ground
for a governor.

The coast is a different country and always was. Sohar and later Muscat traded
with India and East Africa and belonged to whoever could hold a port, which was
frequently not the imam. That division — an elected imamate in the interior and
a commercial power on the coast — is the shape of Omani history for the next
twelve hundred years.
