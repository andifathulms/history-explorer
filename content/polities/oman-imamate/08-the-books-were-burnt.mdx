The Abbasid governor of Bahrayn came into Oman in 893 with twenty-five thousand
men, a thousand of them cavalry and half of those in mail. So many families left
for Hormuz, Basra and Shiraz ahead of him that the flight is itself part of the
record.

He took the northern districts, marched on Nizwa, found it evacuated, pursued
the imam to Samad al-Shan and killed him there, and sent his head to the caliph.

Then the Omanis nearly threw him out. Tribes raised by a chief of the Bani Hina
drove him back as far as the sea at Sib, and would have finished it if a
mounted reinforcement had not come up — two men riding on each camel — and beaten
them badly.

What he did on returning to Nizwa is set down by the chronicler in a single
sentence, and it is the one to sit with:

> He cut off the hands and ears, and scooped out the eyes of the nobles,
> inflicted unheard-of outrages upon the inhabitants, destroyed the
> watercourses, burnt the books, and utterly desolated the country.

## The two things at the end of that list

Mutilation and outrage are what a punitive occupation does anywhere. The last
two items are specific to this country and this polity, and whoever ordered them
understood what he was destroying.

He destroyed the watercourses. Omani agriculture is the *aflaj* — underground
channels, cut by hand, running for kilometres from the water table under the
mountains out to the date gardens, with shares in the flow owned, sold,
inherited and litigated over. That system is the physical basis of everything
the imamate taxed and much of what its scholars adjudicated. Breaking it is not
looting; it is the deliberate removal of a country's capacity to feed itself and
to argue about property.

And he burnt the books. An Ibadi imam was elected by scholars on the strength of
learning, deposed by them on the strength of argument, and judged against a body
of law those scholars maintained in writing. The books were not ornaments of the
state. They were the state's constitution, its case law and its qualification
test.

An occupier who wrecks the irrigation and the library has been advised by
someone who knew how the place worked.

## What came back anyway

His deputy was killed by the population shortly after he left for Bahrayn.

Then, within about thirty years, seven imams were elected and deposed. That is a
remarkable figure. It reads at first as chaos, and it is — but it is chaos of an
extremely specific kind. A country whose nobles had been blinded and whose books
had been burnt went on holding elections, and went on deposing the men it
elected, at a rate of one every four years, for a generation.

The chronicle's editor draws the obvious inference about why they could: the
Omanis took advantage of the disorders the Qarmatians were causing across the
Muslim empire at that period to reassert their independence. When Baghdad was
busy, Nizwa elected somebody.

The efforts were too feeble to keep Baghdad out for good, and the interior spent
much of the following centuries under one outside authority or another. But the
office itself was never abolished, because it could not be: it was not an
institution with a building that an army could burn. It was a procedure. As long
as there were scholars who knew the qualifications and tribes willing to answer,
it could be constituted again from nothing — and over the next thousand years it
was, repeatedly, in the same mountains, by men using the same word.
