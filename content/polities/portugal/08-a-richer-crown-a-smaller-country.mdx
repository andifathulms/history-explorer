Manuel I died in 1521 confident his line was secure and his kingdom's
wealth beyond question, leaving behind sons married into the Habsburg
dynasty and daughters sought by the greatest princes in Europe. His
son and successor, John III, ruled for thirty-six years over a crown
that grew richer while the country underneath it grew smaller - and
by his own contemporaries' account nobody watching it happen would
have called it decline.

## An alliance sealed three times over

John III bound Portugal to the Habsburgs as tightly as marriage could
manage: he married Charles V's own sister, his only son married
Charles's daughter, and his own daughter became the first wife of the
future Philip II of Spain. The alliance kept Portugal out of the wars
convulsing the rest of Europe and freed John to concentrate on
possessions his father's generation had fought hardest to win. A
Portuguese fleet under the king's own brother joined Charles's
expedition against Tunis in 1535, but within the following decade
John abandoned nearly every Portuguese outpost in North Africa -
Azamor, Safi, Alcácer Ceguer among them - concentrating the kingdom's
strength on India and Brazil instead. The old crusading impulse that
had driven Portuguese kings across the strait for over a century had,
within one reign, simply stopped mattering enough to defend.

## A kingdom hollowed out by its own success

What emptied Portugal was the same enterprise that had made it rich.
Young men volunteered by the thousand for the India fleets and the
colonial garrisons and mostly did not return, killed by disease or
warfare or settled permanently abroad; whole families emigrated to
Madeira and, increasingly after 1530, to Brazil. Lisbon's own
population tripled within eighty years as the countryside emptied into
it, while African slaves, imported in growing numbers to work estates
the emigration had left short of labor, came to outnumber free
residents in the capital by mid-century. A nobility that had once
measured itself by crusading service against Morocco now competed
instead for court pensions and colonial administrative posts, sinking,
in one contemporary's own phrase, until personal devotion to the king
had eaten out patriotism itself.

## The Inquisition, and what it cost

John pursued one project with more determination than any other: from
the moment of his own accession he worked to bring the Inquisition to
Portugal, over years of resistance from the kingdom's converted Jewish
community. He got his tribunal in 1536, with the bishop of Ceuta as
first Grand Inquisitor, succeeded soon after by the king's own brother,
Cardinal Henry; the Jesuits followed in 1540, quickly gaining control
of national education. The damage was not immediately visible - John's
own reign is studded with real achievement, the conquest of Ceylon, the
founding of Macau, and above all a literary flowering that produced
Camões's Lusíadas even under the Inquisition's own watch - but the
apparatus John built would go on suppressing the intellectual life his
own reign had still managed to produce, long after the wealth that
had made his crown the envy of Europe stopped translating into any
underlying strength at home.
