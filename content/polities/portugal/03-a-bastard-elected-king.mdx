The death of King Fernando I in 1383 without a legitimate male heir
opened the succession to his Castilian son-in-law, Juan I of Castile -
a prospect much of Portugal found intolerable. João, Master of the
Military Order of Aviz and an illegitimate son of an earlier king,
emerged as the rallying point for resistance, successfully defending
Lisbon against a Castilian siege and winning a smaller victory at
Atoleiros that showed the resistance could actually fight and win.

## A throne declared elective, and a bastard chosen by acclamation

More than a battlefield victory was needed to make João's own claim
legitimate. A Cortes summoned to Coimbra in April 1385 heard the
chancellor João das Regras argue that the Portuguese throne was, by
its own nature, elective rather than strictly hereditary - a
proposition the assembly accepted by acclamation, before also hearing
a papal bull declaring the children of an earlier royal liaison
illegitimate and clearing the way. João was elected king by the same
Cortes that had just redefined how a king of Portugal could be made.

## Aljubarrota

The Castilian invasion that followed was met at Aljubarrota on 14
August 1385, reinforced on the Portuguese side by five hundred English
archers sent by John of Gaunt and by artillery pieces making their
first appearance in Peninsular warfare, and opposed by two thousand
French knights who had joined the Castilian cause. The English archers
repeated, on a smaller field, the kind of execution their countrymen
had inflicted at Crécy and Poitiers, and the Castilian army broke and
fled. João commemorated the victory by founding the monastery of
Batalha on the battlefield itself - a Portuguese echo, in name and
purpose alike, of the Battle Abbey William the Conqueror had raised at
Hastings three centuries before. Portuguese independence from Castile,
and the House of Aviz's own claim to the throne, were both secured on
the same afternoon.
