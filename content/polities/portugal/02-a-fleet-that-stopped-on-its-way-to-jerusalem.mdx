title: A fleet that stopped on its way to Jerusalem

Afonso Henriques had long relied on crusaders passing through Oporto
on their way to the Holy Land, since the pope had declared fighting
the infidel in Spain as praiseworthy as fighting him in Palestine
itself; in 1143 a band of French crusaders had already helped him
raid the country around Lisbon. But it was doubtful, his own
chroniclers thought, that Afonso would have moved against the city
itself so soon after taking Santarém, had word not reached him that a
great fleet of crusaders had put in at Oporto, and that the Bishop of
Oporto had persuaded them to open their holy war by helping take
Lisbon before sailing on toward Jerusalem.

## A fleet of a hundred and sixty-four ships

The fleet, mostly English, had sailed from Dartmouth a hundred and
sixty-four ships strong, under captains including Arnold of Aerschot
and Christian Ghistell over the Germans, Flemings and men of Boulogne,
and Hervey Glanvill, constable of the men of Norfolk and Suffolk. One
of their own number wrote a letter describing the expedition and the
siege that followed - a document rediscovered only in the century
before Stephens wrote, making 1147 one of the best-documented single
episodes in all of early Portuguese history.

## Cintra, Palmela, Mafra, Almada

Lisbon fell after a siege supported by the crusader fleet alongside
Afonso's own forces, and the surrounding strongholds surrendered
without a fight in its wake - Cintra, Palmela, Mafra and Almada all in
that same year. The city on the Tagus that would one day be Portugal's
own capital was, for the first time, in Christian hands, and the
foreign fleet that had made it possible sailed on to Palestine having
fought its holy war, by the bishop's own persuasion, considerably
closer to home than it had originally planned.
