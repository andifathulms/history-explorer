Vasco da Gama had found the route; it fell to Afonso de Albuquerque,
governing Portuguese India after Francisco de Almeida, to turn it into
something that could actually be held. An assault on Calicut itself
ended badly - the Zamorin's palace burned and the city wrecked, but
the populace rose and drove the Portuguese back to their ships,
killing the Marshal of Portugal, Dom Fernando Coutinho, in the
process. Albuquerque drew the lesson that Cochin, the Portuguese base
until then, sat too far south to serve as a real capital for either
trade or political power on the Malabar coast, and fixed instead on
Goa, a port held by the Muslim king of Bijapur.

## A city taken twice in one year

Albuquerque sailed for Goa with twenty ships and twelve hundred men. A
single feat of arms by António de Noronha at Panjim, at the river's
mouth, opened the way, and the citizens - already unsettled by a holy
mendicant's prophecy that conquest by a distant foreign people was
coming - surrendered without further resistance; eight of the city's
leading men brought Albuquerque the keys, and he entered in triumph on
17 February 1510. He did not hold it long. Yusuf Adil Shah, the king
of Bijapur, retook the city by force that August. Albuquerque refused
to accept the loss, waited for reinforcements from Portugal, and on 25
November stormed Goa a second time, killing more than two thousand
defenders and this time holding it for good.

## A capital built to last

Goa became the seat from which every later Portuguese governor in Asia
ruled, and Albuquerque's own administration set the pattern his
successors would follow - keeping the existing village system intact
and taking pains to avoid the appearance of new taxation, a
restraint contemporaries credited with the settlement's own stability.
The single voyage that had ended at Calicut in 1498 had become, within
twelve years, a fortified capital city and the headquarters of a
trading empire that would soon reach from Mozambique to Malacca.
