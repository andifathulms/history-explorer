Prince Henry, a younger son of João I remembered to history as "the
Navigator," never captained a single one of the voyages that made his
own name famous. What he built instead, at Sagres on Portugal's own
southwestern tip, was an institution: an observatory and school of
navigation drawing mathematicians and astronomers from across Europe,
improving the mariner's compass and the charts that Portuguese
captains would carry down the African coast year after year under his
own direction and patronage.

## A route worth an empire

Henry's own governing idea was simple and, in the end, correct: that a
ship could sail around Africa to reach India directly, cutting out the
long, expensive overland and Red Sea routes that had made Venice
Europe's own distributor of eastern goods for centuries. Whoever found
the sea route, he understood, could make Lisbon the new Venice. He
spent more than forty years and a great personal fortune pursuing
that route and died in 1460 without ever reaching it himself - the
southern tip of Africa still ahead of every captain he had sent.

## Calicut

The dream he could not complete belonged to a later generation. Vasco
da Gama, a captain formed in the tradition Henry had built even though
he was born after Henry's own death, rounded the Cape of Good Hope,
fought through hostile receptions at Mozambique and Mombasa, found a
friendly pilot at last at Melinda, and after eleven months at sea
anchored his fleet off Calicut on 20 May 1498. The India he found there
was not the India of a single vast empire but a patchwork of
independent kingdoms - a political fact that would shape how
Portugal's own commercial empire in the East was built in the decades
that followed, city by fortified trading post rather than through
any single decisive conquest. Henry's sea route to India, pursued for
forty years by a prince who never once sailed it, had finally been
found by men who counted themselves, in every sense that mattered, his
own disciples.
