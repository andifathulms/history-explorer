Afonso III came to the throne in 1248 after his brother Sancho II had
been driven into exile and died in Toledo, and rather than the civil
war or clerical dominance that such a beginning might have predicted,
his reign turned out to be the most constitutionally significant of
early Portuguese history - and the one in which Portugal finished its
own reconquest outright. He moved immediately to recover ground lost
during his brother's troubles, taking Faro and the remaining Algarve
strongholds in 1249 and 1250 with the help of the Knights Hospitaller.

## A gain Castile did not welcome

Alfonso X of Castile, "the Wise," was not pleased to see Portugal
expand its own southern frontier without his consent, and only a short
war persuaded Afonso III to accept a compromise: marriage to Alfonso's
illegitimate daughter, and Portuguese possession of the Algarve
reduced to usufruct rather than outright sovereignty. It took another
thirteen years - and a papal ruling in 1262 that formally recognized
Afonso's own son by that marriage as legitimate - before Alfonso X finally
ceded full sovereignty over the Algarve in 1263, letting Afonso III
adopt the title King of Portugal and the Algarve that Portuguese
monarchs would carry from then on.

## An assembly built to check the Church

Afonso III's other lasting achievement was constitutional rather than
territorial. Determined to curb the power of the bishops despite an
earlier oath at Paris promising not to, he summoned a Cortes at Leiria
in 1254 that, for the first time, brought elected representatives of
the towns to sit alongside the nobility and higher clergy - an early
recognition, matched at almost the same moment by Simon de Montfort
and Edward I in England, that only an alliance with the wider body of
the people could check the combined weight of feudal lords and
churchmen. With that Cortes behind him, Afonso dared the interdict
the Church laid on his own kingdom over his second marriage, and
forced the clergy back to their duties regardless.
