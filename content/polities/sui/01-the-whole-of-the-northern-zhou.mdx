The man who reunited China in 589, after three and a half centuries in which no
government had held both the north and the south at once, took the throne by
exterminating the entire ruling family of the state he served.

## The pattern he inherited

By the 570s northern China had been fought over by successor states of the
Xianbei conquest dynasties for two hundred and fifty years, in a sequence Parker
lays out with the flat brutality of the sources he is translating. The Northern
Qi emperor massacred the whole of the Tuoba royal house — seven hundred and
twenty-one people, all but one — and it is, Parker suggests, from this barbarous
example that the custom of rooting out the heirs of each dethroned dynasty took
hold of the Chinese political imagination. In 577 the Northern Zhou emperor
captured his rival's capital and in turn massacred that whole family stock.

> The founder of the Sui dynasty once more butchered the whole of the Northern
> Zhou ruling race, and thus ends what may be called the first act in the drama
> of Tungusic Tartar rule in China.

Yang Jian was a general and regent of mixed Chinese and Xianbei descent, related
to the Northern Zhou imperial house by marriage, who used his position as
regent for a child emperor to remove every possible rival claimant from that
family before taking the throne himself in 581. The killing was not incidental
to the succession. It was the succession.

## What eight years bought

By 589 Yang Jian's new Sui dynasty had conquered the last of the southern
courts, the Chen, ending three hundred and sixty years of division between a
north ruled by conquest dynasties from the steppe and a south ruled by a
sequence of short-lived native regimes descended from the fall of the Han.

No government had held both halves of China since 316. An entire cultural
memory — legal codes, administrative practice, even coinage and weights — had
diverged across the Yangtze for longer than the interval between the Norman
Conquest and the sixteenth century. Sui's reunification was not a restoration of
a system still functioning in living memory. It was closer to what a
sixteenth-century European monarch would have faced trying to rejoin polities
that had gone their separate ways since late antiquity.

## Why the killing was the whole method

Every dynasty that took power on this frontier in this period faced the same
question: what happens to the family whose throne you have just climbed onto.
Sui's answer, inherited from the two dynasties before it and applied with the
same thoroughness, was that the answer is nothing survives.

That method built a state fast — Yang Jian faced no dynastic rival with a
plausible restoration claim for the whole of his reign — and it set the pattern
his own son would apply to him in turn a generation later, and that a general
named Li Yuan would apply to the Sui when its own turn came. A dynasty founded on
the principle that a defeated ruling house must be extinguished entirely has, by
its own logic, told its successor exactly what to do when its own moment
arrives.
