The dynasty that had spent thirty years buying the loyalty of a Turkic khan with
princesses and silk ended with a Turkic khan sheltering its own fugitive empress,
while the man who would found the next Chinese dynasty asked the same khan for
troops.

## The attack at the summer palace

By 615 the Sui court's authority had visibly weakened under the strain of the
Korea campaigns and the rebellions they had provoked at home. The Turkic khan
Sibir, whom Sui's own divide-and-rule policy had spent a generation cultivating
and constraining in turn, judged the moment right to reverse the relationship.

In the autumn of 616, while Yang Di was at his summer residence enjoying the
cooler weather, Sibir made a sudden attack on the imperial party and very nearly
took the emperor prisoner — stopped only by frontier battalions arriving in time.
It was, Parker notes with a certain grim symmetry, not so very far from where the
first Han emperor had been surrounded by the Xiongnu eight hundred years before.

## What Sui's own policy had built

The scale of what Sibir could now do is the measure of how far the divide-and-
rule strategy had actually enlarged Turkic power rather than merely managing it.
His khaganate now stretched from the lands of the Khitan in the east to his
Xianbei kin in the west, commanded — on the figure Parker records — over a
million bows, and held the nominal suzerainty of every Chinese aspirant to the
throne then emerging from Sui's collapse:

> During the anarchy and misery which prevailed towards the close of the Sui
> dynasty, innumerable Chinese took refuge with Sibir, whose power now began to
> threaten the very existence of China... every one of the numerous aspirants for
> the imperial throne faced north and declared themselves his vassals.

Sibir gave asylum to the fugitive Sui empress herself.

## The general who asked for help

Among the aspirants was Li Yuan, a Sui general whose own father had married a
Turkic noblewoman of the Duku family — a European source, Parker notes, even
suggests she may have been Christian, not improbable given a Nestorian mission
would reach the Chinese court within a generation of her grandson's own reign.

Li Yuan sent envoys to Sibir asking for assistance, and the khan sent horses and
three thousand troopers to help him in his campaign in eastern Gansu. When Sui
formally ceased to exist, gifts flowed to Sibir in gratitude; his son and
successor Chula received a condolence embassy, a public mourning at the Chinese
court, and thirty thousand pieces of silk on his own succession — the same
currency of tribute and title Sui itself had spent thirty years distributing,
now paid by its successor to the same family.

Chula, for his part, gave the fugitive Sui empress and a surviving Sui prince a
welcome at his own court, granted the prince rank, and placed ten thousand
Chinese refugees under his nominal authority — a shadow Sui administration,
complete with its own officials, operating inside the Turkic khaganate and dating
its own documents by the reign years of an emperor who no longer had a country.

## What it means that both sides needed the same ally

Sui had built its Turkic policy to keep a united khaganate from ever threatening
China. In its final years, that same khaganate became the single most decisive
factor in who would actually inherit China — sheltering the fallen dynasty's
family with one hand and arming its replacement with the other, because thirty
years of Sui gifts and titles had made every Chinese claimant understand exactly
whose recognition was worth having.

Li Yuan founded the Tang within two years of Sibir's attack on the Sui emperor.
His son would spend the following decade finishing what that alliance had
started, and would eventually turn on the very Turkic power that had made his
family's rise possible — the debt collected in full, and then reversed.
