Sui reunited a country whose two halves had grown two different economies for
three centuries, and then spent a generation building the single piece of
infrastructure that would let them function as one.

## Two economies

The north was the old political centre — the Wei and Yellow River plains, wheat
and millet country, close to the steppe frontier and its garrisons. The south,
under the sequence of dynasties that had held it since the Han collapse, had
become the empire's richest agricultural region: the Yangtze delta's rice paddies
produced surpluses the dry-farming north could not match.

A reunited China needed to move that southern surplus north, to feed the capital
and the frontier armies, at a scale and reliability no existing road network
could support.

## What was built

The Sui did not dig one canal so much as connect and rebuild a series of older,
disconnected waterways into a single system linking the Yangtze to the Yellow
River and on to the region of modern Beijing — a corridor of well over a thousand
miles, engineered with locks, towpaths and, along the imperial sections, roads
planted with willows for the court's own barges.

The labour came from the same conscripted, corvée-obligated population every
dynasty in this history drew on for its great works, mobilised on a scale the
sources describe in the millions across the whole construction programme —
figures that, as with every large corvée project in this history, cannot be
verified precisely but that contemporary and near-contemporary accounts agree
were enormous and that the ordinary population experienced as catastrophic.

Alongside the canal, Sui built and stocked granaries on a scale historians have
called genuinely startling for the period: state granaries positioned along the
new waterway and at the capital, holding reserves that — by later Tang accounts —
were still being drawn down decades after the dynasty that built them had fallen.

## What it did for the state, briefly

For about two decades, it worked exactly as intended. Grain moved north
reliably. The reunified administration could actually provision itself as one
government rather than as two economies loosely governed from the same throne.
The equal-field land allocation system, inherited and extended from the
northern dynasties that preceded Sui, gave the state a registered, taxable
peasantry to draw both the grain and the labour from.

## Why it is the single fact that explains the dynasty

Every later verdict on Sui — that it built brilliantly and governed briefly,
that it left China the infrastructure of the next thousand years and did not
survive to use it — is really a verdict on this one project, repeated at
different scales.

The canal outlived the dynasty that dug it by well over a thousand years and
carried Tang, Song and Ming grain shipments long after Sui itself was a cautionary
tale. But building it, on top of the other corvée obligations the state was
simultaneously imposing, consumed the same conscripted population the dynasty
would need for the campaigns that ultimately broke it. A state that can conscript
a population once, on this scale, for a genuinely transformative work has shown
exactly what it can do to that population again — and the temptation to do so a
second and third time, on a project that did not work, is what breaks the
dynasty in the end.
