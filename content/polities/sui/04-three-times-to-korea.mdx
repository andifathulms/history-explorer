Yang Di inherited a full treasury, a working canal and a pacified northern
frontier, and spent all three on a war against a Korean kingdom that never
submitted, could not be supplied, and broke the dynasty that fought it.

## Why Koguryo

Koguryo controlled the northern Korean peninsula and southern Manchuria, and had
maintained a genuine independence through the whole period of Chinese division —
recognising Chinese suzerainty in form while making its own alliances, its own
wars and its own decisions about tribute. A reunified Chinese empire, having just
spent a decade compelling every other regional power on its borders into a
recognisably subordinate relationship, regarded an unsubdued Koguryo as an
anomaly a properly restored Middle Kingdom could not tolerate.

## The scale of the failure

The first campaign, in 612, is usually given as the largest single military
force ever assembled by a pre-modern state: over a million combat troops on the
Sui's own figures, with a logistics train reckoned at two to three times that
number again, moving along the same canal system the dynasty had just spent two
decades building.

It failed completely. A Sui army that crossed the Yalu and pressed toward the
Koguryo capital was drawn deep into hostile territory, cut off from supply, and
destroyed on the retreat; of several hundred thousand men reported to have
crossed the river, only a small fraction are said to have made it back.

Yang Di tried again in 613, and the second campaign collapsed not to Koguryo but
to a rebellion at home — a general commanding the supply effort revolted rather
than continue supporting a war he judged unwinnable, and the emperor had to
abandon the field to deal with the revolt. A third campaign in 614 achieved a
nominal, face-saving submission and nothing more.

## What three campaigns in three years actually cost

Not primarily the dead of the armies, though those were counted in the hundreds
of thousands. The cost that broke the dynasty was the demand on the same
conscripted population, the same corvée labour and the same canal
transport capacity that the Grand Canal project had already drawn on to its
limit.

Provisioning an army that size meant grain requisitions and transport levies on
top of the canal-construction obligations of the previous decade, falling on a
peasantry that had barely had a season's relief between one enormous state
project and the next. Desertion from the conscription rolls, revolt against local
officials trying to enforce the levies, and outright banditry all spread through
the same years as the campaigns themselves, in exactly the regions the canal and
the war both drew most heavily on.

## The pattern this repeats

A state that can mobilise a population once for a transformative project — as
Sui had for the canal — has demonstrated a capacity its rulers then trust too
far. Yang Di treated the machinery that had moved rice for the empire's benefit
as though it could also move a million soldiers for his own ambition, on top of
the first obligation rather than instead of it, three years running, against an
enemy that did not need to defeat his armies so much as let his own
administration exhaust itself trying to supply them.

Koguryo survived the war. Sui did not survive it by more than four years.
