Sui lasted thirty-seven years, from reunification to collapse, and almost
everything about the three centuries that followed it was decided by what it
built and what it broke in that short span.

## What Tang kept whole

The canal system stayed, and remained the empire's principal artery for moving
southern grain north for the rest of the imperial period — Tang, Song and even
Ming administrations maintained, redug and depended on a waterway Sui's
conscripted labour had dug. The granary system Sui had built and stocked was
still being drawn on by the Tang court decades into the new dynasty; a state that
fell in crisis had, in its more competent years, stored a genuine surplus.

The equal-field land distribution and the administrative structure of
prefectures and counties, the examination system in its early form, the legal
code — all of it passed to Tang with only the modification a new dynasty applies
to make a system visibly its own rather than a wholesale replacement. Li Yuan and
his son did not reinvent Chinese government. They took over Sui's, corrected what
had broken it, and ran the corrected version for the better part of three
centuries.

## What Tang corrected

Almost every specific failure. The Korea campaigns were abandoned as a fixed
policy goal — Tang would eventually subdue Koguryo, but as one campaign among
many rather than as the obsessive, repeated, self-defeating effort that had
broken Sui, and only after building alliances that let somebody else do much of
the fighting. The corvée burden was managed with more attention to what a
population recovering from thirty years of continuous mobilisation could
actually sustain. The Turkic policy that Sui had built on titles and princesses
was, within a decade of Tang's founding, replaced by outright conquest: Tang Taizong
destroyed the Eastern Turkic Khaganate that Sibir had built, and took for
himself the title *Heavenly Khan* — sovereign of the Chinese and of the steppe
at once, a status Sui had spent thirty years paying for and Tang simply took.

## The verdict Tang built its own legitimacy on

Sui's fall became, within a generation, the standard historical example Tang
court advisers used whenever they wanted to warn an emperor against exactly the
kind of overreach that had destroyed his dynasty's own predecessor. Wei Zheng,
the Tang minister famous for blunt remonstrance, invoked the Sui repeatedly and
explicitly as the cautionary case for excessive construction projects, foreign
campaigns and the exhaustion of the peasantry — using the same rhetorical move
Han had made about Qin eight centuries earlier, because it was, by the ninth
century of the pattern, a well-tested argument: legitimacy survives the
machinery of empire only if the machinery is not run to destruction.

## The short dynasty that built the long one

Measured only by its own duration, Sui belongs beside Qin as one of the
shortest-lived unifications in Chinese history — fifteen years for one, thirty-
seven for the other, each ended by the exhaustion of a population its own
administrative brilliance had over-mobilised.

Measured by what it left standing, Sui is closer to being the actual founder of
medieval China than the dynasty that gets the credit for the three centuries
that followed. The canal, the granaries, the reunified administration, even the
lesson in what not to do with a million conscripted men — Tang inherited all of
it, the same way Han had inherited Qin's roads and registers within a few years
of burying the state that built them, and then built the version of it that
lasted.
