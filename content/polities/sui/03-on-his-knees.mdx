The Sui's most successful foreign policy cost nothing in soldiers and worked by
giving a Turkic khan a Chinese princess, a great deal of silk, and just enough
jealousy of his own brother to keep the largest steppe power in Asia from ever
uniting against China again.

## The empire on the frontier

The First Turkic Khaganate, when Sui took the throne in 581, ran from the
Manchurian forest to the borders of Persia — the single largest nomad power that
had faced a Chinese state since the Xiongnu. Its khan Shapolo styled himself, in
Parker's translation of the full title, *the Sheporo Baga Khakhan of the Ili-külü
horde of Great Turkey*, and asserted, formally, an equality with the Chinese
emperor: two suns could not share the sky, but Turkey and China could each have
one.

When Sui displaced the Northern Zhou, ten thousand Turks who had been living well
off a marriage alliance with the old dynasty found their unprofitable arrangement
cancelled, and the Zhou princess who had married the khan Tapar began raiding the
frontier to avenge her family. The new Sui emperor answered with an army that put
Shapolo to flight — and then, having demonstrated that raiding had a cost, spent
the following three decades demonstrating that cooperation had a much larger
reward.

## The method

Sow dissension by exciting jealousies is Parker's own summary of the policy, and
the historical record he translates shows it applied with real patience.

When Shapolo's successor Duli felt slighted by favours shown to a rival branch of
the family, Sui built him a city inside the frontier, granted him the Ordos
country to settle in, and had a large canal dug at forced labour to mark out his
new territory. When Duli's own son and successor, the Sibir khan, grew powerful
enough to be dangerous, the same offer of a princess and a separate title went to
one of Sibir's own brothers — engineering exactly the family rivalry that had
worked before.

## The boat

The high point of the relationship is a single scene Parker preserves in detail.
In 607 the second Sui emperor — Yang Di, whom Parker elsewhere calls, without
much ceremony, *the celebrated madman* — travelled by boat to Duli's encampment
in person. Duli received him on his knees, pledging the emperor in a goblet of
wine. The following year a second audience was granted, more magnificent than
the first.

The Sui court gave the Turkic khan honours above every grandee of the Chinese
empire itself: audience without the usual prostration, permitted to keep his
shoes and his sword in the emperor's presence, exempted even from the taboo
against speaking his own personal name aloud. Two hundred thousand pieces of silk
were distributed to two thousand five hundred assembled tribal chiefs in a single
ceremony.

That is not the behaviour of an empire treating a steppe power as a subject. It
is an empire buying, at a price that dwarfed any single military campaign, the
one thing no army could guarantee: an undivided Turkic khaganate that chose,
consistently, to stay on China's side of every quarrel it had with itself.

## What the policy actually was

Not friendship and not conquest. A permanent, expensive, deliberately maintained
asymmetry: enough silk and enough honour to make loyalty to the Sui more
profitable than unity among the Turks themselves, renewed at every succession
with a fresh marriage and a fresh title, so that the largest nomad confederation
on Sui's border spent its energy on its own succession disputes instead of on
the frontier.

It worked for the whole of Sui's short reign. It also meant that when the
dynasty finally did collapse under the weight of its own wars in Korea, the
Turkic khan it had spent thirty years cultivating was the one power in Asia with
both the strength and the standing to become, briefly, the arbiter of who would
rule China next.
