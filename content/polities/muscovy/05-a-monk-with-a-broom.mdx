Ivan III died in 1505 having brought Novgorod, Tver, Yaroslavl, Rostov
and Perm under Moscow's own rule, but the work of absorbing every last
independent or semi-independent Rus' principality was not yet finished.
His son Vassilii III spent the next twenty-eight years completing it,
picking off what remained one territory at a time: Pskov surrendered
its own independence in 1510, Riazan followed in 1517, and Chernigov
and Novgorod-Seversky were folded in by 1523 after their own last
independent ruler conquered a rival prince and was promptly thrown into
a Muscovite dungeon himself for his trouble.

## A city fought back from Lithuania

The most consequential single acquisition was Smolensk, seized from
Rus by Lithuania early in the fifteenth century and won back by
Vassilii in 1513 - restoring to Moscow a city that had been outside
direct Rus' rule for a full century. Between his own father's reign and
his, the territory answering to Moscow more than doubled, from a
little over 15,000 square miles to at least 25,000. What Ivan III had
begun as the absorption of Moscow's own last rival appanages, Vassilii
finished as something closer to a single, undivided Great Russian
state.

## "The Empire is not yet wholly cleansed"

Contemporaries understood what was happening even as it happened. One
Muscovite chronicle hailed Vassilii as the final consolidator of Rus'
outright, and a story kept alive in the capital captured the same
feeling more vividly still: when the last independent prince still
holding an appanage under Moscow was thrown into prison, a monk was
seen walking the streets of the city carrying a broom. Asked what it
was for, he answered that the empire was not yet wholly cleansed, and
that the time had come to sweep away the last of the dust. By the time
Vassilii died in 1533, there was, in practical terms, no dust left to
sweep - no rival Rus' prince remained who could still be described as
anything other than Moscow's own subject.
