By the middle of the fifteenth century Muscovy was still one
principality among several, bordered by Novgorod's vast northern
territories, by Tver to its own north, and by Lithuania to the west.
Within a single reign - Ivan III's, from 1462 to 1505 - that changed
so completely that contemporaries could no longer picture the Rus'
lands as anything but Moscow's own.

## Absorbing the neighbours, one method at a time

Ivan's acquisitions came by whatever means was available in each case.
In 1463 the princes of Yaroslavl simply swore fealty and gave up their
independence. In 1474 the princes of Rostov sold what remained of
their own territory outright. Perm submitted in 1472. Novgorod the
Great was the largest prize and the one that took force: through the
1470s Ivan's armies brought the republic and its enormous northern
hinterland under direct Muscovite rule, and in the aftermath thousands
of Novgorodian boyars and merchants were deported to central Muscovite
provinces while Muscovite servitors were settled on the confiscated
Novgorodian land in their place - a deliberate redistribution meant to
make reconquest of the old republic's loyalties as difficult as its
military reconquest had been.

## A Byzantine marriage for a new kind of throne

In 1472 Ivan married Sophia Palaiologina, a niece of the last Byzantine
emperor who had been living in Italy since Constantinople's fall to the
Ottomans. The match brought Greek court ceremony, a claim to
succession from Byzantium's own imperial line, and a marked change in
how the Muscovite ruler began to present himself - no longer merely the
senior of several tribute-paying Rus' princes but a sovereign styling
himself heir to a fallen empire. The boyars resented the Greek retinue
that came with her, and resented still more the ceremonial distance
Ivan increasingly kept from men who had once treated their prince as
scarcely more than first among equals.

## The tribute that was never paid again

The last formal test of Muscovite subjection to the Golden Horde came
in 1480, when the khan Ahmed brought an army to the river Ugra. Ivan
took up a position facing him, then withdrew toward Moscow - a retreat
that panicked the capital's own citizens, who are recorded accusing
their ruler of betraying them to the Tartars rather than praising his
caution. No battle was fought. Ahmed's army eventually withdrew
without forcing a crossing, and the tribute relationship that had bound
every Rus' prince to the Horde since 1238 was never restored. Ivan's
own subjects, in the moment, did not yet see him as untouchable. What
the stand at the Ugra shows is how narrow the gap still was, in 1480,
between an emerging Muscovite sovereign and a nervous appanage prince
whose citizens still felt entitled to reproach him to his face.
