title: A halfway villa becomes a throne

The first mention of Moscow in the chronicles is not flattering. In 1147
the prince of Suzdal, Yuri Dolgoruki, invited a neighbouring prince to
what the record calls a mighty dinner at a spot on his southern border -
a halfway stop on the road between Suzdal and Kyiv, apparently no more
than his own country villa. Nine years later Yuri had a ring of wooden
walls built around it. For the rest of the twelfth century and most of
the thirteenth, Moscow was not even a settled princely seat: rulers
passed through it, none stayed, and for stretches of years it had no
prince at all.

## The youngest son gets the smallest prize

That changed in 1263. Alexander Nevsky, prince of Novgorod and Vladimir,
died that year, and his youngest son Daniel, still a minor, was given
Moscow as his own principality - the smallest and least regarded portion
of his father's holdings, assigned to the junior line precisely because
it was worth the least to anyone senior. From that year on the town had
a permanent ruling house of its own, and every Muscovite prince who
followed, down to the last, traced his line back to that same
overlooked younger son.

## What made a junior appanage worth having

Moscow's geography, unglamorous as it looked on a map of princely
prestige, sat where three overland roads and a river system meshed:
routes from Novgorod, from Smolensk, and from Kyiv itself converged
there, along with a river connection linking the Upper Volga to the
Oka. Colonists moving north from a war-torn south passed through Moscow
first and often stopped there, giving the principality an early density
of settlement its rivals lacked. None of this made Moscow rich by
comparison with Novgorod's trade or Suzdal's older prestige. It made
Moscow defensible, well-peopled, and, crucially, positioned to profit
from whatever else its princes could add to it.

## A dynasty with nowhere higher to fall from

Being junior carried one further advantage nobody could have planned
for: Daniel's line had no grand inheritance to lose and no old rivalry
to defend, only territory to gain. Every other branch of the same
princely house was already committed to older feuds and older claims.
Moscow's princes, starting from nothing, spent the following century
adding to what they had piece by piece - through purchase, through
inheritance, and, before long, through service to the one power that
now stood over every Rus' principality alike.
