Ivan IV's father, Vassilii III, died when Ivan was three, leaving a
minor heir and, in the source's own recorded words attributed to the
adult Ivan looking back, a mother who died herself when he was still a
child. What followed was a regency, and then a prolonged struggle among
rival boyar clans - the princes Shuiski chief among them - who treated
the young Grand Prince less as a sovereign to be served than as a
prize to be controlled. One faction seized the palace by night raid.
Another had a leading boyar of the rival house murdered outright.
Ivan grew up watching men who owed him formal loyalty fight over who
would actually rule in his name.

## A model no earlier Muscovite ruler had followed

At sixteen, in 1546, Ivan told his startled court that before he
married he wished to look into how his ancestors - the old Suzerain
Princes of Moscow, and his more distant kinsman Vladimir Monomakh -
had handled succession to the throne. The court had not expected this;
Ivan's political education, by the source's own account, had proceeded
in secret, without his tutors' guidance. What emerged from that private
study was Ivan's own decision, formed before any minister proposed it
to him, to take the title of Tsar and to be crowned to it with a formal
Church rite rather than simply inheriting his father's style of Grand
Prince.

## An old legend put to a new use

The precedent Ivan reached for was a story, already old by his time,
that Vladimir Monomakh - whose own mother had been a Byzantine
emperor's daughter - had received imperial regalia and a solemn
coronation from Constantinople itself, and that every Suzerain Prince
of Moscow since had, in effect, inherited a divinely sanctioned
imperial dignity through him. Whether the legend recorded any actual
historical event, or was assembled from older material to serve exactly
this purpose, its use in January 1547 announced something concrete: the
prince crowned that month claimed to be receiving back a title his own
line had always been entitled to, not inventing one.

## What the boyars could no longer contain

The coronation did not end the political turbulence of Ivan's youth -
Moscow burned in a catastrophic fire later that same year, and a
popular uprising followed it, both of which he later described in
vivid, guilt-ridden language. But it marked the point at which a
prince who had spent his childhood watching boyar factions fight over
his throne asserted, in a single ceremony, that the office itself now
stood above any of them - Tsar and Autocrat, a title no previous
Muscovite ruler had formally worn, for a state that had spent nearly
three centuries becoming able to support the claim.
