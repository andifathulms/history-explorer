Every Rus' prince by the fourteenth century owed the Golden Horde
tribute and periodic homage at its court. Ivan, Daniel's third
successor and known to history by the nickname Kalita - "the money-bag"
- distinguished himself from every rival by never once arriving at that
court without a substantial gift for the khan, his favourites, and his
officials. It was, by the chronicle's own account, an axiom among the
Tartars that a visit from the Muscovite prince meant gold and silver for
everyone concerned.

## Punishing Tver, rewarding Moscow

The payoff came in 1327, when the neighbouring principality of Tver
resisted Mongol authority. The khan entrusted Kalita with putting the
revolt down, and Kalita's own troops helped devastate the whole
principality so thoroughly that a chronicler recorded, in plain terms,
that they made the Russian land desolate - all of it except Moscow. In
return, in 1328, Kalita received the Suzerain throne of Rus: from that
year on, no other prince held the senior title, and Moscow became
responsible for collecting the whole federation's tribute rather than
merely its own.

## What tribute-collecting actually built

The office turned out to be worth far more than the title attached to
it. Collecting tribute from every other Rus' principality gave Moscow's
princes both a legitimate reason to interfere in each of their
neighbours' affairs and a steady stream of revenue that no rival
principality could match. The chronicle credits Kalita's tenure with
forty years of relief from Tartar raids across Northern Rus, since a
khan whose tribute arrived reliably through one trusted collector had
far less reason to send punitive expeditions of his own. Moscow grew
rich, and stayed largely undisturbed, precisely because it made itself
indispensable to the power it answered to.

## A battle that changed nothing and everything

Two generations later, Kalita's grandson Dmitri chose a different
relationship with the Horde. In 1380, rather than paying tribute to the
khan Mamai, he met Mamai's army in the field at Kulikovo and defeated
it outright - the first time in the century and a half since the
Mongol invasion that a Rus' prince had beaten a Mongol army in open
battle. Tribute resumed within two years, under a different and more
threatening khan, and Moscow's submission was not in fact over. What
Kulikovo established was narrower but lasting: that the arrangement
Kalita had built Moscow's fortune on could, when the moment called for
it, be fought rather than only managed.
