Every surviving account of how the Nanda dynasty began agrees on one point
and disagrees on almost everything else: the man who founded it had no
legitimate claim to the throne he took.

## What the traditions agree on

Mahanandin, last king of Magadha's ruling Shishunaga line, is said to have
fathered a son by a woman of low caste — a Sudra, in the Puranic telling.
That son, Mahapadma, grew up outside the succession and took the throne
anyway: killing the king he served, exterminating the rest of the ruling
family under the pretence of acting as guardian to its heirs, and then
begetting the sons who would eventually inherit what he had seized. The
Vishnu Purana brands him an avaricious usurper whose reign marked the end
of Kshatriya, high-born, rule in Magadha and the beginning of rule by men of
low degree. The Buddhist tradition, less concerned with caste than with the
family's wealth, dubs the last king of the line "Dhana" — Riches — a name
that reads as a pointed comment on where the dynasty's priorities lay from
the start.

## An empire nobody can date

Beyond the fact of the usurpation, the sources fall apart. The Puranas
count two generations — Mahapadma and eight sons — reigning a full century.
The Jain tradition stretches the same two generations to a hundred and
fifty-five years. The Buddhist chronicles add contradictory legends on top
of both. None of these figures can be reconciled with anything else known
about the period, and modern reckoning settles, faute de mieux, on
something closer to forty years for the whole dynasty — long enough for
one energetic usurper and a handful of short, disputed reigns among his
sons, and no longer.

What survives clearly enough to trust is the shape of the thing: a family
of ambiguous, probably genuinely low birth, that took an already dominant
Magadhan kingdom by force and held it for two generations before losing it
to force in turn.
