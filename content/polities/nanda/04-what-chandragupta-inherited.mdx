Whatever the truth of the Nine Nandas, no source disputes how the dynasty
ended: the last king, Dhana Nanda in Buddhist memory, was deposed and
killed by Chandragupta Maurya around 322 BC.

## An ending that mirrors the beginning

Chandragupta himself, by the tradition Smith finds most credible, was an
illegitimate member of the Nanda family — connected closely enough to
claim a stake in what he was taking, distant enough to have no path to it
except by force. The revolution that put him on the throne is said to have
involved the extermination of everyone else related to the fallen king, a
detail no more surprising here than it had been when Mahapadma did the same
thing to the Shishunagas two generations earlier. A dynasty founded by
killing a king and clearing out his family ended the same way, at the hands
of a man with almost exactly the same kind of claim its own founder once
had.

## The inheritance itself

What Chandragupta actually took possession of was not a country to be
built from nothing but a fully assembled state: the wealth later
generations remembered as proverbial, an army whose reported size had once
turned back Alexander's advance without a battle, and a capital,
Pataliputra, already the unquestioned centre of power in the Gangetic
plain. The work Mahapadma and his sons had spent two generations
accumulating — by force, on top of what Bimbisara and Ajatashatru's
earlier conquests had already built — became, with a single change of
ruling family, the foundation the Maurya empire was built on rather than an
empire it had to conquer for itself.

The dynasty that left the least respected name in the tradition left the
largest single inheritance any successor in early Indian history received
ready-made.
