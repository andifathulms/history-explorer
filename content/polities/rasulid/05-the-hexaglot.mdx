Some time in the third quarter of the fourteenth century, a Rasulid sultan
compiled a dictionary. It gives vocabulary in six languages in parallel columns:
Arabic, Persian, Turkic, Greek, Armenian and Mongol. It is known as the Rasulid
Hexaglot, and it was edited over twenty-five years by four scholars, three of
whom died before it was finished.

Consider what it is evidence of. A ruler in Ta'izz, in the Yemeni highlands,
had reason to compile working vocabularies of the languages of Anatolia, the
Caucasus, Iran and the Mongol world — not classical Arabic and Persian, which
any educated man had, but the spoken forms, arranged for use. The Greek and
Armenian and Mongol sections are among the very few transcriptions of the living
vernaculars of the period that exist anywhere, which is why linguists care about
the manuscript and why Brill published it.

The reason a Yemeni sultan needed them is Aden. The port was where the Indian
Ocean met the Red Sea, and the people passing through it were merchants,
envoys, pilgrims, sailors and slaves from everywhere the trade reached. A court
that ran a customs house on that scale was dealing with all of them, and the
Hexaglot is the practical residue: somebody wanted to be able to talk to the
people who arrived.

This site prints no square-kilometre figure for the Rasulid state and no
population, because none of the sources consulted publishes one, and the
influence counts are all null. A reader working from the measures block alone
would conclude this was a small polity of no consequence.

It was a small polity. The Hexaglot is a better description of what it was
connected to than any number this site could put in a field — and the argument
for having the page is that a corpus which measures reach in square kilometres
will systematically undervalue every polity whose importance was that things
passed through it. Srivijaya held a strait and reads as a gap in the extent
axis. Palmyra taxed a road. Aden taxed a monsoon.
