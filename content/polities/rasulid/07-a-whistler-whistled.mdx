On a Thursday in December 1369 the governor of Zabid rode to the palace, and so
did the emir of the city, and the tax collector of the valley, and the overseer
of the country, and the lord of Fashal. They had agreed among themselves to get
ready for the troops.

The troops in question were the 'Awarin, irregulars of Zabid and the northern
districts, and what they wanted was to be paid on the same footing as the
regular army. They had sworn an oath to each other in the night.

About ten of their notables came to the governor to put the demand. He reviled
them, reproached them, upbraided them, and ordered the soldiers to seize them.

He did not know how many more of them were standing outside the palace gate.

The soldiers rushed the ten; the ten fought back; and then, in the chronicler's
phrase, a whistler whistled. The whole city turned — the 'Awarin who lived in
it, the 'Awarin who had come down from the north, and the Arab tribesmen who
happened to be there — and the army was plundered in one instant.

The governor had counted on the strangers in the city siding with him. It had
filled up with garrison men from the northern cantons, and he assumed they would
say whatever he said. Every one of them, the chronicler notes, was ready for
disorder and for what could be got out of it.

He ran, and the commanders ran, and the soldiers scattered. He was caught in a
room of the palace and killed at the hour of sunset prayer. In the morning he
was washed, shrouded and buried inside the city, near the Lotus-bush Mosque by
the Shubariq Gate. By sunrise on the Friday the sharifs had arrived and were
pitching their tents in the eastern garden.

## What the entry is really telling you

That is not a rebellion against the dynasty. Nobody in it wanted a different
sultan; they wanted their pay. And it is recorded in a court chronicle written
to glorify the dynasty, with the date, the hour, the names of the five officials
who had met that morning, and the mosque near which the dead man was buried.

Which is the point. The Rasulid state was the best-documented government Yemen
ever had, and what the documentation shows in its later decades is a machine
that had to be wound constantly. Judges and emirs going out on circuits to get
in the revenue of the northern cantons and coming back to court with the money.
The sultan moving between Ta'izz in the highlands and Zabid on the plain by
season, holding the festivals in a garden by a watercourse. And, threaded
through it, revolt after revolt in the same coastal towns, put down and
recurring.

The famine of 1391 in the same district reads the same way: the rains late, the
prices up, the cattle dead, the streams stopped, corn at ninety-odd ducats, and
then the rain arriving at the end of Ramadan, the streams flowing, prices
breathing more freely by the middle of the next month, and the new corn coming
to market — followed immediately by an emir setting out to collect the revenue
of the north.

## Why it held anyway

A state that has to reconquer its own tax base every few years usually does not
last two hundred and twenty-five.

This one did, and the reason was Aden. The customs of the port arrived whether or
not the Tihama was quiet, and they arrived in quantities no Yemeni government
had ever had, and they paid for the troops who put the districts back in order
and for the judges who went out with them.

The arrangement was sound for as long as the ships came to Aden. It contained no
provision for their going somewhere else.
