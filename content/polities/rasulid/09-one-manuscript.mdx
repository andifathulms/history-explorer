The Rasulid court of the late fourteenth century had two men in it worth naming
together. One was Firuzabadi, chief judge of the kingdom, who compiled the
*Qamus* — for centuries the standard dictionary of the Arabic language, used
from Morocco to Java by people who had no idea it had been finished in Yemen.
The other was Khazraji, who wrote the history of the dynasty he served.

That pairing is the Rasulid afterlife in miniature. A state built on a customs
house produced, at its own expense and for its own prestige, work that outlived
it by centuries and travelled far further than its armies ever did.

## What the chronicle is, and where it stops

Khazraji's history stops in 1400, at the death of the seventh sultan. He died
himself in 1409. The dynasty went on for another fifty-four years.

So the best-documented state in medieval Yemen has almost no documentation of
its own collapse. The years when Aden's trade was diverted up the Red Sea, when
the revenue that had paid for everything stopped arriving, when the family fell
to fighting over what was left — those years are outside the book. The chronicle
covers the decades when the machine worked, week by week, and goes dark before
the interesting part.

That is a common shape and it is worth naming when it occurs. Court histories
are commissioned by courts. They are most detailed exactly where the court was
most confident, and they end when the money for copying them does.

## A single copy

What survived of it is stranger still.

When the book was translated in the 1880s it was from what its translator
believed to be a unique manuscript — one copy, in the India Office Library in
London, transcribed by hand by a British lexicographer who then made his own
transcript a gift to Cambridge in thanks for an honorary degree.

He was candid about why he had bothered. His interest, he wrote in the preface,
was Aden: the ancient emporium that had been the centre of the maritime commerce
of the world until the Cape route drew the traffic away and sapped the source of
its wealth, and which was in his own day the British Empire's most important
coaling station.

Which means the chain runs like this. A dynasty of Turkmen soldiers claiming
descent from the kings of Ghassan built the richest state Yemen ever had on the
customs of a port. The port lost its traffic to a new sea route, and the state
died of it within a lifetime. Four centuries later the same port was refuelling
British steamships on the way to India, and an Englishman working for that
empire copied out the dynasty's chronicle by hand because he wanted to know how
the harbour had been rich before.

The wealthiest polity in Yemen's history came through to the modern world as one
manuscript, kept because the harbour it was written about had become useful
again for a different reason.
