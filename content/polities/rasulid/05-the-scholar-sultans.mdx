Royal patronage of scholarship is ordinary and can be recorded of thirty
pages. Rulers who were themselves working scholars are not ordinary, and the
Rasulids produced several.

Al-Malik al-Ashraf Umar, who ruled for barely two years in the 1290s, wrote a
treatise on astronomy and the construction of instruments, a work on medicine,
one on agriculture, one on veterinary science, and a genealogy of his own house.
An astrolabe he made with his own hands survives and is in a museum. He was not
commissioning these; he was writing them, and the astronomical work includes
instructions for constructing the instrument the reader is holding.

<Figure id="astrolabe" />

Al-Malik al-Afdal al-Abbas, who ruled from 1363 to 1377, compiled an
agricultural almanac correlating the Yemeni farming year with the several
calendars in use, and the six-language glossary that is the subject of the next
chapter.

There is a Rasulid literature of this kind — on agronomy in particular, which
matters in a country of terraced hillsides where the difference between a crop
and a famine is water management — and a modern scholarship devoted to editing
it. The dynasty also founded madrasas at Ta'izz and Zabid and endowed them
properly, and Zabid in this period was a centre of Shafi'i legal study with a
reputation across the Indian Ocean.

<Figure id="brazier" />

The site has no field for this and has said so before, most recently on the
Muzaffarid page about Hafez. What it can do is put the observation where the
comparison is available. Political weight and
cultural output come apart — that the Artuqids built machines while
acknowledging nine sovereigns, that Trebizond produced Bessarion while paying
everyone. The Rasulids are the case where they do not come apart at all: the
richest state in the region's history was also its most productive court, and
the money and the books came from the same customs house at Aden.
