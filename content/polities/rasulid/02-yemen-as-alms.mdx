A deputy becomes a sovereign in two acts. He puts his name on the coinage, and
he has it read in the Friday prayer. Nur al-Din Umar did both in the year 630,
which is 1232, and the dynasty's own chronicler notes it as the moment: he
commanded money to be coined in his name, and ordered the preachers to offer
public prayer for him in every region of Yemen.

Then he spent the next three years getting somebody else to say it was true.

## The diploma

Sovereignty in this world was not a thing a man simply took. It was a thing a
caliph confirmed, and the caliph was in Baghdad — powerless, four decades from
being killed by the Mongols, and still the only source of the document that made
a ruler a ruler rather than a soldier who had won.

So Nur al-Din sent a large offering to al-Mustansir at Baghdad and asked for the
diploma of sovereignty, requesting appointment as the caliph's lieutenant, which
the chronicler notes is the ordinary practice of princes. The answer came back:
the diploma will reach you by the eve of the day of sacrifices.

It did not. The caliph sent it with the Iraqi pilgrim caravan, and the caravan
never arrived: the Bedouin blocked the road and filled in the watering places
along it, the pilgrims were held up until the season had passed, and every one
of them turned round and went back to Baghdad. Nobody from Iraq made the
pilgrimage that year.

The next year the curtain of the Ka'ba came from Baghdad, and with it a message
that the diploma would come by sea instead, by way of Basra. It did. The
caliph's envoy found the sultan at al-Janad, climbed into the pulpit of the
mosque, and delivered it in public:

*O Nur al-Din, the mighty one greets thee and says: I have bestowed Yemen upon
thee as an alms, and I have set thee over it.*

Then he put the caliph's robe of honour on him, in the pulpit, in front of the
congregation.

A whole country given away as *sadaqa* — charity — by a man in Baghdad who could
not have collected a dirham of its revenue, to a soldier in Yemen who already
held it and had been coining money in his own name for two years. Both parties
knew exactly what the transaction was, and both wanted it done in a pulpit where
people could see.

## Mecca, and the money

The other half of the strategy was the holy cities, and it was conducted almost
entirely with money.

Nur al-Din sent a large treasure and a force to the sharif of Mecca, who used
them to throw the Egyptian garrison out of the city. He sent gold and silver
lamps to hang in the Ka'ba. He made the pilgrimage himself, on blood-camels, in
comfort — and was wounded to find that the sharif he had paid for slipped out of
Mecca rather than perform it in his company, and came back only once the sultan
had gone home.

Then five hundred Egyptian horsemen arrived under five commanders, one of them
named Gabriel, and the sharif left for Yemen with them at his back. The pattern
holds for the next century: Yemen could buy Mecca, and Egypt could take it, and
whichever of them had a force there at the moment of the pilgrimage was the
power whose name was said.

## The ancestors

There was one more thing to acquire, and it could not be bought or fought for.

The family was of Turkmen origin. Its ancestor Rasul had been in Egypt in
Ayyubid service and was sent down to Yemen when Saladin sent his brother to take
the country. That is a perfectly respectable origin for a soldier and no origin
at all for a king of the Arabs.

So the dynasty's own history opens somewhere else entirely. Before it reaches
any Rasulid it goes through Saba and his sons Himyar and Kahlan, the great dam
at Marib and the springs of Bilqis, the dream that warned of the dam's collapse,
the dispersal of the Azd, Khuza'a taking charge of the Ka'ba, Aws and Khazraj
going up to Yathrib — and then Jafna going north with Ghassan, the Ghassanid
princes of the Syrian frontier, and their last king. Only after that does it
arrive at a man called Rasul in Egypt.

The Rasulids, in other words, claimed to be Ghassanids: the Arab kings who had
held the desert frontier for Rome, six centuries earlier and two thousand
kilometres away, and who trace in the same genealogy back to the kingdom at
Marib. It is not true. It is also the most expensive-looking pedigree available
in Arabic, and a dynasty that had just been given its country as alms by a
caliph it could not see had every reason to want one.
