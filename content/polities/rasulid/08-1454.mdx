title: What happened when the ships stopped

From the 1420s the Mamluk sultan Barsbay set about moving the Indian Ocean trade
off Aden and onto Jeddah, where Cairo could tax it. From the Egyptian side it
was one half of a programme that also made the state the sole buyer of pepper at
Alexandria. From the Yemeni side it was the removal of the thing the Rasulid
state consisted of.

It was not done in a day and it was not done only by decree. Merchants were
required, pressed, inducted and eventually compelled; the Mamluks had the naval
reach in the Red Sea to make Aden's welcome less attractive than Jeddah's
compulsion, and once the sharif of Mecca's customs income depended on Cairo the
Hejaz end of the route was closed too.

The Rasulid collapse follows the revenue with a lag. The last decades are
factional: contested successions, the mamluk regiments taking sides, tribal
confederations in the highlands withdrawing recognition, and Zaydi imams from
the north taking back districts they had lost two centuries earlier. In 1454 the
last Rasulid was gone and the Tahirids — a Yemeni family of highland landowners
who had been men of consequence in the Rasulid state, and no relation to the
Khurasanis of the same name — took what was left.

Two things follow that are worth keeping. The first is that the Rasulid state's
rise and fall are the same fact seen twice — a polity built on the customs of one
harbour is exactly as durable as the reasons ships have to enter it. The second
is that the Portuguese reached Calicut forty-four years after the last Rasulid
sultan. The route Barsbay had fought to control was itself about to be
circumvented, and the Mamluk page records what that did to Cairo. Aden did not
live to see it.
