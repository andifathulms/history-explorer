---
title: The deputies who stayed
drafted_from: smith-1974
phase: formation
---

Saladin's brother Turanshah conquered Yemen in 1173, and this site's Ayyubid
page records the branch that was sent there as one of the pieces the
confederation was divided into. It held the country, badly and intermittently,
for fifty-six years. When the last Ayyubid prince of Yemen left for Syria in
1229 he appointed a deputy to hold it for him, from a family that had come south
in Ayyubid service.

The deputy did not hold it for him. Umar ibn Ali ibn Rasul had the khutba read
in his own name, took the title al-Malik al-Mansur, and founded a state that
lasted two hundred and twenty-five years — considerably longer than the Ayyubid
confederation that had appointed him.

The corpus has recorded that move before and will again. The Tulunids and
Ikhshidids did it to the Abbasids in Egypt; Toghtekin did it to the Seljuks at
Damascus; Zangi did it at Mosul. A deputy left in charge of a province by a
distracted overlord is the single most common way a polity begins on this site,
and it is worth noticing that in almost every case the overlord had no
alternative: governing Yemen from Cairo required either an army permanently
stationed there or a man on the spot who could be trusted, and Cairo could not
afford the first.

The family's origins are the interesting complication. Rasul — the name means
messenger, and was reportedly a title earned by an ancestor who had served as an
envoy — was of Turkmen stock, and the family had arrived in the Arab world with
the Oghuz migrations like the Seljuks and the Artuqids. Their own court
historians said otherwise. The Rasulid genealogy as the dynasty published it
traced the house to the Ghassanids: the Arab federate kings of the Roman
frontier whom this site carries as the Jafnid record, six centuries earlier and
two thousand kilometres to the north.

Nobody has to believe it, and this site's `legitimation` field records `descent`
anyway, because the field asks what was asserted in public. What is worth
noticing is which ancestors were selected. A Turkmen family ruling Arabs in the
most self-consciously Arab country in the world chose the most prestigious Arab
kings on offer — and did it at a moment when the actual Ghassanids had been gone
for six hundred years and were remembered mainly in poetry.
