Aden is a harbour inside a dead volcano on the corner of Arabia, and in the
thirteenth and fourteenth centuries it was the hinge of the trade between the
Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean.

The monsoon made it so. Ships from Gujarat, Malabar and the Persian Gulf sailed
west on one season's wind and could not continue up the Red Sea, which is
difficult sailing against contrary winds and full of reefs; goods were landed,
warehoused, taxed and reloaded onto Red Sea craft for Egypt. Aden was where that
happened, and whoever held Aden took a percentage of everything Europe bought
from Asia.

Vallet's book is an argument that the Rasulid state should be understood as the
administration of that percentage. It is not a claim made from inference: the
dynasty left behind fiscal and administrative registers — the *Nur al-ma'arif*
among them — that almost no medieval Islamic polity did, listing tariffs by
commodity, the customs procedure, the officials, the presents given to visiting
merchants and the rates at which each was assessed. This site's
`revenue_basis` field reads `trade-toll` first for that reason, and the coding
rests on documents rather than on a chronicler's impression.

What the sultans did with the money is the rest of this page: the madrasas at
Ta'izz and Zabid, the endowments, the scholarship, and a court that received and
sent embassies to Cairo, to Delhi, to the Ilkhanate, to Ethiopia and to China.
They also spent it on the Hejaz, which is the next chapter.

The state had one strategic weakness and it was the same as its strength. A
polity whose treasury is a customs house depends on ships choosing to stop
there, and ships stop where they are made welcome and where the alternative is
worse. The Rasulids understood this perfectly — the registers show deliberate
inducements to merchants, remissions, gifts, protection — and it worked for two
centuries, until somebody with more force than they had made the alternative
compulsory.
