What Characene did was unload ships and load boats, and take a percentage.

The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, a Greek merchant's handbook of the first
century AD, describes the Gulf traffic; Pliny gives distances and cargoes;
Isidore of Charax — a man of this city — wrote the *Parthian Stations*, an
itinerary of the road from Antioch across Mesopotamia and Iran to Arachosia,
stage by stage with the distances, which is one of the most useful geographic
texts to survive from antiquity and exists because somebody here needed to know
how far things were.

The goods are the ordinary list of the ancient long-distance trade and are worth
naming because they explain the position. Pepper, cinnamon and spices from
India and further east; pearls from the Gulf itself; ivory, tortoiseshell,
cotton textiles, precious stones. Going the other way: silver, glass, wine,
coral, linen, and coin. Roman gold and silver turn up in Indian hoards in
quantity, and a substantial part of it went through this delta.

The kingdom minted continuously — a long series of tetradrachms and drachms
with Greek legends that decay over three centuries into unreadable imitation,
which is the same process this site's Indo-Greek record describes in the
Punjab — and the coin series is most of what the dynastic reconstruction rests
on. This record's `peak` and `last` ruler fields are null for that reason.

It also had competition it could not control. The Palmyrenes ran the desert
route from here to the Mediterranean; the Parthians took a share upstream; and
when the Romans wanted the Indian trade they went by the Red Sea instead, which
this corpus's Ptolemaic and Rasulid records both describe. A transit kingdom
holds a chokepoint only for as long as the chokepoint has no alternative, and the
Gulf always had one.
