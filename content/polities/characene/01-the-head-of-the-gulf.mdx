The Tigris and the Euphrates come together and run into the head of the Persian
Gulf through a delta of marsh and date palm. Everything arriving by sea from
India, Arabia and East Africa had to be landed there and transferred to river
craft for the journey up to Babylonia and onward to Syria — and everything going
the other way had to be transferred back.

Alexander founded a city at that point. It was destroyed by flooding, refounded
by Antiochus IV, destroyed again, and refounded a third time in the 140s BC by
Hyspaosines, the Seleucid satrap of the region, who raised the ground level on
an artificial mound, put his own name on it, and around 127 BC began calling
himself king.

That is the commonest way a state begins in this part of the world: a governor
left in charge by an empire that has stopped being able to remove him. The
Seleucids were losing their provinces in exactly these decades — to the
Parthians in the north-east, to Bactria in the east — and Characene is the piece
that came off in the south.

The kingdom lasted three hundred and fifty years as a Parthian vassal, which is
longer than most of the successor states that came off the Seleucids, and it did
it by being the place the goods changed hands.

The name of the city turns up most often in other people's inscriptions. At
Palmyra, eight hundred kilometres north-west across the desert, merchants set up
monuments honouring the men who had brought a caravan safely home from Spasinou
Charax on the Gulf. There was a resident Palmyrene community in the port, and
one inscription records a Palmyrene whom the king of Characene appointed
governor of Tylos — which is Bahrain.
