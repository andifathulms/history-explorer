---
title: Three polities on one toll
drafted_from: wiesehofer-1996
phase: aside
---

Characene lived on tolls and on nothing else, and it is not alone. A whole
class of polities did the same across sixteen centuries and half the world —
Palmyra, Derbend, the Nabataeans, the Zurayids of Aden, the Rustamids of
Tahert, Srivijaya, Trebizond, and a run of them on the Melaka strait from Pasai
to Johor. Three state the type most clearly.

Palmyra taxed a road across a desert. An oasis with water in a place with none,
midway between Damascus and the Euphrates, whose merchant families financed the
caravans and raised the archers that escorted them, and whose surviving tariff
inscription of AD 137 lists the duty on slaves, purple wool, olive oil and
camel loads.

Derbend taxed a pass three kilometres wide between the mountains and the
Caspian — the only practicable land route between the steppe and Iran, walled
by a Sasanian emperor and held for two centuries by an emirate that lived on
what went through it.

Characene taxed a transfer: the point where sea cargo had to become river
cargo, because ships could go no further.

The three are separated by eight hundred years and three thousand kilometres
and they are the same object. A small permanent settlement on a route with no
practical alternative, producing little itself, whose entire political history
is other people's need to pass — and in all three cases a polity that outlasted
much larger neighbours precisely because it was worth more to everyone
functioning than conquered.

The differences are instructive too. Palmyra used its position for something
else for thirteen years, and is remembered almost entirely for those thirteen.
Derbend never tried, and nothing that happened there is worth dating. Characene
sits between the two: it never attempted anything either, and the one event in
its history is a Roman emperor arriving and going away again.

Mecca is the near miss. The sharifs there lived on the pilgrimage as much as on
tolls, and a destination is a different kind of asset from a route — it is paid
for rather than taxed, and it cannot be bypassed by anyone, which is a stronger
position than any of these three ever held.
