The kingdom ended in 222 and the job did not, because the job was geography. The
Tigris and Euphrates still met and still ran into the Gulf through a delta, and
sea cargo still had to become river cargo somewhere.

The Greek name for the port at that point was Apologos. In Arabic it is
al-Ubulla, which is the same word, and it is still the same word a thousand
years later — one of the very few Greek place-names in Mesopotamia that survived
the language that put it there.

## Why Basra is where it is

Ubulla sat on the estuary and was feverish. So when the Muslims founded their
own city in the delta they built it twelve miles inland, on the desert edge,
where the air was better, and called it Basra.

The two were joined by canal. The Nahr Ma'qil came down from the north-east,
bringing the ships from Baghdad; the Nahr al-Ubulla ran south-east from Basra to
the Gulf at Abadan. Those two channels and the estuary enclosed what everyone
called the Great Island, with Ubulla at its south-eastern corner where its canal
met the water.

Ibn Hawkal in the tenth century reckoned that Basra's canals exceeded a hundred
thousand, of which twenty thousand would take a boat. The gardens along the
Ubulla canal were held to be one of the four earthly paradises. Mustawfi, four
centuries later, describes palm groves so thickly planted that you cannot see a
hundred paces, and dates good enough to export at a profit to India and China.

Where the Ubulla canal met the estuary there had been a whirlpool that wrecked
ships. According to Ibn Hawkal it was got rid of by an Abbasid princess — some
say Zubayda — who loaded a number of vessels with stones and sank them on the
spot until the thing was blocked.

## What happened on the canals

The delta was not only prosperous. On the Nahr Abu'l-Khasib, one of the smaller
southern canals, the Zanj built their stronghold in the ninth century — the
fortified city they called al-Mukhtara, which held out against the caliph's
armies through fifteen years of continuous war before the rebellion was put
down.

Then the Mongols came through, as they did everywhere, and this countryside went
with the rest. Qazwini in the thirteenth century describes Ubulla and its
neighbour Shikk Uthman as ruined. Ibn Battuta, a century after that, found
Basra still populous but strung out among ruins — the mosque of Ali standing two
miles from the inhabited quarters, the old city wall traceable two miles beyond
that, and only three quarters of the town still lived in. Ubulla he found
reduced to a village.

## And then it moved back

The last turn is the one worth the chapter.

Medieval Basra, the inland city, is now the ruins called Zubayr. Modern Basra is
not there. It was built on the estuary at the mouth of the canal — on the site
of Ubulla, which is to say on the site of Apologos.

Fifteen centuries after Hyspaosines raised his mound above the flood and put his
own name on the city, and after a Greek port name had passed into Arabic, and
after the harbour had been moved twelve miles inland for reasons of health and
then abandoned, the traffic went back to the same square mile of mud. Everything
that ever mattered about Characene was true of the ground rather than of the
kingdom, and the ground is still there doing it.
