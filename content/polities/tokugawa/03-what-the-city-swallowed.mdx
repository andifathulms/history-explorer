Under the fifth shogun, Tsunayoshi, the money the Tokugawa peace had
accumulated finally found somewhere to go, and the years it went
there, 1688 to 1704, gave the whole era its name: Genroku.

## A merchant who lived more splendidly than a daimyo

Neither the shogun nor his court nor the great feudal houses were
hoarding anything. They spent lavishly, on presents, on ceremony, on
the constant search for rarities to give each other, and the money
landed, in the end, in the hands of the artists, craftsmen and
merchants who supplied it. Lacquerware, sword-furniture, painting and
every branch of decorative craft reached a level of refinement the era
is still named for. Some merchants grew richer than the nobility they
served: Kii-no-Kuniya's establishment at the entrance to Ueno covered
a whole city block, its private quarters said to outshine any daimyo's
residence in Edo, until his own extravagance drew the authorities'
attention and his fortune was confiscated outright. Yodoya of Osaka
met the same end in 1707 - stripped of a fortune that included two
hundred acres of rice land merchants were legally forbidden to own,
gold and silver objects, tea equipage, and cash and bullion worth
something over two hundred thousand pounds sterling by contemporary
reckoning. The great commercial houses that still mattered two
centuries later - Mitsui, Sumitomo, Konoike - were all established and
already prospering in these same years.

## Where the money did not go

It did not go to the people who grew the rice everyone else's wealth
ultimately rested on. The cultivator's position, already poor, grew
worse through Tsunayoshi's reign: new taxes on produce that had once
gone untaxed, corvée labour that had once carried partial payment now
demanded for nothing, and land that had once been reclaimed for
cultivation now falling out of use rather than being extended. Roughly
eighty percent of the population lived on a bare subsistence while the
shogun, his favourites, the daimyo and the whole hangers-on population
of Edo's mansion quarters spent freely on a prosperity built on that
subsistence - and accumulated nothing themselves, since what they
spent went straight into the same merchant houses growing rich on the
other end of the transaction.

## A currency debased to cover the gap

The government's own extravagance eventually emptied its reserves too.
A generation earlier the koban, the gold coin struck under Ieyasu, had
carried nearly eighty-six percent gold. In 1695 the ratio was cut to
just over fifty-six percent, the difference pocketed as seigniorage to
cover a treasury the court's own spending had drained. Several further
debasements followed over the next two decades, of both the gold coin
and the smaller silver denominations, managed by officials who had
long since stopped asking hard questions of the men above them.

Genroku is remembered as the high summer of Edo culture, and by every
measure of what money could buy and what craftsmen could make, it
was. It was also the moment the gap between the city that was
spending and the countryside that was paying for it became
permanent rather than incidental - a debt the shogunate's finances
would spend the rest of its existence trying, and mostly failing, to
close.
