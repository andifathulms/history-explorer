Keiki's resignation left an uncertain question genuinely unsettled:
whether real power would now rest with the Tokugawa house itself, with
a council of the great domains, or with the imperial court where it
formally belonged. The Aizu clan, the Tokugawa's own most loyal
supporters, still guarded the gates of the imperial palace, and the
samurai coalition pressing for full restoration had no intention of
letting the question hang unresolved for long.

## A palace taken by force in the emperor's own name

On 3 January 1868, troops from Satsuma, Tosa, Echizen, Aki and Owari
seized the palace gates outright. The court nobles who had surrounded
the boy emperor were dismissed and replaced with men loyal to the
coalition's own cause, and the purged court issued an edict, in the
mikado's name, declaring government restored solely to the imperial
throne. The bakufu and the office of shogun were abolished by that
single decree.

## A second thought that came too late

Keiki's own retainers reacted with fury, and Keiki himself soon regretted
a resignation he had already given, wishing himself back in the power he
had voluntarily surrendered. He left Kyoto with the clans still loyal to
him under the stated aim of calming his followers' anger, but with the
real intention of seizing Osaka and cutting the coalition's own
communications instead. When the court sent princes to invite him to
accept a still higher position within the new government, he agreed -
then abandoned the agreement almost immediately, yielding to counsel
that urged him to reoccupy Kyoto by force and settle the matter with the
sword.

## An office that would not be revived

The court refused to let him approach the capital under arms, and the
coalition's own forces, positioned behind fortified barriers on the
roads leading into Kyoto, made the attempt to reverse the resignation
by force a doomed one from the outset. What had begun, in November
1867, as a single reluctant man's decision to step down became,
within two months, an irreversible political fact: the office
Yoritomo had created in 1192, held by three successive houses across
six hundred and seventy-six years, ended with the Tokugawa's own last
holder trying and failing to take back the one decision that had
already cost him everything.
