Ieyasu's son Hidetada spent his own reign chiefly executing his
father's plans and consolidating what had already been won. It was
Ieyasu's grandson Iemitsu, invested with the shogunal title by the
mikado's own hand in 1623, who brought the whole system to the form
later generations would recognise as its finished shape - acknowledged,
by this account, as the ablest of every Tokugawa ruler after the
founder himself.

## An honor that hardened into a leash

Iemitsu's signature innovation required every daimyo in the country to
spend alternating periods of residence in Edo, initially presenting the
arrangement as an honor - the shogun himself would come out to greet
arriving lords in the suburbs, and they in turn swore allegiance in
ceremonies sealed with their own blood. Over time the obligation
tightened steadily, until what had begun as a mark of respect had
become, in substance, plain vassalage: every lord's wife and children
remained permanently in Edo as hostages against his own good behaviour,
whatever province he actually governed.

## A door closed one plank at a time

It was under Iemitsu's rule that the Shimabara rebellion of persecuted
Christian peasants required a two-month siege to suppress, and it was
under his rule that the response to that crisis and to decades of
missionary activity reached its final form: Dutch traders confined to
the artificial island of Deshima, all other foreign commerce excluded
outright, and the country sealed against its own subjects leaving as
much as against foreigners arriving. A coastline that had once
competed to attract Portuguese ships and Jesuit missionaries closed
itself, within a single lifetime, into the most isolated major state in
the world.

## A capital built to be watched over as much as lived in

Beyond the political architecture of hostage-taking and enforced
attendance, Iemitsu's Edo grew into a genuinely improved city - new
aqueducts still serving it centuries later, watchtowers raised against
the fires that threatened any city built largely of wood. The
combination proved durable: a capital made safer and more splendid at
the same time its system of control over the rest of the country grew
harder for any single domain to escape.
