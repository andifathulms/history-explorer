Yoshimune, the eighth shogun, came to office in 1716 with a
preparation none of his six predecessors had matched: as a young lord
in his native Kishū he had spent his days hunting and fishing among
farmers and fishermen rather than in the classroom, and as a young man
in Edo he had wandered its merchant quarters, often with a single
attendant or none, until he understood ordinary city life better than
the scholars training at the shogunate's own university ever would.

## Three questions and no lecture

On taking office he summoned the Great Councillors and asked how much
money was in the treasury. None of them could say. He asked how much
rice sat in the Bakufu granaries. Nobody knew that either. He asked
how many turrets the castle had. Again, silence. Yoshimune said
nothing further and let the point make itself: men who did not know
the basic facts of what they governed were not fit to govern it, and
from that day the Council took the details of administration a great
deal more seriously than it had under his two immediate, largely
absent predecessors.

## A city rebuilt against its own fires

The most visible of Yoshimune's reforms addressed the hazard that had
already burned Edo down more than once: its wooden construction and
thatch or shingle roofing, which turned any outbreak into a citywide
catastrophe once sparks caught the wind. His minister Ōoka Tadasuke
ordered thatch and shingle replaced with tile, a change landlords
resisted for years on grounds of cost until a run of major fires
forced the issue, and organised, in 1718, Edo's first citizen fire
brigades - every street required to supply its own night watch and
thirty firemen on call. The first recruits, drawn from shopkeepers and
apprentices, proved unequal to the physical demands of the work and
were replaced within a year by building labourers, whose companies,
known by their assigned characters as the i-ro-ha brigades, became a
fixture of the city for the rest of the shogunate's existence.

## A country farmed twice over

The reform with the largest material consequence was land reclamation,
pursued with a vigour the previous half-century's neglect had made
urgently necessary. By 1750 more than twice as much land was under
cultivation in Japan as in the year of Sekigahara a century and a half
earlier - something like eleven million acres against five million -
and in some years the rice yield under Yoshimune's administration
reportedly touched sixty million koku, a figure large enough to strain
belief until one remembers that newly reclaimed marshland is far more
fertile than land cropped continuously for a century. After Yoshimune,
no comparable expansion of cultivated land was ever achieved again;
what few later reclamation projects existed in the northern fiefs were
offset by arable land going out of use elsewhere.

## What the reforms could not fix

None of it solved the structural problem Genroku had left behind.
Officials and vassals paid partly or wholly in rice found their fixed
stipends worth less as the reclaimed harvests pushed rice prices down,
even as merchants who dealt in cash prospered from the same abundance
- an increase in the country's total wealth that impoverished exactly
the class the whole system depended on to administer it. Yoshimune's
own reign is remembered, correctly, as the moment competent government
returned to the shogunate after decades of drift. It is remembered
less often as the reign that first showed, in concrete numbers, that
a richer harvest and a poorer samurai class could arrive in the same
decade, from the same cause.
