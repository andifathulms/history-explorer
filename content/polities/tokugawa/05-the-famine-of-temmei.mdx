Between 1783 and 1787 famine killed something between a million and a
million and a quarter people in Japan - more than half again the
combatant losses of the Seven Years' War, fought across three
continents by most of the great powers of contemporary Europe. It
happened inside a country that had spent a century and a half sealed
off from exactly the kind of outside grain trade that might have
eased it.

## An eruption on top of a famine

The disaster had two causes arriving together. Mount Asama erupted in
1783, burying farmland in Kōzuke, Kai and Suruga under feet of ash and
ruining that year's entire crop across a wide belt of central Japan;
contemporary accounts put the immediate death toll at twenty to
thirty-five thousand. At the same time, harvests were failing
independently across the north, in Ōshū and Dewa, where government
granaries stocked decades earlier under an eighth-shogun-era policy
had long since been emptied and never replenished. Only one fief in
the whole north, Uesugi's domain of Yonezawa, had kept a reserve of
grain against exactly this kind of emergency.

## What the regent recorded

The Regent's own account of what followed in the north, preserved and
quoted in later chronicles, is the source itself calls it - almost
beyond belief:

> A trustworthy man, who had travelled in this district, told me that
> in a village which had previously contained 800 houses there were
> only 30 left, the inhabitants of the rest all having died... in all
> the houses he saw bones and skulls scattered about the floor... The
> price paid for a dog was 500 mon, sometimes even as high as 800 mon,
> a rat 50 mon. A rare work of art found no purchasers and could not be
> exchanged for a go of rice. If a person died he was of course eaten
> by the survivors. Those who died of starvation, however, could not
> be eaten, because their flesh decayed so soon. Some people,
> therefore, killed those who were certain to starve and put the flesh
> into brine so as to keep it for a long time.

The same account records a father who asked a neighbour to kill his
own dying son so the flesh could still be eaten, and then killed the
neighbour in turn to keep the whole of it for himself. "In these
times," the chronicler adds, "stealing and incendiarism were not
considered wrong and went unpunished."

## Rice riots in three cities at once

By 1786 flooding along the Tone river had ruined the Kantō's own
harvest, while drought withered crops in the north with no rain at
all. The following year rice prices climbed to three or four times
their normal level as dealers, anticipating the shortage, bought up
stock from the samurai class itself and held it rather than sell -
some of it left to rot, worm-eaten, in storehouses while people ate
seaweed in the streets. When petitions asking the city magistrates to
force dealers to sell went nowhere, mobs began destroying rice
merchants' houses and godowns in Edo on the night of 20 June, and by
the 24th every rice dealer's premises in the city had been wrecked.
The rioting spread within days to Kyoto and Osaka without any
coordination between the cities - the same grievance producing the
same response independently, three times over. The government's own
officers proved unable to arrest a single rioter; only the autumn
harvest and emergency rice imports from other provinces finally broke
the crisis, by which point the price of rice had settled at roughly
double its pre-famine level and stayed there for years.

## What the census recorded

The non-samurai population of Japan stood at 26,010,000 in 1780. By
1786 it had fallen to 25,086,466; by 1792, allowing for a slight
recovery, it stood at 24,891,441 - the lowest figure any Tokugawa-era
census would ever record. Seven lean years, as contemporaries called
them, had erased more than a million people from the country's own
count of itself, in a state that had spent a century and a half
keeping foreign famine relief, foreign grain and foreign ships alike
outside its own sealed coastline.
