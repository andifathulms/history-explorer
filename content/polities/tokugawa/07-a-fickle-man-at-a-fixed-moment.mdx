Two centuries of enforced seclusion left the shogunate looking, on the
surface, at the height of its own power when Commodore Perry's American
squadron arrived to force open ports the bakufu had kept closed since
Iemitsu's own reign. Beneath that appearance the balance had already
shifted: the reigning shogun was ineffective, and the mikado in Kyoto,
Emperor Kōmei, made no secret of his own contempt for a shogunate he
regarded as a nest of usurpers and for the foreigners it was now being
forced to accommodate.

## A talented domain grooming its own alternative

Provincial domains excluded from real power under the Tokugawa system
had spent decades quietly building exactly the kind of administrative
and military capacity the bakufu itself was losing. Satsuma in
particular, under an energetic prince whose own students went on to
lead the coming upheaval, became a centre of manufacturing and
intellectual energy whose graduates - men like Saigō and Ōkubo - would
shape the revolution more decisively than any single battle did.

## A shogun chosen because no one better was available

Successive shoguns after Perry's arrival struggled visibly with the
crisis. When the young shogun who had negotiated the initial treaties
died in 1866, the office passed to Keiki - a man who had repeatedly
declined the position and who, by the account of his own former
associates, possessed private virtues but a fickleness that made his
actual course of action impossible for anyone to predict from one week
to the next. His chief popular appeal rested on nothing more than his
opposition to opening additional ports to foreign trade.

## A resignation offered under pressure that would not relent

By October 1867 a powerful coalition of samurai and former daimyo -
drawn from Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa and allied domains - openly urged Keiki
to resign and restore government to the form it had held before the
shogunate's own founding, seven centuries earlier. The pressure proved
irresistible. On 9 November 1867 Keiki tendered his resignation as
Sei-i Tai Shogun, a decision that satisfied almost no one for long,
least of all the man who made it.
