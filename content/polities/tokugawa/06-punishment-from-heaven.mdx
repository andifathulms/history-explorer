A second great famine struck in the 1830s - six lean years this time,
caused by cold, drought, insects and flood in different regions in
different seasons, with the same result as before: tens of thousands
dead while merchants who held rice stocks refused to sell below famine
prices. What made this famine different was who finally answered it.

## A police inspector who sold everything he owned

Ōshio Heihachirō had been a police inspector in Osaka, a post he
inherited from his father and held with enough integrity to earn the
regard of the city magistrate he served under. When that magistrate
was recalled to Edo and replaced by a man Ōshio found corrupt and
indifferent, he resigned and turned to teaching, drawing students to
a philosophy that prized conscience and generous impulse over the
rigid, officially sanctioned orthodoxy he had been raised in and had
come to despise.

As the famine worsened through 1836 and into 1837, Ōshio petitioned
the city magistrate to act. He was ignored. He sold everything he
owned and spent the proceeds on relief himself - a gesture he knew
was no more than a drop in an ocean, and one that found no imitators
among Osaka's wealthy. In March 1837 he distributed a manifesto titled
"Punishment from Heaven" to the city's temples and shrines, accusing
the officials of having "no respect for the will of the Emperor" and
acting "in utter violation of the laws established by the Tokugawa
Shogunate," and organised a rising meant to kill the city's
magistrates, seize the castle, and force the merchant houses to
distribute their hoarded wealth to the starving.

Betrayed the day before by one of his own men, Ōshio went ahead
anyway. He and his followers set fire to their own houses and to
grand mansions across the city, inviting the destitute to take what
they wanted, before government troops overwhelmed them by weight of
numbers. Eighteen thousand houses, eleven hundred storehouses and five
bridges burned in the two days of fighting. Ōshio escaped, was tracked
down weeks later, and burned the house he was hiding in rather than
be taken alive. It was, Murdoch notes, the first time since the siege
of Shimabara two centuries earlier that samurai had made common cause
with a popular rising against the shogunate - and within a year,
similar risings had broken out in Echigo and Mikawa in open imitation.

## The reformer who imitated the wrong parts of the reform

Ōshio's rising forced the shogunate's ablest minister of the day,
Mizuno Tadakuni, to act on what a small circle had recognised for
years: that the whole system needed reform on the model of Yoshimune's
own Kyōhō measures a century earlier. Mizuno took office in the Great
Council in 1834 and spent the following decade rebuilding his own
standing through careful, patient management - financing the retired
shogun's new palace through voluntary daimyo contributions rather than
forced taxes after an earlier attempt provoked bitter resistance, and
surviving a bribery scandal within the palace bureaucracy that nearly
ended his career before it began in earnest.

When he finally launched his own reform program in 1841, he announced
it explicitly as a return to the spirit of Kyōhō. What he actually
delivered was something narrower. Where Yoshimune had led by personal
example and pressed reform on daimyo and samurai alike, Mizuno was
careful never to offend anyone above his own rank, and aimed the
reform's full severity at Edo's own commoners instead. Guilds -
kumiai, wholesalers, the whole apparatus of merchant monopoly that had
controlled Osaka and Edo trade for generations - were abolished
outright by edict in 1841-2: no more guild dues, no more wholesale-only
trading, prices cut twenty percent by decree and posted outside every
shop for inspection. Alongside it came a sweeping sumptuary crackdown,
enforced by the city magistrate Torii - nicknamed "the Viper" for his
zeal - who closed theatres, story-telling halls and teahouses, forbade
women to take samisen lessons, restricted merchants to plain cotton,
hemp or spun silk, and had people caught dressed above their station
stripped in the street.

## What both reforms actually did

The guild abolition, meant to break monopoly pricing, instead
collapsed the credit and trust the whole trading system had run on;
commercial volume shrank, producers and prices both suffered, and
within a decade the merchants of Edo were petitioning for the guilds'
own restoration - granted in 1851, though on more open terms than
before. The sumptuary crackdown provoked open resentment rather than
the moral improvement Mizuno intended, and gave corrupt constables a
fresh source of bribes to extort from the very citizens the reform was
meant to discipline. Mizuno's own colleagues began warning him the
measures were too severe; by late 1843 his allies had resigned or been
dismissed, and within a month Mizuno himself followed them out of
office.

Ōshio's rising and Mizuno's reform were, on their surface, opposite
responses to the same crisis - one an act of despair against a
government that would not act, the other the government's own belated
attempt to act. What they shared was a diagnosis nobody in power was
willing to follow to its conclusion: that a system built on extracting
a fixed rice surplus from an increasingly exhausted peasantry, to
support a samurai class it could no longer afford and a merchant class
it would not tax, had run out of room to patch itself with edicts.
The bakufu that faced Perry a decade later was already, by its own
ablest reformer's own failure, out of ideas that had not already been
tried and had not already failed.
