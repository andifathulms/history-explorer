Kalita's 1328 reward closed the Suzerain title to Tver for good, but it
did not end the principality's own resistance to Muscovite primacy. A
later prince, Michael Alexandrovich - grandson of the Michael broken by
Kalita's campaign - tried in the 1370s to reverse the verdict by
reaching outside Rus entirely: appealing to Lithuania for military
backing against Moscow, and, more damagingly still in the eyes of his
own countrymen, allying himself with the pagan Tatar khan Mamai. It was
a gamble no rival Rus prince of his generation was willing to make, and
it cost Michael the one asset Tver could not afford to lose - the
sympathy of the rest of Northern Rus.

## A coalition, not a conquest

When Moscow's Prince Dmitri took the field against Tver again in 1375,
he did not have to fight Michael alone. Nineteen other princes joined
his standard, some of them longtime Muscovite dependents but others,
Kliuchevsky's own account makes clear, moved by genuine outrage at a
Rus prince who had tried to turn Lithuania - the standing oppressor of
Orthodox Christians on Rus's own western border - and a pagan khan
against his own countrymen at once. What had once been a contest
between two rival princely houses for the Suzerain throne had become,
by 1375, something closer to all of Northern Rus closing ranks against
one house that had put outside help ahead of its own people's loyalty.
Within five years, that same coalition of princes would stand with
Moscow on the field of Kulikovo and hand the Golden Horde its first
defeat by a united Rus army - a national standing Tver's own gamble had
helped hand to its rival outright.

## A diminished principality, still capable of governing itself

Tver did not disappear after 1375. Decades later, its Suzerain prince
Boris Alexandrovich was still governing a functioning state: a 1427
treaty with Vitovt, Grand Duke of Lithuania, bound every lesser prince
of Tver's own princely house - uncles, brothers, nephews of the ruling
line - into strict obedience to him, on pain of forfeiting their own
appanages if they entered any other suzerain's service. It was the same
kind of internal consolidation Moscow's own princes were carrying out
among their subordinate kin during the same decades - proof that Tver,
even permanently shut out of the Suzerain title and thoroughly
discredited by Michael's failed gamble two generations earlier, still
had the institutional strength to govern its own house in order. That
strength would not, in the end, be enough to save it.
