Tver outlasted the loss of the Suzerain throne by a century and a half,
but it did not spend those decades recovering ground against Moscow.
By the closing years of Tver's own independent existence, its rulers had
turned to an alliance with Lithuania for the kind of outside backing that
might, in an earlier generation, have come from resisting the Horde
directly - and it was that alliance Ivan III of Moscow used as his own
pretext for a final reckoning.

## Defections that settled the matter before the siege began

The military campaign that followed was almost an afterthought. Long
before Ivan's own preparations for a punitive expedition were complete,
Tveran boyars and officials had already been transferring their loyalty to
Moscow's service in steady numbers - drawn, as the historical record
makes clear, by the plain advantages of serving a wealthier and more
powerful ruler rather than by any grievance against their own prince.
Two appanage princes of Tver itself joined this drift to Moscow before
Ivan's army had even taken the field. When Ivan's forces finally reached
the city's gates, yet another group of local boyars and princes crossed
over and swore allegiance to the Muscovite ruler on the spot.

## Treason, in the losing side's own word for it

Tver's own chronicle does not describe its principality's fall as a
military defeat. It calls the pattern of defection izmena - treason - and
lays the blame for the principality's collapse squarely on it, a judgment
a second contemporary writer independently confirms. The city itself
surrendered in 1485 without a battle being fought at its walls, brought
down less by Muscovite arms than by its own leading families concluding,
one after another, that their future lay in Moscow's service rather than
in continued loyalty to a prince who could no longer outbid his rival for
anything - not the Suzerain throne, long since lost, and not, in the end,
even his own boyars' loyalty.
