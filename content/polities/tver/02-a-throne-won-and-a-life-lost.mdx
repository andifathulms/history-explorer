---
title: A throne won, and a life lost

Michael of Tver reached further than any prince of his line before or
after him: he held the Suzerain throne of Vladimir itself, the senior
title of all Rus, at a time when Moscow's own princes were still
maneuvering to take it from him rather than governing from it
themselves. For a Tver prince to hold the position his own principality's
founder had negotiated with as an equal two generations earlier was, in
one sense, exactly the outcome Tver's early diplomacy had been building
toward.

## A rivalry that Michael did not survive

The throne did not stay in his family. Yuri, prince of Moscow, contested
Michael's hold on it directly, and the dispute between them ended at the
court of the Golden Horde itself rather than on any battlefield in Rus.
Michael was put to death there, in circumstances Moscow's own princes
had had a direct hand in provoking. His body was returned to Rus and,
in a detail the chronicle records with evident shock, thrown down naked
before Yuri's own tent - a display of contempt severe enough that even
the Tartars present are said to have found it discomfiting. Whatever
satisfaction Yuri drew from the moment, it marked the point at which the
contest for the Suzerain throne stopped being a matter of seniority or
diplomacy and became, openly, a matter of which rival family was
willing to go further.

## A peak reached and lost within one generation

Tver would not hold the Suzerain throne again after Michael, and his
own death set in motion the resentments that would drive his son
Alexander toward open resistance against the Tartars a decade later - a
resistance that cost the principality far more than the throne it had
already lost. Michael's own reign marks, in retrospect, both the height
of Tver's ambitions and the moment those ambitions collided fatally with
a rival house that had already learned to fight by other means than
seniority or valor.
