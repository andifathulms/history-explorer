Alexander, Michael's son, inherited a principality with every reason to
resent the Horde and the Muscovite rivals who had used it against his own
father. Where Moscow's princes met Tartar demands with steady tribute
and personal flattery of the Khan, Alexander tried something no other
Rus prince of his generation was willing to risk: he urged his fellow
princes to stand together against the Tartars rather than submit, and,
pushed past the point of tolerance by Tartar exactions on his own city,
joined its citizens in killing a party of Mongol officials sent there.

## A punishment meant to be unforgettable

The Khan's response bypassed Alexander's own resistance and went
straight to the rival most eager to carry it out. Ivan Kalita of Moscow
was entrusted with chastising Tver for the killings, and he executed the
assignment with a thoroughness that went well beyond what the offense
alone required - Tver's own principality was left desolate, in the
chronicle's own words, while Moscow's territory, conspicuously, went
untouched by the same campaign. Alexander himself survived the
immediate disaster, taking refuge at Pskov, but the principality he had
tried to lead into open defiance was, in practical terms, broken.

## A reward that ended the contest for good

Kalita's reward for the devastation he inflicted was the Suzerain throne
itself, granted by the Khan in 1328 - and from that year forward, the
title never again left Moscow's hands. What Tver had once negotiated
for as an equal and, under Michael, briefly held outright was now
permanently closed to it. The principality survived Alexander's own
generation, and would continue to exist for another century and a half,
but it never again seriously contended for the one title that had, for two
generations, defined what its own princes were fighting each other's
Muscovite cousins over.
