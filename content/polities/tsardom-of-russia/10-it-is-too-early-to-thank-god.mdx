Alexander II had signed a modest plan to bring elected representatives
into government discussion on 13 March 1881. The next day he was dead,
and the plan died with him - along with, for a generation, any serious
prospect of Russian constitutionalism.

## Two bombs

Revolutionaries had been hunting Alexander for years, mining streets
in St. Petersburg and rehearsing bomb drills under the supply officer
Kibalchich's instruction. On 13 March, returning from a review, his
carriage was struck by a bomb thrown by the young Nihilist Rysakov at
a signal from Sophia Perovsky. The tsar was untouched; he dismounted to
check on wounded Cossacks of his escort and spoke not unkindly to the
arrested bomber. At that moment a second assassin, Grinevetsky, said
"it is too early to thank God" and threw a second bomb between his
feet. Alexander's legs were crushed and his stomach torn open; he
asked only to be taken home to die, and did, within two hours.

## A son who had opposed his father's reforms

Alexander III inherited a throne, a murdered father, and a completed
draft reform his own tutor Constantine Pobedonostsev talked him out of
publishing. Physically enormous - strong enough, by report, to bend a
horseshoe by hand - and by temperament the opposite of his father's
Liberal instincts, he had opposed Loris-Melikov's late reforms while
still heir, and read his father's murder as their vindication rather
than their unfinished business. His accession manifesto, drafted by
Pobedonostsev, proclaimed "faith in the power and the right of
autocracy" and cost the government its two most reform-minded
ministers in the first joint resignation of its kind in Russian
history.

## The counter-reforms

What followed over the next thirteen years was systematic reversal
rather than mere reaction. The police ran down the assassination
conspiracy completely, executing its organizers and effectively
destroying the revolutionary movement that had produced it - the
survivors themselves later admitted finding no public sympathy for what
they had done. Pobedonostsev, presiding over the Holy Synod, pursued
religious dissent of every kind: Stundists banned from meeting,
Dukhobors driven to emigrate to Canada, Leo Tolstoy himself eventually
excommunicated for his doctrine of non-resistance. A new Zemstvo law in
1889 rigged local elections toward the gentry and let governors strike
provisions at will; the Land Captains created the following year, drawn
from the poorer gentry and answering directly to the Ministry of the
Interior, folded judicial and administrative power over peasant life
into a single office peasants themselves recognized as serfdom's
partial return. Universities lost the autonomy Alexander II had granted
them in 1884, and repeated waves of student unrest - Kazan and St.
Petersburg in 1882, four Russian universities in 1887, thirteen
thousand students on strike nationwide by the century's end - were put
down with expulsions, conscription into the army, and, in Kiev in 1900,
Cossacks surrounding a meeting of a thousand students outright.

## What the reversal left behind

Alexander III's own reign is not remembered for catastrophe the way
his father's death or his son's would be - Bunge's finance ministry
still abolished the hated poll tax and founded a Peasants' Bank even
amid the reaction, and the empire he left in 1894 was, on the surface,
stable. What thirteen years of systematic reaction actually did was
remove every safety valve his father's own reforms had opened,
leaving his son Nicholas II a state that had trained a generation of
students, minorities and peasants to expect nothing from petition and
everything from confrontation - the same generation that would make
1905 impossible to absorb quietly.
