The assembly that had elected Michael Romanov did not disappear
overnight. The Zemsky Sobor - clergy, boyars, gentry and townsmen
meeting in national session - remained in real use through Michael's
whole reign and half of his son Alexis's, confirming Alexis's own
succession and, in 1648, producing the Ulozhenie, Russia's first
full law code. But its representative character shrank with every
decade the dynasty it had created grew more secure: a 1634 Sobor was
summoned and dismissed in a single day, later sessions were convened
only for whichever class the government needed to consult on a given
question, and by 1682 the body that had once debated a dynasty's
legitimacy for weeks had dwindled to a crowd in Red Square, instructed
in advance which name to shout.

## A single letter split a church

The deeper crisis of Alexis's reign began, on its face, as a matter of
spelling. Patriarch Nikon - a peasant's son who had risen to become
the Tsar's own intimate friend and unofficial regent during his
absences - undertook in 1654 to correct the Russian liturgy against
Greek originals, fixing errors as small as a single mistranslated
letter in the name of Jesus and as significant as the number of
fingers used to make the sign of the cross. The reaction was neither
small nor confined to scholars: preachers like Avvakum, who
preserved his own defiance in a vivid prison memoir, declared the
corrected books the work of Antichrist, and whole monasteries refused
to accept them. Nikon's answer was punishment rather than persuasion,
and the resulting schism - the Raskol - outlived Nikon's own fall from
favor in 1666 by centuries; the monks of Solovetsky, besieged for
resisting the new books, held out until 1676, and the "Old Believer"
communities the schism produced would go on to furnish the discontented
backbone of peasant rebellions for generations, Pugachev's included.

## A regency run from behind a curtain

Alexis died in 1676 leaving two frail sons by his first marriage and,
by his second, a vigorous young son named Peter - and when the elder
son Fedor died in 1682 without an heir of his own, the succession
question briefly revived the Sobor's old function one last time. A
crowd in Red Square proclaimed the ten-year-old Peter Tsar over his
sickly older half-brother John, and Peter's own capable half-sister
Sophia answered by inciting the palace guard to riot, using the chaos
to have herself installed as regent for both boys at once - a throne
still preserved today with two small seats and a curtain, from behind
which Sophia could prompt her brothers' decisions. Sophia governed
ably for seven years: she secured Kyiv permanently for Russia by treaty
with Poland in 1686, sent expeditions against the Crimean Tatars, and
concluded Russia's first treaty with China at Nerchinsk in 1689. None
of it protected her once her own younger half-brother grew old enough
to object to sharing power. Warned of a plot against him, Peter fled by
night to the Trinity Monastery, watched the streltsy regiments desert
Sophia's cause one by one, and had her confined to a convent when he
returned to Moscow that September - not yet ruling outright himself,
but no longer a boy anyone could regent on behalf of, either.
