By early 1613 a national council - clergy, boyars, gentry and Cossack
delegates gathered from across what remained of a functioning Muscovite
state - had driven a Polish garrison out of the Kremlin and faced the
question the Time of Troubles had left unanswered: who should be Tsar
now that no candidate could claim an unbroken hereditary right to the
title.

## An argument settled by a Cossack, not a genealogist

The Council's debate was genuinely open. A written opinion nominating
Michael Romanov, a young relative of the old dynasty through its female
line, provoked outrage when it was first read out - the assembly
demanded to know who had submitted it and on whose authority. It was a
Don Cossack ataman, stepping forward with his own written nomination
for the same candidate, who broke the deadlock: once his opinion had
been read, the chronicle records, the council agreed to be of one mind.
Michael's own claim was genealogically real but far from the strongest
available; what mattered more was that almost no faction actively
opposed him.

## A plebiscite before the vote

The Council did not simply proclaim its choice. Trusted emissaries were
sent to towns across the country to sound out opinion before any final
decision, and returned reporting that ordinary people had already
settled, independently, on the same name. Only then, on 21 February
1613, did every member of the Council submit a written opinion naming
Michael, and only then was the choice put to the crowd gathered in
Moscow's Red Square, who answered with his name as one voice.

## A dynasty founded on consent rather than inheritance

Michael Romanov was sixteen when he accepted a throne that had bankrupted,
starved and nearly extinguished itself in the space of fifteen years. The
house that would rule Russia for the following three centuries began its
reign not on the strength of an unbroken bloodline - the very thing the
Time of Troubles had shown could run out - but on the considered
agreement of an assembly and, the sources insist, of the country beyond
it. Every later Romanov claim to legitimacy rested on the memory of that
election as much as on the blood it happened to run through afterward.
