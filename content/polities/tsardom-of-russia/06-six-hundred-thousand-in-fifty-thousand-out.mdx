Napoleon invaded Russia in June 1812 with some 600,000 men, the
largest army Europe had yet assembled, meaning from the start to force
Alexander I back into the alliance their two empires had briefly
shared before it collapsed. He did not intend a war of conquest - he
told his own officers Moscow was "only a stage on the road to India" -
but Alexander gave him no opening to negotiate. The Russian armies
retreated for months rather than risk annihilation in open battle,
trading Smolensk and territory the French army badly needed for time
it could not actually use, until the two forces finally met at
Borodino on 7 September. Each side lost forty thousand men and some
forty generals in a single day of fighting Napoleon himself judged too
large and too poorly surveyed to command as he had commanded his other
battles. The Russian army survived largely intact; the road to Moscow
did not.

## A capital set alight by its own governor

Napoleon entered an undefended Moscow days later. Its governor,
Rostopchin, had already emptied the city's prisons, removed its fire
engines, and left instructions to burn what he could not hold; fires
broke out within days of the French occupation and, fed by munitions
stores in the Kremlin itself, drove Napoleon out to a country estate
outside the city. He waited there for a peace offer that never came.
Alexander, informed of Borodino, had already said plainly that he and
Napoleon could not both reign; told of the fire, he answered that
after such a wound every other loss was trivial, and that he would
grow a beard and live on potatoes in Siberia before negotiating. With
Moscow burned and no Russian government willing to talk, Napoleon had
nothing left to occupy and no reason left to stay.

## A retreat that became a rout

The retreat that followed turned into one of the worst catastrophes
in the history of European warfare. Marshal Ney, covering the rear
guard, fought his way across a frozen river on foot rather than accept
a Russian general's offer of an honorable surrender - "a Marshal of
France never surrenders," he is recorded telling him - and reunited
with Napoleon's main column days later. At the Berezina river in late
November, with Russian armies converging from two directions,
Napoleon's engineers bought the army's escape at a cost of thousands
of stragglers left behind on the eastern bank. What survived the
Berezina still had to cross the rest of Russia in the depths of
winter, harried constantly by Cossack raiders, men sometimes throwing
themselves into burning houses simply to feel warm one more time. Of
roughly 600,000 men who had crossed into Russia that June, some 50,000
came out again by the same road.

## Two brothers, and a shout for a constitution nobody explained

Alexander did not live to enjoy the triumph for long; he died in
December 1825 with the succession badly unsettled, since his named
heir, his brother Constantine, had secretly renounced his own claim
years earlier without telling the next brother in line, Nicholas. In
the resulting confusion, each brother briefly proclaimed the other
Tsar from a different city, and conspirators within the Guards seized
the opening. On 26 December two thousand soldiers formed up outside
the Senate shouting for "Constantine and Constitution" - many of the
rank and file, by Pares's own account, believing "Constitution" to be
the name of Constantine's wife rather than a form of government.
Nicholas, reluctant to use force, first sent the city's governor to
negotiate; he was shot from behind and killed for his trouble. Only
then did Nicholas order cannon brought up, and grapeshot cleared the
square in two volleys.

## A reign that governed by detail rather than by idea

The rising the Decembrists had staged failed for want of any support
outside their own circle, but it defined Nicholas's entire reign that
followed: five ringleaders hanged, others exiled to Siberia, and an
emperor who treated the whole affair as a personal wound he
investigated in exhaustive detail himself. Nicholas ruled for thirty
years afterward as a careful, limited, intensely dutiful bureaucrat -
a ruler of parade-ground discipline and real administrative reform in
narrow fields, but with no interest in the larger political questions
the Decembrists had raised and every instinct bent toward preventing
their like from rising again. He was still on the throne, and Russia
still an empire that had not fought a major European war since
Napoleon's retreat, when an allied fleet appeared off Sevastopol in
1854 and exposed exactly how far the army's own reputation had outrun
its actual condition. Nicholas died in March 1855 with the Crimean War
still undecided, leaving his son Alexander II an empire about to
discover just how much rebuilding the peace that followed would
require.
