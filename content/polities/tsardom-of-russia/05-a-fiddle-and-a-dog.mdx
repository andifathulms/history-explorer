Peter the Great died in 1725 without a settled succession law of his
own to replace the one he had abolished, and the four decades that
followed put six rulers on the throne in his place - three women, a
boy of twelve, an infant of one, and, last of them, an adult Pares
sums up simply as an idiot. That last ruler, Peter III, succeeded his
aunt Elizabeth in 1762 and spent six months insulting every Russian
instinct at once: concluding an alliance with Prussia after fighting
it for years, ordering Orthodox icons removed from the churches,
and publicly threatening to divorce his own wife and send her to a
convent. That wife, a German princess born Sophie of Anhalt-Zerbst who
had taken the name Catherine on her own marriage, had long since
decided privately that she would rule Russia herself one day. In June
1762, with the Guards regiments already secured for her cause, she
had herself proclaimed empress in St. Petersburg while Peter dithered
at a nearby palace over whether to fight, flee or negotiate. He chose
a humiliating abdication instead, asking only to keep his fiddle, his
dog, his slave and his mistress - and was dead within weeks, killed
in a scuffle during dinner by the same guards who had custody of him,
one of whom wrote to Catherine afterward that none of them could
remember exactly what they had done.

## An empress who read Voltaire and answered Pugachev

Catherine governed for the next thirty-four years with a command of
both statecraft and self-presentation that startled the courts she
corresponded with. She read law, natural history and Enlightenment
philosophy for pleasure, wrote to Voltaire and Frederick the Great as
intellectual equals, and was, by her own account, more than a match
for Austria's Joseph II in the actual give-and-take of European
diplomacy. That command was tested most severely in 1773, when a
Cossack named Pugachev, claiming to be her own murdered husband
returned from exile, raised a rebellion that drew in Tatars, Bashkirs,
escaped convicts and peasants across the Volga and the Urals - burning
gentry estates, hanging hundreds of priests and officers, and coming
close enough to Moscow to panic the capital before Catherine's
generals finally ran him down. Brought to Moscow in an iron cage,
Pugachev was executed in January 1775 - Catherine had forbidden the
use of torture even at his trial - but the rebellion's scale ended any
serious talk of easing serfdom for a generation.

## A partner in dividing a neighbor

Catherine's reign cost Poland-Lithuania its own independent existence
entirely. The partition scheme was Frederick the Great's own
suggestion rather than Russia's, but Catherine, playing him as
skillfully as she played every other European counterpart, turned it
to Russia's lasting advantage across three successive divisions,
absorbing the largest share of Commonwealth territory of any of the
three partitioning powers by the time the last one closed in 1795.
Her other conquests were more straightforwardly her own. In 1783 she
annexed the Crimean khanate outright, ending three centuries of Tatar
raiding from the peninsula and giving Russia, for the first time, an
undisputed coastline on the Black Sea to match the Baltic window
Peter had won two generations before. She toured her new southern
provinces in a triumphal progress the following years, Prince
Potemkin staging the reception - the empire he helped her build had,
by the time she died in 1796, very little left in common with the
struggling Muscovite tsardom Michael Romanov had once been elected to
save.
