Peter, sole Tsar from 1696, spent the first thirteen years of the Great
Northern War fighting for something no earlier Muscovite ruler had ever
possessed: a coastline on the Baltic that was not controlled by Sweden.
He built the city that would prove it before he had actually won the
war for it.

## Building the capital inside the siege

In May 1703, having taken a Swedish fort at the mouth of the Neva,
Peter renamed it Saint Petersburg and began building his new capital on
ground he did not yet securely hold. A fortress on an island guarded
its harbour; a Swedish flotilla attacked the unfinished city outright,
and Peter, serving in his own advance guard, helped capture several of
the attacking ships himself. Few rulers have founded a capital on
territory still actively contested by the power it was built to face
down.

## Poltava

The war's decisive battle came in 1709 at Poltava, where Peter's army
broke the Swedish force so completely that its survivors, fleeing to
the Dnieper, were run down and forced to surrender before they could
cross it; the Swedish king escaped to Ottoman territory with barely an
escort. Peter marked the victory that night by drinking, in his own
words, "to my teachers in the art of war" - toasting the captured
Swedish generals whose earlier defeats of him had taught him how to
win this one. From the battlefield he wrote that the first stone of
Saint Petersburg's foundation had now truly been laid.

## An empire declared, not merely conquered

Twelve more years of campaigning secured the Baltic coastline for good,
and in November 1721, on Archbishop Feofan Prokopovich's initiative,
the Senate and the Church jointly hailed Peter as Peter the Great and
conferred on him and his successors the title of Emperor. No Muscovite
ruler had ever formally worn that title before. Peter had secured his
own long-sought window on Europe by force of arms; the title that
followed announced, in the same act, that Russia intended to be counted
among Europe's own great powers rather than merely trading with them
from a safe distance.
