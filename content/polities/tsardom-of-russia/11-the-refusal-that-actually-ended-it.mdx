A general strike in October 1905 stopped the capital cold - electric
light switched off, newspapers unpublished, schools closed by their own
staff and pupils. Sergius Witte, freshly returned from negotiating peace
with Japan, convinced a deeply reluctant Nicholas II that some kind of
constitution was now unavoidable. The manifesto issued on 30 October
promised an elected Duma with real legislative power: no law was to
pass without it. It was the first formal limit any Russian sovereign had
placed on his own authority since 1547.

## A promise narrowed as fast as it could be

The government spent the months after the manifesto writing its own
concessions back down to the narrowest terms it could defend, fencing
off large parts of the budget and the fundamental laws from the new
Duma's reach before it had even been elected. The retreat was real, and
so, afterward, was the limit: autocracy had conceded that it answered to
something beyond its own will, and no later government fully withdrew
that concession even while working constantly to minimise it.

## A decade of an uneasy compromise

For the following decade the Empire lived with an arrangement almost
nobody involved actually wanted: an emperor who still believed in his
own unlimited authority in principle, and a Duma whose existence
contradicted that belief in practice. The compromise held through
repeated dissolutions and reactionary interference, weakened but did not
break the state, and was still in place when a war with Germany and
Austria-Hungary began to strain it past what either side could manage.

## Pskov, March 1917

By March 1917 strikes and a mutinous Petrograd garrison had made the
capital ungovernable, and Nicholas's own generals told him plainly that
abdication was the only way to save the army's cohesion. Travelling
toward the capital, he found the rail line blocked and turned aside to
Pskov, where two delegates of the new Provisional Government met him
with the same request. He signed without real resistance on 15 March,
intending at first to pass the crown to his son and, once told the
boy's illness made that impractical, to his brother Michael instead.

<Figure id="ts-abdic" />

## A crown nobody would pick up

Nicholas still expected a Romanov to succeed him. Michael did not oblige.
Meeting with the Duma's leaders the following day, he declined to accept
the throne unless a future Constituent Assembly asked him to take it - a
condition that was never met. It was that refusal, not Nicholas's own
signature the day before, that left the monarchy with no one to hold it.
The dynasty did not fall to any army, foreign or domestic; it fell,
in the end, because the one man left with a claim to it chose not to
pick it up.
