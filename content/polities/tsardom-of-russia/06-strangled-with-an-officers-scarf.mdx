Paul had waited to be tsar since the age of ten, convinced the throne
was his by right and watching his mother rule for thirty-four years
without it ever coming to him. When his turn finally arrived in 1796
he was forty-four, and he spent his five years on the throne reversing
as much of Catherine's Russia as he could reach before someone reversed
him.

## A tyrant with a program

Paul was not simply erratic, though he could be: he dined in his crown,
told a Swedish envoy that no one in Russia mattered except whoever was
currently speaking to him, and forced the whole army into tight
Prussian uniforms with powdered pigtails and busbies down to the
buckles. Underneath the theatrics were real policies. At his own
coronation he signed a law fixing the order of succession by strict
primogeniture, ending the free-for-all Peter the Great's own
succession law had allowed; on the same day he limited a serf's
obligatory labor for his landlord to three days a week, with none on
Sundays - the first legal limit any tsar had placed on serfdom, and the
opening of a debate about the institution that would not close until
1861. He repealed Catherine's charter to the gentry and replaced their
local privileges with his own bureaucracy, earning the label of
Russia's first bureaucratic tsar.

His foreign policy swung just as hard. Having announced Russia's need
for peace after decades of war, Paul then joined Austria and England
against revolutionary France, sent his ablest general Suvorov - whom
he had first humiliated with exile for a minor breach of protocol - to
sweep the French from Italy and cross the Alps into Switzerland, and,
when the alliance soured, planned an invasion of British India with
Napoleon himself: a joint force marching through Central Asia on
instructions so vague that his own general was told simply to gather
what maps he could and improvise past Khiva.

## The conspiracy his own son knew about

Paul's domestic terror - officials dismissed without warning, critics
exiled to Siberia on a whim, married couples who slept arm in arm for
fear one would be taken in the night - alienated exactly the class he
depended on to rule. He handed his own security to Count Pahlen,
military governor of St. Petersburg, who was in fact the organizer of
the conspiracy against him; when Paul asked Pahlen point-blank whether
a plot existed, Pahlen answered that he "had all the threads of it in
his hands," which was true in a sense Paul never suspected. Paul's own
heir, Alexander, appears to have consented to his father's deposition
on condition his life be spared - a condition nobody enforced. On the
night of 23 March 1801, conspirators forced their way into Paul's
bedroom in his newly built palace, demanded his abdication, and killed
him in the struggle that followed: knocked down, then strangled with
an officer's scarf.

Alexander inherited a throne his own silence had helped clear for him,
and a reputation that would follow him for the rest of his reign: that
he had let his father die to get it.
