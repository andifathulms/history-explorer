Alexander spent his first four years as tsar trying to build the
constitutional, educated Russia his grandmother's own tutors had
taught him to imagine, and his next five losing that project to
Napoleon one battlefield at a time - a reign that opens in reform and
closes, before 1812 ever arrives, in a French alliance forced on him at
gunpoint rather than chosen.

## The Unofficial Committee

Alexander's earliest years brought a real, if narrow, wave of change.
He abolished torture by one of his first decrees, restructured Peter
the Great's old colleges into modern ministries with a single
responsible minister at the head of each, and poured money into
education on a scale no previous reign had matched: six new
universities founded, forty-two secondary schools established, the
1804 university statute built on real self-government. A small circle
of reform-minded friends, the Unofficial Committee, studied plans for
a constitution and concluded Russia needed an educated public before
one was practical - a conclusion that let the deeper political
question wait indefinitely. A law of 1803 let landowners free their
own serfs with land if they chose to; almost none did, but Alexander
kept a personal vow to grant no more crown peasants into private
serfdom, a smaller but real check on the institution's growth.

## The wars come anyway

Foreign affairs cut the reform program short before it went much
further. Napoleon's seizure and execution of the Duke of Enghien on
neutral territory outraged Alexander enough to put his own court into
mourning; when France pointedly reminded him that his own father's
assassins had never been punished, the relationship curdled for good.
Russia joined Austria, Sweden and Naples against France, and Alexander
personally rode out with his army in 1805 full of the confidence reform
had given him - straight into Napoleon's trap at Austerlitz, where the
allies mistook a staged retreat for a real one, attacked the ground
Napoleon had left open on purpose, and were cut to pieces. Alexander
sat on the ground after the defeat and wept: "we are babies in the
hands of a giant."

Prussia's own defeat at Jena and Auerstadt the following year left
Russia carrying the war alone against an enemy already inside Poland.
The armies fought each other to a bloody standstill at Eylau in a
snowstorm in February 1807, and Napoleon finally broke Bennigsen's
Russian army outright at Friedland that June. Alexander had nothing
left to fight with and no ally left to fight beside; he met Napoleon on
a raft in the middle of the Niemen at Tilsit and signed a peace that
made Russia, on paper, an ally of the empire that had just beaten it -
free to take Finland from Sweden and eye the Danubian provinces, bound
in exchange to enforce a blockade against British trade that Russia's
own economy could not actually afford to keep. It was a forced peace
both men knew would not last, and the five years it bought were spent,
on both sides, preparing for the war that came anyway.
