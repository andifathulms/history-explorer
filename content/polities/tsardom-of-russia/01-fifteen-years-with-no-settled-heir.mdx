The title Ivan IV assumed in 1547 outlived him by more than three
centuries, but the dynasty that first held it very nearly did not
survive his own children. Ivan's elder surviving son, Theodor, was
gentle and by most accounts unfit to govern; a younger son, Dmitri, died
as a child at Uglich in circumstances that were never fully settled to
everyone's satisfaction. When Theodor himself died childless in 1598,
the Rurikid line that had ruled Moscow since Daniel's own century ran
out entirely.

<Figure id="ts-dmitry" />

## A brother-in-law becomes Tsar

Power in Theodor's reign had already rested largely with his
brother-in-law, Boris Godunov, a capable administrator from a family
that had only recently risen to prominence among the old Muscovite
boyar houses - most of them, by the sources' own account, had never
heard of the Godunovs before the Oprichnina. On Theodor's death Boris
had himself elected Tsar rather than seize the office outright, but an
elected Tsar, with no dynastic claim of his own, gave every ambitious
rival exactly the opening Ivan IV's coronation had been designed to
close off.

<Figure id="ts-godunov" />

## The dead boy comes back, twice

The opening arrived in the shape of a man claiming to be the Tsarevich
Dmitri, alive after all and rightfully owed the throne Boris held. This
first pretender found real support - enough of it that he entered
Moscow and ruled briefly as Tsar before being killed in a palace
uprising. He was not the last: a second man later made the identical
claim, with the identical name, and gathered his own following in turn.
Between Boris's death in 1605 and 1613, Muscovy had no fewer than four
men wear or claim the crown, a Polish garrison occupying the Kremlin
itself, and, for stretches of those years, no functioning central
government at all.

<Figure id="ts-falsedm" />

## What the crisis actually settled

The fifteen years of chaos later took the name Smuta - the Time of
Troubles - and they answered, in the end, a question Ivan IV's own
coronation had never had to face: what happened to the office of Tsar
when there was no one left with an unambiguous hereditary right to it.
The answer, worked out amid war, famine and civil war rather than by
any settled law, is what the record's second chapter takes up.
