Alexander II inherited an empire whose army had just been humiliated in
the Crimean War and whose entire agricultural economy still rested on
the bonded labour of tens of millions of serfs. He chose to attack the
second problem directly, over the sustained resistance of the class
whose wealth depended on it.

<Figure id="ts-alex2" />

## Pushing a reform past its own committee

The drafting process took years and cost Alexander two chairmen of his
own reform commission - one worked himself into collapse and died still
urging his sovereign onward, and Alexander replaced the second, a
reluctant conservative, with his own brother specifically to force the
scheme through a resistant Main Committee. When a deputation of gentry
asked to argue against the draft in person, he had them turned away. "This
I desire, I demand, I command," he told the Council of State when it
threatened to stall.

## A decree read aloud in every church in the empire

The Emancipation edict was drafted by one official and rewritten by the
Metropolitan of Moscow himself, signed on 3 March 1861, and read out
that spring in every church across the empire. Tens of millions of
people who had been legally property became, at a stroke, legally free.

<Figure id="ts-manifest" />

## Freedom with a bill attached

The land settlement that came with emancipation left almost everyone
dissatisfied. Peasants received allotments too small to live on
independently and were required to pay for them over decades through
redemption payments that many never finished paying; landowners
considered themselves inadequately compensated for what they had lost.
Neither side's grievance was resolved by any of the further legislation
that followed through the 1860s and beyond. The reform that ended one
source of unrest - bonded labour itself - left behind a second one, land
hunger, that would outlast the tsar who signed the edict and, in time,
outlast the dynasty he belonged to.

## A door half-opened

Alexander's own reign showed the same pattern that would recur through
the rest of the empire's life: a state capable of forcing through
sweeping change from above, and consistently unable to make that change
land in a way that actually settled the grievance it addressed. The
emancipation of 1861 was the first and largest instance of a dynamic that
the twentieth century's opening years would repeat at greater cost.
