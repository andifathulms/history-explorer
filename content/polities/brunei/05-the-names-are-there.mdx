By the middle of the nineteenth century the sultanate still nominally reached
from Sarawak to Maludu Bay, and in Spenser St John's assessment it "possesses
no power, and exercises little influence over its dependencies."

St John was Britain's consul-general in Borneo and an ally of James Brooke, and
his account has to be read with that in front of you: he was describing a
government whose territory his friends and his country were in the process of
acquiring, and his description argues for the conclusion the acquisition
needed. Take the judgements sceptically. The detail is another matter, because
he lived there and counted things.

## The machinery, still turning

The structure was intact and entirely recognisable.

The sultan was not a despot and never had been: he had to consult his chief
officers on any great occasion, and important documents carried at least two of
their seals. Below him stood the four wazirs — the *bendahara* for home
affairs, the *di-gadong* for revenue and government stores, the *pemancha* who
also handled home affairs and could stand in for the bendahara, and the
*temenggong* who was supposed to guard the coast and lead expeditions. A fifth
officer, the shahbandar, handled commerce and the strangers in the port.

Each of the four great officers was entitled to eight assistants of noble
blood.

And there St John records the sultan saying something that does more work than
any of his own conclusions. The glory of Brunei — Darussalam, the Abode of
Peace — had departed, and he could find few men who cared to be promoted into
offices that brought "neither profit nor consideration."

*The names are there, but the reality is gone.*

## What was actually running the town

Real influence had moved sideways, to men the constitution did not account for.

There was a class of ministers drawn from the ranks of the people, chief among
them the *orang kaya di-gadong*. Little of importance was undertaken without
consulting them, because they were known to have a following; and at the death
of a sovereign their weight was decisive. St John thought that if they had ever
acted together they could have carried anything they wanted against any
opposition.

Meanwhile the nobility was poor to a degree he called almost inconceivable in a
rich country, and the outlying provinces were "one by one falling from them".

## How the poverty was paid for

This is the part of the chapter that is hard to read and should not be
softened.

Brunei held at least twenty-six thousand people, and about half of them
depended directly or indirectly on the nobles. Their followers were sent out to
collect from the villages, and would demand double what was owed so as to keep
the difference. Where a village could not pay, the children were taken. Where
the means were genuinely exhausted, the children were carried off into slavery.

St John names a man, Sirudin, who at one time brought back seventeen children
taken that way from the people of Tutong.

A structure of ranked offices, careful seals and consultative government was
still standing, and what it was doing at the far end of its authority was
selling children. Those are the same sentence. The second is what happens to a
state that keeps its constitution and loses its revenue, and it is the
condition Brunei was in when the men with the treaties arrived.
