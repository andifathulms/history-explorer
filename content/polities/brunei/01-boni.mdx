In 1408 a ruler of Boni — the name Chinese records give this polity — travelled
to Nanjing with his family and a large party, was received by the Yongle
emperor, fell ill, and died there. He was buried with imperial honours outside
the city, and his tomb is still there.

Three years later Parameswara of Melaka did almost exactly the same thing, and
came home with a seal, robes, a patent of appointment as king, and a Ming
guarantee against Ayutthaya that turned a new settlement into a polity other
powers had to deal with.

Brunei's ruler got a funeral.

## The difference between a visit and a grant

That is not a trivial distinction and it is worth being exact about, because
the two journeys look identical in outline.

What is recorded of the Boni ruler's visit is a tribute mission, a reception,
and a death. What is recorded of Parameswara's is an instrument he brought back
and used. A ruler who returns holding a seal from an emperor can put it in
front of his neighbours; a ruler who dies honourably abroad leaves his heirs a
story.

Aceh made the same kind of approach to the Ottoman sultan in the 1560s and got
cannon — which turned out to be more useful than a seal and was not the thing
it had asked for. Asking is not receiving, and being received is not being
authorised.

## The king list

Brunei's own genealogies begin the sultanate in 1368, with the accession of
Muhammad Shah, the ruler traditionally said to have converted.

Saunders is careful that the early list is a claim rather than a chronology.
Chinese records have Boni sending missions from the tenth century; the
genealogies compress, rearrange and Islamise the early sequence in the way
dynastic genealogies do; and the two bodies of evidence cannot be lined up name
by name.

So 1368 is not the date Brunei began. It is the date Brunei says it began —
which is a claim about identity rather than about chronology, and a perfectly
real thing to record as long as it is recorded as that.
