In 1408 a ruler of Boni — the name Chinese records give this polity — travelled to
Nanjing with his family and a large party, was received by the Yongle emperor,
fell ill, and died there. He was buried with imperial honours outside the city,
and his tomb is still there.

This corpus's Melaka record's second chapter describes Parameswara doing almost
exactly the same thing three years later: sailing to the Ming court in person,
being received, and coming home with a seal, robes and a stone tablet. Melaka's
`legitimation` carries `investiture` for it, and that record argues the value was
right to be general because a Chinese emperor granting recognition is the same
transaction as a caliph granting it.

Brunei's `legitimation` does not carry the value, and the difference is worth
being exact about.

## Why not

What is recorded of the Boni ruler's visit is a tribute mission, a reception, and
a death. Parameswara came back with an instrument — a seal, a patent of
appointment as king, and a guarantee against Ayutthaya that this site's Melaka
record treats as the thing that made a new settlement into a polity other powers
had to deal with. Coding rule 8 asks for a grant of authority publicly asserted
by the recipient, and the asserting is the part Brunei's record cannot show.

It is the same call this corpus's Aceh record makes about the Ottoman embassy of
the 1560s: the best-fitting request for `investiture` anywhere in the region, and
what came back was cannon. Asking is not receiving, and receiving hospitality is
not receiving authority.

Three records in the archipelago now sit on the same distinction, and only one of
them carries the value.

## The king list

`span.start` on this record reads 1368, the accession of Muhammad Shah in the
Brunei genealogies — the first ruler this sultanate counts as its own, and the
one traditionally said to have converted.

Saunders is careful that the early list is a claim rather than a chronology, and
this record follows him. Chinese records mention Boni sending missions from the
tenth century; the genealogies compress, rearrange and Islamise the early
sequence in the way dynastic genealogies do; and the two bodies of evidence
cannot be lined up name by name.

What the date says is not "Brunei began in 1368". It is "this is where the
sultanate says it began", stated as such — which is a different position from the
one this site takes on Ternate and Tidore, where the traditions are left off and
the span starts at documentation, and the same position it takes on the Sunda
record's traditional date of 669.

Three positions on three king lists in one region. What makes that defensible
rather than arbitrary is that each record says which one it took and why.
