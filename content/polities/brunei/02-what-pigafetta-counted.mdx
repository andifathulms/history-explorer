The survivors of Magellan's expedition came to Brunei from Tidore in 1521, and
Antonio Pigafetta wrote down what he saw: a city of some twenty-five thousand
households built out over the water, elephants in the ruler's train, brass
artillery on the walls, porcelain and silk in quantity, and a court that
received them with more ceremony than they had encountered anywhere.

Nothing happened in 1521. A ship's company that had lost its captain in the
Philippines put in on its way home, stayed a while, and one of them wrote it
down.

That is worth a date anyway. Brunei's own archive is thin and its genealogies
are a claim rather than a chronology; Pigafetta is an outsider with no stake,
writing close to the event, describing what he could see. Twenty-five thousand
households on stilts over the water, brass guns on the walls, elephants in the
ruler's train, porcelain and silk in quantity, and a reception more elaborate
than anything the expedition had met between Seville and Sulawesi.

For a polity known mostly from later court tradition, a stranger's inventory is
the firmest evidence there is.

## What the numbers are worth

Pigafetta counted households, not people, and he was estimating from a boat.

Even discounted heavily the figure describes a city of a size that European
visitors of the period did not expect and generally did not find. The brass
artillery is the detail that tends to get skipped: Brunei was casting or buying
guns and mounting them on its walls a generation before the Portuguese reached
Borneo in force, which is the same story Aceh, Patani and Makassar tell later
in the century.

The expedition sailed on and Brunei went back to being documented by almost
nobody for another fifty years.
