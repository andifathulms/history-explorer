The survivors of Magellan's expedition came to Brunei from Tidore in 1521, and
Antonio Pigafetta wrote down what he saw: a city of some twenty-five thousand
households built out over the water, elephants in the ruler's train, brass
artillery on the walls, porcelain and silk in quantity, and a court that received
them with more ceremony than they had encountered anywhere.

The turning point on this record is typed `reform`, and nothing was reformed in
1521.

This site's Pasai record makes the same wrong choice for Ibn Battuta's fortnight
in 1345 and explains it: the nine turning-point types can hold a battle, a siege,
a treaty, a revolt, a succession crisis, a conversion, a capital move, a
catastrophe and a reform, and there is no value for *a description of what was
already there*. For a polity whose own archive is thin, an outsider's dated
account is often the single most valuable piece of evidence about it, and a
turning-point list that could only hold events would leave it off.

Two records now carry that mismatch, both for the same reason, and both visits
were made by men travelling with somebody else's expedition. It is worth
recording as a pattern rather than as two accidents — and the pattern is a small
argument for a tenth type that this site has not made and is not making here.

## What the king list is worth

`span.start` reads 1368, the accession of Muhammad Shah in the Brunei
genealogies.

Saunders is careful that the early list is a claim rather than a chronology, and
this record follows him: the sultanate's own account of its beginnings was
written down much later, and it does the work such accounts usually do. The date
is on the record because it is the sultanate's own and it is stated to be that.

It is the third position this site has taken on a Southeast Asian king list in
one region. Ternate and Tidore start where documentation begins and leave the
traditions off. Sunda starts at a traditional date and says so. Brunei starts at
a traditional date because the tradition is itself the thing being dated — the
point at which this polity says it became what it is.
