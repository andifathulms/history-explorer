For most of its long decline, Brunei was a colonial afterthought too small
and too poor to be worth fighting over - which is precisely why it kept
being given away. Oil changed the arithmetic. On 5 April 1929 a well at
Seria began producing, and within a decade Brunei had gone from a
negligible strip of coast to the third-largest oil producer in the
British Commonwealth; by 1950 Seria itself was the largest single field
the Commonwealth had.

## What the money bought before independence ever came up

The wealth arrived early enough to change how Britain treated Brunei
rather than only how Brunei lived. A 1906 treaty, agreed before Seria had
produced a drop, already guaranteed that no further Bruneian territory
would be ceded to neighbouring Sarawak or North Borneo - the piecemeal
disposal that had cost Brunei most of its old reach was, at last,
formally stopped. Oil revenue then gave the sultanate the one thing every
earlier decade of being signed away had denied it: enough independent
weight that its neighbours' governments, and eventually its own
population, had a reason to fight over what stayed inside its borders
rather than simply carve more of them away.

## An armed rejection of the one federation that might have absorbed it

That new leverage showed itself starkly in December 1962, when the Brunei
People's Party rose in armed rebellion and seized much of the state,
including the Seria oilfield itself, before British troops put the revolt
down. The rebellion's own politics were entangled with a bigger question
Sultan Omar Ali Saifuddin III was already wrestling with: whether Brunei
should join the new Federation of Malaysia then being assembled around
it. He had first welcomed the proposal, then rejected it once it became
clear Malaya expected a substantial share of Brunei's own oil revenue in
return - the one issue on which a small, oil-rich sultanate had genuine
room to refuse a much larger neighbour, and did.

## Waiting for the right moment to be alone

Malaysia formed without Brunei in 1963, leaving the sultanate a British
protectorate rather than a federation member, and it stayed that way for
two more decades while the Sultan judged the regional security climate
too unsettled to risk full independence outright. Indonesia's 1975
annexation of East Timor was one of the events that shaped that
caution; so was Malaysia's own eventual decision to stop backing the
Sultanate's internal opponents. Only once those pressures had eased did
Brunei conclude, on 1 January 1984, the independence its oil wealth had
been quietly making possible for half a century - a state that kept its
name, its dynasty, and this time kept the deciding word over what
happened to what was left of it.
