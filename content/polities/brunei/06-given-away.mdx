Brunei at its height under Bolkiah, around 1500, claimed most of the coastline
of Borneo — an island the size of France and Germany together — and had
installed a related Muslim ruler at Manila.

Spain took Manila bay in 1571, attacked Brunei itself in 1578 and held the town
for a few weeks before disease drove the force out. That decade cost Brunei the
northern half of its reach. What was left, it kept for nearly three hundred
more years, and then gave away.

In 1841 the sultan granted Sarawak to James Brooke, an English adventurer who
had helped put down a rebellion there.
Brooke's family ruled it as a private
kingdom until 1946.

## An ending with no battle in it

Most polities end in a way you can put a date and a place on. Brunei's was
delivered piecemeal, by signature, over fifty years, to two chartered companies
and one private Englishman.

There is no siege to describe and no conquest to date. Brooke was granted
Sarawak for services rendered against a rebellion, and his family kept it as a
private kingdom for a century. North Borneo went to a company in 1881. The 1888
protectorate covered the remainder, which by then was a strip of coast between
two territories that had been given away.

What makes it strange is that every one of those transfers was a legal act
performed by the sultan. Nobody took Brunei. Brunei was signed away in pieces
by the people who held it, under pressure, for consideration, over two
generations.

## And the state is still there

Brunei exists. The dynasty that founded the sultanate is on the throne now,
which makes it one of the longest continuously reigning houses anywhere.

This account stops in 1888 on a simple test: a polity disposes of its own
external relations, and a protectorate does not. What Brunei recovered in 1984
is a different kind of object from the sultanate described here — a modern
state with the same dynasty and the same name, which is not nothing, and is not
the same thing.
