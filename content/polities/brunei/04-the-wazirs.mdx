Nobody can state Brunei's rule of succession, and it is not because the sources
are silent. It is because the office of sultan was one part of a structure, and
what the sources describe is the structure.

Brunei was governed by ranked noble houses. Four great officers — the *wazirs*
— stood between the sultan and everything else: the Pengiran Bendahara, the
Pengiran Di-Gadong, the Pengiran Pemancha, the Pengiran Temenggong. Below them
a graded nobility of *pengiran* held rights over named districts and over the
people in them, transmitted by inheritance.

The sultan came from the ruling house. Which member, and whether he was
replaced, was worked out among the wazirs and the senior pengiran, and the
record shows contested successions, depositions and periods of two claimants.

## The same office, elsewhere

The senior wazir's title is *bendahara*, and the word turns up across the Malay
world doing the same work.

At Melaka the bendahara was held for most of the fifteenth century by a single
family, and the *Malay Annals* show him choosing between claimants and
restraining rulers — until a sultan had him executed on a bribed accusation and
took the state's ballast out with him. At Johor in 1699 the bendahara had the
sultan killed on his way to the mosque and took the throne himself, ending the
Melaka royal line.

Three sultanates, three bendaharas, and in each case the thing that actually
decided who ruled was not a rule about ruling.

That is the point worth carrying away. A Malay sultanate of this kind is not a
monarchy with ministers attached. It is an arrangement among houses in which
one house supplies the ruler, and the others decide which member and for how
long. Asking what its law of succession was is asking a question in the wrong
shape — the honest answer is that the succession was an outcome of the
arrangement, and the arrangement was the constitution.
