Turanshah took Aden, and then had to decide where to govern from.

He had come down from Egypt, broken the power that held the coastal plain, and
extracted from its ruler both a great deal of money and whatever information he
wanted before carrying him along to Aden and taking the port. He settled first at
Zabid, which was the obvious choice: it had been the capital of the lowland for
three and a half centuries.

He formed an unfavourable opinion of its air.

So he went up into the mountains with his physicians to look for somewhere with
a healthy atmosphere and good water, and their choice fell on a place called
Ta'izz, where he founded a city.

That is where the customs of Aden went. Ta'izz became the seat of government for
Turanshah, for the Ayyubids who followed him in Yemen, and then for their own
freedmen the Banu Rasul, who ruled from it for two and a quarter centuries. The
richest transit harbour in the Indian Ocean spent the next three hundred years
paying for a court in a highland town that had been picked out by doctors on
health grounds.

## What ended, in one sentence

The chronicler who records this puts a line under the whole period, and it is
worth quoting because it is not the line a modern account would draw.

With the fall of the last of the lowland dynasties, he writes, Arab sovereignty
came to an end in Yemen, and supreme rule was thereafter held by the Ghuzz — the
Turks — and by their freedmen.

That is a South Arabian historian, writing in the fourteenth century, treating
1173 as the year Yemen stopped governing itself. Everything from the Ayyubids
onward is, in his reckoning, foreign rule by Turks and by the slaves of Turks;
and the last of the native dynasties to hold real power were the Zurayids of
Aden and the men who had just destroyed them.

## The branch that had nowhere to go

The other thing that ended was a religious allegiance, and this one left no
successor at all.

The Zurayids had taken the office of chief missionary for the Cairo caliphs —
handed to them, in fact, by Queen Arwa herself when she went the other way, with
a remark about the Sulayhids knowing well enough what had become of the hidden
imam. So Aden was the seat of the Isma'ili obedience that recognised the actual,
living, reigning Fatimid caliph, against a Yemeni highland mission that
recognised an infant who had disappeared.

Then Saladin abolished the Fatimid caliphate in Cairo, and Saladin's brother took
Aden. Within two years the branch that had backed the caliph who existed had no
caliph and no state, and the branch that had backed the child nobody could
produce had neither of those things and did not need them.

One of those communities is still here. It is not the one that was right about
where power was.
