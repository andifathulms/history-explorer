Aden did not become independent in a year. It became independent in four
instalments, each one a smaller fraction of the same tax, negotiated with the
same creditor.

The creditor was Arwa. When Ali al-Sulayhi married his son to her in 1069 he
settled the city of Aden and its district on her as her dowry, and from then on
the revenue of the port was hers personally rather than the state's. It came to
about a hundred thousand dinars a year — sometimes rather more, sometimes rather
less.

At that point Aden was held for the Sulayhids by a family called the Banu Ma'n,
who had taken it along with Lahj, Abyan, Shihr and Hadramawt before the
Sulayhids arrived and had been left in place as governors. They paid the queen
her tribute as long as Ali al-Sulayhi was alive. When he was killed on the
pilgrim road they declared themselves independent.

## The two brothers

Al-Mukarram marched down, took the city, and ended the Banu Ma'n. Then he did
something that shaped everything afterwards: he did not appoint a governor. He
appointed two, and divided the port between them.

Al-Abbas ibn al-Karam was given Ta'kar-Aden, the fortress next to the city gate,
with authority over the trade coming in by land. His brother Mas'ud was given
al-Khadra, the castle commanding the water, with authority over the coast and
the shipping and the command of the city itself.

Both were made to swear to the queen, because the revenue they were now
collecting was her dowry.

The arrangement is worth pausing on, because it is the reason there was a
Zurayid dynasty at all rather than a Zurayid governor. Aden's income divided
naturally into a land half and a sea half, and the two halves were given to two
men of one family who did not answer to each other. It made the port stable and
it made it permanently two things, and a hundred years later a war between the
two halves would begin with a dispute about how the revenue was being split.

## Four instalments

The default came in stages, and each stage waited for a funeral.

While al-Abbas and Mas'ud lived, they paid the queen in full. When they were
dead, their sons declared themselves independent at Aden. The queen's general
marched on them; there was a war; it ended in a treaty under which **half** the
revenue would go to her.

When that general died, the princes of Aden stopped paying the half. Another
Sulayhid was sent down and made a new agreement, for **a quarter**.

Then a rebellion broke out elsewhere in the highlands, and the princes of Aden
stopped paying again. This time nobody came. The queen's leading men were dead
by then, and the officer Cairo had sent to reorganise her government, Umara
notes with what looks like contempt, made no attempt to do anything about it.

From full revenue to half to a quarter to nothing, in three renegotiations, each
one conducted on the death of whoever had enforced the last. No independence was
ever declared and no rebellion succeeded. The tax simply got smaller until it
stopped, and a farm of the customs became a state.
