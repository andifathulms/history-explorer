title: The branch that lost

In 1130 the Fatimid caliph al-Amir was murdered in Cairo and the Isma'ili world
split over who had succeeded him. One party recognised the cousin who took the
throne in Cairo, al-Hafiz, and the line of caliphs after him. The other held
that al-Amir's infant son al-Tayyib was the true imam, that he had gone into
concealment, and that the community should be led in his absence by a chief
missionary rather than by whoever sat in Cairo.

Yemen decided both ways, and the line ran between the highlands and the coast.
Queen Arwa, from Jibla, took the Tayyibi position and appointed the first of the
chief missionaries who have continued the line to the present day; the Sulayhid
page tells that half. The Zurayids in Aden recognised al-Hafiz.

It is easy to read this as the port choosing the side its trade lay on, and that
reading is available: Aden's business was with Egypt, and the Fatimid state was
the customer. It is also insufficient. The Hafizi position was the
institutionally orthodox one — the caliph in Cairo was the caliph in Cairo — and
the Zurayids were recognising the constituted authority of the mission they
belonged to, which is what this record's `caliphal-investiture` coding means
here.

The outcome is what makes the choice worth a chapter. Hafizi Isma'ilism did not
survive the fall of the Fatimids by very long and has no community today. The
Tayyibi line Arwa chose became the Bohra communities of Gujarat and is alive.
Two polities ninety kilometres apart made opposite decisions about the same
question in the same decade, and one of them is still being lived in.
