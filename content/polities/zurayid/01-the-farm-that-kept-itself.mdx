---
title: The farm that kept itself
drafted_from: kay-1892
phase: formation
---

Ali al-Sulayhi, having taken most of Yemen for the Fatimid mission, needed
someone to hold Aden and remit its revenue. Around 1080 the arrangement settled
on two cousins of the Banu Zuray', a family of the Hamdani tribe of Yam, who
held the port and its customs between them and paid an annual sum up to the
Sulayhid court in the highlands.

This was a tax farm, not a principality. The distinction matters for what
follows, because the Zurayids did not rebel, secede or conquer anything. They
went on collecting, and the state above them fell apart around them, and at some
point the payment stopped going up the mountain and nobody was in a position to
come and ask for it. This record dates the polity from the appointment rather
than from any declaration, because there was no declaration to date it from.

The edge to the Sulayhid page is therefore `partitioned from`. That is the
weakest and most ambiguous of the eight types in this vocabulary, and it is the
correct one here: what the Zurayids acquired was not independence granted or
seized but a piece of a state that had ceased to be able to hold its pieces
together. Queen Arwa's authority contracted to Jibla and its district; Sanaa
went to the Hamdanid sultans; Aden stayed with the men who were already in it.

What made Aden worth the trouble is the subject of the next chapter, and it is
the reason this small dynasty of tax farmers ended up mattering more than the
highland state it came out of.
