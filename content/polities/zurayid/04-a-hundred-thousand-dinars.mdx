In 1152 the ruler of Aden bought a kingdom.

The Sulayhid state had been left by Queen Arwa to a Himyarite lord named Mansur
ibn al-Mufaddal, who had no use for most of it. The Da'i Muhammad ibn Saba
purchased from him the whole of what had belonged to the Sulayhids: twenty-eight
castles and cities, Jibla and Dhu Ashraq and Ibb among them, for a hundred
thousand dinars.

That figure is worth setting against the other one. A hundred thousand dinars was
roughly what Aden's customs brought in for a single year when they were being
paid to the queen as her dowry. The highland core of the state that had once
owned Aden — its capital, its fortresses, its towns — was bought by the port for
about twelve months of the port's own revenue.

Mansur retired to his two fortresses at Sabir and Ta'izz, and divorced his wife.
She was called Arwa, after the queen, and was a Sulayhid herself. Muhammad ibn
Saba went up into the highlands, made Jibla his residence — the town Arwa had
governed from for fifty years — and married the woman Mansur had put away.

The poets, Umara says, outdid themselves on the subject of the fortresses.

## What the money was for

There is a temptation to read a purchase like this as decadence, and the man who
made it does not support the reading. Umara knew him personally and describes
somebody entirely deliberate.

He was generous and liked to be praised for it, and was a skilful improviser of
verse himself. He kept a court of jurists, judges, poets, a physician who was
also an astrologer. On a feast day outside al-Juwwah, Umara saw him sitting in
full sun with thirty poets pressing round him, and heard him say: tell them to
stop crowding, and say it loudly, because I am not moving from this spot until
every one of them has finished. He paid all thirty.

Another night at the same palace, with ten poems in front of him, he asked his
courtiers what the authors should get. Nobody suggested more than a hundred
dinars. Make it three hundred, he said. It is little enough.

None of that is idleness. A dynasty of tax-farmers that had defaulted its way
into sovereignty had no lineage worth the name and no religious authority of its
own, and what it had instead was money and the sense to spend it on the people
who decided what was said about you.

## Umara steals the prize

The most human scene Umara records anywhere is at that court, and he is the
villain of it.

At the palace of al-Hajr, in a garden, with several poets present, the ruler
improvised two lines in an unusual metre and offered the robes he was wearing
and the money on his person to whoever completed them first. The poets were
slow. The chief judge of al-Janad, a famous extemporiser, got there first — and
was sitting next to Umara.

Umara took the paper out of his hand, got the words ready on his lips, stood up,
and recited them.

He won the money and the robes. He also wrote it down, decades later, in his own
history, in a sentence he plainly liked: *his shot hit the mark, but I had
purloined the arrow.*

The dynasty that could buy a kingdom for cash also produced a court where the
best-documented theft is of two lines of verse — and the man who committed it is
the reason any of this is on record.
