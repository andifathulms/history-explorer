The Croats trace their own arrival in the region that still bears
their name not to the Illyrians who lived there under Roman rule, but
to a Slavic tribe that followed in the wake of the Lombards and
Avars. Seton-Watson records the tradition that the Chrobati were
invited into Illyria by the Byzantine emperor Heraclius around 634
specifically to drive the Avars out - and that, having done the job,
they stayed on for generations as nominal vassals of Constantinople
rather than moving on. A kindred Slavic people, the Serbs, settled at
the same time farther east, taking the territory that would become
Serbia, Bosnia and Montenegro. Along the Dalmatian coast, meanwhile,
Roman institutions and culture survived in the old maritime towns
largely because Venice's own rise as a sea power kept the eastern
Adriatic from drifting entirely out of the western world's orbit -
without it, Seton-Watson judges, Rome would likely have lost the
coast altogether once Byzantine authority there grew too shadowy to
matter.

## A liturgy Rome could not suppress

The defining tension of the Croats' own early history, in
Seton-Watson's reading, was the pull between Latin and Byzantine
culture - a division sharpened rather than resolved by the mission of
Cyril and Methodius among the Slavs in the ninth century. Rome
recognized their introduction of a Slavonic liturgy at first, then
revoked the concession after Methodius's own death; but the practice
never fully disappeared. Seton-Watson notes that the Glagolitic rite,
built on that same Slavonic liturgy, was still in active use in
several hundred parishes across Dalmatia, Istria and southern
Croatia when the nineteenth-century national revival rediscovered it
- a thousand years of intermittent opposition from Rome that had
never quite managed to extirpate it.
