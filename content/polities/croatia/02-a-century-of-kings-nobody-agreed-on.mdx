Duke Tomislav's assumption of the royal title, some time around 924
or 925, is one of the few facts about tenth-century Croatia that
sources even attempt to date - and Fine is careful to note that even
this rests on a single, disputed letter attributed to Pope John X. What
came after is worse. Tomislav died around 928, and the sources that
try to list his successors contradict each other completely: different
chroniclers give different names, different numbers of intervening
rulers, and different dates, to the point that Fine judges the entire
question unresolvable and, more importantly, beside the point - a
corrected list of names would still tell nothing about what any of
these rulers actually did, since nothing else about them survives.

## A kingmaker, a civil war, and pirates who never really stopped

The one episode that does emerge clearly from the wreckage is a civil
war in 949, in which the nobleman Pribina - ban of three districts and,
by Fine's account, probably the strongest single military power left in
the kingdom - overthrew and killed a ruler named Miroslav and put
Miroslav's brother Kresimir II on the throne in his place. The unrest
that followed let peripheral territories slip away: the Neretljani of the
Dalmatian coast, never fully subdued to begin with, returned to piracy,
and much of Bosnia was lost to a revived Serbia under Časlav. Croatia
clawed some of it back once Časlav died around 960 and Serbia fell
into its own disorder, letting Kresimir recover western Bosnia - proof,
if nothing else, that the kingdom Tomislav had built could survive a
civil war and a lost province and still pull itself back together.

## Byzantine favor, a divided inheritance, and a Venetian hostage

Kresimir II's successor Stjepan Drzislav (969-97) went further,
securing Byzantine recognition as head of the Dalmatian theme - a
formal acknowledgment of exactly the kind of regional standing
Tomislav's own generation had only claimed. It did not survive his
death. Drzislav left his lands divided among three sons, and the
division produced the same result a divided inheritance produced
almost everywhere in this period: civil war, with Venice intervening
against the eldest, Svetoslav, who ended up exiled to Hungary while
his younger brothers Kresimir III and Gojislav split what remained.
Kresimir III, caught between a hostile Venice and a nephew backed by
Hungary, chose Byzantine protection instead - formally submitting to
Basil II and receiving a patrician's title - and held on as a Byzantine
client until the emperor's death in 1025 let him drop the pretense of
submission and rule as an independent king again in fact as well as
name.

## Two reigns that quietly rebuilt what a century of chaos had cost

His son Stjepan I (1030-58) used that independence to expand rather
than merely defend: a larger navy, real successes against both Venice
and Byzantium, and the annexation of Carinthia in the northwest.
Stjepan's own son, Petar Kresimir IV (1058-74/75), inherited a kingdom
strong enough that Byzantium, increasingly distracted by the Seljuks in
the east and the Normans in Italy, chose to court Croatia rather than
contest it: in 1069 the empire formally made Kresimir its own
representative over the Dalmatian theme, letting the existing Byzantine
officials in cities like Zadar keep their posts while now nominally
answering to him. It was the closest a Croatian king had come to
uncontested regional supremacy since Tomislav's own reign a century
and a half earlier - and it was under this same Kresimir that a young
autonomous ruler of Slavonia named Zvonimir, son-in-law of the
Hungarian king, agreed to bring his own territory under Croatian
suzerainty in exchange for local independence, a leading role in state
affairs, and the throne itself should the childless Kresimir die without
an heir.
