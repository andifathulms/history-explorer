By the early tenth century the Croats felt secure enough in their own
position - remote enough, or simply confident enough after
Charlemagne's own conquests had shown them they had little to fear
from Constantinople - to take a step their earlier dukes had not:
Duke Tomislav assumed the royal title outright. A council held at
Split in 914, in the presence of the papal legate representing Pope
John X, already records him bearing the title Chroatorum Rex. The
choice of overlord mattered: Seton-Watson notes that Tomislav
recognized the suzerainty of the Papal See rather than either empire
pressing on Croatia's own borders, doing three generations earlier
what King Stephen of Hungary would later do for his own kingdom.

## Zvonimir's crown, and its limits

The kingship Tomislav had founded reached what Seton-Watson calls its
most famous moment under one of his successors, Zvonimir, who in 1076
received his own crown at Split directly from the hands of the Legate
of Pope Gregory VII - a second papal coronation confirming the same
choice of allegiance Tomislav had made a century and a half before.
But the achievement proved personal rather than institutional.
"Zvonimir's greatness died with him," Seton-Watson writes bluntly,
and no comparable king followed to carry the kingdom's own standing
forward. Zvonimir left no son, and the native royal line he had
crowned himself the most celebrated member of simply ran out - leaving
Croatia's own future to be decided not by another coronation, but by
the civil war that followed a dynasty's extinction.
