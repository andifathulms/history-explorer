A dozen years of Hungarian intervention, a brief and then extinguished
native kingship under Peter, and the loss of Biograd had left Croatia
without a king of its own by the time Coloman moved to settle the
matter for good. In 1102 the Croat nobles resolved it by recognizing
Coloman, King of Hungary, as their own sovereign. Coloman did not
rest his claim on a single ground. Seton-Watson records him asserting "the triple claims of
conquest, inheritance and election" together - his own armies had
already completed work his uncle Ladislas I had left unfinished, the
widowed queen of the childless Zvonimir had been a Hungarian princess
giving Coloman a hereditary thread to pull on, and the nobles'
recognition itself supplied the third leg the other two lacked on
their own. Coloman sealed the whole arrangement with a coronation at
Biograd and a new title that named all three of his crowns at once:
Rex Hungariae, Croatiae atque Dalmatiae.

## A union that respected what it absorbed

What followed was not, in Seton-Watson's own account, an outright
annexation. He is careful to note that the precise relationship
between Hungary and Croatia was not yet clearly defined under
Coloman himself, but that the king - following what Seton-Watson
calls the tolerant traditions of the House of Árpád - respected
Croatia's own privileges and independent standing rather than folding
the kingdom directly into Hungary's own administration. It was a
personal union built on a claim assembled from three different kinds
of legitimacy at once, and it proved durable enough to outlast the
native Croatian kingship it replaced by many centuries - even as the
crown Tomislav had first taken from a papal legate in 914, and
Zvonimir had worn again in 1076, passed for good into a Hungarian
king's own hands.
