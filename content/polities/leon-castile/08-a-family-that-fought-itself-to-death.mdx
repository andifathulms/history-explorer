Pedro I's reign turned violent from close to its own beginning. His
marriage to Blanche of Bourbon lasted barely days before he returned
to his mistress María de Padilla, and two bishops he found willing to
declare the marriage null could not stop the pope's own continued
pressure to take Blanche back. Pedro answered pressure of every kind
the same way: when his half-brothers moved against him, he gave
himself up, in his own chronicler's words, to "a riot of
assassinations," killing three of them along with several of their own
kinsmen before a peace was signed in 1361.

## A crown claimed by the brother who survived

Henry of Trastámara, the ablest of the surviving half-brothers, had
by 1363 allied with Pedro IV of Aragon - a king already engaged in his
own struggle against Castile and glad of a pretext to damage a
neighbor he distrusted. That year Henry first set up a formal claim to
the Castilian crown itself. To press it, the allies hired the White
Companies, mercenary veterans of the French wars looking for new
paymasters now that peace in France had made them unwelcome guests
there; under the French knight Bertrand du Guesclin, funded jointly by
the Avignon pope and Pedro IV, the Companies swept most of Castile
into Henry's hands by 1366, and Henry had himself crowned king.

## Nájera, and the vengeance that undid it

Pedro turned to England, then still holding much of western France,
and secured an army under Edward, the Black Prince, in exchange for
concessions he had promised. At Nájera in April 1367 the Black
Prince's army broke Henry's, and Henry fled again to France. What
Pedro did with the victory cost him the alliance that had won it: his
vengeance on his enemies was severe enough to disgust the English
commander outright, and Pedro compounded the offense by failing to
honor the concessions that had bought English help in the first place.
The English withdrew, a fresh rising broke out in Castile, and Henry
returned once more with a new army. Pedro was crushed at Montiel in
1369, besieged in the castle he fled to, and taken by a trick. The two
half-brothers finished the war the way it had been fought throughout -
in person, hand to hand - and it was Henry, helped at the decisive
moment by one of his own partisans, who killed Pedro and ended a
reign both sides had spent a decade calling cruel.
