The succession to Castile's throne in the 1460s and 1470s was a
genuinely open question, tangled in accusations about the legitimacy
of King Henry IV's own daughter Juana, whom much of the nobility
suspected was not his child at all. Henry's sister Isabella had at
various points been recognized as heir and then set aside again as
the political wind shifted, and when it came time for her to marry,
Henry IV pressed a match with the king of Portugal.

## A choice made against the crown's own wishes

Isabella rejected that proposal. In 1469 she married Ferdinand, heir
to the throne of neighbouring Aragon, without her brother's approval -
a decision that would matter far more than any single marriage
usually does. When Henry IV died in 1474 without resolving the
succession, Isabella was proclaimed queen, and much of the nobility
that had supported Juana's claim, along with the king of Portugal
himself, went to war against her instead. Isabella's forces won, and
by a 1479 treaty she was confirmed as queen of Castile while the
unfortunate Juana withdrew to a convent.

## Two crowns, one household, no single kingdom

That same year, Ferdinand inherited the throne of Aragon on his own
father's death. For the first time, a political union joined the
greater part of Christian Spain - Castile and Aragon together under
one royal household. It was not a merger: Castile and Aragon kept
their own separate laws, their own separate Cortes, and their own
separate administrations, bound together only by the fact that the
same two people now wore both crowns. What that union would go on to
accomplish, starting with the one piece of the peninsula still outside
either crown's reach, unfolded over the following thirteen years.
