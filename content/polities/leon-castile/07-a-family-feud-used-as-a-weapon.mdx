By the 1480s the kingdom of Granada, the last Muslim state left in
Spain after two and a half centuries of independent survival, was
tearing itself apart from within. The emir Abul Hassan, consumed by
his own infatuation with a slave concubine named Zoraya, faced open
warfare from his brother El Zagal and his son Boabdil, each heading
rival factions against him and against each other. War with Castile,
renewed in 1481, gave Ferdinand every reason to let that internal
chaos work in his favour rather than settle it by force alone.

## Treachery as a tool of statecraft

Ferdinand took Boabdil prisoner twice during the war and released him
both times - not from mercy, but to set him loose against El Zagal, who
had by then become emir in his own right, all while making promises of
peace he had no intention of keeping. El Zagal was deceived by similar
means. A decade of this combination of open war and deliberate
manipulation of Granada's own succession crisis let Ferdinand enter
the Granadine plain and lay siege to the city itself, defended with
real courage by Boabdil and what remained of his following.

## The Alhambra changes hands

The siege camp of Santa Fe was built to sustain the effort through
the winter, and after months of fighting the defenders, worn down by
hunger rather than beaten in open battle, were forced to capitulate.
On 2 January 1492, Castilian troops occupied the Alhambra. Boabdil and
his household left for North Africa not long after - and later legend,
which turned his departure into a tale of a coward's tears looking
back at the city he had lost, has little basis in what the historical
record actually shows about him. What ended that January was not
simply one dynasty's rule over one city: it was the last independent
Muslim government anywhere in a peninsula the Christian kingdoms
descended from Asturias had been fighting to reclaim, piece by piece,
for very nearly six hundred years.
