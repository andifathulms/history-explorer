Ferdinand III came to the Castilian throne in 1217 and spent his
early years, like nearly every king before him, fighting his own
father and his own nobles before he could turn south. By 1225 those
domestic wars were settled, and the campaigns that followed would
prove more consequential than any Castilian king's since Alfonso VI.
Córdoba fell in 1236, Murcia became tributary in 1241, and the reign's
culminating achievement came with the surrender of Seville itself in
1248.

## A crusader who allied with the enemy he was crusading against

Ferdinand's own piety was genuine enough to earn him sainthood after
his death, but it never hardened into the uncompromising intolerance
later Christian-Muslim conflict would show. He allied at one point
with the Almohad emperor, restoring him to his own African throne, and
accepted the alliance of Granada's Muslim ruler during the campaign
against Seville. When Louis IX of France invited him to join a crusade
in the East, Ferdinand is said to have declined with a line that
summed up his whole approach to holy war: "There is no lack of Moors
in my land." His Christianity, in other words, stayed practical and
resolutely local.

## A union that finally held

The single external event of Ferdinand's reign that mattered most for
the kingdom's own shape came not on a battlefield but at a deathbed:
on the death of Alfonso IX of León in 1230, Ferdinand incorporated
León into Castile permanently, overriding his father's own attempt to
leave the kingdom to daughters from an earlier marriage. Every
previous union of León and Castile, going back to Ferdinand I's own
reign, had eventually been undone by a will dividing the realm among
heirs. This one was not. With Ferdinand's death in 1252, a chronicler
of the age could note without exaggeration that the era of the
Castilian crusades - and, with it, the long habit of unmaking every
union this kingdom's own kings had built - had come to an end.
