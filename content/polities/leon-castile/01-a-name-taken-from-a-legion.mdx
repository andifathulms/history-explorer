When Alfonso III of Asturias was forced from his throne in 910, the
kingdom he had spent his reign expanding was carved into three pieces
among his own sons. One portion took the name León, centred on the old
Roman city that had once housed the Legio VII Gemina - and inherited,
along with the name, Asturias's own claim to stand first among the
Christian states of the peninsula.

## A kingdom born divided, and staying that way

León's own early history did little to make good on that inherited
claim. The portion of Alfonso III's realm that became León was, from
the start, only one of three roughly equal fragments, and its
relationship with its two siblings - Galicia, paired with the
Portuguese territory to its south, and Asturias itself - was no more
stable than the family quarrel that had produced the split in the
first place. Kings of León spent much of the following century locked
in the same pattern that had troubled Asturias before them: contests
for the throne that both sides were willing to settle by inviting
Muslim military help against their own Christian rivals, and a
nobility, particularly in Galicia, in near-constant low-grade war
against the crown.

## A frontier county with a name of its own

On the kingdom's own eastern frontier, a different pattern was taking
shape. A cluster of counties exposed directly to raiding from the
south had, by the tenth century, become known collectively as
Castile, for the numerous castles built along its border - a name
that described a landscape shaped entirely by war. The counts who held
this frontier had already begun a habit that would define Castile's
whole later relationship with León: repeatedly declining to answer
the kings of León and Oviedo's own summons to royal service, treating
obedience as negotiable rather than owed.
