title: An empire that outlived only its own king

Alfonso VII was recognized king in his grandfather's own domain in
1126, and spent his first decade fighting the same wars every León-
Castile king of the century seemed born into - against his own
stepmother Teresa and her son Afonso Henriques of Portugal, against
Aragon and Navarre after the death of Alfonso the Battler. Not until
the 1140s, with the Almoravid state collapsing into a second era of
fractured taifa kingdoms, could Alfonso finally turn south with real
force behind him. His conquests between 1144 and 1147 were vast, if
short-lived - the Almohades, entering Spain from Africa in their turn,
took back most of what he had won.

## An emperor by his own declaration

Like Ferdinand I before him, Alfonso took the title of emperor - a
claim that, in the mid-twelfth century, meant nothing less than sole
temporal ruler of Christendom in succession to Rome, and that may also
have been a pointed answer to the same pretensions of the Holy Roman
Emperors reigning in Germany. For a time the claim looked like more
than a title. Alfonso brought the kings of Aragon and Navarre, the
counts of Barcelona and Toulouse, several lesser princes of Spain and
southern France, and even some Muslim taifa rulers to swear him fealty
as their feudal sovereign - the nearest any single Christian ruler had
yet come to a peninsula-wide empire in fact as well as in name.

## Undone by the same hand that built it

The confederation Alfonso assembled had no real strength behind the
oaths that held it together, for the separatism it was meant to
override ran too deep to override for long. Alfonso proved that
himself: at his death in 1157 he divided the realm he had spent his
reign expanding into two kingdoms again, Castile for one son and León
for the other - undoing, by his own testament, the very unity his
imperial title had claimed to represent.
