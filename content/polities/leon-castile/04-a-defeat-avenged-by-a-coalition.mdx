By the late twelfth century a new North African power, the Almohads,
had absorbed the fractured taifa kingdoms into a single province of
their own empire, pursuing the earlier Arab elite so thoroughly that
Berbers became virtually the only Muslim element left in the
peninsula. In 1195 Alfonso VIII of Castile challenged the Almohad
ruler Yacub to open battle and was routed at Alarcos - a defeat made
worse by the fact that the kings of León and Navarre, who had promised
Alfonso their support, failed to deliver it in time.

## A coalition built from an old grievance

War followed against the two kings who had abandoned him at Alarcos,
but the quarrel was eventually settled, and Alfonso VIII spent the
years afterward assembling a very different kind of army: a genuinely
combined Christian force drawing on León, Castile, Navarre and Aragon
together, swollen further by foreign crusaders drawn by what felt, for
once, like a real crusading cause. Most of those foreign volunteers
deserted before the fighting actually started, but the Iberian
contingents held together.

## Tolosa

The armies met at Las Navas de Tolosa in Andalusia in 1212, and the
combined Christian force broke the Almohads decisively - a victory
later historians would treat as the single turning point of the whole
reconquest. The Almohad state did not long survive the defeat; by 1228
the old pattern of fractured taifa kingdoms had begun reappearing
across the territory it had once unified, and one by one the
Christian kings absorbed them. Only a single Muslim state would escape
that fate: a small emirate founded at Arjona in 1230 that took shape,
within a decade, as the kingdom of Granada - the one piece of Muslim
Spain that would still be standing two and a half centuries later.
