Alfonso X "the Learned," or "the Wise," came to the throne in 1252 with
a scholar's mind and, by his own contemporaries' account, a soldier's
courage - undercut by a variable, stubborn streak that turned modest
setbacks into needless quarrels. His campaigns against the remaining
Muslim states won only minor ground, and his ambitions in Europe
reached further than Castile's actual weight could carry: a claim to
Navarre abandoned rather than risk war with Aragon, a dormant claim to
Gascony traded away when his sister married the future Edward I of
England, and in 1267 an election as Holy Roman Emperor that a rival
candidate's own German backers made sure never became more than a
title on paper. Judged purely by what he won or held, it was a reign
that looked, to more than one observer since, like a failure.

## An encyclopedia of law that outlived the king who never enacted it

What Alfonso actually left behind was not a conquest but a code. Legal
diversity had been Castile's rule since the Visigothic Fuero Juzgo,
patched over by hundreds of separate local charters rather than
replaced by anything general. Alfonso's own father, Ferdinand III, is
believed to have begun work on a comprehensive code meant to apply
across the whole kingdom; Alfonso completed it, alongside two lesser
legal compilations of his own reign that never became binding law in
their own right. The one that mattered was the Leyes de las Siete
Partidas - the Laws of the Seven Parts - a work far more ambitious
than a simple lawbook, drawing on Roman and canon law to describe not
only legal procedure but the theory of kingship itself.

<Figure id="partidas" />

## Law waiting for a king strong enough to use it

The Partidas were not formally promulgated as the law of the land in
Alfonso's own lifetime, and for generations they functioned more as a
reference for jurists than as binding statute. Their influence outran
their formal status anyway: later Castilian kings drew on them to
assert royal authority the Partidas had already described in
theoretical form, and the code would go on informing law far beyond
Castile's own medieval borders, into the codes colonial Spain carried
to the Americas. A king remembered by his contemporaries chiefly for
what he failed to hold ended up handing his successors the clearest
statement of what a king of Castile was entitled to claim.
