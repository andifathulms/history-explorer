Alfonso VI, who reunited his father's divided inheritance after
years of civil war, took up the campaigns against Muslim Spain with a
success dramatic enough that legend attached itself to it almost
immediately. In 1082 he is said to have ridden his horse into the sea
at Tarifa, on Spain's southernmost tip, and declared: "This is the
last land in Spain, and I have trod it." The real substance of his
reign came three years later, at Toledo.

## A promise made and broken in the same campaign

Alfonso had promised to restore Toledo's own deposed Muslim ruler to
his throne once the city was recovered from a rebellion; instead, in
1085, he took the city for the crown of León-Castile outright. Toledo
became, from that point, the military centre of the whole reconquest
and the channel through which Muslim learning began to shape Castile's
own culture directly rather than only through raids and tribute. The
broken promise to its former ruler was not the only term left
unhonoured - Alfonso had also pledged that Toledo's Muslim population
could keep its principal mosque for worship, but in his absence the
monks of Cluny persuaded his own queen to convert the building into a
church instead, an early sign of a stricter crusading spirit beginning
to enter Spain from beyond the Pyrenees.

## The mercenary Castile's own legend outgrew

It was in Alfonso VI's reign that Rodrigo Díaz of Vivar, remembered as
"the Cid," built the career that later literature would turn into
Christian crusading legend. The real man bore little resemblance to
that legend. Banished from Castile on accusations of embezzling
tribute money he had collected for the king, Díaz took service with
the Muslim ruler of Zaragoza and won his lasting fame fighting both
against and, more than once, on the same side as Christian kings -
his own title, Cid, meaning simply "lord" in the language of the
Muslim soldiers who first used it for him. He eventually seized Valencia
for himself in 1092, ruled it as its own king until his death in
1099, and married one daughter to a Barcelona count and another into
the royal house of Navarre - a career built on loyalty to his own
army rather than to any single faith or crown.
