Castile's transformation from a rebellious frontier county into a
kingdom in its own right came, fittingly, from outside León
altogether: by the will of Sancho the Great of Navarre, Castile
became legally a kingdom in 1035, under a ruler named Ferdinand.
Ferdinand I wasted little time turning that legal status into
practical dominance, soon overwhelming the king of León and uniting
the whole of northwestern Spain under his own rule.

## Tribute from the south, and a title that overreached it

After settling his wars with Navarre by 1054, Ferdinand I turned his
attention south with real religious conviction, launching campaigns
against the fractured Muslim taifa kingdoms and bringing many of them
under tribute. His success bought León-Castile a level of regional
dominance Asturias's own kings had never approached - but it did
nothing to change the family habit that had split Alfonso III's
kingdom in the first place.

## The same mistake, repeated on purpose

At his death, Ferdinand I divided his own hard-won realm into three
kingdoms - Castile, León and Galicia - plus two lesser principalities,
distributed among his sons exactly as Alfonso III's own kingdom had
been split a century and a quarter earlier. A long civil war followed,
from which Alfonso VI emerged as sole ruler of his father's entire
domain by 1072. That Ferdinand I, who had personally profited from
reuniting a divided kingdom, chose at his own death to divide it again
says something about how little the underlying instability of these
Christian kingdoms had actually changed - success against the Muslim
south solved nothing about what happened to a kingdom once its king
died with more than one son to provide for.
