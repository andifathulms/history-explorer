The 1909 treaty fixed a border. It did not settle the question of who the
people on the Siamese side of it were governed by, or why - and the argument
it left open has outlived every government that has tried to close it.

## An imam's petition, and its answer

Haji Sulong, an Islamic scholar who had returned to Patani after decades
studying in Mecca, spent the 1940s pressing Bangkok for exactly what the
1909 treaty had refused: religious courts under Islamic law, Malay taught
in schools, and local officials answerable to the region rather than
imposed from the capital. He was arrested in 1948 and disappeared in
Thai police custody in 1954, presumed murdered - and his petition, rather
than being forgotten, became the founding grievance of every movement that
followed him.

## From petitions to guerrillas

The Barisan Revolusi Nasional, formed in the 1960s, and the Patani United
Liberation Organisation, founded in 1968, replaced Haji Sulong's appeals
to Bangkok with armed insurgency aimed at outright independence. Decades
of intermittent guerrilla activity in the southern border provinces
followed, ebbing and flaring without ever fully resolving into either
victory or peace, alongside a smaller rival faction, the Gerakan
Mujahidin Islam Patani.

## The violence that returned

On 4 January 2004 raiders stormed an army depot in Narathiwat, killed
four soldiers and made off with hundreds of weapons - the opening act of
a resurgence far more sustained than anything in the previous two
decades. The government declared martial law within a day. On 28 April
that year, security forces stormed militants who had barricaded
themselves inside the historic Kru Se Mosque in Pattani, killing all
inside; and on 25 October, at Tak Bai, seven protesters were shot dead
and at least seventy-eight more died of suffocation or crushing while
being transported, bound, in army trucks after a demonstration outside a
police station.

Those three events did for the twenty-first-century insurgency what Haji
Sulong's disappearance had done for the twentieth: they turned a
political grievance into a body count with names attached, recruiting for
the movements that used them far more effectively than any ideology on
its own could have.

## An argument still running

The line drawn in 1909 divided one Malay-speaking, Muslim world into a
Malaysian side and a Thai one, and everything since - Haji Sulong's
petition, the guerrilla decades, the mosque and the checkpoint - has been
an argument about what obligation the state on the Thai side of that line
owes to the people it drew inside it. Unlike Patani's own conquest, its
partition, or the removal of its ruling line, this last chapter has no
date it can be filed under. It is still being fought over the way the
earlier ones were fought over before they had endings.
