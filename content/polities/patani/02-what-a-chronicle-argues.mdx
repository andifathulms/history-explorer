Almost everything known about Patani from the inside comes from one text, the
*Hikayat Patani*, and it was written by people with a position in an argument
that was still open.

It is worth knowing what kind of book that makes it. It is not a chronicle in
the sense of a year-by-year register. It is a sequence of episodes with a moral
shape — the same form as the *Sejarah Melayu*, the Melaka court's chronicle
written in exile at Johor, and like that text it is simultaneously the only
view from within and a document with something to prove.

## What this one is arguing about

Succession, and specifically the legitimacy of a line that ran through women
for a century.

The text spends a great deal of its length on the four queens and on the events
around each accession, and it is at pains to establish that each was the right
person. That is not how a chronicle behaves about an uncontroversial rule. It
is how a chronicle behaves when the arrangement it is describing needs
defending — and the *Hikayat* was compiled, in the form we have, after the
queens were over and a different dynasty from Kelantan held the throne.

So the most detailed evidence for Patani's most distinctive feature comes from
people who had an interest in how that feature was remembered.

The *Sejarah Melayu* is elegy — survivors writing about a lost city. The
*Hikayat Patani* is closer to a brief.

## What follows from that

Nobody can say whether Patani had a rule of succession.

Women succeeded women for a hundred and four years, which looks like a rule and
was not one: descent ran through the founding dynasty, and the dynasty had run
out of men. Whether anything more principled than that was ever agreed is a
question only the *Hikayat* could answer — and the *Hikayat* is the witness
with the strongest motive to say yes.

That is a harder kind of ignorance than a missing document. The document
exists, it is detailed, and it is arguing.
