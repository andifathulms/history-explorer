Siam took Patani in 1786, deported part of the population north, and carried off
the guns. That is the conquest, and it is not really when the sultanate ended.

In 1808 the territory was broken into seven small provinces under separate
rulers, which is a standard device for making a place ungovernable as a unit. In
1902 the last of the ruling line was removed and the area was absorbed into the
Siamese administrative system.

And in 1909, under an Anglo-Siamese treaty, Britain and Siam drew a line. Kedah,
Kelantan, Terengganu and Perlis went to the British side and are now in Malaysia.
Patani stayed on the Siamese side and is now the southern provinces of Thailand.

The line runs through what had been one Malay-speaking, Muslim, Jawi-writing
world, and it was drawn by two states neither of which was in it.

## Endings that are documents

Patani is not the only one. Brunei ended in 1888, having been given away in
pieces to two chartered companies and a private individual, and the pieces are
now in three countries. Sulu's fourth chapter turns on a single word in an 1878
contract, whose translation determined which state a coastline belongs to today.

None of those is a conquest, whatever else it is. Nobody stormed anything. The
instrument in each case is a signature, generally applied a long way away, and
often by parties who had never seen the place.

That is the harder thing to convey about how these sultanates ended, because a
conquest has a date and a battle and somebody to blame. Patani's ending took a
hundred and twenty-three years and three separate instruments — a deportation, a
partition, and a treaty between two empires — and the last of them is the one
still being argued about.
