Patani began inland and moved to the sea.

The founder was born Phaya Tu Antara, son of Phaya Tu Kerub Mahajana of Kota
Mahligai — an interior seat, some way from the coast — and what he did was
relocate. He took the court down to a landing place on the Gulf shore of the
peninsula, and the settlement that grew there became Patani. He is remembered
afterwards as Sultan Isma'il Syah, which is the other half of the story.

## Two things happened at once

The move and the conversion are not separable, and both are about the sea.

An inland ruler lives on what the country round him produces and on whoever he
can compel. A ruler at a landing place lives on what passes. Going down to the
coast meant entering a world of Muslim shipping — Gujarati, Arab, Malay — in
which being Muslim was not incidental to doing business but close to a
condition of it.

Patani's own tradition attributes the ruler's conversion to people from Pasai,
who were present in the town as a trading community. That is a specific and
plausible route: Pasai was the oldest Muslim court in the region, four hundred
miles west across the peninsula and the strait, and its merchants were exactly
the people a new port on the Gulf would be dealing with.

Patani has long been held to be one of the cradles of Islam in Southeast Asia,
and the claim is older than Melaka's. D'Eredia, writing in 1613, says Islam was
adopted at Patani and Pahang before it reached Melaka; Islam was certainly
established at Trengganu, close by, no later than 1386. When exactly it arrived
here nobody has established.

## A disputed pedigree

The origins are confused in the tradition, and the confusion is itself
informative.

The oral and written accounts disagree about the immediate origins of the
ruling house and about whether its connections on the peninsula were Thai or
Malay. That looks like the residue of a period in which Patani's predecessors
had been eclipsed by their neighbours — and when a new Patani rose to
prominence in the late fifteenth or early sixteenth century, its pedigree
became something worth arguing about. Factions inside the state identified
their own fortunes with competing cultural traditions and political
connections, and each had a version of where the dynasty had come from.

Only two rulers are named before a datable event: Isma'il Syah and Mudhaffar
Syah, and then a rebellion against the Thai monarchy of Ayutthaya in 1564.
Before that the record is a set of claims about ancestry made by people with
reasons.
