Patani spent two hundred and eighty years as a nominal tributary of Ayutthaya
that behaved as an independent state, and there is no contradiction in that. On
the peninsula the tribute bought the appearance of submission and the substance
was negotiable — until somebody tested it.

In 1634 Raja Ungu refused the overlordship her predecessors had accepted and a
large Siamese army and fleet were beaten off.

In 1786, nineteen years after Ayutthaya itself was destroyed by a Burmese army,
the dynasty that had replaced it at Bangkok took Patani, deported a large part of
the population north, and carried off the town's great cannon.

## Who did it, and why the field is empty

`ended.by` is empty, and the reason on this page is unlike any of the other
thirteen empty ones across the archipelago.

The polity that took Patani is the Chakri dynasty at Bangkok, and this site has
an odd relationship with it. It is the single item in the `successor_claims` of
this corpus's Ayutthaya record — a claim entered under the rule that a claimant
does not need a page here, for a dynasty that rebuilt Ayutthaya's city plan on a
new river bend and re-promulgated its law code.

So the conqueror is named on this site, as a claim on another record, and cannot
be named in this field, which takes ids.

What that record's last chapter says explains the rest. The Siamese state that
reassembled itself after 1767 was a different and more centralising thing than
the one that fell — it had lost its capital, its archive and its literature, and
what it built back was tighter. Patani's two hundred and eighty years of
negotiable tribute ended because the party on the other side of the negotiation
had been replaced by one that did not negotiate.

This record stops in 1786. Patani was administered from Bangkok afterwards, split
into seven small provinces in 1808, and finally partitioned in 1902 under an
Anglo-Siamese arrangement that put the boundary between Thailand and Malaysia
where it still is — through the middle of what had been this sultanate.
