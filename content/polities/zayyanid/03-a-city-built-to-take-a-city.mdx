---
title: A city built to take a city
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987
phase: peak
---

In 1299 the Marinid sultan Abu Yaqub Yusuf came east and invested Tlemcen. He
did not storm it. He built a city outside it.

Al-Mansura went up on the plain in front of the walls: a walled town with its
own great mosque and minaret, palaces, baths, markets and a mint, intended to
receive the trade that Tlemcen could no longer handle while it was shut in.
The siege lasted eight years. The idea was not to destroy the Zayyanid capital
but to replace it — to move the crossroads a few kilometres and let the besieged
city starve of the only thing that made it worth having.

It nearly worked. It ended in 1307 when the sultan was murdered by one of his
own eunuchs and the Marinid army went home to deal with the succession, and the
Zayyanids came out of the gates and pulled al-Mansura down. The minaret is
still standing in the fields, on its own.

Almost every siege in the medieval record is a contest for a place. This one
was a contest for a *route*, and the attacker's method was to offer the
merchants a better address.

The rest of the fourteenth century ran the same way with less imagination. Abu
al-Hasan of Fez took Tlemcen outright in 1337 and held it eleven years, until
the disaster in Ifriqiya that broke his army near Kairouan; the Marinids
took it again in 1352 and held it seven more. Each time the Zayyanid house
survived somewhere, came back when Fez was distracted, and resumed. The kingdom
reached perhaps a hundred thousand people in the 1330s, built the Tashfiniya
madrasa whose tilework the Marinids then copied, and went on collecting the
customs.

This is a kingdom whose entire achievement is continuity under pressure, which
is the hardest thing there is to put a number on. It held no impressive
territory, produced no empire and carried no doctrine anywhere. It lasted more
than three centuries in the most contested corridor in North Africa, and the
duration is the achievement rather than the setting for one.
