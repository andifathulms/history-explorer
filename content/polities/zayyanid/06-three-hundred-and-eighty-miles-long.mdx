Leo Africanus saw the kingdom in its last decades and gives its dimensions
before anything else, because the dimensions are the problem:

> This kingdome stretcheth in length from east to west 380. miles, but in
> bredth from north to south, that is, from the Mediterran sea to the deserts
> of Numidia not aboue fiue and twentie miles: which is the occasion that it is
> so often oppressed by the Arabians inhabiting the Numidian deserts.

A ribbon of country between a sea it did not control and a desert it could not
police. The kings, he goes on, had always tried to buy the goodwill of the
Numidians with great gifts, "but they could neuer satisfie their insatiable
couetice. A man shall seldome trauell safely through this kingdome" — and then,
in the same breath: "howbeit here are great store of merchants, perhaps either
because it adioneth to Numidia, or else for that the way to the land of Negros
lieth through it."

Unsafe to travel and full of merchants. That is the kingdom in one sentence.

## A dish of boiled horse-flesh

Leo's version of the eight-year siege has a detail the chronicles do not. The
Marinid Yusuf had built his fort on the east side and the city was starving.
The citizens went in a body to their king and begged him to have pity on their
want. To acquaint them with his own supper, he showed them what he had: a dish
of boiled horse-flesh and barley. They saw then that the king's estate was
little better than the meanest citizen's. He called an assembly, told them it
was more honourable to die in battle for their country than to live in that
condition, and they resolved to fight the next day.

That night Yusuf was killed by one of his own people. The citizens marched out,
scattered the leaderless army, and found provisions enough in the enemy camp to
end the famine.

## The second and greater damage

The next Marinid siege had no such ending. About forty years later Abu'l-Hasan
built his own town two miles west of Tlemcen, besieged the city for thirty
months, assaulted it daily and put up a new fort every night, until his forces
came in. He carried the king of Tlemcen home to Fez, had him beheaded there,
and had the body thrown out among the filth of the city. Leo calls it the
second and the greater damage the city sustained.

Then the Marinids decayed, and Tlemcen was repaired and refilled. Under Abu
Tashfin it had held sixteen thousand families; after the recovery it reached
twelve thousand. Each trade had its own quarter, on the plan of Fez, though Fez
was built more handsomely. There were five colleges, sumptuously built — some
by the king of Tlemcen and some by the king of Fez, which is a strange thing to
find in a town two Moroccan sultans had starved. There were two inns kept for
the merchants of Genoa and Venice.

## Fifteen thousand ducats out of one ship

The customs are the whole fiscal history of the kingdom, and Leo gives a
figure. A single Genoese ship put in carrying goods enough to supply Tlemcen
for five years; the tenth part of it, paid to the king as duty, came to fifteen
thousand ducats.

That is what everybody had been fighting over for three centuries. Not the
ribbon of dry country, not the twelve thousand families — the ten per cent.

## What went in 1521

A great part of the city was inhabited by Jews, who had once all been
exceedingly rich, and who wore a distinguishing turban. On the death of the
king in 927 of the Hijra, Leo writes, they were all so robbed and spoiled that
they were brought almost to beggary.

He records it in the same tone as the harbours: something that had been part of
what made the place valuable, and was not any more. Oran and Mers el-Kebir,
the kingdom's two great trading ports, where the Genoese and the Venetians
came, had been taken by Ferdinand the Catholic a decade before — to the great
inconvenience, Leo says, of all this kingdom. The king who lost them was driven
out by his own subjects.

Three hundred and eighty miles long and twenty-five wide, with the ports gone,
the merchants' community ruined and the desert side unpoliceable. What was left
was the road, and the road was about to acquire two new owners.
