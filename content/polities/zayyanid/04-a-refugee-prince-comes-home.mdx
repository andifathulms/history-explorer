---
title: A refugee prince comes home
drafted_from: ibn-khaldun-de-slane

Abu Hammu Musa II spent his early life as a refugee. When the Marinids
overran Tlemcen and scattered the Zayyanid royal family across the
Maghreb, the young prince fled east with his uncle, slipped past the
soldiers sent to catch him near Bejaia, and found asylum at the Hafsid
court in Tunis - where the chamberlain Ibn Tafraguin received him with
the honors due a prince of the blood and refused, more than once, to
hand him over when the Marinid sultan Abu Inan demanded his
extradition.

## Restored by the tribes Fez could not control

Abu Hammu's return home was arranged not by the Hafsids but by the
Dawawida, a powerful branch of the Riyah Arabs, who came to Ibn
Tafraguin in 1358 with a proposal: let the prince go, and they would
escort him into Zayyanid territory themselves and keep the Marinid
sultan too occupied defending his own province to threaten Tunis. The
Hafsids fitted him out with a royal retinue and the insignia of
sovereignty and sent him off under Arab escort. Abu Inan died before
Abu Hammu had even crossed the desert, and the prince pressed on to
Tlemcen anyway, routing the garrison's Marinid-allied reinforcements
in the field and then blockading the city itself with cavalry for
three days. On the fourth day his forces broke in by force; the
Marinid governor fled under escort toward Fez, and on 9 February 1359
Abu Hammu entered his own capital, mounted the throne, and received
his subjects' oath of loyalty. He spent the years that followed
expelling the remaining Marinid garrisons from every town in the
kingdom his family had lost a generation earlier.

## A war fought to avenge a father-in-law, and lost

Abu Hammu's reign was rarely at peace, but no campaign cost him more
than the one he waged against the Hafsids themselves. When his own
father-in-law, the Hafsid claimant Abu Abdallah, was killed in battle
outside Bejaia in 1366, Abu Hammu marched east with a large army -
thousands of Zayyanid regulars alongside Zoghba Arab allies - declaring
he meant to avenge him. The siege stalled under Bejaia's walls for
days while Abu Hammu built siege engines and pressed the attack. It
ended in catastrophe. His own Zoghba allies, secretly negotiating with
the Hafsid claimant Abu Zayyan - Abu Hammu's own cousin, whom the
Hafsids had released from custody specifically to peel the Zoghba away
from him - broke and ran in the middle of the decisive engagement on 5
Dhu'l-Hijja 767 (August 1366), and their flight turned the battle into
a rout. Abu Hammu's baggage, treasury and even his own wives fell into
Hafsid hands, the captured women handed over to his rival cousin; Abu
Hammu himself was unhorsed trying to cut his way through the crush and
escaped only through the loyalty of his vizier.

## A death arranged inside his own household

Abu Hammu ruled for another two decades after the Bejaia disaster,
long enough to see a young scholar named Ibn Khaldun serve at his
court and later write his history. It did not end peacefully. The
scheme that killed him in 1389 was set in motion by Hilal, a
Catalan-born freedman who had been Abu Hammu's own favorite before
being given to his son Abu Tashfin - and who, Ibn Khaldun's own
account states plainly, was the principal author of the crime that
killed his former master. Abu Tashfin succeeded his father on the
throne, with the same Hilal installed as his chamberlain - a
transition from restoration to assassination that closed out, within
one long reign, both the high point of the dynasty's medieval power
and the family violence that had unmade Zayyanid successions before
and would again.
