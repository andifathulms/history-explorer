title: Between two empires, again

The fifteenth century reduced the kingdom to a client. It was at various points
a tributary of the Hafsids at Tunis, of the Marinids and their Wattasid
successors at Fez, and of the Crown of Aragon, which wanted the grain and the
trade and was prepared to pay for a friendly ruler. Tlemcen went on being rich
and stopped being independent in any sense that would have been recognised by
Yaghmurasan.

Then the western Mediterranean became a frontier between two empires that had
no interest in the central Maghreb as such.

Spain took Oran in 1509 and Bougie in 1510 and planted a fortress in the
harbour at Algiers, as part of a campaign of coastal presidios running from
Morocco to Tripoli. The Ottomans arrived from the sea in the person of the
Barbarossa brothers and made Algiers a base. Between them the Zayyanids became
what this site's Hafsid page describes Tunis becoming at exactly the same time:
not a party to the contest but an instrument in it, installed and deposed as
each side needed a local name.

The Ottomans took Tlemcen in 1551. The last Zayyanid ruler fled to Spanish Oran,
was baptised, and lived out his life there under the name Carlos. By 1556 the
territory was a beylik of the Regency of Algiers.

Two things to record.

The kingdom had also become, briefly, a refuge. After the expulsions from Spain
in 1492 — the year this site's Nasrid page ends — several thousand Jewish
refugees settled in the kingdom, and Andalusi Muslims came in far larger
numbers over the following century, into Tlemcen as into Fez and Tunis. The
three successor states of the Almohad empire absorbed the population of a
fourth that had ceased to exist.

And the three-way division held to the end. The Almohad page on this site names
Hafsid, Zayyanid and Marinid as the states the Maghreb divided into, and all
three are now here. They lasted 345, 321 and 221 years respectively, none of
them ever conquered the others for long, and the borders they settled into are
approximately Tunisia, Algeria and Morocco. That is an unusually durable
outcome for a partition nobody designed.
