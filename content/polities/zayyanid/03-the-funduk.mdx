title: Where the gold changed hands
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987

Tlemcen had a *funduk* — a walled compound with warehousing, lodging and a
chapel, where foreign merchants lived, stored goods and did business under
their own consul and, within limits, their own law. Genoese, Venetian, Pisan
and Catalan merchants held them across the Maghreb by treaty, and this site's
Hafsid page records the same arrangement at Tunis and Bougie.

What made the Tlemcen one matter is what arrived there.

Gold came north out of the western Sudan by camel across the Sahara — the trade
this site describes from the far end on its Mali page, where Mansa Musa's
pilgrimage is the same metal moving on the same routes, and from the middle on
its Almoravid page, where a Saharan movement took both termini before it took
Morocco. Tlemcen and Sijilmasa were where it reached the settled Maghreb.
European merchants came south to meet it, and the exchange happened in a
compound like this one.

The mechanics are the interesting part, because they cut across everything else
this site records. Genoese bills of exchange circulated at Tlemcen. Christian
merchants took credit from Muslim ones and the reverse. The gold went north and
became the coinage of the Italian city-states — the Genoese genovino and the
Florentine florin, struck from the 1250s onward, are made substantially of West
African gold that came through ports like these. The commercial law that made
that possible had to work across a religious frontier that the chronicles on
both sides treat as the organising fact of the age.

And this is the same kingdom whose capital was besieged for eight years by a
neighbour trying to divert exactly this traffic, in the chapter before. The
al-Mansura siege makes sense only if you know what the funduk was worth.

The site's `revenue_basis` for this polity reads trade-toll and land-tax, and
the first value is carrying almost all the weight. A kingdom the size of three
provinces, permanently threatened by two larger neighbours, financed itself for
three centuries on a customs house — and the reason the neighbours kept coming
was that they wanted the customs house rather than the kingdom.
