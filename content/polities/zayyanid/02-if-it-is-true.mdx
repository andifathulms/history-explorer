Ibn Khaldun records that people once traced Yaghmurasan ibn Zayyan's family
back to Idris, the sharif who founded the first Muslim dynasty in Morocco, and
that Yaghmurasan replied, in the Berber of his own nation:

> If it is true, it will profit us with God. But in this world we shall owe our
> success only to our swords.

Every other dynasty in the region spent its energies on exactly the claim he
was waving away. The Idrisids had the descent, the Almohads had the mahdi, the
Saadis would make sharifian blood the price of ruling Morocco for the next four
centuries. Ibn Khaldun's own comment on the genealogy is that it is an
assertion without proof, that nomads know very little about such things, and
that what truth there may be in it is known to God alone.

## Confirmed by the empire he was leaving

The founding was less dramatic than a secession usually is. Yaghmurasan's
brother had commanded at Tlemcen for the Almohads and died in 1236; Yaghmurasan
took over with the approval of all the Abd al-Wadid tribes, the towns of the
central Maghreb hastened to obey him, and the Almohad caliph al-Rashid sent him
a diploma confirming him in the government of Tlemcen. He became a sovereign by
being appointed one, and simply outlived the authority that had appointed him.

## Assembling a kingdom out of other people's quarrels

What followed is a masterclass in acquiring territory without much fighting for
it.

The Maghrawa and the Toujin, the Zenata confederations to his east, watched him
begin taking the towns of the central Maghreb and appealed to Abu Zakariya, the
Hafsid who had just detached Ifriqiya. The Hafsid came, took Tlemcen, and on
his way home confirmed each Zenata emir in command of his own tribe and
country, and authorised them to surround themselves with the emblems of
sovereignty and appear before him so equipped.

Yaghmurasan took Tlemcen back and went to work on the emirs instead.

The house of Mendil, which led the Maghrawa, fell into a series of fratricides.
In 1270 one brother, Omar, undertook to put Yaghmurasan in possession of
Miliana in return for being made chief of the Maghrawa; the bargain was
executed, and Yaghmurasan found himself master of the confederation with its
own chief under his orders. Two other brothers, trying to cut Omar out by the
same method he had used, sold him the city of Ténès in 1273 for twelve thousand
pieces of gold.

A city bought outright. Not besieged, not stormed — purchased from the ruling
family's losing faction.

## What it looked like from inside

There is one glimpse of the court in Ibn Khaldun's account and it is about
pride. Al-Abbas ibn Mendil, having made peace with Yaghmurasan, came to Tlemcen
and was received with every flattery, and at a reception told the company he
had once seen a single horseman hold off two hundred. Members of the Abd
al-Wadid family present called him a liar. He left the city in a rage and went
back to his tribe. Yaghmurasan discovered later that the story was true and
that the horseman had been al-Abbas himself.

His son Othman spent the 1280s and 1290s taking the rest — Médéa, Mazouna,
Ténès again — until the Maghrawa had lost every town and every stretch of open
country they held and were pushed back into the mountains, and their chief took
ship for Morocco to beg for help.

Within two generations a governor's brother had turned a diploma into a kingdom
running the width of the central Maghreb, on no doctrine, no genealogy and no
claim beyond the one Yaghmurasan had stated at the start.
