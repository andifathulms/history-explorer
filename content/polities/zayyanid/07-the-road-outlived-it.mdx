A dynasty that had spent three centuries taxing people passing through was
replaced, in 1551, by an Ottoman beylik that taxed people passing through. The
crossroads did not notice.

What Tlemcen kept doing afterwards is visible in a book written eighty years
later by a man born there.

## The manuscript from Guadix

When al-Maqqari came to describe the omens that Granada had received before its
fall, his authority was a manuscript he had read in the author's own hand: the
work of Abu Abdallah Muhammad ibn al-Haddad al-Wadi-ashi — from Guadix — a
learned hafiz, secretary and historian who, the note says, resided at Tlemcen.

That is one sentence and it contains the whole afterlife. An Andalusi scholar
from a town in the Granadan sierra, displaced, living out his life in the
central Maghreb, writing the history of the country he had lost; and a Tlemceni
a generation or two later reading his handwriting and copying him out. Tlemcen
had stopped being a capital and had not stopped being a place where a man could
put a library.

## The road from Oran

The last movement al-Maqqari records went the other way along the same route.
When the Moriscos were finally expelled from Spain in 1610, many thousands went
to Fez and thousands crossed from Oran to Tlemcen, while the greater part took
the road to Tunis.

Few reached where they were going. They were set upon and robbed on the way,
and al-Maqqari specifies the worst stretches: the roads to Fez and to Tlemcen.
Those who sailed for Tunis mostly arrived intact.

Read that against Leo Africanus, writing ninety years earlier, on the same
kingdom: *a man shall seldom travel safely through this kingdom*, because the
strip is too narrow to police and the Arabs of the desert cannot be bought off.
The dynasty had gone, the Spanish had taken Oran, the Ottomans had taken
Tlemcen, and the specific hazard of that specific road was exactly what it had
been. Some facts about a country belong to its geography and not to whoever is
collecting the customs.

## What the three states left

The Almohad empire came apart into Ifriqiya, the central Maghreb and Morocco
inside forty years. Tunis lasted 345 years, Tlemcen 316 to 321 depending on which capture you
count as the end, and Fez 221. None of them ever held another for long, and the
frontiers they wore into the ground are close to the ones between Tunisia,
Algeria and Morocco now.

Tlemcen is the one that made the case hardest. It was the smallest of the
three, the poorest in land, the most often occupied, and it was the one whose
survival was least explicable by anything except that both its neighbours
needed the road open and neither could keep it. A state can be built on being
indispensable and impossible to digest, and this one was, for longer than most
states built on anything else.
