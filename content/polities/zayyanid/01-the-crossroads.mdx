---
title: The crossroads

Yaghmurasan ibn Zayyan was the Almohad governor of Tlemcen who stopped being
one in 1235, six years after the man at Tunis had done the same thing — this
site's Hafsid page — and nine years before the Zenata confederation to his west
began taking Morocco. The Almohad empire produced three successor states within
about thirty years, and this is the one in the middle.

Being in the middle is the whole of its history.

Tlemcen sits in the high country of the central Maghreb at the point where two
trade systems cross. The east-west road between Morocco and Ifriqiya runs
through it; so does the north-south route from the Mediterranean coast at Oran
and Honein down to the Saharan oases and, beyond them, the gold of the western
Sudan that this site's Almoravid and Mali pages describe from the other ends.
Whoever held Tlemcen taxed both.

Yaghmurasan understood the position immediately and overreached on it
immediately. He took Sijilmasa in 1264, which briefly put the two great northern
termini of the trans-Saharan trade under one authority — and he lost it to the
Marinids eleven years later, which is the first instance of the thing that
happened to this kingdom for three centuries.

The Zayyanids were never the strongest power in the Maghreb and were always
worth taking. The Marinids to the west wanted Tlemcen; the Hafsids to the east
wanted Tlemcen; and both were larger. What the kingdom had instead was a
position everybody needed to remain functional, walls, and a talent for
outlasting the states that occupied it.

Ibn Khaldun, who lived there under Abu Hammu Musa II and served him, wrote that
science and the arts developed there with success and that it produced scholars
whose reputation reached other countries. He also wrote the theory of why
dynasties like this one do not last, which this site's Hafsid page describes,
while working for three of them in turn.
