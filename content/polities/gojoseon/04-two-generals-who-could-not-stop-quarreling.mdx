U-gu inherited Wiman's ambition without his grandfather's restraint. He
gathered refugees and malcontents around his own court, cut off
diplomatic relations with the Han emperor, and blocked neighbouring
tribes from sending their customary embassies across the border into
China - provocations that Emperor Wu could not simply overlook, whatever
their actual cause. When an imperial envoy sent to remonstrate with U-gu
returned empty-handed after the king refused to yield, the frontier
tribe responsible for escorting him killed the envoy on his own return
journey - an act U-gu had not ordered, but one the emperor held him
responsible for regardless.

## An army scattered, then a war that could not be avoided

In 107 BC Han forces invaded under two generals, and the opening
engagement went badly for the empire: U-gu's own defenders broke the
invading column, which took ten days simply to rally into an orderly
retreat. Frightened by his own success and its likely consequences,
U-gu sent his son to China as a hostage along with five thousand horses
and an escort of ten thousand troops - an offer of submission undone
almost immediately when the accompanying Chinese envoy demanded the
escort disarm before crossing the border, and the prince, suspecting a
trap, fled back to his father's capital instead.

## A siege prolonged by its own commanders' quarrel

The two Han generals renewed their invasion with a larger force,
crossed the border unopposed, and laid siege to P'yŭngyang - a siege
that dragged on for months in large part because the generals spent as
much energy accusing each other of treason as they spent prosecuting
the war itself. One general was recalled to China in disgrace and
executed; the other continued the siege alone through the following
summer.

## A city taken by its own traitors, not by the enemy

What finally ended the siege was betrayal from within rather than
victory from without. A conspirator inside the city assassinated U-gu
himself; even after the king's death the people refused to surrender
until a second traitor opened the gates to the besieging army, and the
enraged citizens tore that man to pieces before he could reach the
safety of the Chinese camp. The kingdom Wiman had built on one act of
treachery ended, eighty-eight years later, on two acts of treachery
committed by his own subjects - a symmetry Korean chroniclers would go
on to note for centuries afterward.
