Tradition places Korea's first kingdom in the twenty-fifth year of the
legendary Chinese emperor Yao - 2332 BC by later reckoning - when a
divine being's son, Whan-ung, descended from heaven to a mountain in
what is now P'yŭng-an province with three thousand spirit companions,
gathered them beneath an ancient tree, and proclaimed himself ruler of
a kingdom he governed through spirit ministers of wind, rain and cloud.

## Two animals, one patient enough to become human

Whan-ung's own transition from spirit to human ruler came through a
story every Korean schoolchild would later learn: a tiger and a bear,
both longing to become human, were told to eat garlic and mugwort and
remain hidden from sunlight for three times seven days. The tiger's
patience failed and it left the cave early, still a tiger. The bear
endured the full term and emerged a woman, whose wish for a child
Whan-ung answered himself. Her son, cradled beneath the same tree where
his father had first proclaimed his kingdom, was found and made king by
the wild tribes of the peninsula. This was Tan-gun, "Lord of the
Pak-tal Tree."

## A teacher for nine wild tribes

The peoples Tan-gun found - nine tribes the tradition calls the Ku-i,
living on gathered fruit and wearing woven grass, sheltering under
trees in summer and in dug-out shelters in winter - received from him
the rudiments of settled civilization: the relationship of ruler and
subject, the rite of marriage, the arts of cooking and building, and
the practice of farming fields their ancestors had only foraged. He
made P'yŭngyang his capital, a city that would remain the kingdom's
seat through everything that followed.

## A reign no single lifetime could hold

Tradition credits Tan-gun's own dynasty with lasting more than a
thousand years, down to the arrival of a Chinese sage named Gija around
1122 BC - a span far beyond any one man's natural life, which suggests
that the name itself may describe a line of native rulers rather than a
single founder who somehow reigned across fourteen centuries. Whatever
the truth of any one figure within it, the tradition agrees on this
much: Korea's political history, by its own reckoning, opens with a
kingdom already old by the time anyone thought to write its story down.
