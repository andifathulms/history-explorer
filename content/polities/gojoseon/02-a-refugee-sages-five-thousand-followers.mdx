Gija was, by his own family's account, a prince of the Shang royal
house, uncle and advisor to the dynasty's last and most infamous ruler
- a tyrant whose court, tradition insists, included a fox spirit
disguised as a beautiful concubine and whose cruelties toward his own
subjects were legendary even before the dynasty that produced them
fell. When a rebel army overthrew the Shang and founded the Zhou
dynasty in its place, Gija - the sole survivor of the three ministers
who had tried to restrain the old regime's excesses - refused the new
emperor's offer of high office out of loyalty to the fallen house he
had served.

## A colonizer's careful preparation

Rather than serve the dynasty that had destroyed his own family's
kingdom, Gija chose exile, and prepared for it with unusual foresight.
He gathered roughly five thousand followers - not merely soldiers, but
men trained to teach farming, silk cultivation, weaving, medicine,
music and a hundred other useful trades - and led them east into the
Korean peninsula, settling eventually at P'yŭngyang, the same city that
had served as Tan-gun's own capital.

## A code that fit the people it was written for

Gija's laws were short and pointed directly at the failings of the
society he found: murder answered with death by the same method as the
crime, brawling fined in grain, theft punished by enslavement redeemable
through payment. Rather than govern through his own five thousand
followers alone, he trained local men for administrative office,
adapted his teaching to the Korean language rather than imposing his
own, and is credited - though the evidence has not survived - with
reducing that language to writing for the first time.

## A kingship granted, not demanded

China's new Zhou emperor made Gija king of Joseon, but pointedly did not
require his allegiance as a vassal in doing so - permitting him even to
send envoys to honor the tombs of the fallen Shang dynasty he had once
served. The kingdom Gija built this way endured, by tradition, for
nearly a thousand years, the single longest-lived experiment in
Korea's own ancient history, before a very different kind of guest
undid everything it had built.
