title: A father imprisoned by his own son

Kyŏn Hwŏn had more than ten sons by his many concubines, and of them
all he loved his fourth, Keum-gang, best — robust, intelligent, and, in
his father's eyes, fit to inherit a kingdom his elder brothers had done
more to help win. In 935 Kyŏn Hwŏn passed over his own eldest son,
Sin-geum, and named Keum-gang his successor instead. The decision broke
the kingdom Kyŏn Hwŏn had spent decades building.

## A conspiracy answered by a father's imprisonment

Sin-geum did not accept his disinheritance quietly. He led a conspiracy
that seized his own father, had Keum-gang put to death, and installed
himself on the throne — leaving Kyŏn Hwŏn imprisoned in Keum-san
monastery, doubly humiliated by a throne now "insecure" without the
"masterly genius" that had built it in the first place. For three
months the old warlord sat confined by his own son's guards.

## An escape, and a surrender to the enemy of a lifetime

Kyŏn Hwŏn did not stay imprisoned. He got his guards drunk, escaped to
Ka-ju, and from there sent a letter to Wang-gön — the rival he had
fought for the better part of thirty years — surrendering himself and
asking for asylum against his own son. Wang-gön granted it without
hesitation, sending a warship and a high official to escort the old man
to Koryŏ's own capital, where he was housed, staffed and granted the
revenues of an entire prefecture. The founder of Later Baekje spent his
final months as an honored guest of the kingdom he had spent his life
trying to destroy.

## A father's army against a son's

The reckoning came quickly. As 936 opened, Wang-gön marched south at
the head of eighty-seven thousand men, with Kyŏn Hwŏn himself riding in
the invading army against the son who had deposed him. Sin-geum's
remaining force, holed up in a mountain fortress, was hunted down and
destroyed — thirty-two hundred men taken, fifty-seven hundred killed.
Sin-geum's two younger brothers, who had helped drive their father from
power, were executed; Sin-geum himself, judged less culpable, was
spared. Kyŏn Hwŏn did not live to see much of the aftermath: he died on
the field itself, the source says, of chagrin that Sin-geum had escaped
the same fate as his own two brothers. Later Baekje, the kingdom he had
carved out of a collapsing Silla thirty-nine years before, ended with
him.
