Kyŏn Hwŏn was born Yi, a name he changed to Kyŏn at fifteen, and rose
through Silla's own army step by step before the kingdom's collapsing
authority gave him a wider field. Tradition traced his fierce nature to
infancy: left alone in a field while his father worked and his mother
labored nearby, the story goes, a tiger came upon him and let him nurse
from her rather than harm him. Whatever the truth of the legend, by the
890s Kyŏn Hwŏn had thrown off his own allegiance to Silla outright,
gathering "desperate criminals, outlaws and other disaffected persons"
around him and beginning the conquest of the south and west. Within a
month he commanded five thousand men.

## A title claimed, then walked back

Having seized the district of Mu-ju, Kyŏn Hwŏn proclaimed himself King
of Southern Silla — and then, judging he had overreached, quietly
modified the title to the more modest "Master of Men and Horses." The
retreat cost him nothing in practice; his own following kept growing
regardless of what he called himself, fed by a Silla court too consumed
by its own corruption to organize a real response.

## A revolt becomes a kingdom

Four years later, in 897, Kyŏn Hwŏn judged his own position secure
enough to formalize it. He established a permanent headquarters at
Wan-san, in what is now Chŏn-ju, and named his kingdom New Baekje —
reviving, on the same southwestern ground, the name of a state Silla
and Tang had destroyed together two and a half centuries before. The
revival was more than sentiment: it gave his revolt a historical claim
of its own, one distinct from the northern rebellion a rival bandit
leader named Kung-ye was building at the same time out of Goguryeo's
own former territory.
