Ali ibn Mahdi held Zabid for eighty-one days and died. His sons held it after
him, and what they inherited is described in one of the strangest inventories in
Yemeni history.

A man of Jibla told Umara that the wealth of twenty-five Yemeni dynasties had
ended up in Ibn Mahdi's treasury. The kingdom of the Sulaymani sharifs, the
fortresses of the Banu Wa'il who had ruled Wuhaza and were an old and noble
line, the strongholds of the surviving Sulayhids with their attached territories
and revenues, the Himyarite lordships of the southern mountains — all of it
converted into one hoard in one town on the coastal plain.

The Najahid share of it was extracted from the families. None of Fatik's slaves,
Umara says, nor the princesses of his house, nor the nobles of the dynasty died
without leaving large sums behind; and Ibn Mahdi came into possession of their
women and children, who told him where the gold and silver plate was, and the
pearls, and the jewels, and the clothes.

## What the Mahdids got and how long they kept it

Fourteen years.

In 1173 an Ayyubid army under Saladin's brother came down into Yemen, took
Zabid off Ibn Mahdi's son, took the highlands off what was left of the Sulayhid
successor houses, and put the whole country under one government for the first
time in a century and a half. The Tihama and the mountains had spent six
generations proving that neither could hold the other, and were then taken
together by a third party from Egypt in a single campaign.

Umara was writing while that was being prepared, and it has been said that the
expedition was undertaken on his advice — partly, according to the story, so
that Saladin's brother would be far from Cairo if the Fatimid restoration his
friends were plotting came off. If that is true, then the Yemeni exile who wrote
the history of these dynasties also arranged for the army that finished them,
and was hanged the following year by the man who sent it.

## Jayyash's history

The other thing the Najahids left is a book, and it is almost entirely lost.

Abu't-Tami Jayyash — the man who cut off Ali al-Sulayhi's head and came back
from India disguised as an Indian — wrote a history of Zabid, the *Kitab
al-Mufid*. A king in a dynasty of purchased soldiers who was also, on the
evidence of his own narrative, a good writer with an eye for the detail that
gives a scene away: the man who steps out of the hillocks and walks in front of
them until dawn, the exclamation he cannot stop himself making, the old vizier
running after him barefoot with his cloak trailing.

By the fourteenth century the book was already described as exceedingly rare. It
does not survive. What survives is the long stretch of it that Umara quoted
inside his own history, which is why the killing of the Sulayhid and the chess
games at Zabid can be read in the first person eight and a half centuries later.

So the record of a slave dynasty was written by one of its kings and preserved by
a lawyer of the town who was executed in Cairo for the wrong loyalty — and both
of them are the reason there is anything here beyond a list of names and dates.
