Ali al-Sulayhi rose in the Haraz mountains in 1047, took the highlands within a
few years, and then came for the coast.

In 1060 he had Najah poisoned — the accounts say by a slave girl sent for the
purpose — and took Zabid. Najah's sons got out to the Dahlak islands, in the Red
Sea off the Eritrean coast, and stayed there for seven years with no territory
on the mainland at all.

<Figure id="dahlak-space" />

Almost nothing comes back from that. A dynasty reduced to a garrison on an
island, with its city held by the man who murdered its founder, is normally
finished. The Najahids returned.

<Figure id="dahlak-tomb" />

In 1067 Ali al-Sulayhi set out on the pilgrimage with his wife Asma and an
escort, and Najah's son Sa'id al-Ahwal caught him on the road. Ali was killed,
his head was set on a spear outside Zabid where his widow could see it from the
house she was held in, and the Najahids had their city back. Ali's son
al-Mukarram raised the highlands, stormed Zabid the following year and got his
mother out, and the Najahids withdrew and came back again after he left.

That is the pattern for the next ninety years, and it is the reason both these
pages exist. Neither power could finish the other, because each held ground the
other could not occupy: the Sulayhids could come down the escarpment and could
not garrison a plain whose climate killed highlanders, and the Najahids could
hold the plain and could not go up. The division outlasted both dynasties. When
the Ayyubids arrived in 1173 they had to take Yemen twice, once at each altitude.

The doctrinal division mapped onto the geographical one exactly, which made it
harder to settle rather than easier. Zabid named the Abbasid caliph in the Friday
prayer and Sana'a named the Fatimid. A war between a Sunni coast and an Isma'ili
highland was also the local form of the quarrel between Baghdad and Cairo, and
both capitals had reason to keep it going.
