Ali ibn Mahdi rose at the fortress of ash-Sharaf, on the mountain wall above the
plain, and the place is half the explanation of what followed. To reach it you
went a day's journey up a narrow valley between two mountains and then half a
day up a torrent bed. The valley below opened into gorges in which an army could
lie hidden with its baggage for a month before anyone suspected it was there.

His method was not conquest. His raiders came down into a district of the
lowland, plundered it and burned it, and went back into the gorges at first
light, where nobody could reach them. His standing orders were to drive off the
cattle and the slaves and to kill the prisoners and any animal that could not
march. Umara, who met him in 1154 at the court of the prince of Aden and was
offered command of all his followers, says plainly what the orders were for:
they fed his men's greed, terrified his enemies, and ruined the country.

It worked. The people left. The land stopped being ploughed and the roads went
empty.

## The city

Then the chiefs of Zabid destroyed their own best officer. The qa'id Surur
al-Fatiki had risen higher than they could bear, and Ali ibn Mahdi arranged his
death in the summer of 1156. After that, in Umara's phrase, the closed gates of
evil were thrown open against the Abyssinian dynasty and the bonds of its
stability were unloosed.

Ibn Mahdi came down from the fortress to a place half a day from Zabid, and the
country people began going over to him — the more easily because his men kept
meeting brothers and cousins among the ploughmen and camel-drivers and
cattle-herds in Najahid service, and turned them.

The siege itself is the part Umara insists on, and he is not a writer with any
reason to flatter Zabid's rulers. He had been told by several men who watched it
that no people ever withstood an enemy so firmly. There were seventy-two
separate engagements. The citizens went on fighting as their numbers fell and
went on fighting when the hunger got bad enough that they were eating carrion.

## What they were asked to pay for help

In the end they sent to the Zaydi sharif at Sa'da, in the far northern
highlands, and asked him to save them.

He came, wanting the city. His terms were that they should make him king — and
that they should first kill their own lord, Fatik. They agreed. The slaves rose
against their master and killed him.

Umara sets out at that point exactly who had just been murdered by whom, in a
single line of descent: Fatik son of Jayyash son of Najah; and Najah the
freedman of Marjan, and Marjan the freedman of Husayn ibn Salama, and Husayn the
freedman of Rushd the steward, and Rushd the freedman of the house of Ziyad. Six
generations of purchased men, ending with a lord killed by his own slaves at the
request of a sharif who had promised to defend a city he could not defend.

He could not. The war went on, the citizens fell back behind their walls, and Ali
ibn Mahdi took Zabid on a Friday in Rajab of the year 554 and sat down in the
government house. Umara dates the fall precisely, to the fourteenth of that
month — the first days of August 1159 — where the conventional date given for
the end of the dynasty is the year before.

He had two months and twenty-one days to enjoy it, and then he died.
