The last Najahid century repeats the dynasty's own founding, which is either
irony or the ordinary working of the arrangement, depending on how much weight
one puts on the pattern.

The later sultans were young, or weak, or both, and the government passed to
their viziers — Mann Allah and after him others — who ruled Zabid in the
sultan's name and disposed of sultans who objected. It is precisely what the
Najahids had done to the Ziyadids in 1022, conducted by the same kind of men in
the same offices, and it took about the same length of time.

The end came from outside all of it. Ali ibn Mahdi was a preacher from the
highlands with a following raised on an austere and demanding doctrine — the
sources describe a movement more than a war band — and from the 1140s he
blockaded and raided the Tihama until Zabid could not feed itself. He took the
city in 1158 and founded the Mahdid dynasty, which held it for fifteen years.

This record's `ended` is `dynastic replacement` with an empty `by`, because the
Mahdids are not in this corpus. Neither are the Ziyadids the Najahids replaced,
which makes this page an entry with a gap at both ends — no predecessor edge and
no successor edge, in a region whose thread is `false` and does not require one.
Hard rule 7 covers it: a polity with no edge is complete. This one is complete
and slightly lonely.

What followed put an end to the whole arrangement. In 1173 Saladin's brother
Turanshah came down into Yemen with an Ayyubid army, took Zabid from the Mahdids
and the highlands from what was left of the Sulayhid successors, and the country
was ruled by one power for the first time in a century and a half. The corpus
picks that up on the Ayyubid page, and the dynasty the Ayyubids left behind when
they went home is the Rasulid record two pages over.
