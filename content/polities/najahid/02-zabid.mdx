The Tihama is the coastal plain between the Red Sea and the mountain
escarpment: hot, humid, low, and — where the seasonal floods coming off the
highlands can be spread across it — very productive. Zabid sits in the middle of
that, on the Wadi Zabid, in country that grew grain, cotton, indigo and dates,
and the dynasty on this page lived on it.

The city was the intellectual centre of lowland Yemen for six hundred years. Its
Shafi'i legal schools drew students from across the Indian Ocean, and the claim
that the algebraic notation later used in the Maghreb passed through Zabid is
made in the literature on the subject. Under the Najahids and their successors
it accumulated a density of mosques and madrasas that has left it a
UNESCO-listed town and, since the 1990s, a heavily endangered one.

The Red Sea is the other half. The ports below Zabid — Ghulafiqa and others —
took a share of the traffic between Aden and Egypt, and the dynasty's connection
to the Dahlak islands off the African coast, which mattered greatly in 1060, was
a trading connection before it was a refuge. This record's `revenue_basis` field
carries `land-tax` alone and mentions the ports only in this paragraph, because
nothing consulted here quantifies what the Najahids took from them and the site
does not code a value it cannot support.

What the dynasty did not have was the highlands. Yemen's political geography is
vertical: the coastal plain and the mountains above it are different climates,
different economies and, for most of recorded history, different states. A
Tihama power could be rich and could not be safe, because the escarpment
overlooks it and an army coming down has the advantage of height and can go back
up.

In 1047 a man came down it.
