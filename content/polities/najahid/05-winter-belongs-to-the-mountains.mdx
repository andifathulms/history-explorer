For a long stretch of Jayyash's reign the Tihama had two governments a year, and
both of them knew it and worked out the accounting.

The Sulayhid prince Saba ibn Ahmad held the fortresses on the escarpment
directly above the coastal plain — closer to it than any other mountain
stronghold in Yemen — and his Arabs came down every year at the approach of
winter. Jayyash would withdraw when they did, though never far. Saba would
collect the revenues of the plain through the winter and the spring. When the
weather turned the Arabs went back up the mountain, sometimes fighting their way
and sometimes not, and Jayyash re-entered his city.

On his return the Qur'ans were opened, his subjects prayed for his prosperity,
and the jurists came out to ask God for the prolongation of his reign.

## The convention

Here is the part that is genuinely strange, and it is recorded on both sides.

When Saba settled accounts with the tax collectors, he made allowance for what
Jayyash had already taken during the summer and the autumn. And when Jayyash
settled with his governors and collectors, he made allowance for what Saba had
taken over the winter and the spring.

Neither of them taxed a village twice for the same year. Two states at war,
each with a claim on the whole plain and a doctrine that made the other a
heretic, had arrived at a working revenue convention, and Saba is separately
noted for not letting his men squeeze the people while he had them.

It is a picture of what a war looks like when both sides have concluded, without
saying so, that it will not be won. The fighting was real and so was the
bookkeeping.

## How it broke

It broke from the inside, as these things do.

The vizier Khalf ibn Abi Tahir — the same man who had gone with Jayyash to India
and helped put him back on the seat — advised Jayyash's son to imprison his
father, seize the treasury, and appoint a different vizier. The son did it.
Then Khalf broke a hole through the wall of his own prison, got out, and went
over to Saba, who received him well; and from the mountain he spent his time
telling Saba exactly how the Tihama could be ruined.

The arrangement was renegotiated, on advice, into a fixed sum paid to Saba in
place of the seasonal half. The effect was the opposite of what was intended.
Once the Arabs were being paid rather than fighting for it, their appetite for
the country grew and they began to think themselves secure in it.

They were not. A freedman of Sa'id ibn Najah named Rayhan al-Kahlani came on
them at night by the gate of Zabid with ten thousand men, against three thousand
horse and ten thousand foot drawn up in the open, and destroyed them. Almost all
were speared. Saba escaped on foot among the fugitives and was picked up by a
rescue party towards morning.

The Arabs did not come back down to the Tihama after that. The plain was the
Najahids' again, entire and all year — and the dynasty that had it spent its
remaining decades with its sultans in the hands of their own viziers, which is
the subject of the next chapter and was already the pattern when Jayyash's son
locked him up on a vizier's advice.
