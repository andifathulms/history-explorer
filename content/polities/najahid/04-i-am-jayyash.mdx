The fullest account of how the Najahids got their city back was written by the
man who took it, and it survives because a Yemeni historian a century later
copied it out at length.

Jayyash, son of Najah, was on Dahlak with his family after Ali al-Sulayhi had
driven them off the mainland. The eldest brother killed himself there. Jayyash
studied. His full brother Sa'id — al-Ahwal, the squint-eyed — quarrelled with
him over a piece of treachery against the island's prince that Jayyash had
stopped, went to Zabid alone, hid in the house of a man of the lower orders
named Mula'ib who was loyal to the family, and dug himself a hiding place in
the man's grounds. From it he wrote to his brother that the Sulayhids were
about to fall and to come at once.

Sa'id rose with seventy men. Not one of them had a horse, and they had no
weapons but iron nails mounted on palm-sticks. On the way out of the city they
met a mounted soldier, killed him, and Sa'id took the horse.

## The march

They went by the coast road to avoid the royal highway, expecting to be
intercepted. Word had run ahead of them anyway: al-Sulayhi had already sent five
thousand Abyssinian spearmen — most of them, Jayyash says, the Najahids' own
servants and kinsmen — with orders to bring him the heads of the squint-eyed
Sa'id and his brother. There was hardly a mosque or market or road in the
country where the rising was not being discussed, and Sa'id had been swearing
publicly that he was the master of the day.

They walked barefoot, day and night, with the one horse led among them, Sa'id
calling out that tomorrow's sun would not rise on his enemy still alive. One
night, lost in a valley, a man stepped out from behind the hillocks, told them
they had strayed, and walked in front of them until daybreak, when they lost
sight of him.

At the camp they were taken for al-Sulayhi's own servants and nobody looked at
them.

## The well

What broke Ali al-Sulayhi was not the attack. It was a sentence.

His brother recognised the raiders and shouted for him to mount. Al-Sulayhi
answered that he was fated to die nowhere but at ad-Duhaym and the well of Umm
Ma'bad, and he had always understood that to mean the famous well of that name
far to the north, where the Prophet had halted on the flight to Medina. Then one
of his own men said: defend yourself, for that is the well of Duhaym ibn 'Abs,
and that mosque stands where the tent of Umm Ma'bad stood.

He never moved from where he was standing. They struck off his head with his
own sword — Jayyash says he struck first, and one of Najah's slaves speared him
— and Jayyash mounted the head on the shaft of the royal umbrella, ordered the
drums beaten, and took the dead man's horse.

Al-Sulayhi's brother charged and killed several of them before he was pulled
down. *Kill us both*, said the man grappling him, *for my people will rejoice
that I did not die at the hands of the vile.* Sa'id ran them both through with
one spear.

## Asma, and the advice not taken

Sa'id rode to the mosque where al-Sulayhi's wife was and told her to come out
and greet the two sultans. She came out with her face uncovered and answered him
with a line of pre-Islamic verse: none tramples so insolently as the feeble
boaster, none is so arrogant in humbling as the man who has been abased.

Then Jayyash gave his brother advice, and recorded it, and recorded that it was
refused. Spare the Lady Asma, he said, and the hundred and seventy Sulayhid
princes travelling with her, and the thirty-five Qahtani chiefs; send her back to
her son with a letter saying the feud is satisfied and the kingdom recovered. Do
that and the tribes will not dispute your rule over the Tihama. Refuse and their
sense of wounded honour will bring them down on you.

Sa'id answered with a proverb about not sparing a viper once you have crushed
her tail, and had them all killed. Jayyash watched an old man try to shelter
behind his son and a spear go through both of them.

He never forgot, he says, the sight of the head on the umbrella shaft, or the
Qur'an reader's voice at that moment: *Thou givest sovereignty unto whom Thou
willest, and Thou takest it from whom Thou willest.* Nor a court poet's couplet
improvised on the spot, on how much better the man's head looked on the canopy's
stem than his face had looked beneath its shade.

Three days later Sa'id entered Zabid with two thousand horses and three thousand
camels of plunder, the two heads carried before Asma's litter. He lodged her in
a house and had the heads set up facing her window.

## The chess player

Sa'id's triumph did not last. The Sulayhid queen contrived his death some years
later, and Jayyash went to India for six months with his vizier, then came back
to take Zabid on his own.

He did it in disguise. He sent the vizier ahead to spread the news that he had
died in India and to ask for a personal amnesty, then came down from the
highlands got up as an Indian, with his beard spread over his face, his hair and
nails long, and one eye covered with a black cloth. He took lodgings near the
palace and started sitting each morning on the bench outside the gate of Ali ibn
al-Kumm, vizier to the governor — a man he had heard say, in a temper, that if
he could find a dog of the family of Najah he would make him king of Zabid.

The vizier's son challenged the Indian to chess and lost, and went in furious to
tell his father, who said that nobody at Zabid had ever beaten him except
Jayyash son of Najah, and Jayyash was dead in India. The old man came out and
played the Indian himself. Jayyash, careful now, played him to a draw, and was
taken into the household as a friend.

He nearly gave himself away twice. Struck by the end of a whip one day he let
out the exclamation he always made when startled — *I am Abu't-Tami* — which is
his own kunya, and had to claim on the spot that his name was Bahr. The second
time was final. Playing the son again, deliberately losing to keep the father
sweet, he let the young man crow, and the young man reached over and pulled the
black cloth off his eye. Jayyash stood up in a rage and said: *I am Jayyash.*

The old vizier ran after him barefoot with his cloak trailing, produced a copy
of the Qur'an, and swore an oath on it; Jayyash swore one back. That night the
vizier had al-Sulayhi's own palace, the Dar al-'Izz, emptied, carpeted and
curtained, and installed the Indian slave girl Jayyash had bought in India in
it. Jayyash entered it after dark and found that she had given birth to his son
al-Fatik between sunset and evening prayer — on the night, he says, that a dream
had promised him the kingdom back.

Five thousand Abyssinian spearmen were already in and around the city, summoned
by letter and paid with ten thousand dinars called in from a debt.

## Which year

The date of the killing is not settled, and the disagreement is worth stating
because it moves everything around it by fourteen years.

Umara gives 12 Dhu'l-Qa'da 473, which is April 1081, in the passage that
narrates the ambush, and Jayyash's own account gives the ninth of the same month
of the same year for the departure from Zabid. One line in Umara's text says
instead that the year was 459 — 1067 — and that this is the correct version. Two
other chroniclers give 459; two more, and Umara everywhere else, give 473.

Kay, who edited and translated the text, argued that the sentence preferring 459
is a later interpolation and that a comparison of dates requires 473. Modern
accounts generally keep 1067, and that is the year given elsewhere here. The
eyewitness narrative belongs to the other.
