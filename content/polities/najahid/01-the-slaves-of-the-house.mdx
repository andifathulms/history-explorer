Zabid was founded in 820 as the seat of an Abbasid governor, Muhammad ibn
Ziyad, sent to hold the Yemeni coastal plain for Baghdad. The Ziyadids he
founded ruled it for two centuries and are not on this site, which is a gap this
page is standing next to rather than filling.

What happened to them is the thing that happened to a great many dynasties in
this corpus, and the site has recorded it from Samarra onwards. The later
Ziyadids left the government to their servants. The servants were Abyssinian
slaves — bought across the strait from the Horn of Africa, which is the natural
slave frontier for a Red Sea polity in the way the Kipchak steppe was for Egypt
— and by the end of the tenth century one of them, Husayn ibn Salama, was the
effective ruler and is remembered for building mosques and cisterns and
milestones the length of the Tihama.

In 1022 another of them, Najah, ended the pretence. He removed the last Ziyadid,
took the state, obtained a diploma from the Abbasid caliph and ruled Zabid in
his own name for thirty-eight years.

This site's Mamluk page argues that the slave-soldier system's recurring failure
is that an army which can make a ruler eventually makes one it prefers. The
Najahid case is the same mechanism with the frontier moved. The men were bought
in Africa rather than on the steppe, the state was a coastal plain rather than
an empire, and the sequence is identical: purchase, promotion, the office of
vizier, and then the throne.

The page records the coding `slave-soldier` on that basis, and it is worth
noticing where the value now appears in this corpus. Egypt under the Tulunids,
Ikhshidids, Ayyubids and Mamluks. Syria under the Zangids and the Burids.
Khurasan under the Ghaznavids. Yemen under the Rasulids. And here, in 1022,
earlier than most of them, on the other side of the Red Sea from where the
literature usually looks.
