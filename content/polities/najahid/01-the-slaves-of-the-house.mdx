Zabid was founded in 820 as the seat of an Abbasid governor, Muhammad ibn
Ziyad, sent to hold the Yemeni coastal plain for Baghdad. The Ziyadids he
founded ruled it for two centuries.

What happened to them happened to a great many dynasties in this period. The later
Ziyadids left the government to their servants. The servants were Abyssinian
slaves — bought across the strait from the Horn of Africa, which is the natural
slave frontier for a Red Sea polity in the way the Kipchak steppe was for Egypt
— and by the end of the tenth century one of them, Husayn ibn Salama, was the
effective ruler and is remembered for building mosques and cisterns and
milestones the length of the Tihama.

<Figure id="great-mosque" />

In 1022 another of them, Najah, ended the pretence. He removed the last Ziyadid,
took the state, obtained a diploma from the Abbasid caliph and ruled Zabid in
his own name for thirty-eight years.

The recurring failure of a state whose army is bought is that an army which can
make a ruler eventually makes one it prefers. The Najahid case is that mechanism
with the frontier moved. The men were bought in Africa rather than on the
steppe, the state was a coastal plain rather than an empire, and the sequence is
identical: purchase, promotion, the office of vizier, and then the throne.

Egypt went the same way under four successive houses, Syria under two, Khurasan
under the Ghaznavids. What is unusual about Zabid is the date. This happened in
1022, earlier than most of them, on the wrong side of the Red Sea from where the
subject is usually studied.
