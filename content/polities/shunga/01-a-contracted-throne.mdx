Bana, writing at Harsha's court eight centuries later with access to records
no longer known to survive, gives the coup its own line: "reviewing the
whole army, under the pretext of showing him his forces, the mean general
Pushpamitra crushed his master, Brihadratha the Maurya, who was weak of
purpose." Pushyamitra imprisoned the dead emperor's minister, took the
throne for himself, and founded the dynasty history knows as the Shungas.

## What was left to inherit

The empire Pushyamitra took was not the one Ashoka had ruled. Pataliputra
remained the capital, and the central Gangetic provinces — what would
later be called Bihar, Tirhut, and the United Provinces around Agra and
Oudh — still answered to it, with authority extending south as far as the
Narmada river. The north-west, lost piece by piece since Ashoka's death,
was gone; there is no evidence the Shungas, any more than the last weak
Mauryas before them, ever exercised authority in the Punjab.

## An epigraphic footnote and a literary witness

Almost nothing survives from the dynasty's own hand. A Barhut inscription
opens with the formula "during the reign of the Sungas," which is the one
contemporary epigraphic confirmation that the dynastic name itself was
current at the time rather than a later label imposed by chroniclers.
Beyond that single line, what is known of the Shungas comes overwhelmingly
from later literature — the Puranas, and Bana's romance composed for an
entirely different king's court centuries afterward — rather than from
anything the dynasty inscribed about itself.

A general who had killed his own emperor took a kingdom already reduced
from its Mauryan extent, kept the old capital, and left almost no record of
his own reign except the word of the dramatist and the chronicler who came
long after him.
