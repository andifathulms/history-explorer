Having survived a Greek army at his own gates, Pushyamitra — by now an old
man, with a grown grandson already serving in the field — set out to make
the survival formal. He revived the ashvamedha, the horse-sacrifice, an
ancient rite that only a paramount sovereign was entitled to perform, and
whose performance was itself the proof of the claim.

## How the rite worked

A horse of a particular colour was consecrated and turned loose to wander
for a year. Wherever it went, the ruler of that territory faced a choice:
fight the army following behind it, or submit. If the horse's guardians
secured submission everywhere it wandered, its owner returned in triumph
with the defeated rulers in his train, and the horse was ceremonially
sacrificed at a great festival marking the claim as proven. If they
failed, the whole enterprise ended in humiliation rather than glory.

## The grandson's part

Command of the guard following the horse was given, at least nominally, to
Pushyamitra's young grandson Vasumitra — the same prince who had served in
the field during his father Agnimitra's viceroyalty in the southern
provinces. Vasumitra encountered and routed a band of Yavanas, western
foreigners, who challenged the horse on the banks of the river Sindhu,
between what is now Bundelkhand and the Rajputana states — quite possibly
a detachment of the same Greek force that had besieged Madhyamika during
Menander's invasion, still operating in the region rather than having
withdrawn entirely.

## What the rite settled, and for whom

With the Yavanas defeated and no other rival left standing, Pushyamitra
carried out the sacrifice at his capital with the magnificence tradition
demanded, and — at least in the account preserved through Bana's drama —
was formally justified in his claim to rank as paramount power of northern
India. It was a claim resting entirely on recent military success rather
than on birth: a general who had killed his own emperor to take the throne
proved his right to it, in the end, the same way he had taken it — by
force, formally staged and ritually confirmed, with his own grandson doing
the fighting that made the ceremony more than theatre.
