Pushyamitra has a reputation, in a certain strand of later Buddhist
writing, as a persecutor who destroyed monasteries and put a price on the
head of every Buddhist monk in his kingdom. Smith, weighing the sources
available to him, finds the specific charge against Pushyamitra himself
unproven — but the reason it is worth examining at length is not that the
individual claim is safe to dismiss. It is that the question of whether
religious persecution happened at all in ancient India was, in his own
day, a live scholarly argument, and Pushyamitra sits right at the middle of
it.

## A debate about the wrong default

Rhys Davids had argued that organised religious persecution was essentially
foreign to Indian civilisation before the Muslim conquests — a claim
Hodgson, Sewell and Watters all disputed with specific counter-examples.
The case against Pushyamitra by name does not hold up well under that
scrutiny. But the case that persecution never happened at all holds up
considerably worse, once other well-attested instances are set beside it.

## The instances that are not in doubt

Shashanka, the king of Bengal whose murder of Harsha's brother sits at the
opening of the following century's history, is independently and
repeatedly described as a Buddhist-persecuting king by Xuanzang — a
near-contemporary witness with no obvious motive to invent the charge.
Outside India entirely, Tibetan chronicles record a persecution of
Buddhism under King Langdarma around 840 AD, and the Khotan annals
preserve a comparable episode. In southern India, a documented persecution
of Jainism is recorded independently of any of the northern material.

## What that comparison is for

None of those cases makes the specific accusation against Pushyamitra any
more credible on its own evidence. What it does is correct the shape of
the question. The debate was never really about whether an Indian king
could persecute a religious minority — plainly some did, in well-attested
cases spanning six centuries and three regions. The debate is about
whether this particular accusation, against this particular king, made
by sources writing well after his death and with their own reasons to
resent the dynasty that killed a Buddhist-tolerant emperor to found itself,
is one of the true cases or one of the invented ones.

Pushyamitra's reputation for persecution turns out to be a live question
precisely because the category it belongs to is real. That is a harder
thing to settle than either "the Shungas never touched Buddhism" or "the
Shungas destroyed it" — and it is the honest answer the evidence actually
supports.
