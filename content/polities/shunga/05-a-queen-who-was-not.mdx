Pushyamitra died about five years after Menander's retreat, having reigned
long and eventfully, and was succeeded by his son Agnimitra — the same
prince who had governed the southern provinces as viceroy throughout his
father's wars. Agnimitra reigned only a few years. What followed was a
sequence of short, disordered reigns that reads less like a dynasty than
like a list of casualties.

## The actor's death

Sujyeshtha, probably Agnimitra's brother, succeeded him and was followed
seven years later by Vasumitra — the same grandson who had guarded the
sacrificial horse against the Yavanas as a young man. After Vasumitra came
four reigns so short that all four together total only seventeen years,
strong evidence of a court in continual crisis. One incident from this
period survives in specific enough detail to have been remembered on its
own merits: Sumitra, another son of Agnimitra, "inordinately devoted to
the stage," was surprised in the middle of a performance by his own
attendants, absorbed among his favourite actors, when a man named
Mitradeva "severed his head with a scimitar, as a lotus is shorn from its
stalk."

## The tenth king

The ninth king, Bhagavata, is credited with a long twenty-six-year reign
about which nothing else is recorded. The tenth and last, Devabhuti — also
given as Devabhumi — is remembered only for licentious habits and for the
manner of his death: killed while engaged in what Bana's account calls a
discreditable intrigue.

The plot that ended him had been arranged by his own Brahmin minister,
Vasudeva Kanva, who by this point was already running the state in all but
name behind a dissolute figurehead. Vasudeva had a slave-woman disguised
as the queen sent to the king "in a frenzy of passion" — Bana's own
phrase — and it was she, at the minister's instance, who took his life.

## The name Mitradeva shares

The man who had beheaded Prince Sumitra mid-performance, years earlier, was
very probably a member of the same family that now finished the dynasty
off: Mitradeva and Vasudeva Kanva both belonging, on the evidence of the
name, to the house of the Kanvas — meaning the same family had been
positioned close enough to the Shunga throne to assassinate a prince and,
a generation later, to murder the last king who sat on it and take the
throne for themselves. Contemporary opinion, Smith notes, held that the
later Shunga kings held little real power and were puppets in Brahmin
ministers' hands — an arrangement not unlike the Maratha rajas' later
relationship to their own Peshwas. The dynasty founded by an army
commander who killed his emperor at a troop review ended a hundred and
twelve years later, murdered in bed by the family that had been quietly
running it for some time already.
