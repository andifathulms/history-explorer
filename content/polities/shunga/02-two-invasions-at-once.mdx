---
title: Two invasions at once
drafted_from: smith-1904

In the later years of Pushyamitra's reign, the dynasty he had founded by
killing one emperor came close to being unmade from two directions at once.

## From the west

Menander, a relative of the Bactrian king Eucratides and ruler of Kabul and
the Punjab, set out to match Alexander's own exploits and advanced deep
into Indian territory with a formidable army. He annexed the Indus delta
and the peninsula of Kathiawar, occupied Mathura on the Jumna, besieged the
town of Madhyamika near modern Chitor in Rajasthan, invested Saketa in
southern Oudh — and, by the time his advance was finally checked, was
threatening Pataliputra itself.

## From the east

At close to the same moment, Kharavela, the Jain king of Kalinga on the Bay
of Bengal coast, invaded Magadha from the other direction. His own
inscription, cut into the rock of the Hathigumpha cave at Udayagiri and
badly damaged but still legible in outline, claims he humbled the king of
Magadha and advanced as far as the Ganges. Whatever ground he actually
took, the advantage does not appear to have lasted, and the campaign
reads, from what survives of the record, as a raid on the eastern frontier
rather than a lasting conquest.

## What held

Menander's was the graver threat, and it was repelled only after a severe
struggle — Pataliputra's approaches fought over directly rather than a
distant province being quietly ceded. The Greek king retired to his own
territory, though he appears to have kept his western Indian conquests for
some years afterward rather than surrendering them along with the eastern
campaign. It was, as it turned out, the last time a European general would
attempt to conquer India by land. Every invasion that followed, down to
Vasco da Gama's arrival by sea in 1502, came from the water rather than
across the passes — which makes the defence of Pataliputra in the 150s BC
the final chapter of a story that had opened with Alexander at the
Hyphasis a century and a half before, and the Shungas, for one campaign,
the ones who closed it.
