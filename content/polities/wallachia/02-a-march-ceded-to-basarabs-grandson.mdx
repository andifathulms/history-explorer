Basarab's own break with Hungary did not settle the relationship
between Wallachia and its northern neighbor for good; it only changed
its terms. Louis the Great, who succeeded his father Charles Robert on
the Hungarian throne in 1342 and built a kingdom Seton-Watson credits
with reaching further and more successfully than any Hungarian
foreign policy before or after, spent much of his reign constructing a
belt of frontier marches along his kingdom's southern and eastern
edges rather than attempting outright conquest of the lands beyond
the Carpathians. One of those marches, the district of Făgăraș, he
granted to Vladislav - called Vlaicu in the chronicles, Basarab's own
grandson and Wallachia's ruler by the 1360s - in the hope of drawing
Wallachia into Hungary's own orbit of influence.

## An alliance forged at Vidin

The clearest evidence of what that grant bought Hungary came in 1365,
when Louis, alarmed at the Ottoman capital's transfer from Bursa to
Adrianople that same year, led a rapid expedition to seize Vidin from
its Bulgarian tsar rather than let him drift into Ottoman vassalage as
a neighboring Bulgarian principality already had. The campaign won
Louis the immediate alliance of Vlaicu's Wallachia - the first
recorded encounter, in Seton-Watson's own account, between Hungarian
and Ottoman forces in the region - though Vidin itself was recovered
by the Bulgarians within four years, and Louis's attention turned
increasingly to Poland, which he inherited by treaty in 1370, and to a
crusade against the Ottomans that the papacy's own hesitation left
unfunded and unfought.

## A church that chose Constantinople over Rome

Louis's ambitions for Wallachia were not only territorial. The
Hungarian crown actively promoted Catholicism among the Wallachian
nobility, and Basarab's own family had at times leaned toward Rome
under direct marriage and diplomatic pressure, encouraged by Pope
Urban V himself. Political calculation won out over that religious
courtship: in 1359, under Basarab's son and successor Nicolae
Alexandru, Vlaicu's own father, the Wallachian ruling house appealed
instead to Constantinople and secured the creation of a Metropolitan
of Ungro-Wallachia, an Orthodox hierarchy independent of the Bulgarian
bishoprics of Silistra and Vidin it had previously answered to. It was
a choice that placed Wallachia's own religious institutions decisively
outside the sphere of the Catholic patron whose military marches
would, within a few years, be drawing the principality politically
closer under Nicolae Alexandru's own son.
