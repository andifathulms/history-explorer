The scattered political units south and east of the Carpathians in the
thirteenth century - kniezates and voivodeships, some of them recognized
by the Hungarian crown as military outposts against non-Christian
raiders - offer no clean single moment a historian can point to and call
the founding of Wallachia. Voivode Litovoi confronted the Hungarian king
directly in the 1280s and died in the fighting; his brother Bărbat,
captured, paid a considerable ransom for his own freedom. By the turn of
the century, the various provinces between the mountains and the Danube
had come under one authority centered on Argeș - a dominion whose own
first ruler is named, in a document dating only from 1332, as a certain
Tihomir. That the document naming Wallachia's earliest known ruler
postdates the state's actual founding battle by two years is one of the
smaller oddities of how this history survives.

## A vassal who stopped acting like one

Tihomir's successor, Basarab, inherited a principality still formally
subject to the Hungarian crown - a relationship that, as late as 1324, was
proceeding on ordinary terms according to a surviving document. The
following year, a Cuman count's son was denounced to the Hungarian king
for having praised Basarab's own power as beyond challenge and called him
"unfaithful to the Sacred Crown." Tensions escalated further once the
Papacy itself began praising Basarab as a defender against non-Christian
peoples, directly undercutting the Hungarian king's own attempts to paint
him as an ally of the Turks and Tatars instead.

## Four days in a pass that changed names

War came in 1330. The Hungarian king, Charles Robert, led an army south
to bring Basarab back into line, and the two forces met in a mountain
pass for four days of fighting, from the 9th to the 12th of October. The
Hungarian court's own chronicle, written within a generation of the
event to legitimize the succeeding dynasty, describes the rout in vivid
terms: Romanian forces raining arrows down from the ravines onto a
Hungarian column trapped on a road too narrow to maneuver, "like fish in
the net," with princes and noblemen falling alongside common soldiers.
Charles Robert himself barely escaped, and lost his own royal seal in the
retreat.

The battle's traditional name, Posada, does not precisely match the
place named in later documents, and its exact location remains disputed
among historians to this day - a detail easy to lose beneath the larger
fact the battle actually established. Whatever its precise geography, the
victory ended Hungarian authority over the land Basarab now ruled outright,
and later chroniclers and charter-writers alike came to treat it as the
moment a new, independent state had come into being.
