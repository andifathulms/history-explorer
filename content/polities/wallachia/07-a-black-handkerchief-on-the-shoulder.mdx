Constantin Brâncoveanu ruled Wallachia for twenty-six years, longer
than any prince in two centuries, by doing what most of his
predecessors had tried and failed to do at once: play Vienna,
Moscow and Constantinople against each other without committing
fully to any of them. It worked until Russia entered the game
directly. In 1711, as Tsar Peter the Great marched into Moldavia
hoping to rally the Christian Balkans against the Ottomans,
Moldavia's own prince, the scholar-ruler Dimitrie Cantemir, gambled
everything on Russia and signed a secret treaty guaranteeing his
principality's independence and his own hereditary throne. Brâncoveanu,
who had made Peter nearly identical promises, hedged instead - keeping
the Russian army supplied while simultaneously sending the Ottoman
grand vizier a substantial cash payment and assurances of further
tribute to come.

## A gamble that failed, and a caution that didn't save him

Peter's own gamble collapsed first. Overextended and outnumbered five
to one, his army was trapped at Stanilesti on the Pruth on 11 July
1711, and only survived through frantic bribery of the Ottoman high
command. Cantemir fled into permanent Russian exile - he would go on
to help found the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences and never see
Moldavia again - and the Porte replaced him with a Greek client prince
from Constantinople's own Phanar district, Nicolas Mavrocordato, ending
native Moldavian rule outright. Brâncoveanu's own caution bought him
three more years, not permanent safety. Compromised by intercepted
correspondence with both Vienna and Moscow, and isolated after
quarreling with his own Cantacuzene relatives, he had run out of
protectors by the spring of 1714.

## An execution meant to be watched

On 4 April 1714, an Ottoman emissary arrived at Bucharest, threw a
black handkerchief over Brâncoveanu's shoulder to announce his
deposition, and had him and his family marched to Constantinople two
days later while the population of his own capital followed the
carriage in open, helpless lamentation. Imprisoned in the Seven
Towers and offered his life for a ransom of ten million piastres far
beyond his means, Brâncoveanu was executed with his two sons and
his son-in-law before the sultan himself. His youngest son, sixteen
years old, begged to convert to Islam rather than die; Brâncoveanu,
by a French traveler's own eyewitness account, told him to die a
Christian instead. All were beheaded, their bodies thrown into the
sea and their heads displayed at the palace gate for three days.

## What replaced native rule

The Porte's decision, in the aftermath, was not to annex Wallachia
outright but to govern it the way it had already begun governing
Moldavia: through Greek Phanariote princes with no independent power
base of their own and no incentive to intrigue with Vienna or Moscow
the way native boyar dynasties repeatedly had. What followed, across
the next century, was rule by a rotating cast of appointees answering
directly to Constantinople rather than to any local constituency -
the arrangement that would define both Danubian principalities until
the century's end.
