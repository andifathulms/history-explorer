Mircea took Wallachia's throne in 1386 - according to one persistent
tradition, by killing his own brother for it, a violent succession
common enough in the region at the time that it drew little particular
comment. What set his reign apart was not how he obtained the throne but
how long, and how skillfully, he held it against an enemy no earlier
Wallachian ruler had faced: the Ottoman sultans, whose armies crossed the
Danube for the first time during his own reign.

## A battle nobody can quite date either

Wallachian troops had already fought the Ottomans once, alongside the
Serbs at the disastrous field of Kosovo in 1389, before Sultan Bayezid I
sent an army across the Danube to punish Mircea directly for that
alliance. What followed, remembered in Romanian tradition as the Battle
of Rovine, is nearly as difficult to pin down as Posada had been six
decades earlier - historians still dispute whether it happened in
October 1394 or the following spring, and even whether there was one
battle or two. What survives clearly enough is the outcome: Ottoman
chronicles themselves describe a sultan who fled the field, returning
only years later to take his revenge.

## Tribute, an alliance, and a defeat that outlasted it

The revenge came in the form of a punitive campaign that defeated Mircea
outright, and in 1391 Wallachia was entered on the Sublime Porte's own
registers as a tributary state - required to pay an annual sum, in
exchange for keeping its own laws and its ruler's right to make war and
peace independently. Mircea did not treat the arrangement as final. He
allied with Sigismund of Hungary, his own longtime rival, against their
common Ottoman enemy, and the two fought together at Nicopolis in 1396 -
a battle remembered less for Wallachian conduct than for the Western
knights' refusal to heed Mircea's own tactical advice, a decision that
cost the crusading army the battle outright. When the Ottomans followed
Mircea back across the Danube afterward expecting an easy finish, his own
army routed them instead.

## A kingmaker among the men who had just conquered him

Bayezid's catastrophic defeat and capture by Timur at Ankara in 1402
handed Mircea an opportunity no Wallachian ruler had held before or would
hold again for generations: a fractured Ottoman succession he could
actively shape. With Hungary's own king having lost interest in the
Ottoman question in favor of chasing the Holy Roman crown, Mircea acted
largely alone, backing a series of rival claimants to the Ottoman throne
- first Musa, to whom he married his own daughter, then Orhan, then
Mustafa - in a sustained attempt to keep the empire that had already
defeated him once too divided to threaten Wallachia again. None of the
candidates held the throne permanently, and Mehmed I's eventual
reunification of Ottoman territory forced Mircea to accept tributary
status a second time before his death in 1418. He had, in the end,
bought his country decades of relative freedom of action against an
empire that would, within another generation of his death, leave his
successors far less room to maneuver in.
