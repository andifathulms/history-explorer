For much of the nineteenth century, the standard English-language account
of how Wallachia began looked nothing like Basarab's own documented break
with Hungary. William Miller's 1896 history, drawing on the popular
tradition of its own day, instead credits a leader named Radu Negru -
"Rudolph the Black" - who is said to have descended from the Carpathians
around 1290 and established his own rule over the plain below, a full
generation before Basarab's own reign is attested in any surviving
document.

## A legend with no documentary anchor

No charter, chronicle, or archaeological find ties Radu Negru to an actual
historical person the way Basarab's own 1324 dispute with Hungary, or the
1330 battle that followed it, can be tied to dated documents. The Radu
Negru tradition belongs to the same genre as Moldavia's own founding
legend of Dragoș and his hound - a story about the deep past told for
generations before anyone wrote it down, shaped more by what a later
audience wanted its own origins to sound like than by anything that can
be independently verified.

## Why the newer account displaced the older one

Modern scholarship has largely set the Radu Negru tradition aside in favor
of the Basarab reconstruction precisely because the latter rests on
contemporary or near-contemporary written evidence: the 1324 Hungarian
document recording the dispute, the 1325 denunciation naming Basarab
directly, the Hungarian court chronicle's own account of the 1330 defeat.
None of this documentary trail exists for Radu Negru, whose story
survives only in later retellings with no fourteenth-century source
behind it at all.

The two accounts are not simply competing claims to the same throne. They
are evidence of two different kinds of memory - one built from charters
and chronicles that can be dated and cross-checked, the other carried
forward as popular tradition long after the documents that might have
confirmed or refuted it had either been lost or never existed in the
first place. That popular tradition proved durable enough to still be
the standard telling in an English history written more than five
centuries after Basarab's own reign says something about how much more
easily a good story travels than an archival dispute.
