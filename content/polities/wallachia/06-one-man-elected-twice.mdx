title: One man elected twice

Four centuries after Radu's Ottoman-backed accession deepened Wallachia's
own subordination to the Porte, the country's separate existence ended
not in conquest but in a single carefully engineered coincidence. Under
the 1858 Paris Convention, the great powers of Europe had granted
Wallachia and Moldavia the right to elect their own princes separately,
while stopping well short of authorizing an outright union between the
two principalities - a union the Ottoman Porte, still the two countries'
nominal suzerain, had no interest in seeing happen at all.

## A loophole used exactly once

The separate assemblies of Moldavia and Wallachia found the loophole the
Convention had left them. In January 1859, Moldavia's assembly elected
Alexandru Ioan Cuza as its own prince; weeks later, Wallachia's assembly
elected the same man as its own prince in turn. Nothing in the letter of
the Paris Convention had been violated - each principality had exercised
precisely the right of separate election the powers had granted it - but
the practical result was a union of the two thrones under one ruler that
no one had formally authorized and the Ottoman Porte, in particular, had
never intended to permit.

## A recognition that stopped short of a precedent

The Porte's own response, delivered through a firman in December 1859,
recognized Cuza's double rule while pointedly confining that recognition
to Cuza's own person and this single occasion, rather than establishing
any lasting right of union between the two principalities. The Porte was,
in effect, acknowledging an accomplished fact it lacked the practical
power to reverse, while trying to preserve the fiction that Wallachia and
Moldavia remained formally separate tributary states answering to
Constantinople independently of each other.

## An ending with no battle behind it

Full administrative union followed in 1862, folding the two
principalities' governments into a single state that would, within a
few years, take the name Romania. Wallachia's own separate existence -
five centuries old, built on a battle whose location nobody can precisely
name and defended, at its most desperate hour, by a prince whose own
reputation the country never asked for - ended not through defeat but
through a legal technicality two assemblies had the nerve to exploit at
exactly the same moment.
