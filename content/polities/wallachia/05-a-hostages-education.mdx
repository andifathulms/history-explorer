By the 1440s, the room to maneuver Mircea the Elder had bought his
country a generation earlier had narrowed considerably. His grandson,
Vlad Dracul, ruled Wallachia through open political oscillation between
Hungary and the Ottomans - crowned at Nuremberg by the Holy Roman
emperor, yet forced, once he actually held the Wallachian throne, to
accommodate a Turkish power that had grown far stronger than it had been
in Mircea's own day. That oscillation cost him dearly in 1442, when
Ottoman anger at his shifting loyalties forced him to hand over his two
minor sons, Vlad and Radu, as hostages, imprisoned at the fortress of
Eğrigöz on an arid Anatolian plateau.

## Years that shaped a future ruler in silence

Almost nothing is recorded of what the young Vlad - eleven or twelve
years old at the time - actually experienced during his captivity. What
can be inferred from his later conduct is plausible enough: he emerged
from Eğrigöz fluent in Turkish and with a working knowledge of Ottoman
military and political practice, an education in the enemy's own methods
that his later campaigns would put to direct use. His father did not
long survive the arrangement that had sent him there. In December 1447,
John Hunyadi, the powerful governor of Hungary, invaded Wallachia and had
Vlad Dracul and his eldest son, Mircea, executed - Wallachian chronicles
would later claim the elder brother had been buried alive.

## A throne offered, then taken back within months

Six months after that execution, and in the aftermath of a crushing
Ottoman victory over Hunyadi's own crusading army at Kosovo Polje, Sultan
Murad II installed the teenaged Vlad on the Wallachian throne in his
murdered father's place - a puppet appointment secured on terms of
tribute and yearly appearance at the Ottoman capital. It did not last.
Within two months, the previous prince, Vladislav II, retook the throne,
apparently with Moldavian help, and Vlad's first reign ended almost as
soon as it began, sending him back into exile to wait for a second
chance that would not come for another eight years.

## A second reign built on the same enemy's methods

Vlad returned to power in 1456, at twenty-five, after a peregrination
through Moldavia and Transylvania that included time at the court of the
same John Hunyadi whose army had killed his father and brother -
Wallachian politics of the era ran on exactly this kind of pragmatic
proximity between men whose families had every reason to hate one
another. Once he held the throne again, Vlad set about the internal
consolidation his own hostage years had prepared him to understand: a
strong domestic order was the precondition for any foreign policy
independent enough to resist the growing power of Sultan Mehmed II,
fresh from the conquest of Constantinople and already the most formidable
ruler the region had faced.
