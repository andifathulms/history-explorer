A century of Greek administrators appointed from Constantinople's Phanar
district ended the way it began: in violence, though this time the
violence came from a Wallachian peasant leader rather than an Ottoman
executioner, and what followed his death was the return of Wallachia's
own boyars to its throne.

## A century measured in changes of throne

Between Brâncoveanu's execution in 1714 and the regime's own collapse,
Wallachia's throne changed hands forty-one times - a rotation so rapid
that no single reign, with one exception, lasted more than a handful of
years, and princes routinely bought their appointments only to be
recalled, deposed, or executed for treason within seasons of taking
office. Early losses set the pattern: when Austria went to war with the
Ottomans in 1716, its armies took Belgrade and, at the peace of
Passarowitz two years later, annexed Oltenia outright - the five
Wallachian districts between the Iron Gates, the Carpathians and the
Olt river, stripped from the principality by a treaty its own prince had
no part in negotiating and held by Austria for the next two decades.
Wallachian boyars petitioned Vienna that same peace to have native-born
princes restored in place of Greek appointees; the petition went
nowhere, and the arrangement it protested would outlast every man who
signed it.

## A peasant leader who broke with the Greek cause

The crisis that finally ended the regime began not in Wallachia but in
Moldavia, when Alexander Ypsilanti - son of a former hospodar and an
officer in the Tsar's own service - crossed the Pruth in February 1821
at the head of the Greek revolutionary Hetairia, hoping to rally the
Romanian principalities behind a war for Greek independence. Wallachia's
own response took a different shape entirely. A peasant-born but
minor-boyar-raised officer named Tudor Vladimirescu, who had served in
the Russian army and fought alongside Serbian rebels under Karageorge,
raised Oltenia against both the Phanariote regime and the boyar class
itself, proclaiming to his followers that the hour had come to throw off
the yoke of foreign extortioners and native profiteers alike. Vladimirescu
occupied Bucharest without a fight in March 1821, careful throughout to
distinguish his own cause from Ypsilanti's: when the two men finally met,
Vladimirescu answered Ypsilanti's appeal to a shared Christian struggle
with a line that outlived them both - "Greece belongs to the Greeks, but
Romania belongs to the Romanians." Asked by what right he claimed to
speak for his country, he said only: "By the right my sword gives me in
my own homeland."

## A murder that changed nothing Ypsilanti wanted

The two movements could not coexist. Ypsilanti's own lieutenant, an
Albanian officer named Iordache, had Vladimirescu seized and assassinated
in the street after several days' imprisonment - a killing that went
unpunished and that broke the popular uprising's own momentum overnight.
It did Ypsilanti no good. Deprived of Romanian support and abandoned by
the Russian consul, who publicly disavowed the whole enterprise on the
Tsar's own orders, Ypsilanti's forces were routed at Drăgășani weeks
later; he fled across the Transylvanian frontier and spent years as
Austria's prisoner. The Ottomans, eager to separate the Romanian and
Greek causes and free their own forces to concentrate on the revolt still
burning in the Peloponnese, found it convenient to grant the very demand
Wallachia's boyars had been making since 1718: in June 1822, the Porte
appointed one of their own number, Grigore Ghica - nephew of a hospodar
executed for treason nearly half a century earlier, and from a family
long since thoroughly Romanianized - as prince, ending the Phanariote
century on the same terms that had begun it, only reversed.
