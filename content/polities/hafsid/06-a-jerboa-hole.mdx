In 1347 the Marinid sultan of Fez took Ifriqiya, and the Hafsid state ceased to
exist for about twelve years. Ibn Khaldun was fifteen and in Tunis for all of
it.

## How a country changes hands without a battle

Abu'l-Hasan had wanted Ifriqiya for years and needed a pretext. He got one when
the Hafsid heir was assassinated: he had personally signed the document naming
that prince successor, so he could declare the act violated and the war just. He
distributed money to his troops in the camp outside Tlemcen and marched east
with an immense army.

Nobody fought him. The Arab emirs of the Ifriqiyan interior had sent to ask for
his support in a feud of their own and got a favourable answer, after which the
tribes declared for the Marinids as far as the furthest provinces. Then the
lords of the south came in: Gabès, Tozeur, Gafsa, El-Hamma, Nefta, all at once,
meeting him at Oran and swearing allegiance — *some willingly, others from
fear*, Ibn Khaldun says, and he was in a position to know which. They brought
the homage of the lord of Tripoli, who had been unable to travel that far.
Bougie thought about resisting and submitted. Constantine submitted. Algiers
sent a deputation and went home with a set of tax collectors.

The Hafsid princes were handled with care rather than cruelty, which is more
efficient. The amir of Bougie was sent to Morocco with his brothers and given
Nedroma to live in, with a portion of the town's taxes for maintenance. The
sons of another prince went to Oujda on the same terms. At Constantine
Abu'l-Hasan opened the prison, released the Hafsid princes who had been shut up
there by their own family, took them to Tunis and shipped them to Morocco with
the rest.

## The horse

One of them ran. Abu Hafs left Tunis with the tribes that would still ride with
him and made for the desert. A detachment caught up in the territory of
El-Hamma, near Gabès; the tribes scattered after a feeble resistance; Abu Hafs
turned to flee, and his horse put a foot in a jerboa hole and came down under
him.

When the dust settled they saw the amir and his freedman Zafir going away on
foot. The commander arrested them both and put them in irons, and at nightfall
had them killed, in case the Arabs came for them. The heads were carried to
Abu'l-Hasan, who had just entered Béja.

He made his entry into Tunis in September 1347 with a magnificent procession.
Ibn Khaldun's sentence for what was left is the best one in his account of the
century: nothing remained of the brilliant empire of the Hafsid Almohads but a
feeble spark, whose gleam could still be made out at Bône.

The spark was there because Abu'l-Hasan had left it. He installed a Hafsid
prince as governor of Bône as a courtesy to a family he had married into.

## Then he went sightseeing

Having finished, the conqueror went to Kairouan, then Sousse, then Mahdia. He
looked at the monuments in those towns and the remains of the older buildings
the Fatimid kings and the Zirids had put up. He sought a blessing at the tombs
where certain Companions of the Prophet and their immediate successors are said
to lie. Around December he went back to Tunis.

Ifriqiya had been a Roman province, then the Aghlabids', then the Fatimids',
then the Zirids', and a Moroccan sultan who had just taken it spent
his first winter as its owner visiting the graves.

## Twelve years

It did not hold either. Marinid Ifriqiya broke on the same Arab tribes that had
handed it over; the Hafsid princes came back from Morocco; Mahdia went over to
Tunis before the fleet sent to recover it arrived; and in 1359 the people of
Bougie, who loathed the governor Fez had given them, secretly invited the
Hafsid chamberlain to come and take the city. The garrison commander and his
household were put on a ship to Tunis by the mob, and the sultan Abu Ishaq
occupied Bougie the following year.

Hafsid rule was restored and lasted another two centuries, and the shape of it
had changed. The sultan who reoccupied Bougie exercised almost no authority
there, because his chamberlain Ibn Tafragin governed him from the capital and
directed him as he pleased.
