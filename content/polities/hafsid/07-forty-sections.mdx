The Ottomans took Tunis in the summer of 1574 and did not put a Hafsid back.
What they put there instead ran the country for the next three hundred and
fifty years, which is about as long as the dynasty it replaced, and it was
assembled in a few weeks by a man who then sailed home.

## The taking

Sinan Pasha — a Milanese renegade said to be of the Visconti family, at least
according to the story attached to him — arrived to find that the pashas of
Algiers, Tripoli and Kairouan had already agreed to bring their contingents to
him. The Spanish commander Serbelloni evacuated Bizerte and put its garrison
into La Goulette, and manned the fort on the lake with the troops who had been
holding down the population of Tunis. The Turks were investing both before he
had finished.

La Goulette fell first. Everything in it was killed except two or three hundred
men who were put in chains, the governor among them. The fort between the city
and the lake fell a few days later; when the citadel of Tunis went on 3
September its garrison was down to thirty men still on their feet. Serbelloni
was one of them, and was spared because his ransom would be large; Rome offered
to exchange him for several Muslims held in the Castel Sant'Angelo, one of them
a son of Ali Pasha taken at Lepanto.

The Tunisian historians say the two sides lost about ten thousand men each. The
Spanish historians say the Turks lost thirty-two thousand. Rousseau prints both
figures and asks how they are to be reconciled, and does not answer, which is
the correct thing to do with them.

## The machine

Before returning to Constantinople, Sinan built a government. He left four
thousand troops, divided into forty sections, and put at the head of each a
chief who took the title of *dey*. Over them he set a pasha, and named the man.
He instituted a qadi to judge suits between private persons by the civil law,
and named the first one. He organised a Divan on the model of the ones already
working at Algiers and in Egypt. Then he fixed the pay of the troops, the
salaries of the senior officials, the details of the administration and the
official costumes.

The public prayer in the mosques was said in the name of the reigning Ottoman
sultan, and the coin was struck in his name.

That last sentence is the whole transfer. The two things a Maghribi ruler
possessed that could not be delegated were the *khutba* and the mint. For three
hundred and forty-five years both had carried a Hafsid; a caliph's name for a
few months of 1259, a sultan's name for the rest. After 1574 they carried
Constantinople's.

## What it turned into

Rousseau, who despises the arrangement and says so, notes the obvious flaw:
it armed men without control who owed their sudden elevation to nobody, and
like every institution founded on violence it did not last long. In 1590 the
soldiery decided to be rid of the *bölük bashis* whose administration had become
insupportable to the population and to the army alike. The minister of war, who
was one of the conspirators, arranged to be away from his post that day. They
broke the gates of the Kasbah, looted the arsenal, and went through the city
killing their officers.

Out of that, and out of the century of adjustments after it, came hereditary
rule in Tunis again — twice. Power became, Rousseau writes, in some sort
hereditary in the family of Murad, until a coup in 1702 sent the heads of five
of them to the capital to announce it. Three years later the militia elected as
bey an agha named Husayn ben Ali, the son of a Greek renegade recruited in the
Levant to fill out the ranks of the Tunisian soldiery, whose austerity and
ability had carried him up through it. With him, Rousseau says, began a dynasty
that had succeeded itself on the throne of Tunis by heredity down to the year
he was writing in.

An occupying force sent to end a dynasty in Tunis had, within a century and a
half, produced another one — governing the same ground from the same city, paid
for by the customs on the same trade, and founded from inside the garrison that
was supposed to be the alternative to having a dynasty at all.
