---
title: A caliph in Tunis, for about a year
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987

In February 1258 Hulegu had the last Abbasid caliph killed at Baghdad, and for
the first time since the 630s there was no caliph. The following year the
sharif of Mecca — the guardian of the holy cities, whose acknowledgement was
the most valuable recognition available — acknowledged the Hafsid ruler
al-Mustansir as caliph, and had the Friday sermon at Mecca preached in his
name.

It did not last and it never became general. The Mamluks installed an Abbasid
survivor at Cairo in 1261, which is the polity on this site's caliphates page,
and Meccan recognition followed the sultanate that could supply the holy cities
with grain. But it happened, and it is the reason this page exists: for a
stretch of about a year, the office that had been in Medina, Damascus and
Baghdad was located, by the man best entitled to locate it, in Tunis.

The state underneath the title was solid and unusual for this region in being
commercial rather than tributary. Tunis stood where the trans-Saharan caravans
from the Sudan met the Mediterranean, and the Hafsids ran both ends
deliberately: treaties with Pisa, Genoa, Venice and the Crown of Aragon giving
European merchants their own quarters and consuls in Tunis and Bougie, and
corsairing against those who had no treaty, which the sources here treat as a
branch of revenue rather than as disorder. Gold came north, cloth and metal
went south, and the customs were the state's.

That combination is what brought a crusade. Louis IX of France landed at
Carthage in 1270 in an expedition that had been aimed at Egypt and was diverted
here, and it collapsed almost immediately: dysentery went through the camp and
the king died in it in August. The Hafsids had not beaten a crusade so much as
outlasted one, and the treaty that followed put the relationship back on the
commercial footing it had had before.
