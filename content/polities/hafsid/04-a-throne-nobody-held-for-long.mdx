Al-Mustansir's death in 1277 opened seventy years in which the Hafsid throne
changed hands more often than it stayed put, and in which the dynasty's own
provincial governors at Bougie and Constantine became as important as whoever
happened to be sultan in Tunis.

## A caliph who killed his own cousin to hold the title

By November 1295 the throne had passed to Abu Abd Allah Abu Asida, son of a
previous sultan al-Wathiq, who took his own predecessor's title and called
himself al-Mustansir a second time. His first act as ruler was to have a
cousin of the same royal blood put to death, on the plain reasoning that a
prince with a claim as good as his own was a danger simply by existing. It set
the pattern for the rest of the period: the throne was real enough to kill
for, and not secure enough that killing a rival ever quite settled it.

## The martyr and the man who killed him

When this second al-Mustansir died, the men around the palace proclaimed a
relative remembered afterward only by his epithet - Abu Bakr "the martyr."
The title was not a compliment about how he ruled; it described how he left
office. Abu'l-Baqa Khalid, a son of the sultan's own late uncle and governor
of Constantine since 1298, had already been building a base of his own in the
east. He had the martyr killed and took the capital, styling himself with a
grand new title - al-Nasir li-Din Illah al-Mansur - before settling, in the
chronicler's own blunt assessment, into a reign given over to pleasure and to
a violent, bloodthirsty temper.

## The grand sheikh who tried to retire

Abu'l-Baqa's death opened the throne to a man who had spent his whole career
trying to avoid it. Abu Yahya ibn al-Lihyani - grandson of the "long-bearded"
brother the family nickname belonged to, and for years the empire's own grand
sheikh and most senior administrator - had grown tired of public life,
campaigned against Djerba, and set out for Mecca meaning to leave politics
behind for good. He came back from the pilgrimage to find Ifriqiya in enough
disorder that he simply declared himself sultan on arrival at Tripoli, and
the Arab tribes of the interior rallied to him readily enough that he
occupied Tunis without much difficulty.

He did not hold it long against the one rival who mattered. At Constantine,
Abu Yahya Abu Bakr had been building exactly the kind of base Abu'l-Baqa once
had, and by 1316 he was strong enough to invade Ifriqiya outright, collecting
tribute across the Hawwara country while Ibn al-Lihyani's own authority in
Tunis shrank to whatever his Arab allies still chose to defend. When his own
son's army broke in the field, the old sheikh who had once tried to retire to
Mecca found himself asking Christian shippers for passage out. Six ships
carried him and his family to Alexandria, where the Mamluk sultan received him
with honor and where, at last, he was allowed to stop being a ruler.

## What the fighting actually decided

Abu Yahya Abu Bakr was the one man in this whole sequence whose power outlasted
him rather than collapsing at his death. Ruling first from Constantine and
Bougie and only later from Tunis itself, he spent decades doing what none of
his short-lived predecessors had managed - holding the office long enough
that it began to look, again, like a state rather than a prize. It was his own
son whose murder gave the Marinid sultan Abu'l-Hasan the pretext, in 1347, to
take the country his father had spent a lifetime reassembling.
