A Granadan diplomat who travelled the Maghreb for the sultan of Fez in the 1510s
called Tunis the capital of the richest kingdom in all Africa, and then described
a city whose citizens could not farm the ground outside their own walls and drank
rainwater out of cisterns.

Both halves are true, and the gap between them is the Hafsid state.

## The city

Leo counts. The families of the city together with the suburbs come to about ten
thousand. Each trade has its own quarter. There are linen-weavers in great
numbers and the cloth they make is exceedingly fine and sells at a high price all
over Africa, and the linen-drapers are the wealthiest men in Tunis.

He watched the women spin and thought the method worth recording: standing on an
upper floor, they let the spindle down through a window or a hole in the
floorboards into the room below, so that its own weight draws the thread even.

Three suburbs had been built outside three gates by two successive sultans. One
holds three hundred families; one holds more than a thousand, and in it live all
the Christians of the king's guard. The third is at the sea gate, half a mile
from the gulf:

> Hither doe the Genoueses, Venetians, and all other Christian merchants resort,
> and here they repose themselues out of the tumult and concourse of the Moores:
> and this street is of so great bignes, that it containeth three hundred
> families of Christians and Moores, but the houses are verie low, and of small
> receit.

## The two sentences that matter

Then, in the middle of the inventory, Leo explains why a city that rich has so
few rich men in it.

> Rich men here are but few, by reason of the scarcitie of all kinde of graine:
> for a man cannot till a piece of ground, be it neuer so neere the citie, in
> regard of the manifold inuasions of the Arabians.

Grain came from other places — Urbs, Béja, Bône — and the citizens who did farm
did it inside walled fields in the suburbs, each with a pit and a wheel turned by
a mule or a camel to water the upper part of the ground. Leo invites the reader
to consider what crop can be reaped from a field that small, walled round, and
watered by such industry.

The second sentence is about water.

> In this citie they haue no fountaines, riuers, nor welles of fresh water: but
> they all vse raine-water taken out of cesterns.

There is a fountain in the suburbs from which porters carry salt water into the
city to sell, which the inhabitants think more wholesome to drink than rainwater.
The wells that give good water are reserved for the king and his court.

The bread is excellent, he says, though they leave the bran in the flour. The
merchants and most of the citizens live on *besis*, a barley-meal dumpling with
oil or citron broth poured over it, and there is a place in the city where
nothing is sold but barley made ready for it. All the mills except a few on a
river outside are turned by mules and asses.

## What that is a description of

A state whose capital sits on a coastal plain it does not control.

Everything in the Hafsid century that looks like strength is in the first list:
the linen, the customs, the Christian merchant quarter, the king's Christian
guard, the court that Leo says was organised on the Moroccan model and that
produced the most impressive chancery in North Africa. All of it is revenue taken
from things passing through a port.

Everything in the second list is what the dynasty could not do. A capital that
cannot be provisioned from its own hinterland is a capital living on grain
convoys and good relations with whoever holds the road, and a city without fresh
water is a city that can be besieged by anybody willing to sit down in front of
it. Within twenty years of Leo's visit an Ottoman corsair had taken the city and
Charles V had taken it back and reinstalled the Hafsid as a Spanish client; forty
years after that the Ottomans took it for the last time, and the last Hafsid was
carried to Constantinople and killed there.

## The men with the stones

Leo leaves one thing standing outside all the arithmetic.

> There are certaine people in this citie whom a man would take to be distraught,
> which goe bare-headed and bare-footed, carrying stones about with them, and
> these are reuerenced by the common people, for men of singular holines.

And the king of Tunis built one of the city's monasteries for one of them — a man
Leo names, and whose society he calls fond — and endowed it with the most ample
revenues.

That is a Hafsid caliph, in the capital of the richest kingdom in Africa, putting
state money into a lodge for barefoot holy men carrying stones. In a dynasty
that had spent three centuries arguing that it held the Almohad caliphate by
right, it was a reasonable investment: the marabouts were the only authority in
the countryside the state had lost.
