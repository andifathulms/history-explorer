The Almohad empire rested on a claim about a man: that Ibn Tumart had been the
infallible mahdi, and that the caliphate his followers built was the political
expression of that. After the defeat at Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212 the later
caliphs quietly abandoned the mahdist doctrine rather than defend it, and in
doing so removed the argument for their own authority.

The Hafsids are what the abandonment produced at the other end of the empire.
The family descended from Abu Hafs Umar, one of Ibn Tumart's inner circle of
companions, and had governed Ifriqiya for the Almohads since the beginning of
the century. In 1229 the Almohad caliph al-Ma'mun formally repudiated Ibn
Tumart's doctrine, and Abu Zakariya Yahya, governing at Tunis, declined to
follow him — on the grounds, exactly inverted from the usual secession, that
the centre had left the movement rather than that the province was leaving the
centre.

<Figure id="kasbah" />

Provinces that break away usually have to invent a justification. This one had
its justification handed to it. A governor whose family's standing came from
proximity to the mahdi could keep the province simply by remaining loyal to a
doctrine the capital had discarded, and did.

What he kept was worth keeping. Ifriqiya had been the Aghlabids' province and
before them a Roman one, with the agriculture of the Tell, the ports, and
Tunis sitting where the caravan routes running north from the Sudan reached the
Mediterranean. The Aghlabids had farmed the same ground for tribute four
centuries earlier and lasted a hundred and ten years on it. The Hafsids held it
for three hundred and forty-five.
