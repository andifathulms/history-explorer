---
title: Keeping the province when the doctrine went
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987
phase: formation
---

The Almohad empire rested on a claim about a man: that Ibn Tumart had been the
infallible mahdi, and that the caliphate his followers built was the political
expression of that. This site's Almohad page describes what happened to a
structure of that kind when it suffered an obvious divine judgement at Las Navas
de Tolosa in 1212 — the later caliphs quietly abandoned the mahdist doctrine,
which removed the argument for their own authority.

The Hafsids are what the abandonment produced at the other end of the empire.
The family descended from Abu Hafs Umar, one of Ibn Tumart's inner circle of
companions, and had governed Ifriqiya for the Almohads since the beginning of
the century. In 1229 the Almohad caliph al-Ma'mun formally repudiated Ibn
Tumart's doctrine, and Abu Zakariya Yahya, governing at Tunis, declined to
follow him — on the grounds, exactly inverted from the usual secession, that
the centre had left the movement rather than that the province was leaving the
centre.

It is one of the cleaner secessions in this corpus because the justification
was available and did not have to be invented. A governor whose family's
standing came from proximity to the mahdi could keep the province by remaining
loyal to a doctrine the capital had discarded, and did.

What he kept was worth keeping. Ifriqiya had been the Aghlabids' province and
before them a Roman one, with the agriculture of the Tell, the ports, and
Tunis sitting where the caravan routes running north from the Sudan reached the
Mediterranean. This site's Aghlabid page has the same ground being farmed for
tribute four centuries earlier; the Hafsids held it for three and a half
centuries, which is longer than any other polity in this region's history and
longer than most in the corpus.
