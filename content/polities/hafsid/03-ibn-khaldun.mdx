Ibn Khaldun was born at Tunis in 1332, into a family of Andalusi origin that had
left Seville for Ifriqiya when Castile took the city and had served the Hafsids
since. He held office under them, and then — because the Maghreb in his century
was four or five competing dynasties and a scholar with administrative skill was
portable — under the Marinids at Fez, the Zayyanids at Tlemcen, the Nasrids at
Granada, and the Hafsids again. He was imprisoned twice, changed sides
repeatedly, negotiated with Pedro the Cruel of Castile, and eventually left for
Egypt, where he was a Maliki chief judge under the Mamluks and, in 1401, went
out from besieged Damascus to talk to Timur, who questioned him for several
weeks about the Maghreb.

In 1377, in a castle in what is now western Algeria, between employments, he
wrote the *Muqaddima* — the introduction to a universal history — in about five
months.

What it argues is the reason this chapter is here rather than in a history of
scholarship. Ibn Khaldun asked why dynasties rise and fall, and answered it with
a mechanism rather than a moral. Cohesion — *asabiyya*, the solidarity of a
group that has to hold together to survive — is strongest among people living
hard lives on the margins: desert tribes, mountain confederations. That cohesion
lets them take a settled state from a dynasty that has lost it. Having taken it,
they settle: they acquire cities, taxes, luxury, a professional army in place of
kinsmen, and the solidarity that brought them in dissolves over roughly three
or four generations. Then a group from the margins that still has it arrives,
and the cycle runs again.

He was describing what he could see out of the window. The Almoravids came out
of the Sahara and were replaced by the Almohads out of the Atlas, who
disintegrated into the Hafsids, Zayyanids and Marinids, who were exhausting
themselves against each other while he wrote.

But every region of this site is the same book. The Tahirids, Saffarids,
Samanids and Ghaznavids run the pattern in Khurasan. The Seljuks come off the
steppe and are Persianate within a generation. The Karakhanids, the Qara Khitai,
the two Turkmen confederations, the Ottomans out of the smallest beylik — this
corpus has spent every region documenting a cycle that a Tunisian civil servant
set out in five months in 1377, from inside one of the dynasties it applies to.

This page carries no reach figure, no population figure and three empty
influence counts. What the Hafsid century produced is the analytical framework
the rest of this site is, unknowingly, an instance of.
