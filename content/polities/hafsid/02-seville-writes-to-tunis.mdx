Abu Zakariya kept Ifriqiya in 1229 and spent the next twenty years finding out
how much else would come to him without being asked. The answer, briefly, was
al-Andalus.

## What was happening on the other side

Spain had gone to pieces. The Almohad princes were quarrelling over the shreds
of the monarchy; Ibn Hud had usurped the east and Ibn al-Ahmar had the west in
revolt; in 1229 Ibn Hud drove the Almohads out of Seville by force and
imprisoned all of them. A few years later the Sevillans expelled Ibn Hud's
brother and swore allegiance to one of their own notables, a descendant of a
famous jurist, who took a regnal title and was killed with his vizier outside
the walls by a force Ibn al-Ahmar had sent against his camp. Ibn al-Ahmar held the
city for less than a month before the population rose and called Ibn Hud back.
Then Ibn Hud died and they recognised the Almohad at Marrakesh instead and put
a local man in charge, and when that Almohad died in 1242 they had to think
again.

Six changes of allegiance in thirteen years, none of them producing anything
that could defend the city.

## The deputation

When Abu Zakariya took Tlemcen, the people of Seville judged the kingdom of
Morocco to be finished, and decided to recognise the sovereignty of the sultan
of Tunis, as Valencia and Murcia had already done. Jerez and Tarifa followed.
Then all these towns together named a deputation and sent it to Tunis, to ask
Abu Zakariya for one of his relatives to govern them.

He chose a cousin, Abu Faris, and sent him. That is an Ifriqiyan dynasty barely
fifteen years old being asked by the cities of Andalusia to supply them with a
ruler — the Friday sermon said at Seville in the name of a man in Tunis, across
the whole width of North Africa and a strait, with two hostile states in
between.

## And then

It did not hold, and the way it failed is instructive. In 1245 the notable who
had handed Abu Faris the command took the power back for himself, deported him
to Ceuta, made an alliance with the Christian king, and struck off the army
rolls the names of every man who persisted in raiding Christian territory.
Those men murdered him shortly afterwards. Their captain took over the city,
recalled Abu Faris and restored the Hafsid authority.

The Christian king heard this with lively displeasure, broke the truce, took
Carmona and Marchena and sat down in front of Seville. The city refused his
peace terms and handed the command to a council of five — the captain, three
other notables, and a man named Yahya ibn Khaldun, whose great-grandson wrote
this account and mentions the relationship in passing.

Seville fell in 1248. What the episode leaves behind is a fact about the Hafsid
state at its height that no map shows: for a few years in the 1240s the most
credible Muslim authority in the western Mediterranean was in Tunis, and
everybody from Murcia to Tarifa knew it, and it made no difference at all to
what Castile was going to do.
