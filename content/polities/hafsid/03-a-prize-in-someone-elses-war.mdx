The Hafsids lasted three hundred and forty-five years, which is longer than
anything else in this site's Islamic West, and the last of it is the least like
the rest.

The fourteenth and fifteenth centuries were the ordinary difficulties of a
Maghribi state: Arab tribal confederations in the interior that the centre
taxed when it could and negotiated with when it could not, rival branches of
the family holding Bougie and Constantine, and a commercial relationship with
Europe that continued through all of it because both sides wanted it to.

What ended the dynasty was not a Maghribi problem at all. In the sixteenth
century the western Mediterranean became the frontier between two empires that
had nothing to do with Ifriqiya, and Tunis was on it. An Ottoman force took the
city in 1534; Charles V retook it in 1535 and put the Hafsid back on the throne
as a Spanish tributary with a garrison at La Goulette. From that point the
dynasty was not a party to the contest but an instrument in it, restored,
deposed and restored again as the two powers needed a local name to rule
through.

In 1574 the Ottomans took Tunis for the last time and stopped needing one. The
last Hafsid was carried to Constantinople and killed there.

This site does not print his name. The works consulted for this polity differ
on the numbering of the final ruler, and the record leaves the field empty
rather than choosing the most frequently repeated version — the same decision
the Ghurid page makes about its last sultan, and for the same reason. A gap
that says the sources disagree is worth more than a name that says nothing.
