Yue Fei rose from a lieutenant under Zhang Jun to lieutenant-general within a
single year, 1131, and for the next five years was the one commander the
restored Southern Song court could rely on to reverse rather than merely
survive its losses to the Jin.

## A soldier the court could not fully use

His early service brought a warlord's submission and the recovery of
substantial territory from insurgent leaders operating in the chaos the Jin
invasion had left behind. In 1136 he asked the court's permission to attack
the Chinese provinces the Jin had occupied outright - not another defensive
campaign, but a genuine attempt to reverse the settlement of 1127. The
permission was refused, on the advice of the chancellor Qin Hui, who had
already committed himself to negotiating a peace with the Jin that a
victorious general in the field would only complicate.

Yue Fei's own devotion to the war Qin Hui was trying to end made him,
in the chancellor's eyes, the single greatest obstacle to the peace he
wanted. Qin Hui had him removed to a lesser post first, and when that did
not silence him, engineered a formal accusation of treason against Yue Fei
and his son Yue Yun. Both men were committed to the state prison. Their trial
proved nothing but the falsity of the charge - and changed nothing. Despite
the protests of the statesman Han Shizhong and others at court, Qin Hui
secured an imperial mandate for Yue Fei's execution, and it was carried out.

## What the verdict became

Chinese historians never forgave it. The act was met, in the judgment later
generations settled into, with what one nineteenth-century biographical
dictionary calls "the undying execration of historians and of the Chinese
people" - and the record's own treatment of the two men who made the
decision could not be more lopsided. In 1179 Yue Fei was posthumously
canonised; in 1204 he received the title Prince of E. Qin Hui's own name, by
contrast, became a byword in Chinese usage for any official willing to deal
too accommodatingly with a foreign power - a synonym still current centuries
after his death, reserved for exactly the kind of pragmatic peacemaking he
had believed, not unreasonably, was the only realistic policy available to
an exhausted state.

## The peace it was traded for

The peace Qin Hui achieved by removing Yue Fei came the following year, in
1141 - the Shaoxing treaty, in which Gaozong renounced Song's claim to the
lost north and agreed to an annual tribute in silk and silver to the Jin
court that had once held his own father captive. Whether that peace could
have been won without Yue Fei's death, or whether it needed to be won on
those terms at all, was exactly the argument the general's own execution
foreclosed - and the argument China's popular memory has never really
stopped having anyway, four generations of dynasties later, over which of
the two men actually served the country he claimed to be protecting.
