As a boy, Sima Guang saved a playmate from drowning in a water jar by the
simplest method available to him: he picked up a stone and smashed the jar,
letting the water out. Giles offers the anecdote as the first evidence of a
mind that solved problems by attacking the actual obstacle rather than the
conventional one, a habit that shaped both his politics and the four decades
he spent, on and off, compiling the single largest work of history his
country had produced.

## Opposition

Sima Guang rose through the official examinations to the rank of Minister of
State, and used the position to oppose Wang Anshi's reform programme from the
inside for as long as he could. In 1070, unable to check it any further, he
resigned the chancellorship rather than continue administering policies he
believed would fail. He did not return to office until 1085, the year
Shenzong died and the reforms lost the one patron who had kept them running
against the bureaucracy's own judgment. Within months of his return Sima
Guang had dismantled nearly the whole of Wang Anshi's programme; within
months after that, he was dead — reportedly still labouring for the state's
good on his return to the capital, undone by illness rather than politics.
His countrymen called him, on his death, the Living Buddha.

## The Mirror

His resignation in 1070 was not idleness. He used the years out of office to
complete the *Zizhi Tongjian* — the "Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in
Government" — a chronological history of China running from the fifth
century BC to the founding of the Song itself in 960, a title the emperor
bestowed on the finished work in 1084 on the reasoning that "to view antiquity
as it were in a mirror is an aid in the administration of government." As a
student he had rested his arm on a rounded wooden pillow designed to roll and
wake him the moment he began to doze over his books; as an old man compiling
a work that size, the same discipline against half-measures that had smashed
the water jar kept him at it for the better part of two decades.

He was, by the account of his own household, fanatically careful with the
physical books themselves — his library still looked new after years of use,
because he forbade his students from turning pages by scratching at them
with a fingernail and made them use the pads of two fingers instead.

## What he wrote about watching the watchmen

One surviving fragment of his prose, on the office of Censor, reads less like
institutional history than like a warning aimed past his own century. Before
the Han dynasty, he wrote, admonishing the throne had been every subject's
right, "from the highest statesman down to the artisan and trader." The Han
turned that right into an office, charged with weighing the empire's
successes and failures for the throne's benefit and not the censor's own. In
1017 six men were formally appointed to the post; by 1045 their names were
written up on public boards; by 1062 the names were being cut into stone, so
that posterity could point at one and say "there was a loyal man," and at
another, "there was a traitor." Sima Guang's own comment on the practice was
a question rather than an endorsement: "Does not this give cause for fear?"
