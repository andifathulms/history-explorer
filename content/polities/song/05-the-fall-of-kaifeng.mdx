The Jin had begun their revolt as vassals of the Liao, the Tartar power that
had sat on the Song's northern border since before the dynasty's founding.
Song diplomacy helped bring the Liao down, on the reasoning that a weaker,
still-forming Jin was a safer neighbour than a strong, entrenched Liao. The
Jin destroyed the Liao inside eleven years and turned on their former ally
almost as soon as the job was finished.

## The first siege

A Jin detachment crossed into Song territory in 1125. Emperor Huizong fled
south to Nanjing for his own safety and left his son to manage what
resistance could be organised. The Jin general Walipou drove on the capital,
Kaifeng, but the wider Jin leadership had not yet settled how far to press
the advantage — a faction among them argued the Yellow River marked the
natural limit of Jin ambition. Negotiations produced a treaty, and Huizong
returned to his capital believing the crisis over.

<Figure id="sg-qingming" />

Walipou did not share the restraint of his own colleagues. He wanted Kaifeng
occupied outright and refused to honour the settlement. Forced into one
retreat, he came back in greater strength, and this time the armies the new
emperor, Qinzong, sent to meet him were beaten badly enough that the Jin
could lay close siege to the capital itself.

<Figure id="sg-qinzong" />

## The siege

Kaifeng's garrison numbered some seventy thousand men, reinforced by another
thirty thousand marched up from the south, and there was a real prospect —
Boulger allows — that want of supplies might have forced Walipou to withdraw
if the city could simply hold. Prince Kang Wang and the more resolute
officers argued for exactly that course. Qinzong did not share their nerve.
When a Jin assault briefly took the ramparts and gates, it seemed to confirm
his instinct for a negotiated settlement, and only Walipou's own reluctance
to spend lives on the street fighting the Song had prepared for kept the
city from falling by storm there and then. Qinzong went to the Jin camp
himself to arrange peace terms.

<Figure id="sg-ironpagoda" />

The Jin demanded a payment Qinzong knew, even as he promised it, that he
could not raise. When his followers discovered how little of the promised
sum would actually materialise, the outcry among them was loud enough that
Qinzong was detained rather than allowed to return to his own capital.
Huizong and the rest of the imperial family still in Kaifeng were induced to
seek shelter in the Jin camp and were carried off into Tartary; both former
emperors eventually died there, at long intervals from each other and from
home.

## What was left

Walipou was not satisfied with the ransom of the capital and the removal of
the ruling house. He installed a court creature as a puppet emperor over what
remained of Song territory, but the arrangement lasted only as long as the
Jin army stayed close enough to enforce it — the moment Walipou withdrew, the
puppet was set aside.

The dynasty survived in the person of Kang Wang, who took the throne as
Emperor Gaozong and restored Song rule over what territory remained to him.
But the terms of the restoration were permanent: China now had two empires
where it had had one, the Jin holding the north with a stronger, more
vigorous state apparatus than the Liao they had replaced, and the Song
confined — for the rest of the dynasty's existence — to the lands south of a
frontier it would spend the next century and a half trying, and failing, to
push back.
