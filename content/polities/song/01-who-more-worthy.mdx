The Tang fell in 907, and for fifty-three years no government held all of
northern China for long. Five dynasties succeeded one another in the north
alone, each installed by an army that had just finished unmaking the last one,
while the south split into a scatter of separate kingdoms. Zhao Kuangyin, born
near modern Beijing to a family with several generations of minor military
office behind it, served in the guards of the Later Zhou, the fifth and last
of the northern dynasties, and distinguished himself in its wars of
reunification — on one occasion, Boulger records, his presence of mind alone
saved the day at the battle of Kaoping.

## The task left half finished

The Later Zhou's ruler, Chai Rong, had begun the work of putting the north
back together and died with it incomplete. Zhao Kuangyin, by then a senior
general, took it up. The country, Boulger writes, "prayed for peace, and was
anxious to give all the support it could to a man acting for its interests" —
public spirit had gone dead during the years when the throne had been "the
lottery of soldiers," changing hands too fast for anyone to feel invested in
its survival.

The moment came while Zhao Kuangyin was on campaign. His own troops
surrounded him and put the matter plainly. "The Empire is without a master,"
they told him, "and we wish to give it one. Who is more worthy of it than our
general?" Whatever the choreography behind it — and a soldiery announcing to
its commander that it has already decided to make him emperor is not, in any
army, a wholly spontaneous event — Zhao Kuangyin accepted, returned to the
capital, and took the throne without having to fight for it. Signs, his
contemporaries said, had already appeared in the heavens confirming that
Heaven willed it.

## The first acts

He named his dynasty Song, took red as the imperial colour, and styled
himself Taizu. His first measures were a general pardon and a proclamation,
sent even into provinces still defiant of the new centre, ordering the
restoration of law and the preservation of domestic peace. He restored the
privileges of the scholar class, stripped from them during the preceding
disorder, and — though not himself a learned man — funded and protected
learning on the plain conviction that it served the state. He kept the doors
of his own palace open day and night, wanting his house to resemble his
heart, "which was open to all his subjects."

To the army that had made him he applied the same instinct for
institutionalising what had been personal. Where the Five Dynasties had run
on the loyalty of individual soldiers to individual commanders — the exact
mechanism that had put five different men on the throne in half a century —
Taizu built an examined, promoted, salaried force answerable to the office
of emperor rather than to whichever general held its immediate allegiance.
Officers had to demonstrate physical fitness and a working knowledge of
military subjects before promotion; a soldier's advancement ran through the
state's own ranks, not through a warlord's household.

## Reunification, city by city

The task that remained was the actual reconquest of a divided country, and
Taizu pursued it province by province rather than in one campaign. Lieouki
Yuen, Prince of Han and the last of the great regional strongmen to hold out,
weighed the odds, judged further resistance futile, and surrendered his
forces to the emperor's camp in person — absorbed afterward as a minor prince
at court rather than executed, a mercy Boulger is careful to note was not
Taizu's universal practice but was characteristic of it. By such
accumulations of siege, negotiation and outright submission, the fragments
Zhao Kuangyin had inherited from a hundred years of fracture were reassembled
into a single administration for the first time since the Tang's own decline.

The reunification brought a fresh problem into view immediately: the
reassembled Chinese state now had a direct, long border with the Tartar
kingdom of Liao, which had been meddling in Chinese affairs for a century and
had no intention of stopping because the meddling now had a stronger
neighbour to answer to. That confrontation, and the peace it eventually
produced, would occupy the Song's founder and his immediate successors for
decades — but it was a problem a reunited empire could at least face as one
government, which was the entire point of the work Taizu had just finished.
