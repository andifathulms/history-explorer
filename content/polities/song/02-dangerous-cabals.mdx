Renzong came to the throne at thirteen in 1022, and for the first eleven
years of his reign the real ruler of China was his mother, the regent
empress dowager - a capable administrator in her own right, who repealed the
wartime tea and salt taxes and set up a board to keep local officials from
using taxation to oppress the people they collected it from.

## A war he did not choose well

Renzong took the government into his own hands on her death in 1033, and
within a year found himself facing a threat neither he nor his ministers had
taken seriously enough. Li Yuanhao, ruler of the frontier state of Xia in the
northwest, had spent years quietly drilling his own army on the Chinese
model while the Song court assumed a long peace meant a permanently weakened
neighbour. In 1038 he declared himself emperor in his own right rather than
the Prince of Xia the Song court had recognised him as, and the war that
followed went badly for China from the start: a three-day battle at Sanchuan
went to the Xia army, and a second engagement near Yang Moulong ended with
the Song commander Jinfou refusing to leave the field even mortally wounded -
"I ought to die," he is recorded saying, and did, his body found among the
thickest of the slain. The Khitan Liao, watching Song's weakness with their
own interest, used the moment to seize ten frontier cities of their own.

Renzong bought the peace his army could not win. The 1042 treaty with Liao
raised the annual tribute in silk and silver Song had already been paying;
the settlement with Xia the same year restored Li Yuanhao's full dignities
and committed the court to an annual gift of a hundred thousand bolts of silk
and thirty thousand pounds of tea, on top of conceding his right to fortify
his own frontier. It was not a settlement anyone at court could take pride
in. It did buy the twenty years of peace the rest of the reign is actually
remembered for.

## A circle of scholars in government

What that peace let Renzong build was less a military legacy than an
intellectual one. On the recommendation of his minister Fan Zhongyan, he
ordered a college built in every town of the empire, staffed with lecturers
and examiners, and personally prostrated himself before the doors of the
capital's own Confucian academy to mark how seriously he meant it. Late in
his reign he commissioned a full official history of the Tang dynasty,
assembled by a team that included Sima Guang - the finished work running to
two hundred and twenty-five volumes, one of the genuine monuments of Chinese
historical writing.

Fan Zhongyan, Sima Guang, Ouyang Xiu and the other scholars this policy
brought into government worked closely enough together that courtiers
complained to the emperor about a faction operating in the palace's own
corridors - "men in public service should not form a party amongst
themselves," ran the accusation Renzong himself half-credited. Ouyang Xiu's
own written defence survives: their association, he argued, produced results
good for the nation and was not to be confused with cabals formed for an
unworthy purpose. The twenty peaceful years that followed the settlements
with Xia and Liao gave that argument room to be tested, and by most later
judgments, Renzong's own long, unspectacular reign is remembered as having
won it.

## What outlived him

Renzong died in 1063 after thirty-one years on the throne, credited by the
record with greater virtue than ability but real success in the harder task
of keeping his ministers' loyalty and his subjects' affection without any
single dramatic achievement to point to. The scholarly circle his own
patronage had assembled - Sima Guang above all - went on to shape the
argument the next reign would actually be remembered for: whether the state
that Fan Zhongyan's generation had staffed with examination graduates should
also be the state that ran the economy those graduates administered.
