By the end of 1276 the Song cause held nothing but a handful of districts in
Guangdong and Fujian, and even that shrank further the following winter when
the Mongol armies returned from campaigning against Kublai's rebellious
nephew Kaidu and drove the last Song forces off the mainland entirely,
leaving them nothing but their ships and a scatter of islands off the Canton
estuary. The reigning child emperor died in that extremity. A small,
resolute remnant of officers proclaimed another Song prince in his place —
a child given the name Tiping — in 1278, less as the start of a
recovery than, in Boulger's phrase, "the prelude to its fall."

## The last harbour

The general Zhang Shijie took the boy and the fleet to a natural harbour on
an island called Yai, entered only on a favourable tide, and prepared as
best he could for a battle he knew was coming. When the Mongol fleet found
them there, the engagement was stubbornly contested, and there is little
doubt, Boulger writes, that not a single Song ship would have escaped at all
but for a heavy mist that fell over the battle and let Zhang Shijie break
out to sea with sixteen vessels behind him.

The emperor's own vessel had no such luck. Trapped in the press of battle, it
fell into Mongol hands. One of the Song's most loyal ministers, Lu Xiufu,
resolved in that moment to save what he could — not the dynasty, which was
plainly beyond saving, but its honour. He threw his own wife and children
into the sea, took the emperor in his arms, and jumped overboard with him.
Most of the remaining officers followed the same course rather than be
taken. Tiping, the last emperor of the Song, died that way, by drowning,
with his minister.

## What Zhang Shijie tried afterward

Zhang Shijie, still at sea with what was left of the fleet, learned of the
emperor's death and asked the boy's mother to name another Song prince to
succeed him. Grief proved stronger than the appeal to public duty; she threw
herself into the sea rather than be consoled, and no successor was named.
Zhang Shijie refused even then to treat the cause as finished. He sailed
south, was given refuge, ships and fresh troops by the ruler of Tonkin, and
returned within the year intending to seize Canton by surprise and reopen the
war — over the objections of his own followers, who judged, correctly, that
Kublai's power was by then too completely secured for a single squadron to
achieve anything but a local, temporary success before being ground down.

The renewed attack never happened. What ended the Song's last military
effort was not a battle but a storm that scattered the fleet before it could
strike, and Zhang Shijie drowned with it — the same sea that had taken the
emperor and his mother closing, within months, over the last man still
willing to fight in the dynasty's name. A Sung minister once asked, of the
army mustered to save the child emperor, "If Heaven has not resolved to
overthrow the Sungs, do you think that even now it cannot restore their
ruined throne?" Heaven, on the evidence Zhang Shijie's own death supplied,
had resolved exactly that.
