The alliance that destroyed the Jin in 1234 lasted less than a year before
the two victors turned on each other, and the war that followed ran for four
decades before it finally reached Hangzhou.

## An alliance neither side trusted

Song had joined the Mongols against the Jin for the oldest reason a weaker
power allies with a stronger one against a nearer enemy: a Jin envoy had
warned, accurately, that a Song court which let the Mongols finish off the
Jin unopposed would find itself next, but the temptation to see a
generations-old enemy destroyed outweighed the warning. The promised reward,
the restoration of Henan once the Jin fell, evaporated the moment victory was
won - Mongol garrisons simply stayed where they were, and when a proposal
to divide the province in two proved as unworkable as it sounds, both sides
understood the war that followed was not really about Henan at all.

## The first wave, 1235

Ögödei put an army estimated at half a million men into three columns in
1235: one crossing from Shaanxi into Sichuan, two others striking into the
lower Yangtze provinces. The northern half of Sichuan fell within the year,
its garrison commander Kao Kia dying at his post at the town of Mien rather
than surrender it - but the Mongol force that took it also withdrew the way
it had come once the campaigning season ended, "a passing scourge" rather
than an occupation, and Song forces reoccupied what had been depopulated in
the meantime. Ögödei's own attention was elsewhere - the campaigns in Russia
and Hungary his nephew Batu was fighting interested him more than the slow
grinding war in the south - and for the rest of his reign the Song front
received little more than raids.

## Xiangyang, 1268-1273

The decisive campaign waited for Kublai, and for a defector's advice on
where to strike. A renegade Song general convinced him that the war-city of
Xiangyang, paired across the Han river with its twin fortress Fanching,
was the single position whose fall would open both the river route and,
eventually, the Yangtze itself to Mongol shipping. In 1268 a Mongol army of
sixty thousand veteran troops, later reinforced, invested the two cities,
building an entrenched camp ten miles long and constructing their own war
fleet from nothing to contest the Han.

Xiangyang's governor, Lü Wenhuan, held for four years. His garrison had
provisions, by his own claim, for a decade; when the Mongols demanded
surrender he answered by threatening to drag their own renegade guide back
to his abandoned master in chains. In 1270, with the siege already three
years old, the Song commander at nearby Anlu, Li Tingzhi, gathered three
thousand volunteers and a convoy of supplies and ran the Mongol blockade by
river, breaking the chains strung across the Han and fighting through the
Mongol fleet while a second detachment under Zhang Shun held the enemy's
main force occupied for hours. The supply convoy reached Xiangyang to loud
celebration; Zhang Shun's own body was found floating past the walls days
later, the garrison's reminder that the siege itself had not lifted.

A second relief attempt under the same officers' companion, Zhang Gui,
ended the same way - a night run through the blockade, a fighting retreat
against overwhelming numbers once a deserter had warned the Mongols he was
coming, and a last stand with no survivors. The Mongol commander sent his
body back to Xiangyang under flag rather than keep it, whether from
admiration or calculated cruelty the record does not resolve; Lü Wenhuan had
it laid beside Zhang Shun's.

Persian siege engineers brought in for the purpose finally broke Fanching's
defenses with counterweight trebuchets capable of hurling stone at a scale
neither Chinese siege engine had matched; its garrison fought street to
street and chose suicide over surrender once resistance became hopeless.
With Fanching gone and no further relief coming from the corrupt chancellor
Jia Sidao, who had sent an incompetent general and then done nothing more,
Lü Wenhuan accepted terms and surrendered Xiangyang in 1273, entering
Mongol service himself after four years of holding the line for a court that
had largely abandoned him to it.

## What the fall actually opened

Xiangyang's loss did what its capture was meant to do: it put the Han river,
and with it the approach to the Yangtze, in Mongol hands. Emperor Duzong's
own court, informed of the disaster, is recorded retreating further into
drink and indifference rather than reckoning with the minister whose neglect
had produced it. The war that followed moved quickly by the standard of the
four decades that preceded it - Hangzhou itself fell within six years of
Xiangyang's surrender, and the flight to the southern coast that ended at
Yamen was already, by 1273, the only road left open to a dynasty that had
just lost the river its last defensible line had depended on.
