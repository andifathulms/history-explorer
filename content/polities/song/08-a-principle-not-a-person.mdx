The scholar who eventually settled how the Song's own philosophical output
would be read, for every dynasty that came after it, did not appear until
long after the dynasty's political and literary height had passed. Zhu Xi's
official career, between 1130 and 1200, was successful and his literary
output enormous, but Giles is explicit that his lasting importance rests on
something narrower than either: he re-read the Confucian canon and insisted,
against centuries of Han-dynasty interpretation accepted as settled, that the
same words could not mean one thing in one passage and something else in
another.

## A method, not a doctrine

The principle was almost aggressively simple — consistency, applied without
exception to a body of text that earlier commentators had been willing to
read two ways when it suited them. Its effect, Giles writes, was "magical."
From that date forward, the teachings of Confucius were understood, across
the whole of educated China, in the way Zhu Xi said they ought to be
understood — not because his readings were self-evidently correct, but
because the method that produced them refused to leave room for a
competitor.

## What it cost the old God

The consequence Giles traces furthest is theological rather than literary.
The God in whom Confucius himself had believed, as the earliest form of the
written character for the word shows — a rough picture of a man — had been
personal, closer in kind to the God of the Psalms than to an abstraction.
Zhu Xi discarded that reading outright. The Chinese word for God, he
declared in the plainest terms available to him, meant nothing more than
"abstract right": a principle, not a person. He had passed briefly through
Buddhism as a young man, found it unsatisfying, and returned to Confucian
orthodoxy determined to exclude the supernatural from it as completely as he
had excluded it from his own belief. What he built to replace both the
old personal God and the foreign religion he had tried and rejected was a
moral system with no supernatural content left in it at all — a reading
that, Giles notes without much enthusiasm for it, has been accepted as the
correct one more or less ever since.

Zhu Xi worked and taught entirely within the reduced, southern state the
dynasty had become after 1127; the "magical" uniformity Giles describes was
still spreading through Chinese education, on his account, centuries after
Zhu Xi's own death and long after the Song itself was gone.
