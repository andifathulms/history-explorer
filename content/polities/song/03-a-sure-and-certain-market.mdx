By the time Wang Anshi turned forty-eight he had risen, on the strength of a
formidable memory and an even more formidable pen, to confidential adviser of
the emperor Shenzong. What followed, in Giles's account, was one of the most
sweeping programmes of state intervention any Chinese government had yet
attempted — dressed, for the benefit of a population that distrusted anything
without precedent, in the language of a return to older and more correctly
understood readings of the Confucian classics. The dressing convinced almost
nobody in the senior bureaucracy and did not need to: it had the emperor.

## The middleman state

The centrepiece was a scheme aimed, in its own stated terms, at lowering
taxation while raising revenue at the same time — a promise Giles treats with
open scepticism, though he lays out the mechanism without editorial comment.
A district's produce was to be divided three ways: enough set aside to meet
its tax obligation, enough to meet its own needs, and the remainder bought up
by the state at a low fixed rate, to be held against a price rise or shipped
to wherever demand happened to be running high. The farmer got a guaranteed
buyer. The state got to keep the margin a private merchant would otherwise
have taken as middleman.

Beside it ran a system of compulsory state credit: advances to cultivators
secured against their standing crops, issued whether or not the farmer had
asked for one. A militia reform required every family with more than two
adult males to furnish one for military service, and separately obliged
every household to raise a state-subsidised cavalry horse on the government's
behalf. The old corvée obligation — forced labour drafts for public works —
was converted into a money payment instead. State warehouses for bartering
and pledging goods were set up empire-wide.

## What did not work

The most ambitious single measure, and the one Giles singles out as the
scheme's clearest overreach, was a plan to remeasure the entire country's
farmland into equal taxed squares and assess each according to its fertility.
It proved unworkable in practice and drew, like everything else Wang Anshi
proposed, bitter resistance from the officials who had to administer it.

A separate crisis followed his decision to lift all restriction on the
export of copper: the state's own copper cash began disappearing into
melting pots to be recast and sold as raw metal abroad, and a currency panic
followed. Wang Anshi's fix was blunt rather than elegant — he simply doubled
the nominal value of each coin still in circulation, which stopped the
bleeding without addressing why it had started.

## The examinations, and his own reading

He also went after the civil service examination itself, pushing candidates
toward practical subjects — history, geography, political economy — and away
from ornamental style for its own sake. A contemporary author, quoted by
Giles, recorded the effect at the grassroots with some astonishment: "even
the pupils at the village schools threw away their text-books of rhetoric,
and began to study primers of history, geography, and political economy."
Wang Anshi described his own reading habits, by way of justifying the
reform, as omnivorous to the point of eccentricity — medical texts, botanical
treatises, agricultural manuals, anything that might illuminate "the great
scheme of the Canon itself."

He fell from favour before the reign was out, was dismissed to a provincial
post, briefly recalled, and then retired from public life to die not long
after — having lived, Giles notes dryly, to see nearly the whole of his own
policy reversed by the men who came after him.
