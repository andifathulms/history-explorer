Feng Dao served ten emperors across four dynasties in the fifty-three years
of chaos before 960, and described himself to a Liao court willing to employ
him anyway as a man with "no home, no money, and very little brains" — a
statement the Tartar monarch apparently found appealing enough to appoint him
grand tutor to the heir on the spot. Chinese tradition remembers him less
kindly, as a survivor who gave himself the sobriquet later English writers
would translate as "the Vicar of Bray." To foreign readers his more durable
claim is technical rather than moral: some crude form of block printing had
existed since early in the Tang, but it was Feng Dao who first put it to the
production of books. Six years after his death in 954, the Song took the
throne he had spent his whole career outlasting other dynasties to keep, and
printing from blocks became, for the first time, an ordinary trade.

## What the printed word made possible

A literary class scattered by half a century of dynastic churn found itself,
almost as soon as the Song was founded, able to reproduce a text without
copying it out by hand — and Giles is explicit that the effect was immediate:
history, classical scholarship, general literature and poetry were "again
filled with enthusiastic workers," backed by a succession of rulers eager to
encourage them.

The first large project was a re-write of the Tang dynastic history from
scratch. A version already existed, compiled by a scholar of the Later Jin
named Liu Xu, but it was judged unsatisfactory on enough grounds that the
court commissioned a replacement rather than live with it — and the older
work, Giles notes, was never actually superseded: both still stand side by
side among the recognised dynastic histories today.

## Ou-yang Xiu

The new commission went to Ou-yang Xiu and Song Qi together. Ou-yang Xiu had
been raised in poverty, taught to write with a reed by a mother who could not
afford better, and by fifteen was already attracting notice for his ability;
he placed first among the candidates for the highest examination degree. His
public career was turbulent, because he kept taking the position he believed
correct without much regard for what it cost him — a habit that produced
repeated friction with the court he served. His private output, alongside the
dynastic history, ran to an exposition of the Book of Poetry, a study of
ancient inscriptions, anecdotes of his contemporaries, an elaborate treatise
on the peony, and poetry and essays without apparent end.

Song Qi's own career had its lighter moments. Giles records one occasion on
which, feeling the cold at an imperial gathering, he was offered a tippet by
about a dozen court ladies at once — and, unwilling to seem to favour any one
of them over the rest, chose to sit and shiver rather than accept from a
single hand.
