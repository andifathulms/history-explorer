---
title: As many kings as there were nobles

Aragon's nobility never accepted the kind of authority Castile's crown
gradually assembled over its own lords. One office grew out of that
resistance into something no other Spanish kingdom quite matched: the
Justicia, a magistrate charged with hearing complaints against royal
officials and, in time, with mediating directly between king and
nobility. The nobles tried to claim the appointment for themselves and
failed, but they did force James I to recognize that the Justicia
acted in his own right rather than as the crown's delegate - a judge
answerable to the law rather than to the man on the throne.

## A privilege that could unmake a king

The office reached its widest power in 1287, when a league of nobles
calling itself the Union compelled Alfonso III to grant the Privilege
of the Union: the king could no longer proceed against any member of
the Union without the joint consent of the Justicia and the Cortes,
and a standing council would sit beside him to decide matters touching
Aragon and Valencia. If the king broke the privilege, the Union could
depose him and choose another. Alfonso III himself summed up what this
meant for royal authority with a bitter joke of his own: there were,
he said, as many kings in Aragon as there were great nobles.

## A power the crown spent a century taking back

That arrangement did not survive contact with a stronger king. Pedro
IV broke the Union by force and reduced the Justicia from the
nobility's own creature into a royal appointee exercising narrower,
strictly judicial powers - though even Pedro could not simply abolish
the office, and the Cortes went on declaring successive Justicias
irremovable long after the nobles who had first won the privilege were
gone. Kings compelled their own appointees to sign undated letters of
resignation to get around that protection when it suited them, proof
that the office's formal independence and its real security were never
quite the same thing. What survived every round of that struggle was
the underlying principle the Union had forced into writing in 1287:
that a king of Aragon answered to a law his own will did not simply
make.
