James I came to the Aragonese throne as a young child, and spent his
early reign less as a king than as a prize other men fought over -
held for a time in the "dangerous possession" of the French warlord
Simon de Montfort, rescued only through the intervention of Pope
Innocent III, and twice captured outright by his own rebellious
nobles before he was even old enough to rule in his own right. Not
until 1228, well into his teens, did he finally command the situation
himself.

## Majorca, Minorca, Ibiza

Backed by a Catalonia eager for Mediterranean expansion but largely
abandoned by his own Aragonese and western Catalan nobles, who
declined to join the enterprise, James turned to conquest to make his
own name. He took Majorca in 1229, an island that had served for
centuries as a pirate stronghold, and distributed its lands among the
Catalan followers who had actually backed him. Minorca followed in
1232, and Ibiza in 1235 - the whole Balearic archipelago absorbed into
a Catalan cultural orbit it has never fully left since.

## Valencia, won by the nobles who had refused to come

The richer prize was Valencia. James's own nobles gave the campaign
only lukewarm support at first, but as his early successes accumulated,
even those who had stayed home found reason to join him, and in 1238
the city itself fell - an achievement contemporaries ranked alongside
Ferdinand III's capture of Seville that same decade. The wider kingdom
of Valencia followed within a few years, its lands distributed among
James's own nobility, though its Muslim population remained numerous
enough to rise in rebellion twice more before his reign ended. James
had already agreed with Castile that his own conquests would stop at
Castile's southern frontier, leaving Murcia for Ferdinand III to take
himself - a rare instance, in a period defined mostly by rivalry, of
two Christian kings dividing a frontier by agreement rather than by
whoever could reach it first.
