Aragon became a kingdom in its own right through the same 1035
testamentary division that gave Castile to Ferdinand I - Sancho the
Great of Navarre split his own holdings among his sons, and Ramiro I
received the small Pyrenean territory that would carry the Aragonese
name forward. For its first century the kingdom's own history was
mostly a story of steady, unspectacular growth: wars against Navarre,
wars against the Muslim frontier, and the peaceful absorption of
Catalonia's own outlying territory into a kingdom that, by the end of
the eleventh century, already held a real share of north-central
Spain.

## Zaragoza taken, and a will nobody honoured

The kingdom's first great conquest came under Alfonso I "the
Battler," whose marriage to Urraca of Castile had gone badly but whose
campaigns against the Muslim south went a great deal better. In 1118
he captured Zaragoza - an acquisition Aragon's own history would treat
with the same weight Castile gave to the capture of Toledo a
generation earlier - and pushed his raids as far as Murcia and
Andalusia. What actually secured the ground he had won was not the
raiding itself but the ten thousand Mozarabs he resettled in his new
territory.

## A throne left to strangers, and taken back by force of custom

Alfonso I had no son, and in his final years he tried to leave his
entire kingdom to two military religious orders rather than to any
human heir. His own subjects simply refused to accept it. The nobles
of Navarre used the vacuum to elect a king of their own, ending the
union between Navarre and Aragon that had held since Sancho the
Great's own time, while the nobles of Aragon chose Alfonso's brother
Ramiro instead - a man who, at the moment of his own election, was
living as a monk.
