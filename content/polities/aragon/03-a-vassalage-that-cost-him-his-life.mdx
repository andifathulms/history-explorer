Alfonso II, who had inherited Catalonia in 1162 and Aragon proper two
years later, spent much of his reign as a frequent ally of Castile
against the Moslems and, in 1179, as a treaty partner fixing the
future limits of Spanish conquest between the two crowns. He also
inherited nearly all of southern France, a possession that would
matter far more to his son than it ever had to him.

## A fief surrendered to check a kingdom that never checked it

Pedro II "the Catholic" came to the Aragonese throne in 1196 to find
his French dominions in turmoil, worsened by the spread of the
Albigensian heresy through Provence's own people and, more
dangerously, through its lords. In 1204 Pedro travelled to Rome and
placed his own kingdom in vassalage to the pope, receiving it back as
a fief - an act meant, it has been supposed, to check French
ambitions in the south. It checked nothing. The pope proclaimed a
crusade against the Albigenses regardless, and the crusaders who
answered it were French nobles pursuing what was, in substance if not
in name, a French invasion of Occitania.

## Muret

Under their leader Simon de Montfort, the crusaders won victory after
victory, with a cruelty against Catholic and heretic alike severe
enough to draw censure from a Spanish friar preaching among the
Albigenses at the time, Domingo de Guzmán - later canonized as Saint
Dominic. Pedro tried at first to mediate, and was persuaded by the
pope to recognize Montfort as his own vassal for the lands he had
already taken. When Montfort pressed further regardless, Pedro
declared war on the man the papacy had just asked him to accept as a
subordinate, and met him in battle at Muret on 12 September 1213. The
fight cost Pedro his life, and with it, any prospect that Aragon's own
Occitan possessions would survive the crusade that followed.
