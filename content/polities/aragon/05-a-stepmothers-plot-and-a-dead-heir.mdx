Juan II's final years on the Aragonese throne were consumed by a
family conflict his own second marriage had set in motion. His first
son, Charles of Viana, stood to inherit both Navarre and, in time,
Aragon itself - a prospect Juan's second wife, Juana Enriquez, worked
steadily to prevent in favour of her own children, one of whom, the
future King Ferdinand, would go on to display exactly the same
scheming instincts his mother had shown against his own half-brother.

## Catalonia takes up a prince's cause

When Juan refused to formally name Charles his heir, civil war broke
out - not only in Navarre, where Juana's intrigues had done the most
damage, but across Catalonia and Aragon as well, with the Catalans
taking up Charles's cause with particular enthusiasm. Charles won
enough early success that his father agreed, at least on paper, to
recognize him as successor and appoint him governor of Catalonia. The
agreement had barely been signed when the young prince died suddenly,
and public opinion, rightly or not, blamed poison administered at his
stepmother's own instigation.

## Twelve years of war, ended by exhaustion rather than victory

Charles's death, far from ending the conflict, reignited it. Denied a
legitimate rival claimant to rally behind, the Catalans experimented
with electing their own counts of Barcelona and even floated the idea
of a republic, but a war that dragged on for twelve years, and the
death of one improvised leader after another, wore down the appetite
for further resistance. Peace came in 1472, by which time Juan
himself was blind, widowed again, and largely alone - his own son
Ferdinand having remained in Castile since 1469, married to Isabella
in a match that would matter more to Aragon's own future than
anything the Catalan war had been fought over. When Juan died in
1479, Ferdinand inherited a kingdom already bound, through his own
marriage a decade before, to the crown of Castile - two kingdoms, two
Cortes, two bodies of law, sharing nothing now but the same king and
queen.
