The reigns of Juan I and Martin I mattered more for institutions than
for conquest, and what little territory changed hands moved the wrong
way. Juan I's reign saw the loss of the duchy of Athens, the last of
the Catalan Company's Aegean holdings; Martin's saw only the return of
Sicily to the Aragonese line, an island the crown already expected
back. When Martin died in 1410 without a surviving heir, the throne
his predecessors had spent two centuries building into a Mediterranean
power stood open to dispute.

## A crown decided by argument rather than by war

Two claimants stood out from the rest: Ferdinand of Antequera, regent
of Castile and a son of Martin's own sister, and Jaime, count of
Urgel, descended from a cousin of the dead king. Rather than settling
the question on a battlefield, the parliaments of Aragon, Catalonia
and Valencia each sent three men to Caspe to arbitrate it, and the
nine spent three months hearing the rival cases before announcing
their decision in June 1412. Ferdinand's claim carried the backing of
the Aragonese-obedience pope, Benedict XIII, and of popular and
clerical opinion alike, and it was Ferdinand the nine electors named
king rather than Jaime of Urgel.

## A Castilian on the Aragonese throne

Ferdinand had governed Castile as regent for his infant nephew and now
left that regency to rule a second crown outright, becoming the first
king of the house of Trastámara to sit in Aragon rather than in
Castile. Jaime of Urgel did not accept the verdict quietly, raising a
revolt that Ferdinand crushed within a year of his own coronation -
but the crown Ferdinand took up was, for the first time in Aragon's
history, one an elected panel had awarded rather than one won by
inheritance or the sword alone.
