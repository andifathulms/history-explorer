Ferdinand I's son Alfonso V, called the Magnanimous, took the Catalan
policy of Mediterranean expansion further than any king before him -
and did it so thoroughly that he stopped ruling his own kingdom from
inside it at all.

## Adopted by a queen who needed an army

The queen of Naples, faced with a war against the house of Anjou for
her own throne, invited Alfonso to her aid and adopted him as her
heir. Alfonso spent most of his forty-two-year reign in Italy rather
than in Spain, and made good on the adoption: he broke Angevin
resistance, secured Naples for himself outright, and built a
celebrated royal court there rather than at Zaragoza or Barcelona. He
went on intervening in wars across Italy well beyond what the Neapolitan
succession required, and after Constantinople fell to the Ottomans in
1453 he reportedly considered leading an expedition to retake it - an
ambition that came to nothing, but that shows how far his own horizon
had shifted from the kingdom that had elected his father king four
decades earlier.

## A crown split before it could be inherited whole

Alfonso's absence had a cost at home that his own reign never had to
answer for personally: internal disorder that his successor would
inherit in full. When he died in 1458, Alfonso divided his own
conquests rather than passing them on as one block. Naples went to his
illegitimate son Ferdinand, severing the kingdom he had spent decades
winning from the rest of the Aragonese crown; Sardinia, Sicily and the
older Iberian and Mediterranean possessions passed instead to his
brother Juan, who took the Aragonese throne as Juan II. It was Juan
who would spend the next reign dealing with the disorder Alfonso's own
long absence in Italy had let take root.
