---
title: A mosque out of Ephesus
drafted_from: peacock-yildiz-2013
phase: aside
---

The Aydinid capital was Ayasoluk, which is the hill above Ephesus. In 1375 Isa
Bey had a congregational mosque built there, and it is still standing.

It is a good building — a wide prayer hall under two domes, a courtyard, a
marble portal with interlace carving — and what makes it worth a chapter is
what it is made of. The columns inside came out of the ruins below: Roman and
early Byzantine shafts and capitals, carried up from Ephesus and the ruined
basilica of Saint John, set into a mosque under a Turkish emir a thousand years
after they were cut.

This site has the same practice on the Ghurid page, where the mosque and
minaret raised at Delhi from 1193 were built substantially from the dismantled
fabric of temples, and follows Flood in arguing that reuse of that kind is not
adequately described as either iconoclasm or economy — it was a common medieval
way of appropriating the prestige of what was taken, done by rulers on all
sides of the period's religious lines.

Ayasoluk is the easier case and therefore the useful one. Nobody was
dismantling a functioning Christian church here: Ephesus had been declining for
seven hundred years, the harbour had silted, the great basilica was already a
ruin, and the columns were lying in it. What a builder in 1375 saw was good
marble, cut, on site, in a place with no quarry. The mosque takes its
proportions from the classical material it is built out of, because the
material determined them.

Two chapters of this page are about a beylik that lived by raiding and lost its
harbour. This is what the same polity did with the sixty years afterwards: it
built, in a decayed Roman city, using the Roman city, a mosque that is the best
surviving Aydinid object and one of the better fourteenth-century buildings in
Anatolia.

The corpus keeps finding that the interesting thing a small polity leaves is
not its territory. The Artuqids left a book of machines, the Ziyarids a tower,
the Bavands a genealogy. This one left a building assembled out of an older
one, which is a reasonable image of what the Turkish settlement of western
Anatolia was.
