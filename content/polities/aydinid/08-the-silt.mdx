The Aydinid capital was Ayasoluk, the hill above Ephesus, and its harbour was
already dying when the beylik took it.

Ephesus had been one of the great ports of the Roman world. The Cayster, which
made the plain fertile, also carried silt, and over a thousand years the
coastline moved west; the classical harbour works ended up several kilometres
inland, and the city moved uphill to the citadel and the basilica of Saint John.

What the Aydinids inherited in about 1304 was a decayed Byzantine town on a hill
with a silting anchorage below it, and what they did with it was build.

<Figure id="st-john-basilica" />

Isa Bey's congregational mosque of 1375 is still standing, and the columns
inside it came out of the ruins below.

## What became of the ground

Aydin's territory became Ottoman provinces and its ports became Ottoman ports,
and the naval habit went with them. The Aegean coast the beylik had raided from
supplied crews, captains and shipyards to the Ottoman fleet for the next four
centuries, and the men who beat Venice at sea in the sixteenth century were
working out of the same harbours with the same populations behind them.

Ayasoluk itself did not survive as a port. The silt kept coming, the harbour
closed entirely, and the town shrank to a village beside a ruin. It carries the
dynasty's name now: the province and its capital are called Aydın, after a
Turkmen family that held the coast for a hundred and twenty years, and the
village by the basilica is Selçuk, after the Seljuks, who never governed it.

## The residue

What is left of the beylik is therefore three things, and none of them is
territory.

A mosque made out of a Roman city. A stretch of coast that carries the family's
name on the map. And one afternoon at Birgi in 1333, when a Turkmen prince
showed a Moroccan lawyer a meteorite and had four men hit it with a hammer to
prove it could not be broken.
