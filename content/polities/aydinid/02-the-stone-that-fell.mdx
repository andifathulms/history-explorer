In about 1333 Ibn Battuta arrived at Birgi, in the hills above the Cayster, and
was presented to Mehmed ibn Aydın. What happened next is the best surviving
scene of an Anatolian beylik court doing anything at all.

The king asked him whether he had ever seen a stone that came down from heaven.
Ibn Battuta said he had not. The king said one had fallen in the country around
the city, and sent men to bring it.

> It was a black, solid, exceedingly hard, and shining substance. If weighed it
> would probably exceed a talent.

Then he called for stone-cutters. Four came in, and he told them to strike it,
and they hit it four times together with an iron hammer and made no impression
on it whatever. Ibn Battuta says he was much astonished. The king had the stone
taken back where it came from.

## What a beylik was like from inside

The rest of the visit is the ordinary business of a court that wanted educated
visitors and could pay for them. The emir sent fruit and food for as long as the
party stayed. On leaving, Ibn Battuta was given a thousand dirhams, a hundred
mithqals of gold, clothing, two horses and a slave; his companions were given
money and clothes of their own.

That is a considerable outlay for a few days' hospitality by a Turkmen dynasty
two generations off the plateau, and it is the point. These were not warbands
camped in somebody else's country. They were governments with revenue, and they
spent it on the same things governments in Cairo and Damascus spent it on:
scholars, buildings, and the reputation for generosity that brought more of
both.

## The map in one paragraph

Ibn Battuta then walked on, and his itinerary is the political geography of
western Anatolia in a single sentence each.

Tire, also the prince's. Ayasoluk, whose emir is Mehmed's son. **Smyrna, which
belongs to the sultan of Birgi, and whose emir is Umur Beg, one of the sultan's
sons — *a most excellent prince*.** Magnesia under Saruhan. Bergama under
Bakhshi Khan. Balıkesir under Damur Khan.

And then Bursa, governed by Orhan Beg son of Osman, of whom he says something he
says of nobody else on the coast: that he is the greatest, the richest, the most
extensive in rule and commanding the largest army of all the Turkmen kings, and
that his practice is never to stay a month in one place but to go continually
round his fortresses and districts asking how things stand.

A traveller crossing western Anatolia in 1333 met eight Turkish princes, was
handsomely entertained by several, admired the young governor of Smyrna, and
identified the one who was going to win.
