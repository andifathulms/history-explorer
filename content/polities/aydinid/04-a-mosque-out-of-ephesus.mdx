---
title: A mosque out of Ephesus
drafted_from: peacock-yildiz-2013
phase: aside

The Aydinid capital was Ayasoluk, which is the hill above Ephesus. In 1375 Isa
Bey had a congregational mosque built there, and it is still standing.

It is a good building — a wide prayer hall under two domes, a courtyard, a
marble portal with interlace carving — and what makes it worth a chapter is
what it is made of. The columns inside came out of the ruins below: Roman and
early Byzantine shafts and capitals, carried up from Ephesus and the ruined
basilica of Saint John, set into a mosque under a Turkish emir a thousand years
after they were cut.

Reuse of that kind was ordinary and it is badly served by the two explanations
usually offered for it. It was not simply iconoclasm and it was not simply
economy. Taking the columns of an older and grander building and standing them
up inside your own was a recognised way of appropriating what they had meant, and
rulers on every side of the period's religious lines did it — Christian churches
were built out of temples, mosques out of churches, and churches out of
mosques, for centuries, by people who would have found the modern argument about
it incomprehensible.

Ayasoluk is the easier case and therefore the useful one. Nobody was
dismantling a functioning Christian church here: Ephesus had been declining for
seven hundred years, the harbour had silted, the great basilica was already a
ruin, and the columns were lying in it. What a builder in 1375 saw was good
marble, cut, on site, in a place with no quarry. The mosque takes its
proportions from the classical material it is built out of, because the
material determined them.

The beylik that built it had lived by raiding and had lost its harbour thirty
years earlier. This is what it did with the decades afterwards: it built, in a
decayed Roman city, out of the decayed Roman city, the best surviving Aydinid
object and one of the better fourteenth-century buildings in Anatolia.

Which is a fair image of the Turkish settlement of western Anatolia altogether.
Not a clearance and not a continuation, but a new building standing on old
columns, taking its proportions from them because the columns were what there
was.
