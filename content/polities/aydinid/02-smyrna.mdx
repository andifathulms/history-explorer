title: What happens when the harbour is taken
drafted_from: peacock-yildiz-2013

In October 1344 a naval league assembled by the papacy — Venice, Cyprus, the
Knights Hospitaller — attacked Smyrna and took the harbour castle. They held
it, on and off, for the next fifty-eight years.

That single fortification ended the beylik as a power. The Aydinid state was
built on raiding, and raiding required a defensible harbour to sail from and
return to; without Smyrna the fleets could not operate at scale, and the
revenue that had paid the crews stopped. Umur Bey died in 1348 assaulting the
walls to get it back.

What the beylik became afterwards is the ordinary fate of the small Anatolian
principalities, and it is the reason a page for one of them is worth having
alongside Karaman. Aydin was annexed by Bayezid I in 1390, restored by Timur
after Ankara in 1402 along with the other beyliks, spent twenty years under a
final ruler named Cüneyd who changed sides in every Ottoman civil war he could
reach, and was annexed for good in 1426. Cüneyd was executed with his family.

Set beside this site's Karamanid page the two trajectories are the same
argument. Karaman had mountains and lasted until 1487; Aydin had a harbour and
lasted until somebody else took it. Both were annexed, restored by Timur, and
annexed again. Neither could convert a strong position into a state that
survived losing it.

The Ottomans, who began as the smallest of these principalities, are the
exception this whole region exists to frame — and the thing they had that
neither of these did was Rumelia, on the other side of the water, which meant a
defeat in Anatolia was survivable. This site's Ottoman page makes that argument
from the winning side. These two make it from the other one.
