Aydin was annexed by Bayezid I in 1390, restored by Timur after Ankara in 1402
along with every other beylik the Ottomans had swallowed, and finally annexed
for good in 1426. The thirty-six years between the first annexation and the last
are the career of one man.

Cüneyd was the last Aydinid ruler in anything but name, and he was a
professional survivor of Ottoman civil wars. He changed sides in every one he
could reach. He served whichever Ottoman prince was currently losing and
therefore needed him, extracted a principality as the price, lost it when that
prince lost, and reappeared attached to the next claimant. He held Smyrna, then
did not; held Ayasoluk, then did not; was governor of a province of the empire
he spent his life fighting, twice.

It worked for a remarkably long time and it worked for a structural reason. The
Ottoman succession was contested — after 1402 there were four brothers with
armies, and after that a rebellion led by a man claiming to be one of them — and
every contested succession is an opportunity for somebody with local troops and
no scruples about which side he is on.

Murad II ended it in 1426. Cüneyd was besieged, surrendered on terms, and was
executed with his family. The line stopped there.

## What the beylik never managed

Aydin had a harbour, and lost it, and could not replace it. Karaman had
mountains, and could not be dislodged from them, and could not stay off them.

Neither could convert a strong position into a state that survived losing it,
and that is the thing worth taking from a small record. The Ottomans, who began
as the least promising of the dozen, had Rumelia — territory on the far side of
the water — which meant a defeat in Anatolia cost them a province rather than
their existence. Twice in this period an Ottoman ruler lost Anatolia
comprehensively and came back from Europe.

The beyliks each had one asset. The winner had two, in two places, with a sea
between them.
