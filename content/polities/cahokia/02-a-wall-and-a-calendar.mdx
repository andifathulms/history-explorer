Nearly half of all the radiocarbon dates ever recovered from
Cahokia - forty-five of ninety-five assays - fall between A.D. 950
and 1150, and a full quarter of them cluster even more tightly in
the single century between 1050 and 1150. Whatever this settlement
had been earlier, that century is when it was at its most crowded
and its most active, and the population estimates for the site at
its height, however debated among archaeologists, are not
disputed in their general order of magnitude: more than ten
thousand people, and by some reckonings as many as thirty thousand
- the largest and most densely settled community anywhere within
the present-day United States before European contact.

## A wall around the center

Sometime after A.D. 1100, in the later part of this same peak
century, a large wooden palisade was raised enclosing roughly
eighty-three hectares around the site's central plaza and its
grandest mounds. Excavation shows the wall was deliberately taken
down rather than left to rot where it stood, and later replacements
of it extended construction into the thirteenth century. Whatever
its purpose - excavators have doubted it was primarily defensive -
it drew a clear line between an inner precinct of elite residences
and mortuary buildings and the smaller mound-and-plaza clusters
spreading out beyond it, home to lesser chiefs and to newcomers
drawn from distant regions by Cahokia's own prosperity.

## Circles of posts

Nearby, excavators found the remains of large circles built from
rows of substantial wooden posts, later dubbed "Woodhenges" by the
archaeologist who first mapped them. He read them as calendrical
structures, aligned to track the solar year - a plausible enough
reading, given how precisely Cahokia's own builders had already
shown they could lay out an earthwork like Monks Mound on such a
scale, that a community capable of moving that much earth might
just as readily have wanted a way to track the turning of the
seasons above it.
