No chronicle names the people who built Cahokia or the leaders who
directed them; nothing of the city's own language survives in
writing. What survives instead is dirt, and archaeologists have
learned to read it. Solid-core drilling through the site's central
earthwork, the vast platform known today as Monks Mound, cut nine
holes deep into its layers and found bands of limestone-rich soil
and buried surfaces marking distinct episodes of construction -
roughly fourteen of them in all.

## Eighteen years to a layer

Radiocarbon dates recovered from material bracketing the mound's
growth cluster between about A.D. 900 and 1150, a span of two and a
half centuries into which the fourteen building stages fit at an
average of some eighteen years apiece - each one, presumably, a
distinct campaign of labor rather than a single continuous effort.
Investigators who tried to estimate the workforce such a project
would have required put the figure at around ten thousand people,
an early sign of just how large a population the site was already
drawing to itself.

## The largest earthwork north of Mexico

By the time the mound reached its final form, its base covered
roughly sixteen acres and its summit stood close to a hundred feet
above the surrounding floodplain - the largest earthen monument
built anywhere north of central Mexico, raised entirely by people
who had no draft animals, no wheeled transport, and no metal tools
to move the soil with. Whatever else Cahokia would become, its
central mound alone was already, by the mid-twelfth century, a
statement of organized labor on a scale nothing else in the region
approached.
