Cahokia's decline, like its rise, left no chronicle - only a
thinning record in the ground. The radiocarbon evidence that
clusters so heavily around the peak century of 1050 to 1150 falls
away steadily in the assays dated afterward, and the site's own
archaeological periodization places the whole Mississippian
period - the era of Cahokia's mounds, plazas and palisade - between
about A.D. 1000 and 1400. By the close of that span, the
organized, densely populated center that had once required a
wooden calendar and a wall around its own heart had ceased to
function as one.

## A name borrowed from elsewhere

When the first known European map of the area was drawn in 1796,
it showed no mounds at all directly across from the site of
St. Louis, and the district appears to have lain off the beaten
track of the roads and river routes then in use. The name "Cahokia"
itself was borrowed from an unrelated group living nearby in the
historic era, part of the Illinois Confederation; there is no
evidence they were themselves descended from, or connected to, the
people who had raised the mounds centuries before. Whatever society
had organized the labor of ten thousand people to build a
hundred-foot pyramid and a solar calendar of wooden posts had, by
the time any written record reached the region, already passed
beyond memory - leaving only the earthwork itself to answer for it.
