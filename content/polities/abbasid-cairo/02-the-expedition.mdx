The Cairo caliphate tried to be a state exactly once, in the first year of its
existence, and the attempt killed the caliph who made it.

## Proving who he was

Al-Mustansir had been a prisoner in Baghdad. When the Mongols took the city he
was released in the confusion, got away, and lived among the tribes west of Iraq
until Baybars became sultan and he came to Cairo with ten men of the Banu
Muharish.

The first thing that happened to him was an examination. Baybars came out to meet
him with the judges and the officers of state, he rode through Cairo — and then,
al-Suyuti records, his descent was *certified* through the chief qadi before he
could be acknowledged caliph. An Abbasid arriving from the desert three years
after the line had been publicly extinguished was not simply believed. His
genealogy was checked by the senior judicial officer of Egypt, and only after
that did the oath of allegiance begin, the sultan swearing first, then the chief
qadi, then the great shaykh Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam, then the nobles by rank.

Then the two ceremonies that were the whole point. The caliph rode in the black
mantle to the mosque in the citadel, mounted the pulpit and preached. And in a
pavilion built outside the walls, before the judges and the nobles, he invested
Baybars with his own hands — a black gold-embroidered turban, a violet mantle, a
gold chain for the neck, golden buckles for the feet — while the secretary of
state read the diploma of investiture aloud from a second pulpit. Baybars then
rode back in through the Gate of Succour, the city decorated, the high marshal
carrying the diploma before him and the nobles walking.

Baybars settled a revenue on him and gave him a household: an atabek,
comptrollers, a steward, a treasurer, a chamberlain, a secretary, slaves, a
hundred horses, thirty mules and ten camels.

## Baghdad

Then al-Mustansir asked to go and take Baghdad back, and Baybars financed it.

The sultan escorted him as far as Damascus and furnished the expedition with a
million gold dinars and sixty-six thousand dirhams. The eastern princes came in —
the lord of Mosul, the lord of Syria, the lord of Jazirat ibn Umar. So did a
rival: the amir of Aleppo had set up an Abbasid of his own with the title
al-Hakim, whose name was already on coins and in the Friday prayer, and this
second caliph submitted to al-Mustansir and joined the march.

They took Hadithah. They took Hit. Then the Mongol army met them, a great number
of Muslims were killed, and the caliph was missing. Al-Suyuti reports both
versions and states his own view without softening it: some say he was slain,
*which was apparently the case*; others that he escaped and lay concealed in the
provinces.

November 1261. His caliphate had lasted less than six months.

## What was learned

The line that ran for the next two and a half centuries was not his. It was
al-Hakim's — the rival from Aleppo, who had submitted on the road to Baghdad and
outlived the man he submitted to.

And the lesson was absorbed permanently. No Cairo caliph ever again led an army,
asked for one, or attempted to recover anything. The office spent the following
two hundred and fifty years doing what it had done on that one afternoon in the
pavilion outside the walls: conferring on other people the authority it could not
exercise itself.
