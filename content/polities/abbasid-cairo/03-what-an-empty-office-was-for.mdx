---
title: What an empty office was for
drafted_from: banister-2021

The standard verdict is that the Cairo caliphs were figureheads and puppets,
and the standard verdict is where Banister starts rather than where he ends.
The dismissal is inherited: chroniclers close to the sultanate had little
reason to dwell on them, later Ottoman writing had reason to minimise the line
it had displaced, and modern accounts have generally passed the brevity along.

Three things the office actually did. It invested sultans, and the investiture
was not decorative in a state where sultans were made by faction and murder at
a rate the Mamluk succession made necessary: a new sultan wanted the ceremony,
and the ceremony required the caliph to be there and to consent. It delegated
authority outward, to rulers who had no other way of obtaining it. In 1496
Askia Muhammad of Songhai obtained recognition as caliph of the Sudan from the
Cairo Abbasid — a West African ruler buying a title from a man with no
soldiers, and getting his money's worth. And it was a resource in a crisis:
when the sultanate broke down, the caliph was a piece on the board because he
was the one figure no faction had made.

That last point produced the exception, in 1412, when the amirs could not agree
on a sultan and made the caliph one. It is the only occasion in two and a half
centuries when the office held power rather than conferring it, and it happened
for exactly the reason above: the caliph was the one figure in Cairo who was not
a faction. It is told in full further on.

And it ended the way it began: in the possession of whoever held Egypt.
