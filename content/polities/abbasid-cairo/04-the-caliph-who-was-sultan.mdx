---
title: The caliph who was made sultan
drafted_from: suyuti-jarrett

In May 1412 the sultan Faraj marched against a rebel governor, was beaten, and
fled. The amirs could not agree on a replacement, and did the one thing that had
never been done in Cairo: they made the caliph sultan.

## He did not want it

Al-Musta'in had been caliph since 1405 under his father's covenant. Al-Suyuti is
careful about what happened next, and the care is the interesting part: the
caliph *did not assent to this save after opposition and continuous protest*, and
he made the amirs swear an oath first.

What he made them swear was specific. If he were ever deposed from the sultanate,
they would keep him as caliph.

A man being handed supreme power in Egypt negotiated, before accepting it, for
the right to go back to being powerless. He had judged the situation correctly
and he was still overruled by it.

Then he returned to Cairo, appointed and dismissed ministers, and had the coinage
struck in his name — though his title, al-Suyuti notes, stayed as it was.

## The poem

The most revealing document of the episode is a celebration, and it was written
by the greatest scholar of the age.

Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani — the hadith master whose commentary on Bukhari is still
the standard one — composed an ode for the occasion. The lines al-Suyuti quotes
begin:

> The kingdom is on a sure foundation among us, through al-Musta'in the Just the
> Abbaside. The dignity of the descendants of the uncle of Muhammad hath returned
> to its place after a long oblivion.

*After a long oblivion.* A hundred and fifty years of investing Mamluk sultans
had not, to the scholars of Cairo, counted as the Abbasids holding anything. The
moment one of them actually governed, the learned class produced the language of
restoration immediately, which means the language had been sitting there unused
the whole time.

## Six months

The reality arrived within weeks, and al-Suyuti describes it as an office
geography.

Al-Musta'in lived in the fort. Shaykh al-Mahmudi — the victorious rebel — set
himself up in the *stables*, and was given the administration of Egypt with the
title Nizam al-Mulk. And the nobles, when released from duty at the palace, went
down to the stables to pay their respects there, and the business of government
was transacted in the shaykh's presence. Afterwards his chancellor would go up to
the fort, where the caliph attached his signature.

Then even that was taken. It was represented that the caliph could not sign a
document until it had first been shown to the shaykh. Al-Suyuti's phrase for what
followed is a physical one: the caliph *was now in trepidation and his heart was
straitened*.

In November the shaykh required him to resign the sultanate. Al-Musta'in agreed
on one condition — that he be allowed to come down from the citadel to his own
house. The shaykh refused even that, took the sultanate as al-Mu'ayyad, and in
1414 publicly deposed him from the caliphate as well and proclaimed his brother
Da'ud. Al-Musta'in was moved into a house inside the citadel with guards to keep
people away from him.

## The judgement

And here the office showed what it still was.

Nawruz, governor of Syria, assembled the judges and the doctors of law and asked
for a ruling on what al-Mu'ayyad had done in deposing and imprisoning the caliph.
They gave sentence that it was *unlawful*, and it was agreed to make war on him
for it.

A titular caliph with no soldiers had been set aside by the man who held Egypt,
and the response was a formal legal finding and a civil war. The oath the amirs
had sworn in May turned out to be worth exactly what al-Musta'in had feared and
rather more than anyone else expected.

He was sent to Alexandria and imprisoned there, released some years later and
allowed to return to Cairo — and chose to stay in Alexandria, which suited him,
where the merchants gave him considerable sums. He died there of the plague in
1415.
