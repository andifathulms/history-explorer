On 22 January 1517 the Ottomans beat the Egyptian army outside Cairo. The next
day Selim was prayed for in every mosque in the city. On the 26th he entered
Cairo in state, and Lane-Poole's sentence about that entry is the one to keep:
he came in accompanied by the captive caliph.

The last Mamluk sultan was betrayed and hanged at the Zuwayla gate in April.
Al-Mutawakkil was carried off to Constantinople and imprisoned. When Selim died
in September 1520 his son released him and let him go home to Cairo, where he
lived out his life and died — Lane-Poole says 1538; other accounts say 1543 —
and where, on the version Lane-Poole gives, he bequeathed his title and his
rights to the Ottoman sultan.

## A story doing its work

That bequest almost certainly did not happen, and the interest of finding it
here is that this is a standard English history of Egypt published in 1901, by
the curator who had catalogued the Egyptian coins in the British Museum,
stating it as plain fact in a single subordinate clause.

By 1901 the transfer had been the official Ottoman position for over a century.
It had been useful in 1774, when the empire was negotiating with Russia over
its standing as protector of Muslims it no longer governed, and it was useful
again under Abdulhamid II, who was making the caliphal title do real diplomatic
work from India to central Asia at exactly the time Lane-Poole was writing. A
claim that begins as a negotiating position ends as a line in a reference book,
which is how such claims usually end.

## The objection everybody knew

Lane-Poole records the objection in the same breath, and it is worth having,
because it shows the argument was live rather than settled.

The legality of the inheritance, he writes, is repudiated not only by the Shi'a
but by the majority of learned Sunnis, who are aware that a caliph must belong
to the Prophet's tribe of Quraysh. The Ottomans were not Qurayshi and could not
be made so. What he then concedes is the part that mattered in practice:
whatever they were by right, the sultans of Turkey had been caliphs in fact
over most of Sunni Islam ever since al-Mutawakkil died.

That is the whole afterlife of the Cairo caliphate in two sentences. The office
could not be inherited, and it was inherited anyway, because the thing that
made it real was never the descent — it was being the ruler with Mecca and
Medina in hand, which is what the Cairo Abbasids' own patrons had been for two
and a half centuries and what the Ottomans now were.

## And Egypt

The other consequence Lane-Poole notes has nothing to do with the caliphate and
is the more concrete of the two.

After the conquest, Egypt sank into the position of a mere province of the
Turkish empire, and was administered separately from Syria and Arabia beside
it. The country that had housed a caliph in order to have a caliph — that had
gone to the trouble of finding an Abbasid in 1261 and installing sixteen more
of them — stopped needing one on the day it stopped being the seat of anybody's
government.

The office and the state that invented the use for it went out together.
