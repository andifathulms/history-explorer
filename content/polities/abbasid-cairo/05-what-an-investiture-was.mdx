The transaction this whole polity existed to perform is usually described in
three words — the caliph invested the sultan — and it is worth setting out what
those words covered, because the specificity is the argument for taking the
office seriously.

A new sultan came to the caliph, or the caliph was brought to him. There was a
formal audience with the senior officers, the four chief judges of the legal
schools and the religious establishment present as witnesses. The caliph
delegated authority in a spoken formula and a written diploma, naming the
territories and the powers conferred. The sultan was robed — a specific set of
garments, the caliph's own black in some periods — and received a sword and,
often, a banner. The sultan then swore to uphold the sharia, protect the
frontiers and the pilgrimage, and rule justly. The diploma was read publicly.
Afterwards the sultan's name was pronounced in the Friday sermon and struck on
the coinage, with the caliph's name above it.

Almost none of that involved the caliph doing anything a soldier could not have
compelled. That is the point. The value of the ceremony was that it could not
be performed by anybody else: there was exactly one man in the world who could
confer it, he was in Cairo, and he was in the sultan's keeping.

Two things follow. The first is that the caliph's consent was a real if narrow
lever, because a refusal was expensive and visible — and in a sultanate where
rulers were made by faction and murder, an accession that looked irregular
needed the ceremony more, not less. The second is that the ceremony was
portable. A ruler who could not come to Cairo could be sent a diploma, which is
how a ruler in the Niger valley could be recognised as caliph of the Sudan in
1496 by a man with no soldiers, and treat the recognition as worth the journey.

The office had no territory, no army and no revenue. What it had was a monopoly
on a document, and for two and a half centuries the document was worth
travelling for.
