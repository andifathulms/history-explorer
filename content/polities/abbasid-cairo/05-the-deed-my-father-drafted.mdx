There is one history of the caliphate written from inside the Cairo Abbasid
household. Jalal al-Din al-Suyuti, the most prolific scholar of
fifteenth-century Egypt, finished his *History of the Caliphs* in Cairo in the
1490s. His father was imam to one of these caliphs and drafted their documents.
Suyuti himself, as he says in a single unemphatic clause, was brought up in the
caliph's house and on his bounty.

So the last section of his book is not research. It is family.

## The document

He quotes the deed by which al-Mu'tadid named his full brother Sulayman as his
successor, in 845 of the Hijra — 1441 — because his father wrote it.

It is worth reading for its own sake. The heir is "the sublime seat of dignity,
the learned doctor, the noble, the princely, high in rank and birth, of royal
descent"; the appointment is made "for the sublime Caliphate", "as an Imam over
the Muslims", "for the fulfilment of what is obligatory upon him for the due
government of the affairs of those who believe in one God", and "to follow the
traditions of the orthodox Caliphs and rightly-guided Imams".

The reasoning is careful and entirely internal. The caliph has tested his
brother's condition and become acquainted with his heart and knows of nothing
emanating from him that impairs his claim. If he left the office unsettled he
would burden the men who have authority to loose and bind with the trouble of
choosing, so he has acted out of compassion, to free them from the charge. And
a clause worth its weight: this covenant does not require the approval of
everyone concerned in it, but binds whoever hears it, who must make it known
and call people to submit to it when the time comes.

Every phrase in it belongs to an office that had once governed from the
Atlantic to the Indus. It is being used to hand an empty title from one brother
to another, in a city where a Turkish sultan decided everything, by a man whose
son would grow up in that household and copy the text into a book.

## What he says about them

Suyuti's judgement on these caliphs is not what a reader expecting
embarrassment would predict, and it is not embarrassed at all.

Sulayman — al-Mustakfi — he calls one of the most virtuous of the caliphs:
pious, devout, constant in prayer and in reading the Qur'an, given to silence,
shunning the society of men, of excellent disposition. His brother said he had
never known a serious fault in him since he grew up. The sultan trusted him and
knew his worth. Suyuti's father was his imam and stood high with him.

Then Suyuti says the thing he must have meant, because it costs him something:
that the family of this caliph are excellent in faith, devotion and virtue, and
he does not believe there is on the face of the earth a caliph more virtuous
than these, after the descendants of Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz.

That is a claim about the last four hundred years of the office, made by a man
comparing it to the one Umayyad caliph the pious tradition had ever approved
of.

## The funeral

Al-Mustakfi died at the end of Dhu al-Hijja 854, aged sixty-three. Suyuti's
father outlived him by forty days.

The detail he keeps for the end is that the sultan came to the funeral and
carried the bier himself.

A ruler who held the entire military and fiscal power of Egypt walking under
the coffin of a man who had none — that is what the arrangement looked like
from the inside, to the people it was for. Not a fiction anybody was
maintaining cynically. An office with nothing in it, taken seriously by the man
who had emptied it.
