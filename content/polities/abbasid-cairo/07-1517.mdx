In January 1517 Selim I destroyed the Mamluk army outside Cairo, and among the
assets that passed to the Ottomans was the caliph. Al-Mutawakkil III was taken
to Constantinople, held, and eventually allowed home to Cairo, where he died a
private person on a pension. The office simply stopped being filled.

What happened to the *claim* is a longer story and needs care. The Ottoman
position from the eighteenth century onwards was that the caliphate had been
formally transferred at Cairo in 1517 — that al-Mutawakkil had ceded the office
and its insignia to Selim in a ceremony, and that the Ottoman sultans had been
caliphs by right ever since. The account appears in Ottoman writing of the
1770s, when the empire was negotiating with Russia over its authority as a
religious protector of Muslims outside its borders and had a concrete use for
the argument.

<Figure id="selim-portrait" />

Modern scholarship does not accept the ceremony. The near-contemporary sources
for 1517 do not describe a transfer, Selim did not use the caliphal title in
the way the later account requires, and the story's first appearance is two
hundred and fifty years after the event in a context that explains it.

The office has three endings, and they are all real. It ended at Baghdad in
1258 when Hulegu killed al-Musta'sim. It ended here in 1517, quietly, when the
man who could perform the ceremony was moved to another city and nobody
appointed a successor. And it ended in 1924 when a parliament in Ankara voted.
Of the three, this is the only one where nobody appears to have decided
anything: the office was not abolished and was not transferred. It was carried
off with the furniture and not set up again.
