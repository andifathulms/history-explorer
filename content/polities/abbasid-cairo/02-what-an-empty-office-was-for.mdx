---
title: What an empty office was for
drafted_from: banister-2021
phase: peak
---

The standard verdict is that the Cairo caliphs were figureheads and puppets,
and the standard verdict is where Banister starts rather than where he ends.
The dismissal is inherited: chroniclers close to the sultanate had little
reason to dwell on them, later Ottoman writing had reason to minimise the line
it had displaced, and modern accounts have generally passed the brevity along.

Three things the office actually did. It invested sultans, and the investiture
was not decorative in a state where sultans were made by faction and murder at
a rate the site's Mamluk page describes: a new sultan wanted the ceremony, and
the ceremony required the caliph to be there and to consent. It delegated
authority outward, to rulers who had no other way of obtaining it — this site's
Songhai page records Askia Muhammad obtaining recognition as caliph of the
Sudan from the Cairo Abbasid in 1496, which is a West African ruler buying a
title from a man with no soldiers, and getting his money's worth. And it was a
resource in a crisis: when the sultanate broke down, the caliph was a piece on
the board because he was the one figure no faction had made.

That last point produced the exception. In 1412, after Faraj was deposed and
killed, the amirs could not agree on a candidate and settled on the caliph
himself: al-Musta'in was proclaimed sultan as well as caliph. He held it for
about six months before being set aside and, later, deposed from the caliphate
too. It is the only occasion in two and a half centuries when the office held
power rather than conferring it, and it happened because the office was the one
thing in Cairo that was not a faction.

The end was quick. Selim I destroyed the Mamluk sultanate in 1517 and took
al-Mutawakkil III to Constantinople with the rest of the state's moveable
assets. The Ottoman claim that the caliphate was formally transferred at that
moment is, as this site's Ottoman page says, largely an eighteenth-century
construction — but the line stopped in 1517 either way, and it stopped the way
it had begun, in the possession of whoever held Egypt.
