title: A caliph for Cairo
drafted_from: banister-2021

Hulegu had the last Baghdad caliph rolled in a carpet and trampled in February
1258. In June 1261 an Abbasid prince who had escaped the sack and spent three
years among the desert tribes of Iraq arrived in Cairo, and Baybars invested him
as caliph.

The transaction is legible from both sides. Baybars was a Kipchak slave soldier
who had murdered his way to the sultanate a few months earlier, in a state
founded by a coup, with no lineage, no election and no claim except the army.
The caliph gave him an investiture — a formal delegation of authority from the
office that had been the only non-conquest source of legitimacy in Sunni Islam
for six hundred years. The caliph, for his part, got a household, a stipend, and
the survival of a line that had been extinguished three years earlier.

Al-Mustansir did not last. Baybars sent him east with a force to retake Baghdad,
and he was killed in a Mongol ambush near al-Anbar in October, five months after
his investiture. A distant kinsman was found and installed as al-Hakim, and it
was that line, not the founder's, that ran for the next two and a half
centuries. Sixteen more caliphs followed him.

What was created in 1261 is unlike anything else in this corpus, and it is worth
being blunt about the difficulty. This polity had no territory, no army and no
revenue of its own. Every measure on this page is a gap, and they are not gaps
in scholarship: there is nothing to measure. The site includes it because its
own data pointed at it — the single successor claim counted on the Abbasid
record names this line — and because a corpus that measures polities by reach
and duration should be made to confront one that had a claim and nothing else.
