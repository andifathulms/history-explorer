---
title: A caliph for Cairo
drafted_from: banister-2021

Hulegu had the last Baghdad caliph rolled in a carpet and trampled in February
1258. In June 1261 an Abbasid prince who had escaped the sack and spent three
years among the desert tribes of Iraq arrived in Cairo, and Baybars invested
him as caliph.

The transaction is legible from both sides. Baybars was a Kipchak slave soldier
who had murdered his way to the sultanate a few months earlier, in a state
founded by a coup, with no lineage, no election and no claim except the army.
The caliph gave him an investiture — a formal delegation of authority from the
office that had been the only non-conquest source of legitimacy in Sunni Islam
for six hundred years. The caliph, for his part, got a household, a stipend,
and the survival of a line that had been extinguished three years earlier.

Al-Mustansir did not last. Baybars sent him east with a force to retake
Baghdad and he was destroyed by a Mongol army on the Euphrates within months of
his investiture. The line that ran for the next two and a half centuries was not
his but al-Hakim's — a rival Abbasid whom the amir of Aleppo had already
proclaimed, and who had submitted to al-Mustansir and marched east with him.
Sixteen more caliphs followed.

What was created in 1261 is a strange object and it is worth being blunt about
it. This caliphate had no territory, no army and no revenue. There is nothing
to measure about it, and the absence is not a failure of the sources: there was
nothing there. What it had was a claim, and the claim turned out to be worth
having for two and a half centuries, which is longer than most states with
armies manage.
