The Ya'ariba destroyed themselves over the question their office existed to
answer, and the sequence took about fifty years.

It began among brothers. Sultan bin Saif, who had taken Muscat from the
Portuguese and built the fortress at Nizwa, was succeeded by his son Bal'arab,
who founded and endowed a college at Yabrin and began well. Then his brother
Saif raised a following among the more devout jurists and shaikhs of Oman, and
the two fought each other across the country for years. The chronicle records
what people ended up calling them. Bal'arab was the Butcher. Saif was the Scourge
of the Arabs.

Nobody could say which of them was the elder, or on what ground the country was
divided about which had the better claim. That is the whole difficulty of an
elective imamate in one sentence: when two qualified men of the same house both
rise, the qualifications do not decide between them, and there is no other rule.

## Two imams

By the 1720s the same thing was happening again and worse, because this time
both men were actually elected.

Sayf bin Sultan the second held the office when Bal'arab bin Himyar — a cousin —
was elected imam by part of the population of the Dhahira. Two imams, each
lawfully chosen by an assembly, each with an argument.

Sayf could not beat him. After two or three failed attempts he sent to Makran
and hired a body of Baluchis, all armed with muskets, and put them under his
brother's command. They were cut off almost to a man in a single engagement.

So he applied to Nadir Shah of Persia.

## The audition

The shah promised to help, and then — so the story runs — decided to test the
mettle of the Omani sovereign before committing to it. He sent him a viciously
restive horse, with the message that the agreement would hold only if Sayf could
ride the animal.

The trial took place in the valley behind Muscat, in front of the shah's
messenger and whoever else had come to watch. The young imam got on and coursed
the horse round the valley several times, until it finally leapt the town wall
and broke its legs, and the rider landed on his feet unhurt.

He passed. The Persians came.

## What the story is doing

It is worth pausing on what has been described, because the chronicler tells it
as a feat of horsemanship and it is something else as well.

The elected head of a religious commonwealth — an office whose holder was chosen
by scholars for learning and piety and could be deposed by them for failing in
either — is performing for a foreign king's envoy, on a horse sent to see
whether he is worth backing, in order to obtain an army to use against another
lawfully elected imam.

A few years later the Persians were in the interior on their own account, the
imamate had lost every friend Sayf could rely on for counsel, and his own
officers were recommending a governor of Sohar named Ahmad bin Sa'id as the man
who might get the country back.

He did. And then the assembly elected him instead.
