title: The office, again
drafted_from: wilkinson-1987

This site's Ibadi Imamate of Oman ends in 893, when an Abbasid army invited in
by one faction of the scholars took Nizwa and killed the imam. Its closing
chapter explains that the record stops there because Omani historiography counts
imamates in the plural — distinct constitutions of the same office, separated by
periods of dynastic or foreign rule — and it lists what came in between: the
Nabhani kings, the Buyids, the Portuguese on the coast, and then the Ya'ariba.

This is the Ya'ariba.

By the early seventeenth century Oman had been without a functioning imamate for
a long time. The Nabhani, a dynastic house that had held the interior for
centuries, were spent; the coast was Portuguese, and had been since Albuquerque
took Muscat in 1507 and burned Qalhat; and the interior was divided among tribal
leaders with no arbitrator above them.

In 1624 the scholars at Rustaq elected Nasir bin Murshid of the Ya'ariba imam.
The procedure was the one this site's earlier Omani record describes — the
community's learned men choosing the best-qualified candidate, who could be
deposed if he failed — and it produced, as it had in 750 and 793, a man with
authority over tribes that had recognised nobody.

What he did with it was conventional and effective: he took the interior fort by
fort, reduced the tribal leaders, and turned the imamate into the government of
Oman for the first time in seven centuries. He also began the thing that makes
this page different from the earlier one. He attacked the coast.

The institutions field on this record is identical to the one on the
ninth-century page — `election` for both the succession rule and the
legitimation, `tribal-levy` for the army, from the same source. That is not a
copy. It is the demonstration of the claim the earlier page's last chapter
makes: what persisted in Oman for eleven hundred years was an office and a
procedure, and the states constituted under it are separate entries with the
same constitution.
