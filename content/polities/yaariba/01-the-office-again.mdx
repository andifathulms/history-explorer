title: The office, again

The first Ibadi imamate of Oman ended in 893, when an Abbasid army invited in by
one faction of the scholars took Nizwa and killed the imam. Omani historiography
counts imamates in the plural — distinct constitutions of the same office,
separated by periods of dynastic or foreign rule — and what came in between was
the Nabhani kings, the Buyids, the Portuguese on the coast, and then the
Ya'ariba.

This is the Ya'ariba.

By the early seventeenth century Oman had been without a functioning imamate for
a long time. The Nabhani, a dynastic house that had held the interior for
centuries, were spent; the coast was Portuguese, and had been since Albuquerque
took Muscat in 1507 and burned Qalhat; and the interior was divided among tribal
leaders with no arbitrator above them.

In 1624 the scholars at Rustaq elected Nasir bin Murshid of the Ya'ariba imam.
The procedure was the old one — the community's learned men choosing the
best-qualified candidate, who could be
deposed if he failed — and it produced, as it had in 750 and 793, a man with
authority over tribes that had recognised nobody.

What he did with it was conventional and effective: he took the interior fort by
fort, reduced the tribal leaders, and turned the imamate into the government of
Oman for the first time in seven centuries. He also began the thing that makes
this imamate unlike the earlier one. He attacked the coast.

Everything else about the constitution was the same as it had been nine
centuries before: election to the office, election as the ground of the office,
tribes for the army. That identity is the point. What persisted in Oman for
eleven hundred years was not a state but a procedure, and the states constituted
under it were separate things sharing one constitution.
