---
title: A fleet the field cannot record
drafted_from: wilkinson-1987
phase: aside
---

This record's `military_basis` reads `tribal-levy`, which is accurate and, on
this page more than any other in the corpus, inadequate.

The coding rulebook excludes naval force deliberately and explains why: a fleet
is a domain a state operates in, and the men on it were raised one of the listed
ways like everyone else. The example the rules give is Srivijaya, whose crews
came from coastal chiefs under obligation, which is `client-levy`; that they
went to sea is prose. The reasoning is sound. A vocabulary of seven values
describing recruitment cannot also describe deployment without becoming a list
of things armies did.

The Ya'ariba are the case that tests it hardest. Everything on the two chapters
before this one is naval. The polity's defining acts were the capture of a
fortified harbour, the construction and operation of an ocean-going armed fleet,
the projection of force across two thousand miles of open water, and a
thirty-three-month siege sustained by sea against relief squadrons from Goa. A
reader who saw only the institutions block would find a mountain imamate with
tribal cavalry and a tithe on dates, and would be reading about a different
polity.

The site's answer is the one it has given four or five times now: the field
records what it records, and the chapter carries the rest. That answer is
correct and it is worth keeping count of how often it has been needed. The
Jafnid record could not code an imperial grant. The Sarbadar record could not
code a religious brotherhood under arms. The Mirdasid record could not code an
armed citizenry that answered to no ruler. The Qajar record could not code a
concession sold to a foreign company. And this record cannot say that the army
went to sea.

Those are five different absences and they are not the same complaint. Three of
them are gaps a new value could close and two of them are not — a `naval` value
would break the field's own logic, and this site is not going to add one. The
distinction between a vocabulary that is missing something and a vocabulary that
is deliberately narrow is worth keeping sharp, because a corpus that treats
every awkward case as a reason to expand the schema ends up with a schema that
describes nothing in particular.
