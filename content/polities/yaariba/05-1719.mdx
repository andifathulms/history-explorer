In 1719 the Ya'ariba scholars elected Saif bin Sultan II imam. He was a child.
Other scholars held that the election was therefore invalid, since the
qualifications for the office include the capacity to exercise it, and that a
regency was not something the doctrine provided for.

That is the whole of the dispute, and it destroyed the state.

The argument was legal and the factions it produced were tribal. Oman divided
into two blocs — the Hinawi and the Ghafiri, named for the leaders who assembled
them — and the division outlived the question that caused it by three hundred
years; it is still a category in Omani social life. Rival imams were elected.
The forts changed hands. The country was at war with itself for two decades.

Then both sides did what the losing faction did in 886, on the page this record
follows: they looked outside. One party appealed to Nadir Shah, whose Persian
empire is on this site's Iran after the Mongols region, and Persian troops
landed at Julfar and Muscat in the late 1730s. They came as auxiliaries and
stayed as an occupation, which is the entirely predictable outcome and was
predicted at the time.

Ahmad bin Sa'id, the Ya'ariba governor of Sohar, held his town through a long
siege, negotiated the Persians out, and — the accounts differ on how much
negotiation and how much massacre was involved — cleared them from Oman. In 1744
the scholars elected him imam.

So the office survived again, and the family holding it did not. This is the
second time on this site that an Omani imamate has been ended not by conquest
but by a disagreement among the people qualified to constitute it: in 886 over
whether a deposition was lawful, in 1719 over whether an election was. Both
times the losing side invited a foreign army. Both times the office was
reconstituted afterwards by somebody else.

The next record is what the man elected in 1744 made of it, and the short answer
is that he made it hereditary.
