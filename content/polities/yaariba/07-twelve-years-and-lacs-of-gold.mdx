Sultan bin Saif spent twelve years and, in the chronicler's phrase, lacs of gold
and silver building the fortress at Nizwa.

He could afford it. The same reign had taken Muscat off the Portuguese, wiped out
the two men-of-war they left hovering off the coast, carried the war to Diu and
Daman on the Gujarat coast and come back with an immense booty including the
gold and silver vessels and images out of the churches, and then — the detail the
chronicle bothers to record specially — fostered trade and sent agents abroad to
buy arms and horses, for which a strong demand had sprung up among his subjects.

Then he built a fort in the interior and was buried in it.

## What the money went into

That is the shape of the Ya'ariba legacy and it is unusual for a maritime
empire: the profits of the sea were spent inland.

The great forts of the Omani interior are theirs. So is the college at Yabrin,
founded and endowed by Sultan's son. So, in a real sense, is the standing of
Nizwa and Rustaq as the places where the imamate is properly constituted — a
convention that outlived the dynasty by centuries and was still doing political
work in the twentieth.

A British traveller who reached Nizwa in the 1830s, a hundred and seventy years
after Sultan bin Saif died, reported that its fort was reckoned in the
estimation of all the surrounding country to be impregnable.

## What the successors got

The other thing the Ya'ariba left was an empire, and it passed on intact to men
who had just displaced them.

The East African coast from Mombasa southward, taken off the Portuguese in the
1690s, did not revert or fragment when the imamate collapsed into civil war and
Persian occupation. It was still there in the 1780s to be re-secured by an
expedition, and still there in the nineteenth century to be turned into
something the Ya'ariba would not have recognised: a sultanate with its capital
on an island off Africa, financed by cloves and slaves.

So a dynasty that ended by hiring Baluchi mercenaries, auditioning for a Persian
shah and losing its own office to a governor of Sohar nonetheless handed its
successors a working overseas empire, the forts to hold the interior, and an
endowed college.

## And the office

The last thing is the thing that keeps happening in these mountains.

The Ya'ariba imamate was itself a reconstitution — the third time the same
elective office had been established in the same country, the first having been
destroyed by an Abbasid army in 893. It ended in 1744 in a disputed election
resolved by a foreign army, which is precisely how the earlier ones had ended.

And it was constituted again afterwards. The man who replaced them was elected
imam by the same procedure, in the same towns, by the same kind of assembly. The
form survived every dynasty that held it, including this one, which is why the
chronicle is a history of imams rather than of houses.
