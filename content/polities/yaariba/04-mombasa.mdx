The Swahili coast had been Portuguese since the first decade of the sixteenth
century, and their position on it rested on Fort Jesus at Mombasa — a bastioned
Italian-designed fortress built in 1593, the most substantial European work in
East Africa.

The Omanis were invited. Swahili towns that resented Portuguese customs and
Portuguese garrisons had been appealing to Muscat since the 1650s, and Omani
squadrons had taken and lost Mombasa more than once. In March 1696 the imamate
laid siege to Fort Jesus properly, and the siege lasted thirty-three months —
through plague inside the walls, through two relief fleets from Goa, through the
death of almost the entire garrison. It fell in December 1698.

<Figure id="mombasa-1635" />

With it went the Portuguese position north of Mozambique. Pemba, Kilwa, Zanzibar
and the Mrima coast came under Omani suzerainty; Omani governors were installed,
Omani customs collected, and the Ibadi imamate of the Omani interior became a
power on the African littoral.

It is worth pausing on the improbability. The first Omani imamate was a polity
constituted by scholars in a mosque at Nizwa, governing a
society organised around shared rights in irrigation channels, whose imam was
elected on merit and could be deposed by argument. Nine hundred years later the
same office, constituted the same way, was besieging an Italian-designed
fortress two thousand miles away and taking it.

Nothing about the constitution changed to permit that. The imam was still
elected, still deposable, still primarily a judge. What changed was that the
office had acquired a port, and a port on that coast at that date was an
entry point into a world system.

<Figure id="fort-jesus" />

The domestic monuments belong here too, because they were built with the same
money. Nizwa's great round fort, Sultan bin Saif's, is the largest in Arabia and
was designed for cannon. Jabrin castle, put up in the 1670s by an imam whose
election was itself disputed, is a palace with painted ceilings and a school in
it. And the aflaj — the underground channels the earlier Omani page describes as
the foundation of the whole society — were extended and repaired under the
Ya'ariba on a scale that has left their names on them.
