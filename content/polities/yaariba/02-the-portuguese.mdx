title: Taking the coast
drafted_from: risso-1986

The Portuguese had held the Omani ports since 1507, as part of the system of
fortified harbours by which they attempted to control the Indian Ocean trade
from Sofala to Malacca. Muscat was the northern anchor of it: a deep, sheltered
harbour between cliffs, with two forts above it, commanding the approach to the
Gulf.

Nasir bin Murshid took Julfar and Sohar in the 1640s. His successor Sultan bin
Saif took Muscat in 1650.

That date is the hinge of the whole imamate. An imamate in the mountains with date gardens and a tithe had acquired the best
harbour in Arabia, its fortifications, its shipyards, its customs, and — most
consequentially — the hulls in it. Omani shipbuilding and Omani sailors existed
long before 1650; what the port supplied was the capacity to operate ocean-going
armed vessels at scale, and the Portuguese ships taken in the harbour were the
beginning of a fleet.

Within a decade the imamate was raiding Portuguese Asia rather than defending
against it. Omani squadrons hit Diu and Bombay in the 1660s, Bassein, Daman and
the Konkan coast afterwards; they took ships, ransomed towns, and made the
northern Indian Ocean unsafe for a power that had regarded it as a monopoly. The
English East India Company, newly arrived at Bombay, negotiated with Muscat and
occasionally fought it.

The proceeds of that raiding were substantial, and nothing consulted treats them
as a structural part of the state's income the way the tithe and the customs
were — so the raiding sits in this paragraph rather than being counted as a
revenue base. It probably understates the polity. Saying so is better than
inventing a figure for it.
