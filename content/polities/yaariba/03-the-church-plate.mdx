Taking Muscat was not the end of the Portuguese war. It was the point at which
the Omanis stopped defending a coast and started raiding an empire, and the
Omani chronicle describes the transition in a few sentences without appearing to
notice how large a change it is.

The last of the fighting in the harbour is recorded in the chronicle's ordinary
register, which is to say partly as farce. Two Portuguese men-of-war went on
hovering off Muscat after the forts had gone, so a body of mercenaries was
hired — men, in the chronicler's phrase, to whom death was sweeter than wine to
the lips of a wine-bibber — who went out at them in small boats, destroyed both
ships and killed the crews. In the fighting for the town itself a Portuguese
officer the chronicle calls Cabreta made a last effort with a small band and got
inside; he was surrounded, pelted with rotten eggs, and finished with spears.
God, writes the annalist, thereby rid the Muslims of him and his polytheist
companions.

Then the offensive. Fired by the successes at home, the imam declared a jihad and
carried the war into the enemy's country: he attacked Diu and Daman on the coast
of Gujarat, to the great consternation of the Portuguese, and came away with an
immense booty — including, the chronicle specifies, all the gold and silver
vessels and images belonging to the churches.

That is a small Arabian state, thirty years out of foreign occupation, sending
fleets to sack the Portuguese establishments in India and bringing the altar
plate home. Everything the Ya'ariba are remembered for in the Indian Ocean —
Mombasa, the East African coast, a navy that outfought Europeans for two
generations — begins with that decision, and the chronicle gives it four lines.

Relieved of any fear of invasion, the imam turned to domestic business, and the
chronicler is oddly specific about what that consisted of: he fostered trade, and
sent agents into different countries to buy arms and horses, for which a strong
demand had arisen among his subjects. A population that suddenly wants to import
weapons and cavalry mounts in quantity is a population expecting to use them, and
they did, on each other, within a generation.

One absence in the chronicle is worth recording because of what it says about
the whole record. The date of the capture of Muscat from the Portuguese — the
founding achievement of the dynasty, the event the whole book exists to lead up
to — is not in it. Badger had to reconstruct it from the chronology of other
events in the same reign and settle on 1651 or 1652, and says so. A court
chronicle can carry the rotten eggs thrown at a Portuguese captain and lose the
year its state was made.
