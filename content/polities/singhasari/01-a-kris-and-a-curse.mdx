title: A kris and a curse
drafted_from: robson-1995

Singhasari lasted seventy years and produced the dynasty that ruled Java for
the next two centuries. Its founding story is a murder ballad.

The *Pararaton*, the Javanese chronicle that is the main narrative source for
the dynasty, opens with Ken Arok, a man of obscure and possibly criminal
origins in the service of the lord of Tumapel. He commissions a *kris* — a
Javanese dagger — from a smith named Mpu Gandring, demands it before it is
finished, and kills the smith with it when he refuses. The smith, dying, curses
the blade: it will kill seven men, and the first will be Ken Arok himself.

It then does. Ken Arok uses it to kill his lord, takes his lord's wife and his
lord's territory, declares himself king in 1222, and is murdered with the same
weapon by a stepson avenging the man he killed. The stepson is killed by his
own half-brother. The chronicle runs the pattern down four generations.

That is a sequence of outcomes rather than a constitution, and it is worth
being careful about what kind of document is telling it.

The *Pararaton* was compiled long after the events, its early sections read as
legend, and the dynasty it describes is the one the kings of Majapahit claimed
descent from. So the chronicle of Singhasari's murders was preserved by the
people with the strongest possible reason to make its last king a revered
ancestor — which is either an argument for taking the killings seriously, since
they are not flattering, or an argument that the whole cycle is a literary
construction giving a dynasty a suitably terrible origin. Both readings are
current.
