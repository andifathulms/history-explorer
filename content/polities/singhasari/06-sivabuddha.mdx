Kertanegara was killed in his own palace in 1292 by a vassal. Afterwards he
became a god.

He is known later under the name Sivabuddha, and there is a portrait-statue of
him in the form of the Buddha Akshobhya which still stands at Surabaya. That is
not a memorial in the ordinary sense. It is the king as a Buddha, made for a
dynasty that needed him to be one.

## Two chronicles, two men

The sources cannot agree about what he was like, and the disagreement is
instructive.

The *Nagarakertagama*, composed at the Majapahit court that descended from him,
presents him as a fine scholar. The *Pararaton*, the chronicle of the dagger
and the four generations of revenge killings, presents him as a drunkard.

Both were written after the fact, in Java, about the same man. One is the
account of the dynasty that needed him revered; the other belongs to a
tradition that had been telling the story of this family as a sequence of
murders since Ken Arok. A reader is not obliged to choose. What can be said
with confidence is that he was a great king by the ordinary measures — he
extended Javanese authority further than anyone before him — and that the two
most important Javanese sources for his reign have opposite motives for
describing his character.

## What he actually believed

The part neither chronicle disputes is the religion, and it is stranger than
either portrait.

Kertanegara's zeal was for the *kalachakra* form of Tantric Buddhism, which had
developed in Bengal towards the end of the Pala dynasty and spread from there
to Tibet, to Nepal and into the archipelago. In Java it reached its fullest
expression by fusing with the worship of Siva Bhairava — and the resulting cult
of Siva-Buddha concerned itself particularly with the redemption of the souls
of the dead.

That is why it took. Ancestor worship was already the deep structure of
religion in the islands, and a Tantric synthesis addressed to the fate of the
dead found ground prepared for it.

So the king who was made into a Buddha after his death had spent his life
promoting a doctrine about what happens to the dead — and the dynasty that
deified him was descended from him, needed him, and had every reason to take
the doctrine at its word.

Sivabuddha is the name of a theological position and a political necessity at
the same time, and it is not obvious that anyone at the time would have thought
those two things separable.
