By 1014 Sitric Silkenbeard's Dublin had drawn Leinster and a
scattering of foreign Norse fighters, among them the Manx chieftain
Brodir, into alliance against Brian Boru, the Munster king who had
spent decades building a following strong enough to call himself high
king of Ireland. The armies met at Clontarf, on the plain outside
Dublin's own walls, on Good Friday.

## A battle Sitric watched rather than fought

Sitric himself did not take the field; he stayed within Dublin's
fortifications while his allies fought outside them. The battle broke
decisively against his own side - Brodir's men were driven back, and
in the retreat Brodir himself found Brian's tent, guarded only by a
handful of attendants. According to the near-contemporary account,
Brian rose from prayer, sword in hand, when he saw the attackers
approaching; the two men wounded each other fatally in the exchange
that followed, Brian's own dying blow taking off Brodir's legs before
the Norseman's axe split his skull.

## What Dublin actually lost

Brian's death did not save the battle for Sitric's side - the wider
engagement remained a defeat for the Leinster-Norse alliance, whatever
symbolic victory his own enemy's death represented. Sitric kept his
throne; watching from inside the walls rather than fighting meant he
had a kingdom left to keep. But Dublin's capacity to shape events
across the whole island, the kind of ambition that had drawn it into
alliance with Leinster in the first place, did not survive Clontarf
intact. The kingdom that endured afterward was a wealthy trading town
still, but no longer one capable of contesting Ireland's own high
kingship on its own account.
