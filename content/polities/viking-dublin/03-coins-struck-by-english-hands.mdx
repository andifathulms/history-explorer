The kings who ruled Dublin across the tenth and early eleventh
centuries were, for long stretches, the same men who ruled York, or
close kin to them - a single dynasty whose members moved between the
Irish Sea and Northumbria as circumstances demanded, sometimes holding
both thrones and sometimes losing one while keeping the other. Later
chroniclers styled them, without much exaggeration, "the high kings of
the Northmen of Ireland and England."

## A currency that reveals a working relationship

The physical evidence of that connection survives in an unlikely
place: the coins themselves. Early Dublin's own currency, though
struck in the names of its own kings - Ivar, Sitric, Reginald, Olaf -
was not minted by native craftsmen but by Anglo-Saxon moneyers,
working evidently under some form of arrangement between Dublin's
rulers and their counterparts, or subjects, across the water. A
kingdom capable of directing English mint-workers to strike coin
bearing a Norse king's own name was not merely raiding its neighbours;
it was embedded in their economy closely enough to command their
skilled labour.

## What that reach actually meant

By the reign of Sitric Silkenbeard, who held Dublin through the last
years of the tenth century and the first decades of the eleventh, the
kingdom had become something more settled than the raiding base it had
started as - a town wealthy and organized enough to strike its own
coinage, hold shifting alliances with Irish provincial kings, and draw
on Scandinavian military support from across the Irish Sea when war
came looking for it, as it would in 1014.
