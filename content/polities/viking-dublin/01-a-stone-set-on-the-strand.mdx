Scandinavian raiders had been striking the Irish coast for decades
before any of them chose to stay. Around 852, Olaf the White
established a fortified base where the river Liffey met the sea - a
site with none of the natural advantages of Cork, Waterford or
Limerick, all of which the Norse also held and all of which offered
better harbours, but one that sat centrally within the whole
Scandinavian world of the northern seas.

## Why an unpromising river won out

At the time Dublin was founded, Scandinavian settlers already held
Caithness and Sutherland on the Scottish mainland, the northern and
western Scottish islands, the Isle of Man, and all of England north of
the Humber. For a seafaring people whose interests spanned that whole
arc of coastline, a base on Ireland's own east coast, facing directly
toward Britain, mattered more than a superior harbour further south or
west. Dublin's own position, unremarkable on a map of Ireland alone,
made obvious sense on a map of the wider Norse world.

## A capital before it was a city

What began as a fortified landing point grew, over the following
decades, into the base from which Scandinavian power in Ireland was
organized - not the only Norse settlement on the island, but the one
that would outlast every other, surviving expulsions, internal
rivalries and the rise and fall of individual kings for more than
three centuries after its founding.
