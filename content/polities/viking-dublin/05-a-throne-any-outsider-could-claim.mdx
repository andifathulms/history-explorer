Sitric Silkenbeard kept his throne after Clontarf, and the reign that
followed showed how far Dublin had already become a Christian trading
town rather than a war-band's stronghold. In 1028, mindful of his own
father's pilgrimage to Iona, and urged on by the clergy, Sitric
travelled to Rome - the practice of Christian kings by then, and a
striking one for a Norse ruler of Dublin. He returned to found Christ
Church, endowing it around 1038 with land for a cathedral to the Holy
Trinity. The city that had shaken the island's kings a generation
earlier now measured its standing by pilgrimages and church
foundations instead.

## A crown that changed hands without a battle

What followed Sitric's reign showed just as clearly how much authority
Dublin itself had lost. He "went beyond the seas" and was succeeded in
1036 by Echmarcach mac Ragnaill, who in turn left the kingdom in 1052,
probably to aid the exiled Earl Godwin in England. Into that vacancy
stepped not another Norse claimant but Diarmait mac Maíl na mBó, king
of Leinster, who took the kingship of Dublin by right of his own
descent from Brian Boru's line through marriage - and who is recorded
in the annals afterward not merely as an ally of Dublin's Ostmen but
as "King of Leinster, of the Danish Isles, and of Dublin" outright.
The town's own throne had become an office an ambitious Irish king
could simply add to his titles, its Scandinavian ruling family reduced
to one contender among several rather than the settlement's natural
lords.

## A tributary prize for the rest of the century

Diarmait's son Murchadh extended the family's reach as far as the Isle
of Man, exacting tribute there in 1060 and defeating Echmarcach's own
son; when Diarmait himself fell in battle in 1072 and both his sons
had already died, the kingship of Dublin passed again, this time to
Godfrey, son of Ragnall, of the Manx branch of the old dynasty - and
after his death in 1075, to Muirchertach, son of the Munster king
Toirdelbach Ua Briain, an outsider from a different Irish kingdom
entirely. By the century's end Dublin was less a kingdom in its own
right than a wealthy port town whose kingship was decided by whichever
Irish over-king - Leinster's Uí Chennselaig or Munster's Uí Briain -
could hold it, a pattern of outside domination that continued
unbroken until an exiled king of Leinster, Diarmait Mac Murchada,
brought a foreign army of his own to Ireland a century later.
