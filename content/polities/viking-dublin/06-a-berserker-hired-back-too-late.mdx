By 1170 Dublin's own king, Hasculf, ruled a town whose fate was being
decided less by its own strength than by events elsewhere in Ireland -
an exiled Leinster king's invitation to Anglo-Norman adventurers had
already put a foreign army in the country, and that army's leader,
Richard de Clare, called Strongbow, had his own eye on the city. While
Dublin's leaders were still negotiating surrender terms with the
besieging force outside its walls, Strongbow's men stormed the town
directly, taking it before any agreement was actually concluded.

## Flight, and a hired champion who came too late

Hasculf escaped by ship to the Orkney Islands rather than accept the
new arrangement, and many of the town's remaining Norse inhabitants
were pushed across the Liffey into a separate quarter while their
conquerors settled the original city. Hasculf did not give up the
throne quietly. He returned the following year with a hired Norwegian
champion - a "berserker" in the old sense, a warrior whose fury in
battle was itself supposed to be worth an army - landing at the same
stretch of shore his own dynasty had first claimed for Dublin three
centuries before. The attempt to retake the city failed.

## What Strongbow's capture actually changed

Henry II landed at Waterford the same year Hasculf made his failed
attempt, taking the submission of Strongbow and the other Norman
adventurers and keeping Dublin itself under direct royal control
rather than granting it out as another baron's lordship. The kingdom
Olaf the White had founded on the same shore in 852 - expelled once,
rebuilt once, broken at Clontarf and rebuilt again after that - did
not survive a third loss. Dublin remained a city, and remained
central to whoever ruled Ireland from outside it, but it never again
answered to a king of its own.
