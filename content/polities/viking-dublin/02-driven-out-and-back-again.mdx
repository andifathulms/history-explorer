Norse rule at Dublin was not continuous. In 902 a coalition of Irish
kings succeeded where individual raids and skirmishes had failed for
decades, driving the ruling dynasty and its followers out of the town
entirely. For a decade and a half the Liffey's Norse settlement simply
ceased to exist as an independent power - its former rulers scattered
to Britain and the wider Scandinavian world, some of them turning up
in the historical record fighting in Scotland or northern England
instead.

## Sitric's return

The reversal came in 917, when Sitric, a grandson of Ivar, returned
with a fleet and reestablished Norse rule at Dublin. He was not
returning to an empty inheritance so much as reclaiming one: the same
family that ruled Dublin also had a claim, through the wider Uí Ímair
kindred, to power across the Irish Sea. Sitric Cáech himself would go
on to become king of York as well within a few years of retaking
Dublin, binding the fortunes of the two Norse settlements together for
a generation - a connection that meant a setback for one dynasty in
one kingdom could ripple into the other.

## A kingdom that had already learned it could be lost

The expulsion and return mattered beyond the individual reigns it
touched. Dublin's Norse rulers had already seen their kingdom collapse
once and be rebuilt from outside; the town's later resilience through
further crises - a crushing defeat at Clontarf less than a century
later among them - owed something to a dynasty that had already proven
a lost Dublin was not necessarily a permanently lost one.
