Overami, the twenty-third king in Roth's own list, came to the throne
in circumstances his account describes as tense from the start. Two
of his father Adola's wives were pregnant at the same time; the
guardian charged with reporting the birth of Overami's own half-
brother Orukotu was slow to send word, while Overami's own birth was
announced first, and by the country's own custom the child announced
first, not the child born first, was recognized as heir. Orukotu's own
supporters tried to overturn the decision when Adola died, and Roth's
informant Mr. Punch recalled "awful stories" of the killing that
followed - partly funeral sacrifice for the old king, partly the
suppression of an open revolt in Orukotu's name. Overami secured the
throne, and, aware of how large a faction still opposed him, leaned
openly on European support to shore up his own position for the rest
of his reign.

## An unarmed mission ignored its own warning

That reign ended over a trade dispute four centuries after Aveiro's
first visit. Acting Consul-General James Phillips organized a mission
to Benin City in late 1896, deliberately unarmed so as not to alarm
the king or his own people; local chiefs, including two Roth names
directly, Dore and Dudu, begged Phillips not to proceed. He went
anyway. The mission left Sapele on 2 January 1897 and set out from
Gwato on the 4th; within hours all but two of the mission's white
members, and a large number of their African carriers, were killed.
Roth records the toll as seven Europeans dead, the disaster described
in full by one of the two survivors, Captain Boisragon, in a book
published the same year.

## A city captured, then lost to fire

The British government could not let the massacre stand. Admiral Sir
Harry Rawson led a Punitive Expedition that captured Benin City by 17
February 1897; the city was afterward destroyed by a fire, Roth notes,
"which the soldiery was unable to stem" - an accidental conflagration
rather than a deliberate razing, but one that consumed the physical
capital of a kingdom whose own bronze plaques and ivories, looted in
the expedition's aftermath, would end up scattered through museum
collections across Europe. Overami's own reign, and the kingdom's
independence with it, ended in the expedition's wake - a dynasty whose
founding legend described careful, patient conquest by persuasion and
force in equal measure, closed out instead in a matter of weeks by a
war neither side had set out, at the start of the year, to fight.
