The Punitive Expedition's soldiers found the bronzes and ivories the kingdom
had spent centuries casting and carving where the palace's own alcoves and
walls kept them - Roth's brother Felix, an army surgeon with the expedition,
described clay figures on raised steps in every house, strings of cowrie
shells around their necks, and a "large part of the loot" embedded directly
in the walls themselves, so that soldiers testing for hidden cavities
sometimes put their hands into the bodies built into them.

What happened to that material afterward happened fast. Within six years of
the city's capture, the British Museum held enough of it to be illustrated
in a paper by Charles Read and Ormonde Dalton for the Anthropological
Institute, and Roth's own 1903 book - compiled from museum access the
trustees granted him directly - draws on collections already sitting in
Liverpool as well as London. A body of art that had been made for one
palace was, within a decade of that palace burning, distributed across
museum collections in Britain before the rest of the twentieth century
carried it further still, into Germany, the United States and elsewhere.

## A century of return

The reckoning with that dispersal became, eventually, formal. In July 2022
Germany and Nigeria signed a joint declaration transferring ownership of
the Benin material in German public collections, and that August the
Ethnologisches Museum in Berlin - holder of the largest Benin collection in
Europe after the British Museum itself - transferred title to 512 objects
outright, keeping roughly a third of them on loan in Berlin for an initial
ten years while a new museum was built near the royal palace at Benin City
to house the rest. Germany's own account of the transfer calls it the
largest return of ownership of museum objects from a colonial context to
date. Other institutions holding Benin material have faced the same
question since, with answers ranging from full transfer to continued
display.

## What the palace kept

The kingdom the Punitive Expedition ended did not simply vanish along with
its art. The Oba's title survived the conquest as a recognised traditional
authority within Nigeria, the same royal line Roth's informants had traced
back to Eweka continuing to hold it. A monarchy stripped of its own
independence and most of the metal record of its own history in a single
week in 1897 nonetheless kept the one thing no soldier could carry off: the
throne itself, and the family that has occupied it since before any
European ship reached the coast it sits on.
