The Dutch traders who followed the Portuguese found a kingdom still worth
describing in superlatives. By the time European visitors' accounts thin out
in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, they describe something smaller.

Nyendael, a Dutch agent at Benin around 1700, recorded a court capable of
executing a man for losing a string of coral he had been granted as a mark of
office - five men put to death at once over a single stolen chain "not
worth twopence," by his own account, and a Portuguese sailor's killer held in
prison for a year awaiting a punishment severe enough to satisfy the next
Portuguese ship that came asking for justice. It is a picture of a state that
still ruled by fear as thoroughly as ever. It was not, on the same evidence,
a state at peace with itself.

## The civil war

Lieutenant King, visiting decades later, found a city that had been
"depopulated by a civil war" and never fully recovered: walls largely
destroyed, and an inhabited circumference of no more than two or three
miles where the kingdom's own earlier extent had been far greater. Roth's
sources do not give the civil war a date anyone can be confident of, only its
result - a capital that had shrunk inside its own perimeter and stayed that
size.

## Trade that stopped paying

The same decades saw Benin's European trade lose the importance it had held
under the Portuguese. Roth notes plainly that the country's own disappointing
returns - pepper and ivory that never matched the profits of Portugal's
Indian Ocean trade, a market the Dutch found tedious and overregulated even
by the standards of the West African coast - meant that European visitors,
Portuguese and Dutch alike, did not stay long at a time and eventually
withdrew their own permanent presence from the city altogether. Roth reads
the fall-off in the bronze plaques' own artistic quality as running in
parallel with it: a "quasi-civilization" that had received a real impetus
from the Portuguese contact, declined as that trade decayed, had a second
partial revival with the Dutch, and declined again once that too faded.

## What stayed the same

The kingship itself did not weaken in the way its trade or its territory did.
The court Nyendael described still commanded absolute obedience over matters
as small as a coral chain; the army Dapper had described a century earlier
was still, by every account down to 1897, capable of being called out in
force. What contracted was Benin's own reach outward - the city it ruled
from, the profit foreigners found in trading with it, the territory its wars
had once absorbed - not the authority the king held inside what remained.
