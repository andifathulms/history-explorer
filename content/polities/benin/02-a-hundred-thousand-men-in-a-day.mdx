Eweka's own slow advance through the country, fighting whoever resisted and
talking with whoever proved too strong to fight, did not stop once the
kingdom had a capital. Roth's Dutch and Portuguese sources describe a state
still actively making war on its neighbours for as long as anyone was
writing about it.

The Dutch chronicler Dapper, whose account Roth translates in full, is direct
about the scale of it: the king of Benin "wages great wars against the
neighbouring kings" to the east and north, conquering their towns and
villages for booty of jasper and other goods, and could put twenty thousand
armed men in the field in a single day - eighty or a hundred thousand if the
occasion demanded it. It was enough, Dapper says, to make Benin "much dreaded
by all the surrounding peoples."

## The field marshal

Command of that army belonged to a single office, the Owe-Asserry - field
marshal, or war captain - who held what Dapper calls supreme power, entitled
to act like the king himself for the campaign's duration. Nobles going to war
dressed for it deliberately: scarlet cloth bought from the Dutch, collars of
elephant tusk strung with leopard teeth, tall red caps trimmed with leopard
or civet skin and trailing a horsetail ornament. Common soldiers fought
half-naked above the waist. Discipline was strict enough that nobody was
permitted to yield a step even facing death, and the field marshal alone took
the booty - unless a man managed to keep something back secretly - which did
not stop the army from thanking the king for the honour of being sent to
fight for him.

## What conquest did not do

Submission, once made, did not mean confiscation. A conquered people became
subject to Benin, but individuals were not enslaved outright, and farmland
and other property were not seized by the victors - a distinction Roth's
sources draw explicitly, and one that helped a state built substantially on
war absorb one neighbour after another without needing to hold each of them
down by force afterward.

It was this machinery - a standing capacity for tens of thousands of men, a
war captain empowered to act as the king's own equal in the field, and a
conquest that left a defeated people its land - that turned Eweka's original
slow advance from Ife into a kingdom powerful enough, two centuries later, to
be the first thing a Portuguese sailing captain thought worth describing to
his own king.
