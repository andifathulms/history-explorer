The founding tradition Roth collected from Captain Roupell's court
informants at Benin runs like this: the people of the country sent to
Ife, the holy Yoruba city, for a king, and Eweka came to them with a
few men. He advanced through the land slowly, fighting whoever
resisted and talking cunningly with whoever proved too strong to
fight, and his men took local wives as they went; when they had
children they sent for their families and settled where Eweka had
chosen to rule. When Eweka arrived at what became Benin City he found
only a small town, a scattering of houses. He bought a slave named
Ubini, and when the man died Eweka buried him nearby and decreed that
anyone asking the name of the country should be told Ubini, or Aiye -
the name, in one form or another, the kingdom carried for the rest of
its history.

## A dynasty, not a single date

Roth is candid that no reliable date attaches to any of this. The
tradition that the Bini kings came from the west runs against other
accounts of the migration, and the sheer length of the king list - his
informants named twenty-three rulers before the last king's own
deposition, not counting several minor kings who reigned only briefly
- makes any confident count of years next to impossible. Comparing
that list to other dynasties' own average reign lengths, from the
kings of England to the Askia rulers of Songhai, Roth judges the Benin
tradition likely to have dropped rulers rather than exaggerated their
individual reigns - a founding story preserved carefully enough to
survive centuries of retelling, but never anchored to a single fixed
year the way the kingdom's own later contact with Europe would be.
