Portugal reached Benin by accident of a coastal survey. King Afonso V
had contracted the explorer Fernão Gomes in 1469 to chart a hundred
leagues of West African coast a year for five years, and by 1486 João
Afonso de Aveiro had gone far enough to give Europe its first real
account of the city - reproduced by Roth from Ruy de Pina's own
chronicle: "the land of Beny was for the first time discovered beyond
the Mine... It was from there that came to these Kingdoms the Guinea
Pepper." Aveiro himself died there. What he opened, though, outlasted
him: the King of Benin sent his own captain at the port of Gwato back
to Portugal as ambassador, "a man of prudence and natural knowledge,"
who returned laden with gifts, dresses for himself and his wife, and
a royal message urging the Benin king to embrace Christianity.

## An embassy that kept returning

The relationship proved durable enough to survive Portugal's own
disappointment that Benin's pepper and trade never matched the
profits of the Indies. A 1516 letter from the trader Duarte Pires to
the Portuguese king, quoted at length by Roth, describes missionaries
accompanying the Benin king to war for a full year because the "great
mystery" - baptism - could only be performed once peace returned; at
the war's end, in August, the king had his own son and two of his
leading nobles baptised, built a church, and set them to learning to
read. The tenth king in the dynasty's own list, Esigie, presided over
this era of contact - by the reckoning Roth's informants gave him, the
white men arrived 424 years before the last king's own deposition,
making Esigie's reign the documented hinge between a purely oral
tradition and a kingdom whose affairs Portuguese, and later Dutch and
English, chroniclers began recording in real detail.

## What the trade actually brought home

The material exchange between the two courts was modest by the scale
of Portugal's own Indian Ocean ambitions - a spice prized briefly in
Flanders, slaves bought with brass and copper bracelets called
manillas, ivory and pepper moving out through the port of Gwato - but
it opened a channel that ran for centuries afterward, outlasting every
later European power's own disappointment with what Benin actually
had to sell. The bronze plaques that would later make the kingdom
famous across the world already depict European visitors by this
period: soldiers in feathered hats, matchlocks in hand, a record in
cast metal of exactly the contact Aveiro's own voyage had opened.
