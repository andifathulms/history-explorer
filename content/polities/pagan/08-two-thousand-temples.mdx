The kingdom ended in 1287. Almost everything it was for is still standing.

## What survived the state

Something over two thousand brick monuments remain on the plain at Pagan today,
out of an estimated ten thousand or more originally built. They survived because
brick is worth nothing to a looter, the site was never resettled at scale, and
the merit attached to them made destroying them unthinkable to everyone who
came afterwards.

They also went on being repaired. Pagan remained a pilgrimage site continuously
for seven hundred years; kings of Ava, Toungoo and Konbaung endowed restorations
of temples built by a dynasty they did not descend from, because the merit
belonged to the act rather than to the ancestry.

An earthquake in 1975 brought down a great deal, and another in 2016 did more.
The restoration after the first was thorough and archaeologically careless; the
argument about it delayed UNESCO recognition of the site until 2019.

## The religion

Pagan is where Burma becomes and stays Theravada, and that has not changed in
nine hundred and fifty years.

What the kingdom established was not simply a set of beliefs but an institutional
arrangement: a monastic order holding endowed land, a king whose legitimacy
rested on defending and purifying that order, and a relationship between the two
that involved periodic conflict over property. Every subsequent Burmese dynasty
inherited the arrangement, including the purifications — the *sangha* reforms of
the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries are recognisably the same institution
being managed the same way.

## The script

Burmese is written in an alphabet that took its shape here, derived from Mon,
which came from a south Indian script.

The rounded letterforms are a technical consequence rather than an aesthetic
choice: the writing surface was palm leaf, incised with a stylus, and straight
strokes across the grain split the leaf. So the letters became circles.

The earliest substantial Burmese text is the Myazedi inscription of 1113,
carved in four languages — Pyu, Mon, Burmese and Pali — on one stone. It is the
Rosetta stone of Burmese studies and the reason the Pyu language could be
approached at all.

## The three sisters

The political afterlife is less tidy.

After 1287 upper Burma passed to three brothers of Shan origin who had been
Pagan commanders, and then to a succession of kingdoms — Myinsaing, Pinya,
Sagaing, Ava — while lower Burma went to a Mon kingdom at Pegu. The country was
not reunified for two hundred and fifty years.

A rump line at Pagan continued as vassals for a few decades and then stopped
being mentioned. There is no dynasty afterwards that claims to be the Pagan
dynasty continuing.

What every later Burmese state did claim was the inheritance in a different
form: to be the defender of the religion Anawrahta was said to have brought from
Thaton, in a kingdom whose model was the one on the plain at Pagan. The
Konbaung kings styled themselves accordingly and the *Glass Palace Chronicle*
was commissioned by one of them, in 1829, to write the whole sequence down as a
single continuous story with Pagan at the start of it.

That chronicle is why the Thaton conquest is the version everyone knows. A
dynasty five hundred years later needed a founding, and gave the country one.
