Narapatisithu, whose reign is the one against which Pagan's own architecture
and administration are usually measured, did not come to the throne quietly.
His elder brother, already king, wanted his wife and sent him off on a
manufactured pretext of a war in the north to clear the way for taking her.
Narapatisithu discovered the deception before he had gone far, turned his
column around, and marched back on the capital. His brother was killed by
soldiers sent ahead of him, and Narapatisithu took the throne — and his wife
back — in 1173.

## A trade dispute that ended in a raid

Sri Lanka had long kept a resident agent in Burmese ports to manage a
profitable trade, and Burmese kings had long supplied him with rice and
lodging as a matter of custom. Narapatisithu cut it off: he stopped the
customary support, restricted the export of elephants, imprisoned Sinhalese
merchants, mistreated the Sri Lankan king's envoys, and seized a Sinhalese
princess travelling through his waters. The Sri Lankan king answered with a
naval expedition in 1180 that reached the Burmese coast, killed a governor,
burned villages and carried off captives before the two courts made peace
through the monastic channels that connected them — a reminder that a kingdom
built on rice and temples was not entirely insulated from a sea it rarely
otherwise dealt with.

## The buildings that carry his name

His reign supplied Pagan with two of its most celebrated monuments, the
Gawdawpalin and Sulamani temples, alongside a wider programme of pagodas,
weirs and canals stretching from the Kyaukse irrigation works to an ambitious
and ultimately abandoned canal scheme further south. An inscription he raised
as far off as the Shan hills marks how far his authority actually reached, at
least in name.

## An heir chosen by accident

Narapatisithu died in old age, in the thirty-seventh year of his reign, after
naming as his heir not his eldest son but a younger one — the story handed
down is that the boy's mother had endured a private discomfort of the king's
without complaint, and that the king rewarded her quietly by giving her son
the succession over his elder brothers. Whatever actually decided it, the
chosen son took the throne without apparent resistance from his rivals, and
the dynasty that had itself begun in a private killing changed hands, this
time, without one.
