A Yuan army came down from Yunnan. Narathihapate abandoned the capital, and was
killed by one of his own sons. The Irrawaddy broke into pieces held by Shan
chiefs and no single authority held it again for two and a half centuries.

The chronicles call him Tarokpyemin — the king who fled from the Chinese. That
is a verdict rather than a description, delivered five centuries later by people
writing for a court that needed the fall explained.

## Not a conquest

The Mongols did not take Burma, and it is worth being exact about that, because
"the Mongols destroyed Pagan" is the version everyone knows.

Aung-Thwin's account follows from the chapter before. Pagan was already fiscally
insolvent when the Mongols arrived: the taxable land had gone to the monasteries,
the crown could not raise or pay a force proportionate to what it faced, and the
invasion finished a state that had stopped being able to defend itself rather
than defeating one that could.

And the Yuan did not stay. They did not annex Burma, they did not garrison it,
they did not administer it, and they left. What replaced Pagan was not Mongol
rule but two hundred and fifty years of nobody — three Shan brothers who had
been Pagan commanders, then Myinsaing, Pinya, Sagaing, Ava, and a separate Mon
kingdom in the south.

It is a contested reading and the alternative is respectable: the campaigns of
1277 and 1283 were real, Narathihapate abandoned his capital and is remembered
as *Tarokpyemin*, the king who fled from the Chinese, and a state does not
usually cease to exist in the same decade an empire invades it twice by
coincidence.

What both readings agree on is that no outside power governed Burma afterwards.
Whatever the invasions did, they did not deliver the country to anybody.
