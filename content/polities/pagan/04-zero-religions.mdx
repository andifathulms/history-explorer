Ten thousand Buddhist temples on one plain, and Pagan did not carry Buddhism
anywhere.

That is worth separating out, because scale of devotion and religious expansion
get conflated constantly, and they are different things. Pagan *received*
Theravada Buddhism — from the Mon in the south, from Sri Lanka by sea, and by
whichever route the long argument about 1057 eventually settles. In every version
of the story, it arrived. What Pagan then did was to make itself the largest
single monument to it in the world, on a plain that already believed.

Melaka is the contrast. It also received its religion — Sunni Islam, from Pasai
across the strait — and it also invested enormously. The difference is what
happened next. The ports of the archipelago converted in Melaka's wake, through
its merchants and its marriages, and Islam reached the Moluccas and the southern
Philippines by that route. Something moved.

Nothing moved from Pagan. The Theravada tradition that later reshaped Cambodia
and Siam travelled through Sri Lanka and the Mon world, not through Burma, and
the Burmese reformist ordination lineages that did eventually carry outward are
later than this kingdom.

## The same shape, with letters

The Burmese script tells a similar story from the other side.

It was adapted at Pagan from the Mon script, for a language of an entirely
different family — a substantial piece of work, and the reason Burmese is written
the way it is today. But the parent was Mon, and what Pagan did was take a system
and use it, rather than build one that others then took from it.

A kingdom can be the greatest patron a religion ever had and still be a terminus
rather than a route. Pagan is the clearest case of it anywhere in Southeast Asia:
everything came in, almost nothing went out, and what it built with what came in
is still standing.
