The reason there are ten thousand temples is that building one was the most
effective thing a person with wealth could do with it.

Theravada Buddhist merit-making, at Pagan, meant endowing a religious
foundation: a temple, a monastery, an ordination hall, with land to support it
and bonded cultivators to work the land. The donation was recorded on a stone
placed at the site, listing the donor, the fields, the people, the animals and
the intention. Several hundred of those inscriptions survive, in Old Burmese,
saying with legal precision who gave what.

The system worked for everyone inside it. Kings acquired legitimacy; officials
and merchants acquired standing; the sangha acquired an institutional base; and
the plain acquired the largest concentration of Buddhist architecture ever
built.

## The tax base

Land given to the sangha was given permanently and was free of tax.

That is the whole of Aung-Thwin's argument. The crown's income was a land tax
on irrigated rice land in the dry zone, worked by bonded labour. Every act of
royal merit-making transferred some of that land, and the labour attached to
it, out of the taxable economy for good. Two hundred years of the most pious
kings being the most generous ones is two hundred years of the state's revenue
base shrinking, one donation at a time, in a direction with no mechanism for
reversal.

By the thirteenth century the inscriptions show kings attempting to buy land
back, to reclaim endowments, and to purge the order — moves that a state with a
sound fiscal position does not need to make.

Whether this is *the* explanation for what happened in 1287 is argued about.
It is the explanation the next chapter follows.
