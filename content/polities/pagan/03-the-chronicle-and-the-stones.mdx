The Burmese court's own account of how Pagan became a Buddhist kingdom is a
good story, and the inscriptions do not support it. Both of those facts are
worth having, and the second one is the more interesting.

## What the chronicle says

The *Glass Palace Chronicle*, compiled at Ava in 1829 from older chronicles,
tells it like this.

Anawrahta, converted by a monk from the south, sent envoys to Manuha, king of
Thaton in the Mon country, asking for a copy of the Pitakas — the Buddhist
canon. Manuha refused, and spoke ill to the envoys. Anawrahta marched, took
Thaton in 1057, and brought back the scriptures, the monks, the relics, the
craftsmen and the king.

The chronicle is precise about the numbers and the furniture. He kept thirty
sets of the Pitakas in a *pyatthat* richly fraught with gems and had the Order
give instruction in them. He put the relics in a ruby casket and kept it by his
bed where his head lay.

And of Manuha it says that his glory was such that whenever he spoke, a wheel
issued radiant from his mouth — so that when he bowed before Anawrahta, *that
king was aghast, and his hair stood on end*.

## What kind of document that is

Not a chronicle in the sense of a record kept at the time. It is a nineteenth-
century commission compiling much older material, and the material is heroic.

Kyanzittha, the greatest of the Pagan kings, is hidden as an infant by a Naga
while soldiers kill six thousand people looking for him, and then five thousand
more. He is recruited into the king's service as one of four heroes chosen for
physical feats — one could plough a whole field with sixty yoke of oxen keeping
a straight furrow; one could run down the cliff at Nyaung-u, swim the Irrawaddy
and swim back without touching the far bank. Their horses are demons, and the
chronicle names all five of them.

To its credit, the chronicle knows it is handling traditions. It quotes the
Great Chronicle against the Thaton Chronicle, notes where the two disagree,
and says so.

## What the stones say

Michael Aung-Thwin's work on the Pagan inscriptions put the Thaton conquest under pressure and it has not fully recovered.

There is no contemporary inscription recording a conquest of Thaton. There is no
archaeological horizon at Thaton matching a sack in 1057. The Mon element in
early Pagan's material culture, on this reading, comes from a longer and less
dramatic contact rather than from a single carried-off library, and Theravada
Buddhism was present in upper Burma before Anawrahta.

The counter-case is not dead and the argument is live. What is not disputed is
that the earliest evidence for the story is much later than the event.

## Why a court would invent it

Because it does exactly the work a founding story has to do.

It makes Burmese Theravada a possession legitimately acquired — asked for
first, and taken only after an insult. It makes the dynasty the guardian of a
canon rather than a group of Upper Burma warlords who got lucky. And it
supplies a defeated king whose radiant mouth proves the value of what was taken.

What actually happened is that a state on the dry zone of the middle Irrawaddy
unified the irrigated basin, acquired a religion that was already in the
country, and spent two hundred and fifty years building.

## What is certainly true

The buildings. Something over two thousand brick temples and stupas still stand
on the plain at Pagan, out of a great many more, built between the eleventh
century and the thirteenth.

There is no dispute about those, they need no chronicle, and they are the reason
the argument about Thaton matters so little to what Pagan actually was.
