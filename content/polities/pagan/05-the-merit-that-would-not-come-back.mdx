Pagan was dismantled by its own piety, on a mechanism nobody involved could
have described as a problem, and the process took about a century and a half.

## How a temple was paid for

A Burmese king or noble who wished to make merit built a temple, and endowed it.
The endowment was land, and with the land went the people who worked it and the
revenue they produced, granted to the monastery in perpetuity, recorded on
stone.

The crucial word is perpetuity. Property given to the *sangha* was religious
property. Taking it back was not a fiscal decision; it was an act against the
religion, and no king could do it without putting his own merit — and his
legitimacy, which rested on being a defender of the faith — at risk.

## The arithmetic

Do that for two hundred years in a state whose entire tax base is irrigated
rice land in a fairly small dry zone, and the arithmetic is inexorable.

Aung-Thwin's reconstruction from the inscriptions is that by the thirteenth
century a very large share of the cultivated land of the Kyaukse and Minbu
irrigated basins — the granary of the kingdom — was in monastic hands and off
the tax rolls, along with the labour attached to it. The crown's own domain
shrank while the demands on it did not.

And the incentive ran the wrong way at every level. A king who wanted merit
gave land. A noble who wanted merit gave land. A landholder who wanted to keep
land out of the king's reach could dedicate it and hold it as a monastic
steward. Piety and tax avoidance produced identical documents.

## What was tried

Periodic purifications of the *sangha* — reforming the order, expelling monks
judged lax, and returning improperly held property to the crown. Several Pagan
kings did it, and it worked temporarily and at a cost, because each purification
was an argument with the institution the dynasty's legitimacy rested on.

The building did not stop. The temples of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries
are the largest and most elaborate at Pagan: Dhammayangyi, Sulamani, Htilominlo,
Gawdawpalin. Each of them is an endowment.

## The state that met the Mongols

So when Mongol envoys arrived in the 1270s demanding submission, the kingdom
they were addressing had a magnificent capital, an enormous religious
establishment, and a shrinking fiscal base with which to raise an army.

Narathihapate is said to have had the envoys executed, which is the decision of
a ruler who does not understand what is in front of him. What followed — the
campaigns of 1277 and 1283, the fall of the capital in 1287 — is the subject of
the next chapter, and it is best read as what happens to a state that has spent
its revenue on merit when somebody arrives who wants tribute.

## The general case

This is not a Burmese peculiarity, and it is worth saying so, because the story
is often told as though excessive Buddhist devotion were the specific flaw.

Land alienated in perpetuity to a religious institution that the state cannot
politically reclaim is one of the most reliable ways for a pre-modern government
to hollow itself out, and the same mechanism turns up in the Gupta copper-plate
grants, in the monasteries of medieval Europe before the dissolutions, and in
the waqf endowments of the Islamic world.

What makes Pagan the clearest case is the evidence. The endowments were cut in
stone with the acreage and the labour on them, and they are still standing next
to the temples they paid for.
