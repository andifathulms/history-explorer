The force that ended Yemeni self-rule had not been sent to Yemen.

Cairo had despatched a Mamluk expedition into the Red Sea to deal with the
Portuguese, who were raiding shipping and had attempted Aden. It was a naval
problem and the expedition was a naval answer to it. In Yemen the expedition
found a quarrel with Amir II instead — over supply, over money, over the
Tahirid refusal to be treated as a subordinate — and turned inland. It defeated
him and killed him in 1517, and took Sanaa and Zabid.

<Figure id="qaytbay" />

While it was doing that, Selim I destroyed the Mamluk Sultanate. Qansuh
al-Ghawri had been killed at Marj Dabiq in August 1516 and Tuman Bay was hanged
at Cairo in April 1517. The men who conquered Yemen were the army of a state
that had been abolished, and they found it out afterwards, and went over to the
Ottomans, who thereby acquired a country they had not fought for.

The conquerors were Mamluks, and they are named as such here even though the
result reads strangely: a state destroyed in 1517 by a state that itself ended
in 1517. Assigning the conquest to the Ottomans instead would be tidier and
would be false. The Ottomans acquired Yemen by inheriting the men who took it,
several months after the fact, without sending anybody.

<Figure id="selimname" />

What did not end in 1517 was the Zaydi imamate in the northern highlands, which
had outlasted the Sulayhids, the Ayyubids, the Rasulids and now the Tahirids,
and which took the Ottomans a century to subdue and another thirty years to lose
to. Its record is elsewhere in this region and carries no edge to this one. The
imams were not the Tahirids' heirs and did not claim to be. They were simply
still there, which in this country was the more durable achievement.
