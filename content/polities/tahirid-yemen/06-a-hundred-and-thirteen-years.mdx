What replaced the Tahirids was not another Yemeni dynasty. It was a century of
somebody else's empire, and the interval is worth counting: from the killing of
the last sultan in 1517 to the point at which Yemen was governed from inside
itself again is a hundred and thirteen years.

The sequence, as a later British account sets it out, is short and almost
entirely about the coast.

Egypt came under Ottoman suzerainty in 1517 — the same year the expedition it
had sent killed the Tahirid sultan. In 1538 the Ottoman governor of Egypt
occupied the coastal towns of Yemen and laid the foundation of Ottoman dominion
there. The Portuguese took Aden in 1540. A Turkish admiral took it back. A
governor named Hasan Pasha eventually occupied the whole province in the
sultan's name, and that occupation lasted until 1630, when the Porte decided to
evacuate Yemen on account of its remoteness and the expense involved.

Yemen was then governed by a family descended from a sharif of Mecca, one of
whom took the title of imam.

## The shape of it

Read that again with the dates removed and it is a familiar Yemeni pattern
running one more time.

An outside power arrives because the coast is valuable. It takes the ports
easily and the highlands with difficulty. It holds the country for as long as it
is willing to pay for it, discovers that Yemen costs more than it yields, and
leaves. What fills the space afterwards is not the dynasty that was there before
— that family is gone — but whoever in the mountains can raise men.

The Ayyubids did this in the twelfth century and left the Rasulids behind. The
Ottomans did it in the sixteenth and left an imamate behind. The Ottomans did it
again in the nineteenth. The variable is which empire; the constant is that the
highlands outlast it and the coast pays for it.

## What the Tahirids left standing

The dynasty itself left very little in the way of institutions, having had
sixty-three years and no successor.

What it left was buildings. The madrasa the last sultan finished at Rada' in
1504 — domed, painted, unlike anything built in Yemen before it — was thirteen
years old when its patron was killed, and it is still there. A family of
provincial landowners who governed for two generations and were destroyed by an
army acting on behalf of a state that had already ceased to exist are remembered
mostly because of what they put up in a small highland town while they had the
money.

That is a thin legacy by the standards of the dynasties on either side of them.
It is also the last thing built in Yemen by a Yemeni government for a very long
time.
