In March 1513 a Portuguese fleet appeared off Aden. It was the first European
force ever to attempt the Red Sea, and its commander intended to take the port,
close the strait, and cut the spice route between India and Egypt at its neck.

Aden held.

## How the assault failed

The account written up from the commander's own papers is candid about it,
because the failure was not the defenders' doing in the first instance. It was
the attackers'.

The boats grounded a crossbow-shot from the shore on shelving rock, so the men
had to wade in, and the musketeers soaked their powder doing it. They carried
the scaling ladders on their backs through the water and got them against the
wall anyway.

Then the ladders broke. Not under enemy action — under the weight of Portuguese
soldiers racing each other to be first up. The commander's own company put their
ladders up and swarmed them without waiting for orders; the ladders came down.
The men then ran to the next ladder along and broke that one too, and the
halberdiers sent to prop it up had their halberds smashed and were killed or
wounded under it.

One party did get onto the wall and planted a banner on a turret. Nobody could
reinforce them, because by then every ladder in the fleet was in pieces. The
commander's nephew, wounded and covered in blood, made his way along the
parapet to ask what to do, and was told: *do not excite yourself, for this
orchard cannot produce any other kind of fruit than this.*

They burst open an embrasure to let crossbowmen through. The defenders saw how
few men were on the wall, swarmed the opening, and filled it in with lighted
straw and earth and stones. A ladder was patched together out of the splinters
of the broken ones. It failed too.

The fleet withdrew, went into the Red Sea, found no water and no useful landfall,
and came back out having achieved nothing.

## Why a successful defence was a catastrophe

The Tahirids had beaten off the most formidable naval power then operating in
the Indian Ocean, at their own richest port, and it did them no good at all.

What the attempt announced was that the Red Sea was now contested by a force
that came from the ocean rather than from Egypt or Arabia, and that the spice
route which had already been drifting away from Aden could now be cut at will by
somebody with better ships. Yemen's whole fiscal position depended on that route
touching its coast.

More immediately, it brought the fleets. The next year an Egyptian fleet took
most of the Yemeni ports — not Aden, which held again — and the Mamluk sultanate
began operating in the Red Sea as a matter of survival, because the Portuguese
threatened Egypt's revenue far more than they threatened Yemen's.

Four years later a Mamluk expedition sent into those waters against the
Portuguese would turn inland instead and destroy the dynasty that had held the
wall in 1513.

The defence of Aden was competent, and it was the beginning of the end. The
Tahirids were not conquered because they were weak at sea. They were conquered
because their coast had become strategically interesting to two empires at once,
and neither of those empires was going to leave it in the hands of a family of
highland landowners.
