News of the Treaty of Kiel reached a Norway that, in Boyesen's own
telling, had finally woken from centuries of political torpor and
meant to assert itself. The Norwegians were willing enough to give up
their allegiance to Frederick VI, but denied he had any right to hand
that allegiance to Sweden without their own consent - if Norway had
become a Danish dependency four centuries earlier without being
asked, they reasoned, then sovereignty renounced by the Danish crown
reverted to the Norwegian people themselves, free to bestow it on
whomever they chose. The Danish viceroy in Norway, Prince Christian
Frederik, found this sentiment widespread enough to refuse simply
accepting the treaty's terms, and summoned representative men to meet
him at Eidsvoll in 1814. He had first meant to claim the Norwegian
crown by hereditary right and rule as an absolute monarch; advisors
talked him instead into accepting the crown from the people, bound by
whatever constitution they themselves adopted.

## A question to the peasants

Christian Frederik travelled through the depths of winter across the
Dovre mountains to Trondheim to gauge the country's mood himself,
stopping in Gudbrandsdal to read the inscription on a monument
commemorating the destruction of a Scottish mercenary force two
centuries before - "Woe to the Norseman whose blood does not course
more warmly through his veins when he looks upon this stone" - and
asking the peasants gathered to see him whether they, like their
forefathers, were willing to sacrifice life and blood for their
country. The response encouraged him enough to summon a full assembly
at Eidsvoll: a hundred and twelve representatives, most of them
officials, convened there and on 17 May 1814 adopted a constitution
Boyesen calls extremely liberal for its time, declaring Norway a free
and independent country and electing Christian Frederik king.

## Independence or union, argued out loud

The assembly split openly over what came next. A larger faction led
by Judge Falsen and Professor Sverdrup wanted full independence
outright; a smaller but serious faction around Count Wedel-Jarlsberg
argued that a country as small as Norway, unable to field an army
adequate to its own defence, would end up prey to whichever foreign
power chose to pick a fight with it - and that liberty was actually
safer secured through union with a stronger neighbour than left to
stand alone. The constitution adopted at Eidsvoll settled for
declared independence without resolving that argument in advance,
leaving the question of union or standalone kingship to whatever came
next.
