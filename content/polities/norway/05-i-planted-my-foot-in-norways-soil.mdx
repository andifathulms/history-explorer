Olaf Haraldsson, descended from Harald Fairhair through a different
branch than either of his two royal predecessors, went to sea as a
viking at twelve and spent his youth raiding Sweden to avenge his own
father's death and fighting in England alongside Æthelred's sons
against the Danes. When Earl Erik, then ruling Norway for Cnut, left
the country in 1015, Olaf saw his opening and sailed for home -
cautiously, leaving his warships in England and crossing with two
plain merchant vessels so he could gauge the country's mood before
committing himself. Landing unrecognized, he slipped on the beach as
he came ashore; when he cried out at the apparent bad omen, one of
his men answered that he had not fallen but planted his foot in
Norway's own soil. Travelling in disguise, he was recognized by a
peasant who correctly predicted he would soon defeat Earl Haakon and
win a great victory - which he did, seizing power and forcing
Christianity on the country with the same uncompromising resolve
Olaf Tryggvason had shown two decades before, rejecting whole bands
of men who would not renounce their old gods even as he accepted
outlaws and robbers who agreed to be baptized.

## Outwitting a king with a dam

Olaf's growing power drew Cnut's attention, and by the mid-1020s the
Danish king was actively working to reclaim Norway. When Cnut's fleet
pursued Olaf and his Swedish allies into the Helgeå river in 1026,
Olaf dammed the river above the harbour where Cnut's ships lay
overnight, then broke the dam in darkness, sending a wall of water
crashing into the anchored Danish and English fleet and scattering it
in confusion - a tactical triumph that nonetheless failed to finish
Cnut off, since an ally's timely rescue let him regroup. Cnut answered
not with another battle but with money, systematically bribing
disaffected Norwegian chieftains until, in 1028, he sailed
unopposed into Trondheim and was proclaimed king of Norway himself,
leaving his young nephew Haakon as regent.

## The scratch that became a killing blow

With nothing left to do but flee, Olaf made one last attempt to rally
support, sailing north along the coast pursued by his old rival
Erling Skjalgsson. Separated from his own main force by a ruse,
Erling fought alone until overwhelmed and stood, an old man with grey
hair falling over his shoulders, among a heap of his own dead
followers. Olaf offered him peace after a moment's hesitation - then,
half regretting the mercy, scratched the old man's cheek with an axe
as a mark of shame. One of the king's own men, taking the gesture as
license, immediately split Erling's skull instead, killing the most
powerful chieftain Norway had ever produced. The act cost Olaf what
little support remained to him: his followers deserted in numbers,
enemies blocked every route he tried, and by 1029 he had fled through
the mountains into Sweden, leaving his wife and daughter behind, and
on to Russia, where his own brother-in-law King Jaroslav gave him
refuge. Norway, meanwhile, had become a province of a foreign crown.
