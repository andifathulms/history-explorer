Exiled in Russia, Olaf gathered what forces he could and marched back
toward Norway through Sweden in 1030, determined to reclaim a
kingdom that had already once rejected him. Bishop Sigurd met him on
the road and tried to talk him out of the attempt entirely; Olaf
would not be dissuaded. When he crossed into sight of his homeland
from the mountains, he reined his horse and sat in silence so long
that the bishop finally asked what troubled him - Olaf described a
vision in which he had seen not just Trondheim but all of Norway,
and then the whole world, land and sea both known and unknown to
him. The bishop, dismounting to embrace the king's foot, declared him
a holy man on the spot. On the morning of the battle, Olaf had his
own poet, Thormod Kolbrúnarskald, sing the old war-song Bjarkamál to
wake the camp; his army numbered barely four thousand once five
hundred pagans among them were sent away, against a peasant host ten
thousand strong gathering on the heights of Stiklestad in Værdalen.

## An eclipse over the battlefield

The armies closed with the war-cries "Forward, Christ's men,
crusaders, the King's men!" against "Forward, forward, peasant-men!"
Olaf's position gave him the advantage at first, but the peasants'
sheer numbers told as the fighting dragged on, and his own ranks
began to thin. He broke from the shield-wall around him and led a
last charge into the enemy's front line; his standard-bearers fell
one after another around him. Wounded and leaning against a boulder,
he was struck first by a spear from Thore Hund - who had sworn six
years earlier, over a rune-inscribed spear soaked in his own
murdered kinsman's blood, to avenge him on Olaf - and then finished
by a sword-cut across the throat from Kálf Árnesson. At the moment of
his death, by the saga's own account, the sun turned blood-red and
then black, an eclipse the terrified peasant army took for a sign of
their victim's God's own wrath. Modern reckoning confirms a total
solar eclipse did darken Værdalen that afternoon, 29 August 1030 -
one of the rare saga details astronomy itself can date.

## What survived him

The poet Thormod, badly wounded himself, made his way to a healer's
hut afterward, pulled the arrowhead from his own side with a pair of
tongs, looked at the flesh clinging to the barbs, remarked that the
king had fed him well since he was "yet fat about the heart-roots,"
and died where he sat. A peasant named Thorgils, who had promised
before the battle to bury Olaf if he fell, recovered the king's body,
and within years tales of miracles worked by his blood and by the
touch of his dead hand had spread across the country. St Olaf, red-
bearded in legend, became not merely a martyr but Norway's national
saint and national hero at once - his violent death doing more to
root Christianity in the country than either his own forceful
conversion campaign or Olaf Tryggvason's had managed while either
man still lived. Cnut, unable to spare much attention from his wider
empire, had his own son Sweyn proclaimed King of Norway in Olaf's
place - a foreign boy-king and his mother's unpopular attempt to
remake Norwegian law in the Danish image, which the chiefs who had
once turned against Olaf himself would soon come to regret far more
than the martyr they had helped kill.
