Harald Sigurdsson - Hardråde, "the Hard-ruler" - became sole king of
Norway on his nephew's death in 1047, though the Danish throne Magnus
had held passed instead to Sweyn Estridsson, whom Harald spent years
of inconclusive warfare trying to dislodge without success. Even
allowing for the exaggeration saga-tellers loyal to a Norwegian
audience naturally brought to accounts of a Norwegian captain
outshining the Byzantine empire's own generals, Boyesen's account
treats Harald's Constantinople years - Varangian Guard commander,
veteran of campaigns against the Saracens, accumulator of a fortune
large enough to make him one of the richest men in the North by the
time he came home - as substantially real beneath the romantic
embellishment. At home he ruled with the severity his own byname
promised, crushing a revolt among the Uplanders and defeating a
Swedish force under Earl Haakon that had stopped paying him tribute
its own province owed.

## An earl's invitation

In 1066 Tostig, brother of England's King Harold Godwinson, came to
Norway seeking help conquering England after finding no support in
Denmark - Sweyn Estridsson wanted no part of his own uncle Cnut's old
ambitions. Harald, never one to refuse adventure, sailed that
September with the largest fleet Norway had ever sent abroad: by
Boyesen's estimate as many as three hundred and fifty ships and
thirty thousand men once the Orkney earls' own contingent and
Tostig's forces joined it. He won a battle at Fulford outside York
and took the city, then made camp at Stamford Bridge - leaving a
third of his army under his own son to guard the ships, and letting
the rest go without armour in the warm weather, with no expectation
of any real threat nearby.

## "Seven feet, or so much more as he is taller"

Harold Godwinson's army caught them entirely by surprise. Riding out
to parley before the fighting began, a group of English horsemen
asked for Tostig by name and offered him peace and a third of
England to abandon the Norwegians - Tostig asked what England would
offer King Harald himself, and was told: seven feet of ground, or as
much more as his unusual height required. Tostig, recognizing his own
brother as the rider who had made the offer only after the exchange
was over, sent him back to open the battle. The fighting went well at
first - Harald fought with the same berserker fury the sagas gave
every hero of his line - until an arrow took him through the throat
and he fell dead from his horse. Tostig, refusing his brother's offer
of a truce, took command and fought on until he too was cut down;
reinforcements under Harald's own brother-in-law arrived only to be
overwhelmed in turn. What remained of the great army that had sailed
from Norway that autumn straggled back to the ships under cover of
dusk, leaving so many dead on the field that human bones still lay
scattered there years afterward. Two weeks later William of Normandy
landed at Hastings, and the victory Harold Godwinson had just won at
Stamford Bridge came to nothing.
