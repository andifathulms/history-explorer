Magnus Eriksson's later reign and his son Haakon's brought Norway one
disaster after another. In 1344 a collapsing rock face suddenly
diverted the Gula river, destroying forty-eight farms and drowning
two hundred and fifty people along with their livestock; Iceland
suffered its own earthquake and a violent eruption of Hekla. None of
it compared to what arrived by ship at Bergen in 1349: the Black
Death, already having ravaged Germany, England and southern Europe,
reached Norway aboard an English merchant vessel and spread with
terrible speed across the whole country. In Trondheim the archbishop
and every canon of the cathedral chapter died but one, who was left
to elect a successor alone. Whole districts lost their entire
population; horses and cattle, with no one left to tend them, starved
or wandered off to die in the forest. Centuries of accumulated labour
unraveled within a single generation - fertile valleys reverted to
forest, foxes denned in abandoned farmhouses, wolves prowled through
empty churches, and in many places the dead simply lay where they
fell until the ground itself absorbed them.

## What the plague did not do alone

The survivors, in Boyesen's own account, fell into a sloth and
lawlessness common to societies gutted by plague - fields went
untilled for want of labourers and draft animals, famine followed
directly on the pestilence's heels, and what remained of Norwegian
trade fell into foreign hands as domestic commerce collapsed
outright. But Boyesen is careful to note that later historians did
the Black Death an injustice by making it solely responsible for
Norway's eclipse: other causes, chiefly the political drift and
absentee kingship of the preceding decades, compounded the plague's
damage rather than being caused by it. Magnus Eriksson drowned in the
Bommelfjord in 1374, having outlived his own liberty from Swedish
rule by only three years; his son Haakon followed him to the grave
just six years later, in 1380, dying young like so many Norwegian
kings before him.

## Four hundred and thirty-four years

Haakon and Margaret's only son, Olaf, was proclaimed King of Norway
at his father's death in 1380 - already King of Denmark in his own
right since his maternal grandfather Valdemar Atterdag's own death
five years earlier. Still a child, he ruled in name only, his mother
Margaret and a council of regents governing for him in both
kingdoms. It was, without any single treaty or battle to mark it, the
beginning of the union between Norway and Denmark that would run
unbroken for four hundred and thirty-four years - a union Boyesen's
own verdict calls, from Norway's own side of it, a disaster from
which the smaller partner would not recover for centuries. Olaf
himself did not live to see much of it: he died at seventeen, at
Falsterbo in Scania, with his mother already the real power behind a
throne two crowns now shared.
