Olaf Tryggvason's own childhood, as the sagas tell it, reads less like
royal history than adventure fiction - and Boyesen's own account
warns that separating fact from the embellishments later tradition
heaped on its favourite hero is no easy task. Fleeing Queen Gunhild's
pursuit as an infant, Olaf and his mother were captured by pirates
while sailing for Russia; his foster-father was killed for being too
old to sell, and the boy himself was traded away twice more, once for
a ram and once for a coat and cape, before landing with a master who
treated him kindly. Found years later by his own uncle in an Estonian
market square, he was bought free - and, not long after, spotted the
viking who had killed his foster-father and split the man's skull
with an axe on the spot, a killing that only his uncle's intervention
with the local queen saved him from being executed for. Raised
afterward at the Russian court as a prince, he spent his teenage years
raiding as a viking commander, married a Wendish princess after
service to her father, and, widowed in his early twenties, drifted
into Christianity by way of a dream that sent him to Greece to be
signed with the cross.

## A hood lifted in a marketplace

Olaf's later adventures across Northumberland, Scotland, Ireland and
France are corroborated in part by outside sources: the Anglo-Saxon
Chronicle names him as commander of a Viking fleet that raided the
English coast in 994 and wintered at Southampton, extracting ten
thousand pounds from King Æthelred before Olaf, confirmed in the
Christian faith with Æthelred himself present, vowed never to trouble
England again - a promise he kept, even as his companion Sweyn
Forkbeard went on raiding regardless. It was in England, by the
saga's account, that Olaf won an Irish princess named Gyda: summoned
before her at an assembly where she was to choose a husband among a
crowd of richly dressed suitors, he came dressed plainly with a fur
hood pulled over his head, and Gyda, looking past every glittering
noble present, lifted the hood of the plain stranger and chose him on
the spot.

## Proclaimed at the mouth of the Nid

Word of Olaf's growing fame reached Norway, where Earl Haakon, ruling
in the name of no dynasty but his own, grew increasingly certain that
a surviving grandson of Harald Fairhair would eventually come to
claim his birthright - and sent an agent to lure Olaf home under
pretence of friendship, hoping to have him killed once he arrived.
The plot backfired completely: Olaf sailed for Norway in the spring
of 995, planted a cross on the beach at Moster and had mass said
there, and found on reaching Trøndelag that the peasantry was already
in open revolt against Haakon's own unpopular rule. The earl died in
disgrace shortly after, and the chieftains and peasants of Trøndelag,
gathered at the Øre-thing by the mouth of the river Nid, proclaimed
Olaf King of all Norway - a right the Trønders kept for themselves
from that day on, and the reason Norwegian sovereigns have been
crowned at Trondheim ever since. Olaf then travelled district by
district to receive the homage of the rest of the country, welcomed,
everywhere he went, with enthusiasm.
