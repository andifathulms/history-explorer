The Icelander Snorri Sturluson's thirteenth-century Heimskringla, the
principal source for Norwegian history down to its own time, traces
the royal line back through a saga genealogy of Swedish kings said to
descend from the god Frey himself - among them Aun the Old, who by
legend sacrificed one son to Odin every ten years in exchange for a
decade more of life, and stopped only at the eighth son when his own
people intervened. Whatever thread of real memory runs through such
stories, they are foundation legend rather than history, and Norway's
own documented royal line begins several generations later, with
Halfdan the Swarthy - a king Snorri credits with genuine gifts for
justice and law, ruling Agder and neighbouring districts by
inheritance, war and marriage until he was, by report, the mightiest
king anywhere in Norway.

## A bride who asked for a country

Halfdan's son Harald was only ten when his father died, and it took
years of hard fighting under his uncle Guttorm's regency before the
kingdoms his father had held stayed his. According to Snorri's own
famous account, it was not naked ambition but a woman's rebuke that
turned Harald from a merely successful king into a unifier: when he
sent envoys to court a highborn girl named Gyda as his mistress, she
sent back word that she would not throw away her honour on a king who
ruled only a few counties, wondering aloud why no Norwegian king had
done what Erik had done in Sweden and Gorm in Denmark - conquered the
whole country outright. She would marry him, she said, only if he
conquered all of Norway first. Rather than take offence, Harald
declared the rebuke exactly what he needed to hear, and swore before
God that he would neither cut nor comb his hair until he had made
himself king of a united Norway - or die in the attempt.

## Hafrsfjord

What followed was roughly a decade of campaigning, province by
province, in which Harald reinstalled the local kings who submitted
to him as his own earls - governors who kept a third of the taxes
they collected in exchange for maintaining armed men at the king's
call - and killed or drove out those who resisted. By 872 the
chieftains who still held out realized that none of them could stand
against him alone, and combined their fleets to meet him in the
Hafrsfjord, near modern Stavanger. The battle was long in the
balance; Harald's own berserkers finally broke it, storming aboard
the enemy ships in a killing frenzy that scattered the last
resistance for good. It was remembered, in Snorri's own telling, as
one of the greatest battles Norway had ever seen, and it left Harald
with no serious rival left in the country. At a feast soon after, the
Earl of Møre cut the king's long, unkempt hair before an admiring
court - Harald had gone by the byname Lufa, "the Frowsy-headed," and
came out of that feast as Harald the Fairhaired instead. He kept his
vow and married Gyda, though not before the saga's own record
undercuts its neat romance: Harald had already married another
noblewoman years earlier and fathered three sons by her, a detail
Snorri himself sets down without comment alongside the story he
plainly preferred to tell.
