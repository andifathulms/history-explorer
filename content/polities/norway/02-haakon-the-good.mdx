Harald Fairhair's own sons quarrelled bitterly over his legacy after
his death, and Erik Bloodaxe, who murdered several of his brothers to
secure the throne, ruled with a cruelty that made him widely hated.
His half-brother Haakon had been fostered at the court of King
Æthelstan in England and raised there as a Christian; when he
returned to Norway to challenge Erik, he came not as a conqueror but
as a rival offering something Erik never had - conciliation. Landing
in Trøndelag, he won over the powerful Earl Sigurd of Hlade, his own
mother's old protector, and through him called an assembly of
peasants at which he promised, if they made him king, to restore the
allodial land rights his own father had stripped from them a
generation before. The offer was met with rejoicing, and homage
followed him south through district after district until Erik, unable
to raise an army against him, fled with his family first to the
Orkneys and then to England.

## A gentler king, a durable law

Haakon proved unusually generous to his rivals once in power,
confirming Erik's own nephews as subordinate kings in Viken rather
than deposing them, and extending the same courtesy to other
descendants of Harald Fairhair. With his own position secure, he
turned to reforming the kingdom's institutions, dividing the country
into judicial Thing-districts and codifying laws with the help of
experienced advisors - the Gulating law, the most celebrated of these
codes, would outlast him by centuries. He remained personally
Christian and brought some of his own friends to the faith, but chose
persuasion over compulsion, unwilling to alienate the same peasants
whose support had made him king; between that caution and years of
good harvests, he became, in the saga's own judgment, more loved by
his people than any king who ever sat on Norway's throne - hence the
name they gave him, Haakon the Good.

## The golden helmet at Fitjar

In 961, in the twenty-sixth year of his reign, the sons of Gunhild -
Erik Bloodaxe's own exiled family - returned with a fleet six times
the size of the force Haakon had on hand at his estate in Hordaland.
He chose to fight rather than flee, arming himself in a golden helmet
that flashed in the sun and leading from the front through a battle
his own poet, Eyvind Scald-Spoiler, later commemorated in verse; when
the helmet made him too easy a target, Eyvind pulled a hood over it
to hide him from the enemy. Haakon killed one of the attacking princes
himself in single combat, breaking the enemy's nerve and turning the
battle into a rout - but was struck in the arm by an arrow during the
pursuit, a wound rumoured to have been cursed by Gunhild herself, that
would not stop bleeding. Dying, he refused a friend's offer to have
his body carried to England for Christian burial: "I have lived like
a heathen," he said, "and therefore it is meet that I should be
buried like a heathen." Having no son of his own and a daughter barred
by law from the throne, his last act was to send a ship after the
very sons of Gunhild he had just defeated, inviting them to come back
and take the kingdom he was leaving behind.
