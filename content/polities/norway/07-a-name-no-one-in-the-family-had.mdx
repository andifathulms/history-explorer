Magnus, St Olaf's illegitimate son, was born so weak that no one
expected him to survive the night of his birth, and in the emergency
his father's own poet took it on himself to have the infant baptized
at once - naming him Magnus, after Charlemagne, a name no one in the
family had ever carried, and one Olaf was furious about when he
learned of it later. The child who nearly died that night was not yet
eleven when Norway's own resentment at Danish misrule brought him
home from Russia to be proclaimed king at the Øre-thing in 1035, and
he governed at first through the advice of the same chieftains who
had once turned against his father, before growing into a ruler of
independent judgment. Devoted to his father's memory in a way his own
illegitimate birth made pointed, he began construction of a church in
Nidaros to bear St Olaf's name and had a new, gold- and jewel-
adorned sarcophagus made for him.

## Norway and Denmark, briefly one

War with Harthacnut of Denmark nearly broke out within Magnus's first
years, but chieftains on both sides talked the two young kings into
an agreement at the Brenn Islands in 1038: each would inherit the
other's kingdom if he died without an heir. It seemed a remote
contingency until it was not - Harthacnut died in 1042, and Magnus
was proclaimed king of Denmark at the Viborg-thing without
opposition, uniting Norway and Denmark under one crown for the first
time and winning his own Fairhair line recognition as equals from the
Danish royal house, matching the standing it already held in Sweden.

## An uncle who demanded half a kingdom

Nine years into Magnus's reign, his uncle Harald Sigurdsson - badly
wounded fighting at Stiklestad as a teenager, and since then a
soldier of fortune who had risen to command the Byzantine emperor's
own Varangian Guard through campaigns against the Saracens in Asia
and Sicily - returned to Norway and demanded half the kingdom
outright. When an old peasant at an assembly argued that the king
properly proclaimed by the people held more claim than a newcomer
demanding power by force, Harald turned his anger on Einar
Thambarskelver, who had thanked the peasants for their loyalty:
"happy will the day be when thy helmet shall be laid low... thou
shalt then be a head shorter." Magnus's own prudence kept outright war
from breaking out between uncle and nephew, and the two ruled jointly
- uneasily - until Magnus, thrown from his horse in Zealand and struck
unconscious, fell into a foreboding melancholy that culminated in a
dream of his father offering him a choice: die now, or live on to
become the mightiest of kings while bearing a sin that could hardly be
forgiven. "Choose thou for me, father," he answered in the dream.
Magnus died in 1047, twenty-four years old, having ruled Norway for
twelve and a half years and Denmark for five - mourned, the sources
agree, by the whole of his people. Harald moved immediately to block
the succession Magnus had promised in Denmark to his own rival, Sweyn
Estridsson.
