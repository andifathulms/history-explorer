Sverre's Birchlegs, despite their ragged reputation, proved nearly
impossible to keep beaten - defeated repeatedly, they rallied again
and again with what Boyesen calls wonderful intrepidity and
endurance, and by 1177 they were strong enough to seize Trondheim
outright. King Magnus's forces, nicknamed the Heklungs, remained the
stronger side at sea, where the Birchlegs never fought as well as on
land, and at the Battle of Nordnes near Bergen in 1181 Magnus's fleet
had gained the upper hand until Sverre, standing exposed amid a hail
of missiles without even a shield, began singing a Latin hymn in a
loud, clear voice and came through unharmed. Moments later Magnus
himself was wounded in the wrist boarding an enemy ship, slipped on
the blood-slick deck, and fell - and when word spread that the king
was down, one of his own commanders, assuming him dead, cut the
ropes joining the royal fleet's ships and fled, breaking the battle
line and turning a fight Magnus was winning into a rout he could not
reverse even after getting back on his feet to prove he was alive.

## An offer to share the crown, refused

The war dragged on for three more years with fortunes shifting back
and forth; Magnus, driven repeatedly into exile in Denmark, always
found fresh forces to bring home again. Sverre, hoping to end a
conflict that was bleeding the country dry, twice offered to share
the kingdom with his rival - first outright, then as alternating
three-year reigns - and Magnus refused both times. An archbishop who
had excommunicated Sverre found the ban made no impression on him
whatsoever, while losing his own see to the conflict impressed the
archbishop a great deal; he eventually made his peace with Sverre in
exchange for reinstatement. By 1183 Sverre had run Magnus out of
Norway entirely, seizing his fleet, treasury and crown regalia in a
surprise attack on Bergen.

## Fourteen ships against twenty-six

Magnus made one last attempt the following summer, sailing north with
twenty-six ships and over three thousand men while Sverre, with only
fourteen ships, was off punishing a district that had killed one of
his officers - and found himself surrounded before he had any warning
an enemy fleet was near. The Heklungs, outnumbering the Birchlegs
better than two to one, expected an easy victory. They found instead
a battle that ran past midnight and cost two thousand lives, King
Magnus among them, drowned when he leapt into the fjord rather than
be taken. By morning the clear water of the Sognefjord showed the
bodies of slain chieftains lying on the bottom with fish swimming
around them; Magnus's own corpse was not recovered for two days. His
death broke the last organized resistance to Sverre's throne, and
with it began a reign - built on a claim of royal birth that could
never be proven, only fought for - that would reshape how Norway's
crown itself was won and held for a generation afterward.
