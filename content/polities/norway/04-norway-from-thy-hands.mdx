Olaf Tryggvason spent his five years as king pursuing Christianization
with the same uncompromising force that had marked his whole
adventuring life - preaching first among his own family's following
in Viken, where a generation of earlier missionary contact eased the
way, and using the sword wherever persuasion failed elsewhere in the
country. His downfall came not from that campaign but from a
marriage: he wed Thyra, sister of the Danish king Sweyn Forkbeard, and
when he sailed south to claim her dowry lands in Wendland, Sweyn -
allied with Earl Erik, who had his own father Haakon's death to
avenge, and the Swedish king Olaf - set an ambush. Earl Sigvalde of
the Jomsvikings, entrusted with escorting Olaf home, was secretly in
the conspirators' pay; Sigvalde's own wife tried to warn Olaf as
openly as she dared, and he ignored her.

## A trap sprung behind an island

The allied fleet, sixty or seventy ships strong, lay in wait behind
the small island of Svolder as Olaf's own ships, sailing in loose
order, came into view one by one. Sweyn and the Swedish king each
mistook passing vessels for Olaf's famous flagship "The Long
Serpent," only for Earl Erik to correct them each time; when the ship
finally appeared, gleaming with its golden dragon-head, Sigvalde's
squadron turned suddenly around the island on a prearranged signal,
cutting Olaf off from most of his own fleet. Queen Thyra wept at the
sight of the odds against her husband, and Olaf told her only that
she would get, that day, the wedding-gift from her brother she had so
often asked him for. The battle that followed went hard against the
outnumbered Norwegians from the start, though they held their own
against the Danes and Swedes in the centre until Earl Erik's galley
"The Iron Ram" began systematically cutting Olaf's ships loose from
the line, one by one, isolating "The Long Serpent" itself.

## The bow that broke

As Erik's ship closed for the ramming attack, the archer Einar
Thambarskelver put two arrows near the earl himself before a
counter-shot shattered his bow in his hands. "What was that which
broke?" Olaf called out. "Norway, from thy hands, my king," Einar
answered - and when Olaf tossed him his own royal bow to keep
fighting, Einar bent it, found it too weak for his own draw, and flung
it back. Olaf, already wounded and bleeding beneath his armour without
mentioning it, fought on: hurling spears two at a time from the poop,
distributing fresh swords from a weapons chest when his men's own
blades turned dull, standing in plain view of the enemy's archers
throughout rather than take shelter. When only nine men remained alive
on his own ship, Olaf raised his shield over his head and leapt into
the sea rather than be taken. A loyal follower who resembled him
leapt after him and was fished out by Erik's men in his place; Olaf
himself was never seen again, though a persistent legend held he had
swum to a rescue boat and lived out his days as a hermit in the Holy
Land. He was thirty-six. Queen Thyra, who blamed herself for the
disaster her own dowry claim had set in motion, refused every offer
of honourable return to Norway and died of grief nine days later.
The victors divided Olaf's kingdom among themselves.
