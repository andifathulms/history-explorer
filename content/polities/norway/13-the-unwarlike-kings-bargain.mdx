With Haakon Haakonsson's death, Boyesen's own narrative loses the
continuous thread the sagas had given it until then - what survives
of his son Magnus's reign comes down in fragments, and a slow,
initially barely visible decline sets in that would not fully reveal
itself for generations. Magnus, already of age when he inherited the
throne in 1263, chose peace over his father's unfinished war with
Scotland: he ceded the Isle of Man and the Hebrides to Alexander III
in exchange for four thousand silver marks and a token annual
tribute meant mainly to preserve the appearance that Norway's old
claim still stood. Whether it reflected sound judgment or simple
reluctance to fight, later events largely vindicated the choice -
those island dependencies had cost Norway more than they were worth
to defend, and Scotland's own rise, followed eventually by England's
absorption of it, would have forced Norway's hand sooner or later
regardless. What the bargain cost was less territory than
reputation: the international standing Haakon had built through
decades of naval strength and diplomatic weight visibly diminished
under a son notably less inclined to assert it. Magnus's own lasting
achievement, the one that earned him his byname, was a unified
national law code - the enduring legal legacy of a reign otherwise
remembered as the hinge on which Norway's medieval strength began,
almost imperceptibly, to turn.

## A crown shared with Sweden

The turn became explicit within a generation. When a Swedish
rebellion deposed and executed King Birger's line in 1319, Norwegian
and Swedish magnates alike settled on the three-year-old Magnus
Eriksson - grandson of Haakon Haakonsson through his mother - as king
of both kingdoms at once, uniting Norway and Sweden under one crown
for the first time. The union was nominal rather than real: separate
laws, separate administrations, nothing shared but a king who was
meant to divide his time between both. During Magnus's own minority
his mother governed Norway with a recklessness that emptied the
treasury to enrich her own lover, and when Magnus came of age in 1332
he proved, as a Swede by birth with little feel for Norwegian affairs,
content to leave the kingdom's government largely to itself. Norwegian
magnates eventually forced him, at a meeting in Bergen in 1350, to
make his younger son Haakon co-regent and heir to Norway alone - a
decision that, through a marriage arranged nine years later between
Haakon and the Danish princess Margaret, would draw Norway toward a
very different union than the one with Sweden it was still nominally
part of.
