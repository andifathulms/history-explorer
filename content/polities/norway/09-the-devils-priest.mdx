Norway's throne in the mid-twelfth century belonged to Magnus
Erlingsson, whose father Erling Skakke had spent years ruthlessly
eliminating every rival descendant of Harald Fairhair he could reach,
reportedly telling his own son that letting any survive would only
give the people someone else to crown. One escaped his notice
entirely: a youth in the Faroe Islands named Sverre Sigurdsson, whose
own mother, Gunhild, had been a cook in the household of King Sigurd
Mouth - ordered by the king to kill her child at birth, she fled to
the Faroes instead and, to protect the boy, married a comb-maker
named Unås who had been sent to find her, raising Sverre to believe
he was Unås's own son. A local bishop, taken with the boy's evident
intelligence, began educating him for the priesthood, and Sverre by
his own account expected nothing grander than a bishopric someday.

## A mother's secret

Everything changed the day Sverre was ordained a deacon. His mother
burst into tears at what should have been an honour, and when he
asked why, she told him the truth she had kept from him for twenty
years: he was not Unås's son at all, but the son of King Sigurd.
"If I am born to a crown," Sverre told her, "then I will strive to
win it, whatever it may cost me." He sailed for Norway and spent
months quietly gauging the political ground before revealing himself
to anyone - visiting Erling Skakke's own men-at-arms in disguise as
an amusing Faroese priest and coming away convinced that Magnus's
rule was too popular to challenge directly. A cold reception from a
Swedish earl he had hoped would back him left him with nothing, until
the remnants of a defeated rebel faction called the Birkebeiner - the
Birchlegs, named in mockery for the birch-bark they wrapped around
their feet when they had no shoes - tracked him down and begged him
to lead them.

## Two empty hands, seventy ragged men

Sverre refused them at first, pointing out that poverty was the only
thing he actually had in common with a band of outlaws. It took the
Swedish earl's own mocking suggestion - that the Birchlegs simply
threaten to kill Sverre if he kept declining - to change his mind.
He was about twenty-four when he finally accepted, taking command of
seventy ragged, poorly armed men with nothing else to his name. It
was, on its face, a hopeless venture against a king whose father had
spent a generation clearing away exactly this kind of rival. What
Sverre had that his predecessors in the role lacked was patience,
calculation, and a genuine gift for war - qualities the Birchlegs
would need in full over the decade of fighting still ahead of them.
