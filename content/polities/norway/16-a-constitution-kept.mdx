The great powers had already promised Sweden's Crown Prince
Bernadotte their support in forcing Norway to honour the Treaty of
Kiel, and once Napoleon's abdication that spring freed Sweden from
any distracting foreign complications, envoys arrived from the
courts of Europe ordering Norway to submit unconditionally. Norway
refused, and a Swedish army under Bernadotte crossed the frontier
that summer. Christian Frederik, whom Boyesen describes as devoid of
real warlike spirit, surrendered the fortress of Fredriksten to the
Swedish fleet without firing a shot in its defence and showed more
skill at retreating than at fighting - but Norwegian troops under
Colonel Krebs, poorly supplied as they were, beat back a second
Swedish column at Lier and forced another attacking force to retire
across the frontier after fighting at Matrand. It became clear to
both sides that outright conquest would cost more blood than either
wanted, and with Sweden itself weary after its own German campaigns,
an armistice was signed at Moss on 14 August 1814.

## What survived the surrender

The armistice called for an extraordinary Storthing to negotiate a
lasting peace; when it met that October, it accepted Christian
Frederik's renunciation of the Norwegian crown and elected Sweden's
King Charles XIII in his place - on the explicit condition that he
recognize Norway's independence and govern under the constitution
adopted at Eidsvoll rather than impose one of his own. Bernadotte
accepted these terms on the Swedish king's behalf that November and
personally swore allegiance to the Norwegian constitution; Swedish
troops withdrew, and Christian Frederik went home to Denmark, where
he would later become king in his own right as Christian VIII. A
formal convention the following year fixed the terms of the union
proper, and Norway established its own national bank at Trondheim and
its own supreme court at Christiania.

## A union, not quite independence

Given how desperate Norway's position had looked in 1814 - a small
country resisting the combined will of Europe's great powers alone -
the terms it secured were, in Boyesen's judgment, considerably better
than anyone had reason to expect. What remained incompatible with
real independence was the continued presence in the Norwegian capital
of a Swedish viceroy representing the king's authority there.
Bernadotte himself, who succeeded to the Swedish throne outright as
Charles XIV John on Charles XIII's death in 1818, did not at first
take Norway's independence terms especially seriously, content for a
time to let Norwegians believe in an autonomy the union's own
practice would test for decades to come.
