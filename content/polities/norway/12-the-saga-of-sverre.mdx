Norway's overseas holdings in Orkney and Shetland had cost the
kingdom blood and treasure to defend for generations, and when a
dispute over them brought Haakon into open war with Alexander III of
Scotland, he sailed for Scotland in person in 1263 despite being past
fifty. A storm wrecked a number of his ships on the crossing, but he
pressed on around the western coast, raided Kintyre and Bute, and
fought a battle at Largs near the mouth of the Firth of Clyde whose
outcome depended entirely on which side told it: Scottish accounts
called it a Norwegian defeat, the sagas a Norwegian victory. Whichever
account was closer to the truth, the battle gave Haakon no real
advantage either way, and he withdrew to the Orkneys to winter there,
intending to renew the campaign again once spring came.

## A death timed to his grandfather's own story

He never got the chance. Taken suddenly ill at Kirkwall, Haakon spent
his final days having the sagas of his own ancestors read aloud to
him, working forward from Halfdan the Swarthy through the generations
to his grandfather Sverre's own story - and died quietly, on 15
December 1263, during the reading of Sverre's saga itself. He was
fifty-nine, and had ruled Norway for forty-six years, longer than any
king before him. Every account agrees that he was a wise and generous
ruler rather than a brilliant one - not gifted, in Boyesen's own
judgment, with anything like his grandfather Sverre's genius, but
possessed of the kind of steady, well-balanced good judgment that
made him, in the end, a greater king than a more dazzling man might
have been. His own rival, Earl Skule, had been by most accounts the
more striking personality of the two; Boyesen's verdict is that
Norway was fortunate Skule never got the throne he wanted instead of
the grandson of Sverre who actually held it.
