The Makalanga throne changed hands by force more often than by
settled inheritance. Theal records an earlier usurpation in vivid
detail - a monomotapa named Mokomba killed by his own favourite
Tshikanga, who put twenty-one of Mokomba's own children to death
before Mokomba's surviving relative Kesarimyo raised a force, killed
Tshikanga, and took the throne himself - and the pattern repeated in
the early seventeenth century with the reign of Gasilusere, whom Theal
describes bluntly as "addicted to the use of dacha, and... a cruel,
passionate, faithless tyrant." Gasilusere executed his own
general-in-chief for a retreat the man had not been responsible for,
alienating a large section of his own people in the process - the
kind of rule that left a throne perpetually vulnerable to whichever
rival could raise the strongest army.

## Civil war and a Portuguese-made king

That vulnerability broke into open war under Gasilusere's son and
successor Kapranzine, who showed open hostility to the Portuguese
traders his father had tolerated. In November 1628 Kapranzine ordered
every Portuguese in his territory killed; the intended victims,
warned in time, fled to fortified trading posts at Masapa and Luanze
and held out against the monomotapa's own armies until reinforcements
arrived from the Portuguese settlements at Sena and Tete. Rather than
simply relieve the siege, the Portuguese and their allies proclaimed a
rival claimant, Manuza - a relative of Kapranzine's whose own
inclination toward Christianity, encouraged by the Dominican friar
Manuel Sardinha, had earned him Kapranzine's jealousy and the
Portuguese's favour - as the rightful monomotapa, and marched against
Kapranzine under a banner of the cross. Kapranzine was defeated,
regrouped, attacked twice more, and was beaten back each time; Manuza
took the great place and was acknowledged paramount chief by most of
the surrounding clans.

## A throne signed away

On 24 May 1629 Manuza put his new authority into writing, signing a
document that formally acknowledged himself a vassal of the King of
Portugal - the first time a reigning monomotapa's own throne was
placed under a European crown by treaty rather than left as an
independent, if friendly, trading partner. He promised free passage
for missionaries, respectful treatment of Portuguese officials without
requiring gifts or ceremony, open commerce, and no shelter for
fugitives from Portuguese justice. It was not a conquest - no
Portuguese army occupied the Makalanga heartland, and Manuza's own
authority over his people remained real - but it converted what had
been an independent empire's diplomatic relationship with a coastal
trading partner into a formal, if distant, subordination that would
shape the monomotapa's own standing for the rest of the century.
