Silveira's murder gave Portugal the pretext it wanted for outright
conquest, and within a decade a nobleman who had governed India led an
army inland to take the gold mines by force. It came back defeated
without ever meeting the monomotapa's own main army in the field - a
failure that shaped Portuguese policy toward the empire for the next
half-century, and that Negomo himself, the same ruler who had ordered
Silveira killed, spent his own remaining years managing rather than
repeating.

## An expedition sold as a crusade and meant as a mining venture

Portuguese scholars consulted on the legality of the war built their
case on the Mutapa's supposed persecution of Christians and toleration
of Muslim traders - traders, in fact, that Portugal's own captains at
Mozambique had been selling firearms to for years. The real motive was
simpler. Reports of gold at Manyika and silver further inland had
reached Lisbon, and with Spain growing rich on its American mines,
Portugal wanted a comparable prize of its own. Francisco Barreto, a
former governor of Portuguese India and friend of the murdered Silveira,
was given command, with the flower of the Portuguese nobility
volunteering and four Jesuits attached to the column. The expedition
left Lisbon in April 1569 and did not reach Sena, on the Zambezi, until
December 1571 - delayed for a year and a half at Mozambique Island by
disease that killed over a hundred of his men before he had even begun
the campaign proper.

## A war against the wrong enemy

Rather than march directly on the monomotapa's own court, Barreto's
Jesuit confessor persuaded him to first attack the Tonga of Samungazi,
a Mutapa tributary accused of killing Portuguese traders. It was a
catastrophic miscalculation. Barreto's roughly 650 arquebusiers marched
into Samungazi's territory in July 1572, at the start of the dry season
and just before harvest - the worst possible timing against a peasant
army that could starve an invader by simply retreating with its own
grain. Three separate engagements against Samungazi's forces, some
numbering over ten thousand warriors armed with little more than
spears and cattle-hide shields, cost Barreto forty dead and left the
survivors so weakened by thirst, hunger and disease that he abandoned
the campaign without ever reaching Samungazi's own capital. Of the
roughly 650 men who had marched out with him, only 180 were still alive
when the column limped back to Sena. Barreto himself, by his own
companions' account broken by the failure, died there in May 1573.

## Negomo, watching from the highlands

Throughout the disaster, Negomo Mupunzagutu - the same monomotapa who
had ordered Silveira strangled eleven years earlier - stayed well clear
of the fighting and let the Tonga absorb the invasion meant for him.
When Barreto's beaten army sent an embassy asking permission merely to
pass through to the Manyika goldfields, Negomo answered with calculated
generosity: he offered a hundred thousand of his own warriors to help
finish off the rebellious Tonga, an offer so alarming in its scale that
the Portuguese envoy hastily declined it and asked only to be left
alone. No one on the Portuguese side had needed to guess what an army
that size, deployed instead of merely offered, might have done to
Barreto's column. Command passed to Vasco Fernandes Homem, who
abandoned the direct approach entirely and led a fresh expedition
through Manyika in 1574-75 instead, trading for gold rather than
conquering for it - a pattern of accommodation with Mutapa power that
would hold for the rest of Negomo's reign.

## A poisoned end, and a general's son inherits

Negomo ruled for roughly forty years in total, dying sometime between
1586 and 1589; his own chief wife is said to have taken poison and died
alongside him, a practice the sources describe as customary at a
monomotapa's death. He was succeeded not by one of his own sons but by
Gatsi Rusere, son of Nyandoro Mukomohasha and probably Negomo's own
general - a succession that passed over Negomo's son Mavhura Mhande,
who had expected to inherit from his uncle in turn, and set up the
family rivalry that would erupt into open war within his own reign.
