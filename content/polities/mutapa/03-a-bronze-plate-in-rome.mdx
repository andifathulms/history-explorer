Manuza's own line held the throne for a generation after the 1629
vassalage, and under his successor - who abandoned the family's new
Christianity and returned to the old Bantu faith, to the Dominicans'
own dismay - the mission's fortunes wavered. They recovered decisively
under the reign that followed. Taught by the friar Aleixo do Rosario,
the new monomotapa announced his own conversion, and on 4 August 1652
he and his principal wife Luiza were received into the Catholic
church along with a multitude of sub-chiefs, the monomotapa taking the
baptismal name Domingos. Theal records how far the news travelled:
special services at Rome, an account of the baptism engraved in Latin
on a bronze plate, a grand thanksgiving service at the Dominican
convent in Lisbon. For a European audience convinced of its own
civilizing mission, the baptism of an African emperor was proof the
whole enterprise was working.

## A conversion undone by the sword

The celebration outlived its own occasion by less than two decades.
Domingos, Theal notes in passing, was "murdered by his own captains
eighteen years later" - 1670, by the arithmetic of the source's own
account - a death recorded almost as an aside next to the far more
thoroughly documented baptism eighteen years earlier. No conquering
army toppled the Makalanga throne in 1670; the monomotapa's own
household killed him, the same kind of internal violence that had
already claimed Mokomba, unseated Kapranzine, and shaped every
succession this account can trace. The empire itself did not end with
Domingos - reduced, vassal monomotapas continued to rule in his
family's name for more than two further centuries, into an era this
volume's own documentary record does not reach - but the arc Theal's
book actually follows, from the Sofala trading station in 1505 to a
bronze plate in Rome and a murdered king in 1670, closes here: an
empire the Portuguese never conquered outright, brought instead, piece
by piece, treaty by treaty and conversion by conversion, into a
subordination its own rulers kept fighting each other over even as
the substance of their independence quietly disappeared.
