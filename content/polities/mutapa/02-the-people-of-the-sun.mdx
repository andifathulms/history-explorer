When the Portuguese built their fort and trading station at Sofala in
1505, the dominant people of the country between the Sabi and Zambesi
rivers were the Makalanga - "the people of the sun," in Theal's own
translation of the name. Their territory ran from the Indian Ocean
coast far into the interior, further than any Portuguese observer of
the period could say with confidence, and their paramount chief was
known to the newcomers by a title Theal records simply as monomotapa.
Portuguese writers took the word to mean "emperor," though Theal is
careful to note it was apparently unknown in the Makalanga's own
dialect - possibly adopted from a neighbouring people's word for lord
or possessor of a mountain stronghold, since the ruler's own great
place stood close to the mountain the Portuguese called Fura, now
Mount Darwin, which he would not permit outsiders to climb.

## A trade already old

The Portuguese arrived expecting to intercept a gold trade that had
run through Muslim merchants at Sofala and Kilwa for generations
before any European ship reached the coast. Early factors' reports -
Pedro Vaz Soares in 1513, Francisco de Brito in 1519 - describe a
country where gold and ivory moved inland to coastal traders through
networks the Portuguese barely understood and repeatedly failed to
redirect to their own factory, hampered as much by internal Bantu wars
that closed the trade routes as by any Muslim rival. The empire the
word monomotapa named was, by the time written Portuguese records
begin, already an established, functioning state whose own gold had
built the stone architecture at Great Zimbabwe generations earlier -
a history no Portuguese chronicler recorded, because none of them
knew it had happened.
