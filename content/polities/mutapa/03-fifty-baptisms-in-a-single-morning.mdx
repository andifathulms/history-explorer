On 1 January 1561 the Jesuit missionary Goncalo da Silveira arrived at
the zimbabwe of the young monomotapa Negomo Mupunzagutu, sent by
Portugal and Rome with a single aim: the conversion of the emperor
himself, on the theory that a Christian monomotapa would favour
Portuguese traders over the Muslim merchants who still dominated the
interior gold trade. Negomo received him with striking honour, seating
him on his own private rug in quarters normally closed to outsiders,
and offered him gold, land, cattle and women - gifts Silveira
repeatedly declined, wanting nothing but the emperor's own conversion.
That refusal, so contrary to what Negomo expected of any man, seems to
have done more to win the young ruler's confidence than any gift could
have. After sixteen to eighteen days of instruction, Silveira baptized
Negomo and his mother, giving the monomotapa the name Dom Sebastiao
after Portugal's own king; within weeks he had baptized 250 to 300
more people of rank across the court. Mudenge records how far the news
of an African emperor's conversion travelled - special thanksgiving
services in Rome, an account inscribed on a bronze plate a century
later when a later monomotapa followed his example - proof, to a
Portuguese audience, that the whole missionary enterprise was working.

## A wizard with a dead man's bone

The court factions Silveira's influence displaced did not accept the
defeat quietly. Mudenge describes an alliance of threatened Shona
religious leaders, Muslim traders and a rejected dignitary turning
Negomo against the priest with two charges: that Silveira was a
Portuguese spy sent to map the land for a future invasion, and that he
was secretly allied with Negomo's own rival Chipute, plotting to
unseat him. They added that his baptismal water was sorcery, and that
Silveira himself was a wizard who carried a dead man's bone to bring
drought and death on the country. Negomo, shaken, consulted diviners
who confirmed the charges. Silveira sensed the change and prepared for
death rather than flee - distributing his belongings, hearing nearby
Portuguese in confession - and on the night of 15 March 1561, seven or
eight men strangled him in his sleep on Negomo's order and threw his
body into the Musengezi river. Fifty people baptized that same day
were nearly killed alongside him before Negomo's own newly Christian
nobles talked him out of it.

## What the murder actually settled

Silveira's death did not restore the old religious balance he had
briefly upset, and it did not end Portuguese ambitions at the
monomotapa's court - if anything, his killing became the pretext
Portugal used to send an armed expedition into the interior under
Francisco Barreto within a decade. What it did settle, for a
generation, was the question of whether the empire's throne could be
won by persuasion alone: after Negomo, no monomotapa risked so open an
embrace of a single foreign faction again, and the contest for
influence over the throne that had killed a Jesuit in 1561 continued
instead through trade concessions, marriage and, within a century,
open war.
