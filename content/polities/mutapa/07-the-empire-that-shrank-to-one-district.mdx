---
title: The empire that shrank to one district
drafted_from: mudenge-1988

Domingos's murder in 1670 did not end the line of monomotapas his
family had converted to Christianity in Rome's eyes; it ended their
ability to rule much of anything. Mudenge traces the province-by-
province shrinkage that followed: the Rozvi state had already stripped
away Guruuswa in the west by the late seventeenth century, Barwe and
Manyika broke off early in the same period, and the Zambesi valley
lands were carved up into Portuguese-held prazo estates. By the first
half of the eighteenth century the once-continental empire had been
reduced to two provinces, Dande and Chidima; by the century's end even
Dande was gone, leaving one hot, tsetse-infested district, Chidima, as
the sole remaining possession of Mutota's and Matope's successors -
the state David Livingstone described unflatteringly under Mutapa
Kataruza in the mid-nineteenth century.

## A princedom worth annexing

Reduced as it was, Chidima still mattered enough to fight over.
Mudenge records a rival succession dispute in 1810 between Chipute and
Kandeya, and a further round of civil war and drought under Kandeya I
in the 1820s and 1830s; the line continued through Dzeka, Kataruza and
a second Kandeya into the 1870s. It was Kandeya II's successor Dzuda
who lost what remained of the family's independence outright. In 1884
the Portuguese agents Karizimamba and Firmino Luis Germano attacked
Dzuda and broke his power; the following year the remaining lands of
Chidima were formally annexed to Portuguese Mozambique, and on 10 May
1887 a Portuguese officer forced Dzuda's own surrender at his stockade
on the Hue stream. Dzuda went into exile - the first Mutapa in more
than four centuries of the family's rule to hold no territory of his
own at all.

## A last rising, and a last Mutapa

Exile did not end the family's claim. Dzuda's successor, Chioko
Dambamupute, built an anti-Portuguese coalition through the 1890s out
of allied chiefs, sympathetic spirit mediums and the Zimbabwean
resistance leader Mapondera, whom he made commander of his combined
forces. By 1897 they were raiding Portuguese posts as far as Tete; by
1900 they had been joined by the ruler of Barwe and the forces of
Massangano; by 1901, Mudenge writes, the movement had very nearly
turned the whole Tete district into a liberated zone, and Chioko's
dream of recreating the empire of Munhumutapa looked close to real.
It ended in 1902, when Chioko was killed assisting Barwe's own fighters
against the Portuguese. His heir Kamanika never rebuilt the coalition
Chioko had commanded, and with Chioko's death, Mudenge writes, "both
the dream and the reality of the historic Mutapa state came to an
end" - the fifty-second and last ruler in a line that Mudenge's own
count traces back through Matope to Mutota, the hunter who had first
come north for Dande's salt some four and a half centuries before.
