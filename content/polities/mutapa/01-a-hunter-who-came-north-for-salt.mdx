Long before any Portuguese ship reached the coast, the stone-building
state centred on Great Zimbabwe was already declining - worn down, in
Mudenge's account, by some combination of civil war, overpopulation,
falling gold production and a trade route grown harder to navigate.
As it weakened, Karanga hunters, refugees and traders began drifting
north out of its old heartland toward the salt deposits of Dande and
the Zambesi valley, a region already linked by Muslim traders working
inland from the new sultanate of Angoche. Tradition remembers one of
these arrivals as a man of outstanding ability named Mutota, an
elephant-hunter of the Nzou Samanyanga clan who built alliances
through marriage and trade with the Tonga, Tavara and Mbara peoples
already settled there. Mudenge is careful about how much weight this
tradition can bear - the surviving king-lists, he notes, name barely
half the rulers who probably actually reigned before 1490, and stories
told of Mutota may compress the achievements of several forgotten
generations into one - but the pattern the tradition describes, a
Karanga newcomer converting marriage ties and control of Dande's salt
into growing regional authority, is what founded the state the
Portuguese would later call monomotapa's empire.

## A son who inherited by conquest

Mutota's own succession was remembered as contested and strange:
before his death he was said to have required that any son wishing to
rule first commit incest with his daughter Nyamhita, a condition
Mudenge treats as a garbled legend rather than established fact, but
one that in the tradition marks out Matope, alias Nyanhehwe, as the
son ruthless enough to take the throne over a rival brother. Once
established, Matope did what the tradition remembers him for: he went
to war. His armies pushed east to the Rwenya river, subjecting Tavara
and Tonga communities along the way, and extended his family's
influence, through a sister given in marriage to a local power-broker,
into the territory that became the Barwe kingdom - a chain of
conquest and alliance that Mudenge's sources say had reached the
Indian Ocean coast by the time of Matope's death. Neither Mutota's
rise nor Matope's conquests can be pinned to a reliable year; the
documentary record Portuguese factors would start keeping did not
begin for another two generations. But by the time it does begin, the
state it describes - stretching from the interior salt pans Mutota
had first coveted to a coastline his son's armies had never needed to
cross the sea to reach - was already old.
