Barda'a — Partav to the Albanians, the royal seat and the catholicos' see — was
still the largest city in that part of the world three hundred years after there
was anybody left to call it Albanian.

Ibn Hawkal describes it in the tenth century as a league across, by far the
biggest place in the region, built square, with a fortress, standing three
leagues from the Kura on the bank of its tributary the Tharthur. In the Kura
they caught a fish called *shur-mahi*, salt-fish, which was salted and sold on
to every town round about. The district was called al-Andarab, and it was a
day's journey of continuous gardens and orchards in every direction, producing
chestnuts, filberts and figs. They reared silkworms. The Friday mosque had a
roof carried on wooden pillars and walls of burnt brick under stucco, and there
were a great many bathhouses, and in Umayyad times the provincial treasury was
kept in the town.

## The name of the market

And every Sunday there was a market outside the town at the Gate of the Kurds,
and the market ran for a league along the road.

It was called, locally, *al-Kurki*. The word is Greek. It is *kyriakos*, of the
Lord — the ordinary Christian word for Sunday — and it had come through into
local speech so completely that, the geographers say, Sunday itself was known
there as the day of the Kurki.

Set that beside the dates. The Albanian church was dissolved into the Armenian
one in 705. The alphabet stopped being copied and was lost so thoroughly that
nobody could read a letter of it for a thousand years. The last prince was
killed around 822. And two centuries after that, in a country under Muslim
government, the biggest market of the week was named with a Greek Christian word
for the Lord's day, and the day was named after the market.

Nobody planned that. Nobody was defending it. It is what is left when an
institution goes and the habits it created do not.

## And then even that went

Barda'a did not last either. By the time Yaqut was writing in the thirteenth
century it had already fallen into ruin, though Mustawfi a century later still
calls it a town of some size on the Tharthur. The capital of Arran moved south
to Baylakan, of which nothing at all now remains above ground.

Baylakan's end is worth recording for the manner of it. In 1220 the Mongols
came, and finding no stones anywhere in the surrounding plain for their
mangonels, they cut down the plane trees, sawed the trunks into blocks, and shot
those at the walls and the houses. Then they plundered and burned it. The
population came back afterwards and rebuilt, and the place recovered; Timur took
it at the end of the fourteenth century and rebuilt it again, and dug it a canal
six leagues long from the Aras.

Now there is nothing there, and Le Strange notes that not even the ruins appear
on the Russian ordnance map.

So: a kingdom whose script had to be read out from under other writing, whose
church was absorbed, whose capital fell into ruin, and whose second capital was
knocked down with tree trunks and has left no mark on a map. What survived
longest was the name of a market, and it was in the wrong language.
