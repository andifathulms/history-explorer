There were three Christian kingdoms in the Caucasus and most accounts carry two.
Armenia and Georgia have modern states, modern churches and modern national
historiographies to keep them in view. Albania has none of those, and it is the
one this page is about.

The name is a Roman and Greek one and has nothing to do with the Balkans. In
Armenian it is Aluank; in Arabic and Persian, Arran. The country is the eastern
end of the isthmus — the plain between the Kura and the Caucasus mountains, and
the Caspian coast running north to the pass at Derbend — which is roughly
northern Azerbaijan and southern Daghestan today.

A kingdom is attested there from about the second century BC. Its population
was not one people: the classical sources describe a couple of dozen languages,
and the modern descendant of the language its literature was written in is Udi,
still spoken by a few thousand people in two villages. The royal house was a
branch of the Arsacids, like the Armenian one on the record two entries over,
which is a fact about the Parthian empire's habit of putting relatives on
neighbouring thrones rather than about a shared nationality.

Its history for eight centuries is the history of being between: Rome and
Parthia, then Rome and the Sasanians, then Byzantium and the Sasanians, then
the Khazars to the north and the Arabs to the south. Toumanoff's book is about
what made that survivable, and the answer for all three Caucasian countries is
the same: hereditary noble houses holding their own districts with their own
retainers, which an empire could conquer and could not remove.

This site codes `client-levy` on all three records, from the same source, and
it is coded on almost nothing else in this corpus. It is the standard Caucasian
arrangement and it is not the standard Islamic one, which is why the region
exists.
