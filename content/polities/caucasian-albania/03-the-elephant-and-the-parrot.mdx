title: The elephant and the parrot
drafted_from: dasxuranci-bedrosian

Between about 636 and 680 the prince of Albania kept his country in existence by
being personally useful to every power that arrived, one after another, and the
history written by his own church records how it was done.

The Sasanian empire went first, and Juanshir helped it go: he fought for it,
beat a Persian general badly enough that the man sent the prince of Siwnik to
swear a mighty oath and beg for an alliance, and then, reflecting on the decline
of the great Persian kingdom, went home to his own district. What came next was
Byzantium. He wrote to the emperor Constans as *Juanshir, sparapet and prince of
Aghuania, together with his vassal land of the East*, and got back a treaty, the
titles of ex-consul and first patrician, and insignia enough for twelve thousand
men to distribute among his own followers. Summoned to meet the emperor in
Media, he was taken out of the mourning clothes he was wearing for his wife and
dressed in royal robes, and used the moment to ask for a piece of the True
Cross, which he got, and for which he then built a church.

Then the Khazars came over the Kur, and he beat them; and in a later winter the
king of the Huns invaded with many thousands of cavalry and afterwards wanted to
see Juanshir's face. The chronicler dwells on this because of what it meant to
his readers: no king of Persia had ever managed to look upon the king of
Turkestan in person. Juanshir prostrated himself before the Cross and went, with
his retinue, and the king of the Huns came out in a boat to meet him.

The last power to arrive was the caliphate, and here the history is at its most
detailed and least comfortable. Juanshir watched Byzantium rendered helpless,
became greatly worried about his country, and when the caliph sent messengers
with mighty oaths and promises of countless gifts, he prepared magnificent
presents and went south. He was received extremely well. The caliph tested his
intelligence and concluded, the chronicler says, that of all the lords and
leaders of peoples in his service there was none in whom he had such confidence.
Juanshir went a second time, and this time was seated at the caliph's own table
and asked to conduct the peace negotiations with the envoys who had come from
Constantinople — which he did in a way the Byzantines were grateful for
afterwards, because he spent the talks advising the caliph in their favour.

He was sent home with an elephant, a well-trained and gigantic beast out of
India, and with a parrot. He was also offered the principality of Siwnik and
pressed to take Atrpatakan across the river as well, and he refused both: the
history says he preferred to devote his thoughts to helping his own country. The
one thing he did ask for was a reduction in the tribute, which weighed heavily
on his realm, and he got it, and came home.

What finished him was none of the three empires. In about 680, back at Partav
after a summer in the mountains, he was persuaded out into the palace gardens at
the first hour of night by a man of his own household whose father's house he
had loaded with honours and gifts. The guards were at the gate, unconcerned, some
asleep. The prince had a sword and no shield. In the middle of the garden he
heard the clink of armour worn under the other man's robe and understood, and
turned back towards the palace without saying anything, and was cut down from
behind. He got his sword into the assassin's shield and could not pull it out.
He lived several days, long enough to tell his sons what he was leaving each of
them, and died of the wounds.

The chronicler's grief is the grief of a country that knew what it had lost: the
whole land assembled and mourned him, and the mob armed itself, and there was no
small amount of disturbance in Aghuania. There was nobody to inherit the
arrangement, because the arrangement was not an institution. It was one man that
three empires happened to trust at the same time, which is not a constitutional
position and cannot be handed on.
