Caucasian Albania had its own alphabet, of fifty-two letters, made in the fifth
century — the Armenian tradition credits Mesrop Mashtots, who had shortly before
made the Armenian one, working with a local priest. It was made for the same
reason the Armenian and Georgian alphabets were: a newly Christian country needs
scripture in a language its congregation understands.

Then the church was absorbed into the Armenian one in 705, the manuscripts
stopped being copied, and the script was lost completely. Not partially — for
about a thousand years no one could read it and no substantial text in it was
known to exist.

It came back in three stages, and the manner of the recovery is worth setting
out, because it is the evidence and not an anecdote about it.

In 1937 a Georgian scholar, Ilia Abuladze, found a list of alphabets in a
fifteenth-century Armenian manuscript, one of them labelled Albanian. That
established the letterforms and gave nothing to read.

<Figure id="alphabet" />

In 1975 a fire at St Catherine's monastery on Sinai led to the discovery of a
walled-up room containing manuscripts nobody had catalogued. Among them were
Georgian palimpsests — parchment scraped and written over — and in 1996 Zaza
Aleksidze identified the erased under-layer of two of them as Caucasian
Albanian.

Reading it took another decade, an international project and multispectral
imaging, and the result was published in 2008: a lectionary and a Gospel of
John, the oldest and effectively the only Caucasian Albanian texts in existence.

<Figure id="mingachevir" />
They established that the language is ancestral to modern Udi, which is spoken
by a few thousand people in two villages in Azerbaijan and Georgia.

The Albanian alphabet has no descendants. It was adapted for no other language,
inherited by no successor church, and it died with the institution that
commissioned it. That is not the same as saying nobody has looked — scholarship
has now looked very hard indeed, with multispectral cameras, and the answer is
none.

Compare the South Arabian alphabet of Saba', devised for another frontier
kingdom, which is the ancestor of the script Ethiopia writes in this morning.
Two alphabets made for two Christian kingdoms on the edge of two empires. One is
on shop signs. The other had to be read off the parts of a page that somebody
failed to scrape clean.
