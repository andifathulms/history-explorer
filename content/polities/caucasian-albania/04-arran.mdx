The Arabs took the country in the 650s and did not abolish it. The Mihranid
princes went on governing under Arab suzerainty for another century and a half,
paying tribute, keeping their houses and their church, in exactly the
arrangement this corpus records for a dozen frontier principalities from the
Caspian to the Atlas.

Two things ended it, at different times and in different registers, which is why
this record's span closes as a range.

In 705 the Albanian catholicosate was subordinated to the Armenian one under
Arab authority. For a polity whose distinctness rested on its own church and its
own scripture in its own script, that is the decisive act. Whatever the princes
still governed, there was no longer an Albanian Christianity to be a country
about, and the manuscripts stopped.

Around 822 the last Mihranid prince was killed and the principality was not
reconstituted. The country continued, under the Arabic and Persian name Arran,
as a province with a large Christian population that was gradually reclassified
as Armenian, and then as territory this site's Sallarid-successor dynasties
fought over — the Shaddadid record two entries away is about the people who
ended up holding it.

This record has no edges. Its predecessors are not in this corpus and its
successors are the caliphate and the dynasties that came out of it, none of
which inherited a claim from an Albanian prince. Hard rule 7 says a polity with
no edge is complete, and the Caucasus region's own argument is that this is the
normal case here: nothing in the region inherits from anything else in it.

What Albania left is a language spoken in two villages, a body of Armenian-
language historical writing about itself, and fifty-two letters that had to be
recovered from a fire in a monastery on the wrong continent.
