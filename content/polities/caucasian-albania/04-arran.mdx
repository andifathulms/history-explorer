The Arabs took the country in the 650s and did not abolish it. The Mihranid
princes went on governing under Arab suzerainty for another century and a half,
paying tribute and keeping their houses and their church — the same terms a
dozen frontier principalities from the Caspian to the Atlas were living on.

Two things ended it, at different times and in quite different registers.

In 705 the Albanian catholicosate was subordinated to the Armenian one under
Arab authority. For a country whose distinctness rested on having its own
church, with its own scripture in its own script, that is the decisive act.
Whatever the princes still governed, there was no longer an Albanian
Christianity for a country to be about. The manuscripts stopped.

Around 822 the last Mihranid prince was killed and the principality was not
reconstituted.

## What continued instead

The country went on. Under the Arabic and Persian name Arran it was a province
with a large Christian population, which was gradually reclassified as Armenian
because after 705 there was no other category available for it, and it was
fought over by whichever dynasty was strongest in the region — eventually the
Shaddadids, who were Kurds and had nothing to do with any of this.

Nobody inherited a claim from an Albanian prince. There was no Albanian
successor state, no exile dynasty, no restoration in the ninth century of the
kind Armenia and Georgia both had. The two neighbours lost their crowns earlier
and got them back; Albania lost its church, which was the thing that could not
be got back.

What it left is a language spoken today in two villages, a body of
Armenian-language historical writing about itself, and fifty-two letters that
had to be recovered from a fire in a monastery on the wrong continent.
