The Aghlabid emirate was destroyed in a season, and the reasons it could be
destroyed in a season had been accumulating for thirty years.

The dynasty's whole arrangement rested on three things: the caliph's diploma, the
loyalty of the Arab military class in Ifriqiya, and the acquiescence of the
Maliki scholars of Kairouan. By the 890s it was losing the second and had never
really had the third.

## The jund and the jurists

The Arab troops — the *jund*, descendants of the conquest armies, settled around
Kairouan and Tunis — had been in periodic revolt for the whole life of the
dynasty. Emirs had put down risings in the 820s, the 890s and repeatedly in
between, and the standard remedy was to buy black slave soldiers as a
counterweight, which produced an army loyal to the emir and resented by everybody
else.

The jurists were worse, because they could not be bought and their objection was
public. Kairouan's Maliki establishment held that the Aghlabids drank, taxed
unlawfully, lived in palaces outside the city and behaved like kings, and said
so. Sahnun, the great Maliki jurist of the ninth century, was appointed qadi and
used the office against the court. That quarrel ran for a century and it left the
dynasty with no religious cover at all.

## Ibrahim II

The reign that broke it was Ibrahim II's, from 875, and by every account it was a
reign of terror.

He killed members of his own family, executed officials in numbers, and was
eventually the subject of a complaint to Baghdad — the caliph ordered him to
abdicate, which he did in 902, handing over to his son and going off to fight in
Sicily, where he died besieging Cosenza.

A dynasty whose ruler has to be removed by written order from a caliph two
thousand miles away has stopped being an autonomous power and become an
embarrassment that its patron cannot easily replace.

## What was in the mountains

While that was happening, an Isma'ili missionary had been living among the Kutama
Berbers of the Lesser Kabylie since 893.

The Kutama were inside Aghlabid territory in the sense that a line on a map went
round them, and outside Aghlabid control in every sense that mattered. They had
the ordinary grievances of mountain people about taxation and about lowland
government, and Abu Abdallah al-Shi'i spent fifteen years turning those
grievances into an army with a doctrine.

The last emir, Ziyadat Allah III, took the throne in 903 by murdering his father,
his brothers and his uncles. Six years later he was on a ship to Egypt.
