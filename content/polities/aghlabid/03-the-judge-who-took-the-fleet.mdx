The man who commanded the invasion of Sicily in 827 was the chief judge of
Kairouan, and he was appointed to the command by the emir who had appointed him
to the bench.

Ibn Khallikan's notice of him is short, because Asad ibn al-Furat mattered to a
thirteenth-century compiler as a jurist rather than as a general, and the
military career is a footnote to the legal one. That is itself the most useful
thing about the entry.

## Who he was

His family came from Khurasan and he was born at Harran in northern
Mesopotamia. He came into Ifriqiya with a body of troops the caliph sent west,
spent five years at Kairouan and nine at Tunis, and had the Quran by heart at
eighteen. Then he went
east to study: to Medina, where he heard Malik himself teach; to Iraq, where he
sat with the principal disciples of Abu Hanifa; and to Egypt, where he attached
himself to Ibn al-Qasim and collected the legal questions and answers that became
the *Asadiya*, which he carried back to Kairouan in 797.

De Slane preserves the joke Asad used to make about his own name, which is the
only sentence in the entry that sounds like a person:

> I am Asad — the lion — and the lion is the noblest of animals; my father was
> called Furat, and the Euphrates is the purest of waters; and my grandfather's
> name was Sinan — the spear — which is the best of weapons.

In 817 the emir Ziyadat Allah made him qadi of Kairouan. Ten years later the same
emir gave him the command of the army that was to be sent against Sicily, and did
not relieve him of the judgeship.

## The expedition

The figures are precise and worth having, because the campaign that began the
two-and-a-half-century Muslim history of Sicily was not a large one.

> In the month of the first Rabi, A.H. 212 — June 827 — he sailed for that
> island with nine thousand one hundred foot and nine hundred horse.

Ten thousand men. He won a number of engagements, and died of his wounds the
following year while besieging Syracuse.

The conquest of the island took another seventy-five years and was never
completed by the men who began it. What the Aghlabids had done was find something
for an army to do that was neither at home nor in the caliph's service, and hand
it to a judge whose authority over the Arab troops came from a source the troops
could not easily quarrel with.

## Why a jurist

Because of what the Aghlabid emirate was: a family governing Ifriqiya on the
caliph's behalf, holding an Arab military class it could not trust and a Berber
population it had not assimilated, and needing every scrap of legitimacy it could
find.

An emir who sends his chief judge to command a holy war against Byzantium is
making a statement about what the war is. Asad's own standing came from having
studied under Malik at Medina and Ibn al-Qasim in Egypt, and having brought the
law back with him in writing; he was not a client of the dynasty and could not be
described as one. Putting him at the head of the fleet made the expedition the
community's rather than the emir's.

It also got the *jund* out of the country, which Aghlabid policy required for a
century.

## What he left

Not Sicily. Asad was dead within eighteen months of sailing, and the island was
taken slowly by other men.

What he left was the *Asadiya*: the questions he had put to Ibn al-Qasim in
Egypt, with the answers, written down without much order. Another Kairouan
jurist rearranged them under headings, expanded them and resolved some of them
against the Traditions he had by heart, and the result is the book the Maliki law
of the entire Maghreb has rested on ever since.

That man's name was Sahnun, and Ibn Khallikan has a separate entry for him.
