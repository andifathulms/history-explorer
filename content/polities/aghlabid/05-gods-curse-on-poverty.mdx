The most durable thing the Aghlabid century produced was not a mosque or a
province. It was a book of law, written down at Kairouan by a judge whose father
had come west as a soldier, and it is still the authority for the Maliki school
across North and West Africa.

Ibn Khallikan gives Sahnun a short entry, of the kind he gives to jurists whose
importance he takes for granted. It contains one line of complaint that is worth
more than the rest of it put together.

## The complaint

Sahnun studied under Ibn al-Qasim, Ibn Wahb and Ashhab — Malik's Egyptian
disciples — and afterwards became, in Ibn Khallikan's phrase, the head of the
science in the Maghrib. What he said about the arrangement was this:

> God's curse on poverty! I was a contemporary of Malik, but, having no means of
> going to see him, I was obliged to take lessons from Ibn al-Qasim.

He was born in 776 or 777, in Malik's lifetime, and he could not afford the
journey to Medina. The whole of Maliki law in the west therefore reached it at
one remove, through men who had heard the master and could be travelled to
instead — and a jurist at the far end of the chain knew exactly what that cost
him and said so, in a sentence somebody thought worth writing down.

## The book

Asad ibn al-Furat had brought back from Egypt a set of questions he had put to
Ibn al-Qasim, with the answers, in no particular order. It was called the
*Asadiya*, and Sahnun had written it out from Asad's dictation.

He then rebuilt it. He arranged the questions under separate headings, increased
their number, and resolved some of them against the Traditions he had by heart
from Ibn Wahb's recension of Malik's *Muwatta*. The result is the *Mudawwana* —
the digest — and Ibn Khallikan describes it plainly as *the main authority relied
on by the people of Kairouan*.

He left some points unfinished. The book went out anyway.

## What a law school is for

Sahnun was qadi of Kairouan and had, Ibn Khallikan says, a greater number of
pupils than any other of Malik's disciples, and it was by his means that the
doctrines of that imam were propagated throughout the Maghrib. He died in
November or December 854.

That is the sentence the Aghlabid record turns on, and the dynasty barely appears
in it. What the emirate had done was pay for a capital: a congregational mosque
with a minaret, cisterns to keep a waterless plain supplied, and a court that
employed judges of the first rank and left them alone in the matters that were
theirs. What grew on that was a school — a physical place where a specific body
of law was taught to a specific body of students who then went out and applied
it.

By the time the Fatimids took Kairouan in 909 the Maliki jurists of the city were
the most cohesive institution in Ifriqiya and the most serious obstacle to an
Isma'ili government, and they outlasted it. The Aghlabids ruled for a hundred and
nine years. The rite their capital exported has governed marriage, inheritance
and contract from Morocco to the Niger for eleven hundred.

## The bird

Ibn Khallikan ends, as he often does, with the name.

*Sahnun* was not a family name. It is the name of a bird found in the Maghrib and
remarkable, he says, for its sagacity, and the judge was called after it. He then
notes that whether the word is pronounced with an *a* or a *u* raises a question
of Arabic grammatical form that would take too long to expose here, and that it
has in any case been properly treated by somebody else.

The man who codified the law of half a continent is remembered under the nickname
of a clever bird, and his biographer's last word on him is a footnote about
vowels.
