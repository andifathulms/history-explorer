The Aghlabids sent an annual tribute to Baghdad and kept the rest, and what
they did with the rest is standing in Tunisia.

Kairouan had been founded as a garrison town during the conquests — one of the
*amsar*, planted to hold a province rather than to be a city. Under the Aghlabids it became the capital of the western
Islamic world in the sense that mattered: a place with a court, a legal school,
a scholarly establishment and buildings that made an argument.

The Great Mosque is the argument. Ziyadat Allah I rebuilt it almost entirely in
836, and the building standing today is substantially his: a vast hypostyle
prayer hall on columns quarried out of Roman and Byzantine ruins across
Ifriqiya, a courtyard, and a square minaret in three receding tiers. That
minaret is the oldest surviving tower of its kind anywhere in the Islamic world,
and it is the prototype for the square minarets of the whole Maghreb and
al-Andalus — the form that becomes the Giralda at Seville and
the Koutoubia at Marrakesh three centuries later.

<Figure id="minaret" />

The other Aghlabid building programme is less famous and more revealing. They
built water: a system of great open cisterns outside Kairouan, the largest more
than a hundred and twenty metres across, fed by an aqueduct from the hills and
settling silt in a smaller basin before it reached the main one. A garrison town
on a plain with no river needed that to be a capital at all, and a dynasty that
builds reservoirs is a dynasty that expects to be there in fifty years.

<Figure id="basins" />

They also built the *ribats* — fortified coastal monasteries at Sousse and
Monastir where volunteers garrisoned the shore against Byzantine raiding and
spent the rest of the time in religious exercise. The institution is the frontier equivalent of the Sicily
campaign: a way of directing armed and pious men outward.

<Figure id="sousse" />

That is what a hundred years of a rich province's surplus buys when it is spent
at home instead of being sent east: the oldest minaret in Islam, and a water
system that let a garrison town on a waterless plain become the capital of the
western Islamic world.
