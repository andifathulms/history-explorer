---
title: Seventy-five years for one island
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987
phase: expansion
---

The invasion of Byzantine Sicily began in 827 with a fleet carrying perhaps ten
thousand men, and the island was not fully taken until Taormina fell in 902.
Seventy-five years is a long campaign, and the length is the interesting part:
Byzantine Sicily was defended, fortified and reinforceable by sea, and the
Aghlabids had to build a naval capacity from a province that had not had one.

They built it partly out of necessity. The emirate's Arab military elite was
factious and repeatedly in revolt, and the ruling family found that a permanent
external campaign was the most reliable way to keep armed men occupied and paid
from plunder rather than from taxing Ifriqiya harder. The *jihad* against
Byzantium was doctrinally respectable and politically useful at the same time.

Muslim Sicily lasted two and a half centuries and its inheritance survived the
Norman conquest that ended it. The Norman kings of Sicily kept the Arabic
administrative vocabulary, the tax registers, the irrigation and crops, and for a
time an Arabic-Latin-Greek trilingual chancery; Roger II commissioned al-Idrisi's
geography at Palermo in 1154.

The conquest took seventy-five years and produced a Muslim Sicily that lasted
two and a half centuries, and then a Norman Sicily that kept its administration,
its language and its scholars — which is how an emirate governed from Kairouan
ended up shaping a Latin kingdom whose chancery worked in Arabic.
