Around 1510 Leo Africanus came to Kairouan and found a town of tanners.

He gives its history first, and the Aghlabids are in it, under the name Leo's
sources used. After the Umayyad governors, he says, *one Elagleb was appointed
lieutenant, who gouerned not the towne as a Califa*, and after his death his son
succeeded, *and the gouernment remained vnto his posterie for an hundred
threescore and ten yeeres, till such time as they were depriued thereof by one
Mahdi Califa*.

He also records what they did with it. Under Aghlab's governorship the city grew
so much in inhabitants and buildings that a second town, Raqqada, was built
beside it for the prince and his nobles. And *in his time also the Isle of
Sicilia was woonne*: a captain named Halqama was sent, who built a town there
instead of a fort and called it after himself —

> which name is vsed by the Sicilians euen till this present.

Alcamo, in western Sicily, is still called that. It is the only thing the
Aghlabids named that anybody still says out loud, and Leo noticed.

## The city he actually saw

Kairouan stands on a sandy, treeless plain that grows nothing. Its corn came
from Sousse, Monastir and Mahdia, all within forty miles. Its water came from
springs on a mountain twelve miles off, running down to the town; without them
there was nothing but rainwater in cisterns, which lasted, Leo says, only until
the beginning of June.

Every one of those facts is why the Aghlabid cisterns were built. A capital on
ground with no water and no crops is a decision rather than a site, and it stayed
a capital only for as long as somebody was willing to pay to supply it.

## What it had been

Leo knew, and puts it in two sentences.

> In this citie for certaine yeeres the studie of the Mahumetan lawe mightily
> flourished, so that heere were the most famous lawyers in all Africa. It was at
> length destroied, and replanted againe with newe inhabitants, but it coulde
> neuer attaine vnto the former estate.

The destruction he means is 1057, when the Banu Hilal sacked it and it was
abandoned, a hundred and fifty years after the Aghlabids and fifty after the
Fatimids had left for Cairo. Kairouan had been the capital of the western Islamic
world for four centuries and the seat of the Maliki school for most of that, and
it never got any of it back.

## And what was there instead

> At this present it is inhabited by none but leather-dressers, who sende their
> leather vnto the cities of Numidia, and exchange it also for cloth of Europe.
> Howbeit they are so continually oppressed by the king of Tunis, that now they
> are brought vnto extreme miserie.

That is the last line of the Aghlabid inheritance, six hundred years after the
dynasty ended: a tanners' town on a plain with no water, exporting hides to the
Sahara, taxed to ruin by a king in the city that had replaced it.

The Great Mosque was still standing when Leo passed through, as it is now — the
oldest minaret in Islam, on a site with no water, in a town of tanners. He does
not mention it.
