Lamu drew its own lesson from surviving the Mazaru'i attack the 1807
succession crisis had provoked: that it could not count on surviving
a second one alone. It appealed directly to Imam Sa'id for protection,
and in 1811 Sa'id answered by sending a governor of his own to Lamu
and building a fort there - an Omani foothold planted in Pate's own
archipelago, a few days' sail from the island whose throne the
Mazaru'i had so recently filled by force.

Pate, still carrying whatever Mazaru'i garrison had been left behind
from Ahmed's installation, was now caught between two powers rather
than one. In 1822 Sa'id ordered the Mazaru'i garrison withdrawn from
Pate outright. Pate refused. Sa'id answered with a fleet under his
general Hamad, which took Pate, and with it the dependent towns of
Siu and Faza, by force.

## The end of ruling itself

Nothing in the source describes a battle of any length, a siege, or
resistance that came close to matching what Mombasa itself would put
up against the same Sa'id six years later. Pate simply fell, along
with the towns that had once been counted among its own dependencies
in Fumo Bakari's day. The sultanate that had broken from Oman in 1746
to govern the Juba-to-Kilifi coast on its own terms ended, seventy-six
years later, ruled from Zanzibar rather than from Pate - one more
piece of the same coastal network that Sa'id, within Pate's own
lifetime as an independent state, had already begun assembling from
Mombasa to Lamu.
