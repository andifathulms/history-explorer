Pate did not confine its ambitions to squabbling with Mombasa over
Pemba. It sent its own expedition north to Barawa to press old claims
on the Juba river - the same stretch of coast its sultans had counted
as a dependency since Fumo Bakari's day - asserting a reach of its own
even while its southern quarrel with the Mazaru'i sat unresolved.

That assertiveness cost it, before long, at home. Internal troubles
broke out within Pate itself, and the Mazaru'i, watching from
Mombasa, saw their opening. They landed an expedition on the island
and forced Pate to accept a governor of their own choosing - a
direct Mazaru'i hand on Pate's own throne, the closest either side had
come to actually ruling the other rather than merely raiding it. It
did not last. The governor was ejected in his turn, and Pate returned
to governing itself.

## A recognition that cost nothing

By 1776 the two neighbours had settled into something less costly
than continuous war. Pate agreed that year to recognise the Imam of
Oman's rule - the same Imam whose authority it had rejected thirty
years before - but the recognition arrived without a garrison, without
tribute and without a governor to enforce it. Pate's own sultans went
on ruling precisely as they had while independent; what had changed
was only that Oman now had, on paper, a claim it could point to
whenever it next found the coast worth the trouble of enforcing.
