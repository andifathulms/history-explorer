Sultan Fumomadi's death in 1807 opened the succession Pate could least
afford: a divided one, at the exact moment its most powerful neighbour
still had a governor and an army within reach of the island. Fumoluti
was the candidate a party in Lamu favoured; Ahmed was the candidate
Mombasa's Mazaru'i governor favoured, and the Mazaru'i did not wait to
see which side would win on its own. They marched troops to Pate,
installed Ahmed on the throne themselves, and carried Fumoluti off to
Mombasa as a captive rather than a rival - not exile at a safe
distance, but removal to the one place his enemies could watch him
indefinitely.

Fumoluti's own partisans read the outcome correctly and did not wait
for a second round. They emigrated to Lamu rather than accept a throne
settled by Mazaru'i soldiers, swelling a faction there that Mombasa
now had every reason to distrust. When the Mazaru'i followed up with
an attack on Lamu itself, hoping to finish what installing Ahmed had
started, they were repulsed - the first clear check to Mombasa's
interference in Pate's own succession, and proof that the crisis of
1807 had not, in fact, settled anything at all.

## A throne, but not a free hand

Ahmed reigned afterward as Pate's sultan, but the manner of his
installation was itself the damage. A ruler placed on the throne by a
foreign garrison's soldiers, over a rival that same garrison had
personally carried off, governed with Mombasa's fingerprints on the
succession whether he intended to or not. Pate had broken from Oman in
1746 precisely to rule itself; sixty-one years later, it was Mazaru'i
troops from the very neighbour it had once gone to war with over
Pemba who decided who would sit on its throne.
