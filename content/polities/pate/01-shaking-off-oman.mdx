By the time the Imam of Oman first began installing garrisons on the
Swahili coast, Pate was already the kind of sultanate that measured
its reach in dependencies rather than in the walls of a single town.
Under Sultan Fumo Bakari, whose father Bwana Tamu had ruled with the
Imam's own favour, Pate counted among its possessions Lamu, Manda,
Pemba, and the mainland coast running from the Juba river all the way
south to Kilifi - a stretch of the coast longer than the distance
between Mombasa and Zanzibar.

That reach did not survive contact with an Omani garrison next door
for long. In 1739 the Imam placed a clan of Omani settlers, the
Mazaru'i, in the fort at Mombasa as his own custodians, and within a
few years Mombasa's custodians had made themselves independent rulers
in their own right. Pate followed the same course in 1746, the very
year the Mazaru'i governor Ali bin Osman declared himself free of the
Imam: it withdrew its own allegiance to Oman and went on governing its
dependencies exactly as before.

## A quarrel over Pemba's taxes

Independence from Oman did not mean peace with Mombasa. Pemba Island,
claimed by both, produced a tax revenue neither side was willing to
share evenly, and the dispute broke into open war: Pate seized
Kilindini and besieged the Mazaru'i position there before the two
sides worked out an understanding and Pate withdrew. Nothing was
settled permanently by the fighting - Pemba's revenue remained
contested for decades afterward - but Pate had shown, within months of
breaking from Oman, that it intended to hold its own claims against
its nearest and most dangerous neighbour as readily as against the
Imam who had lost it.
