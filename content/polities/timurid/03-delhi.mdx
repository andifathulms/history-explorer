title: Delhi, as the conqueror's own books tell it

The Indian campaign of 1398 is unusual among Timur's wars in that two accounts
of it survive from inside his own house, and they can be read against each
other. The *Malfuzat-i Timuri* is written in the first person and presents
itself as his memoirs; Sharaf al-Din Yazdi's *Zafarnama* narrates the same weeks
in the third. Whether the memoirs are genuinely his, or a Persian construction
worked up to agree with the *Zafarnama*, has been argued over since the
eighteenth century and is not settled. What can be said is that both books were
made inside the dynasty for the dynasty, and that the passages below are
therefore not accusations. They are the version the Timurids wanted kept.

Three days before the battle for Delhi, the army was camped east of Loni. Amir
Jahan Shah and Amir Sulayman Shah and the other senior commanders came and
raised a problem: since crossing the Indus the army had taken something over a
hundred thousand Hindu prisoners, and they were all in the camp. The day before,
when the Delhi forces had come out to probe, the prisoners had made signs of
rejoicing and cursed their captors, and it was thought that on the day of the
real battle they would break their bonds, plunder the tents and go over. The
commanders' advice was that on such a day a hundred thousand men could not be
left with the baggage, and that setting idolaters free was contrary to the rules
of war. The first-person account records the conclusion without any distance at
all: *When I heard these words I found them in accordance with the rules of
war*, and the order went through the camp that every man holding prisoners was
to kill them, and that anyone who failed to do so would be executed himself and
his property given to whoever informed on him.

Both books then record the same detail, and it is the one that survives reading.
Maulana Nasir al-Din Umar was a scholar and a counsellor who in his whole life
had never killed a sparrow. He had fifteen Hindus in his train, and in execution
of the order he killed all fifteen with his own sword.

Then one man in ten was told off to guard the property and the cattle and the
captives' wives and children, and at the midday prayer the army marched for the
Jumna.

The sack of the city three days later is narrated in a completely different
register, and the difference is the point. The Delhi massacre had a plain
military rationale and is reported plainly. What happened inside the walls had
none, so the first-person account opens with a list of reasons why armed men
happened to be in the city at all — a party of soldiers idling at a gate who
began helping themselves and the amirs sent in to restrain them; ladies of the
harem who had asked to see the thousand-column palace, and their escort; the
officials gone in to collect the levy; a thousand troopers gone in for grain,
oil, sugar and flour; and regiments sent in to seize the Hindus who had taken
refuge there with their families and goods, with orders to ignore any protest.
Six separate innocent errands, and each of them put soldiers inside the walls.

What follows is not softened. The refugees drew their swords rather than be
taken; the fires spread from Jahanpanah and Siri to Old Delhi; fifteen thousand
Turks spent Thursday and all Thursday night killing and plundering, and on
Friday morning the whole army went in and was no longer under anybody's control.
Saturday was the same. Every man got fifty to a hundred captives and none got
fewer than twenty. Only the quarter of the sayyids and the ulama was spared. On
Sunday a body of Hindus who had fortified themselves in the congregational
mosque of Old Delhi were cleared out of it and killed, and Old Delhi was
plundered too.

And then the sentence the whole preamble was built for: *By the will of God, and
by no wish or direction of mine, all the three cities of Delhi had been
plundered.* The sermon proclaiming his sovereignty had been read in the city,
which is an assurance of protection; it was therefore his earnest wish that no
evil should come to the people; but God had ordained that the city be ruined and
had inspired its inhabitants with a spirit of resistance, so that they brought
upon themselves a fate that was inevitable.

One order, before the plunder was distributed, is worth more than the whole
apology. The artisans were to be picked out of the prisoners and set aside; some
thousands were, and were parcelled out among the princes and amirs. But the
builders and the stonemasons were reserved to the sovereign personally, because
he had determined to build a congregational mosque at Samarkand which should
have no rival in any country.

That mosque is Bibi Khanum, and the men who built it were taken out of Delhi in
the same week as the elephants.
