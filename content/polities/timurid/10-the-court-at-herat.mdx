title: Babur's inventory of Herat

Sultan Husayn Bayqara died on the march against Shaibani Khan on 11 Dhu'l-Hijja
911 — 5 May 1506 — which is worth stating precisely, because a date of January
1501 for his death is in wide circulation and is five years out against the
contemporary who wrote the following. When he died, Babur — who was twenty-three, held
Kabul, and would spend the rest of his life elsewhere — stopped his memoirs and
wrote out the whole of the Herat court from memory and report: the ruler, his
sons, his wives, his begs, his scholars, his poets, his painters, his musicians,
each with a sentence or two of assessment. It runs for dozens of pages and it is
the best single description of a Persianate court that anybody wrote, partly
because Babur admired it and partly because he was incapable of flattering
anyone.

He begins with the body. Husayn was slant-eyed and lion-bodied, slender from the
waist down; old and white-bearded he still wore silks of fine red and green, and
a black lambskin cap or a felt *qalpaq*, except on a feast day, when he would
wind a little three-fold turban broad and badly, stick a heron's plume in it and
go to prayers in that.

Then the record, which Babur gives without arranging it to flatter. On taking
Herat he thought of having the Twelve Imams named in the Friday sermon, and Ali
Sher Beg and others talked him out of it; after that everything important was
done by orthodox law. He could not perform the prostrations because of trouble
in his joints and he kept no fasts. He was lively and pleasant, rather
immoderate in temper, with a tongue to match it. He once surrendered a son of
his own to the avengers of blood and had him taken to the court of judgment. He
was abstinent for six or seven years after taking the throne and then, in
Babur's phrase, degraded himself to drink: in nearly forty years of rule there
may not have been a single day on which he did not drink after the midday
prayer, and not one on which he drank before it. He was personally brave —
Babur, who knew what he was talking about, says no man of Timur's line has been
known to match him at slashing with a sword. He put a divan together in Turki
under the pen-name Husayni; many of the couplets are not bad, though the whole
thing is in one metre. And, says Babur, great ruler though he was by the length
of his reign and the breadth of his lands, he yet kept fighting-rams, flew
pigeons and fought cocks, like little people.

The court is catalogued the same way. Sultan Ali of Mashhad, the finest of the
calligraphers, copied thirty couplets a day for the Mirza and twenty for Ali
Sher. Of the painters, Bihzad's work was very dainty, but he did not draw
beardless faces well and used to lengthen the double chin too far — bearded
faces he drew admirably. Shah Muzaffar painted portraits and rendered hair
beautifully, and died young, on his way up to fame. Khwaja Abdullah played the
dulcimer better than anyone. Qul-i Muhammad the lutenist added three strings to
the guitar. And there is Shaykhi the flautist, who had played since he was
twelve, who produced an air at one of the prince's assemblies that Qul-i
Muhammad could not reproduce on the guitar — so that Qul-i Muhammad pronounced
the guitar a worthless instrument, whereupon Shaykhi took it out of his hands
and played the air on it, well and in tune. He could hear a tune once and say
whose flute it came from.

This is the summit of the dynasty and the state underneath it was a province. It
could not recover Transoxiana, could not hold Khurasan against the Uzbeks and
did not survive its ruler by two years. Babur's own explanation for writing the
inventory at such length is not stated, but the shape of it says what he thought:
he was a Timurid recording what a Timurid court had been, at the moment it
stopped existing, for the benefit of a reader who would never see one.
