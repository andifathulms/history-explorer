Timur's empire fragmented among his descendants, and the fragments produced a
century of court culture that outweighed the conquests in everything except body
count.

Shahrukh ruled from Herat and his wife Gawhar Shad endowed it with a mosque and
madrasa complex; his son Ulugh Beg ruled Samarkand, built an observatory with a
sextant of forty metres' radius, and produced star tables more accurate than
anything in Europe until Tycho Brahe. The Herat manuscript workshops under Sultan
Husayn Bayqara and his vizier Ali Shir Nava'i employed Bihzad, the painter whose
work set the standard for Persian miniature for two centuries.

Nava'i also did something with consequences beyond art: he wrote major poetry in
Chagatai Turkish and argued explicitly that it was capable of everything Persian
could do, which established a literary Turkic tradition where courts had
previously written Persian as a matter of course.

The Ottomans, Safavids and Mughals all took this court style as their reference.
Babur, founding the Mughal empire, wrote his memoirs in Chagatai and modelled his
gardens on Timurid ones; the Safavid and Ottoman painting traditions run from
Herat.

Ulugh Beg was murdered by his own son in 1449. The Uzbeks took Samarkand and then
Herat by 1507. What survived was a set of aesthetic and literary conventions
carried by three empires that had each defeated the family that invented them.
