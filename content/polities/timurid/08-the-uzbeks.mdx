After 1449 the eastern half never reassembled. Abu Sa'id held much of Iran for
two decades and was captured and executed by Uzun Hasan of the Ak Koyunlu in
1469, which is where Timurid power west of Khurasan ends. What was left was
Herat under Sultan Husayn Bayqara, and that is the subject of the previous
chapter.

The end came from the north and from relatives. Muhammad Shaybani Khan led the
Uzbeks — Turco-Mongol tribes out of the Qipchaq steppe under a Chinggisid line,
which is to say people with exactly the descent Timur had lacked. He took
Samarkand in 1500 and Herat in 1507, and the Timurid line in Central Asia
finished.

One Timurid got away. Babur, a great-great-great-grandson of Timur, lost
Samarkand to Shaybani twice, gave up on Central Asia, took Kabul, and in 1526
took northern India. The dynasty he founded is the one Europe calls the Mughals,
and it did not call itself that. It called itself Gurkani, after Timur's own
title — the son-in-law, the word Timur had used because he could not call
himself khan.

A man who could not hold Ferghana carried his great-great-great-grandfather's
apology for not being a Chinggisid two thousand kilometres south and made it the
name of an empire.

Shaybani Khan was killed by Shah Isma'il of the Safavids in 1510 near Merv, and
his skull was made into a drinking cup. Babur, briefly allied with Isma'il, held
Samarkand for eight months in 1511 as a Safavid client and lost it again, which
is the last time a Timurid ruled Timur's capital.

What the empire left was not territory. It was a court style — the architecture,
the manuscript, the garden, the Chagatai verse — carried forward by three
empires that had each defeated the family that made it.
