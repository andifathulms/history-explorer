Ulugh Beg governed Samarkand for his father for nearly forty years and was an
astronomer. This is not a hobby recorded politely by later admirers: he built an
observatory on a hill outside the city with a meridian arc of about forty metres'
radius, sunk into a trench so that the instrument could be large enough to be
accurate,

<Figure id="ulugh-beg-sextant" /> and he assembled a working staff of mathematicians around it —
al-Kashi, who computed pi to sixteen decimal places and wrote on decimal
fractions, and Qadi Zada, and after them Ali Qushji.

The output was the *Zij-i Sultani*, a star catalogue of more than a thousand
stars observed rather than copied from Ptolemy, and tables of planetary motion.
The positions were the most accurate produced anywhere until Tycho Brahe a
century and a half later, and the value it gives for the length of the sidereal
year is within about a minute of the modern figure. The catalogue reached Europe
in the seventeenth century and was printed at Oxford.

He succeeded Shahrukh in 1447 and was a bad ruler, which the sources agree on
and which the succession made worse: he could not hold the Timurid territories
together, lost to his nephews, and in 1449 was deposed by his own son Abd
al-Latif and beheaded on the road while ostensibly leaving for the pilgrimage.
The son was killed six months later.

The observatory was abandoned and eventually demolished; the trench containing
the arc was rediscovered in 1908.

<Figure id="ulugh-beg-madrasa" /> Ali Qushji left for the Ak Koyunlu court and
then for Istanbul, where he entered Mehmed II's service — one of the several
threads by which Samarkand's learning reached the Ottoman and Safavid and Mughal
courts.

Ulugh Beg's forty years and his father's are the summit of the dynasty, and they
have to be read together. The decades in which the Timurid state produced the
most were the same decades in which it stopped being able to defend its own
succession.
