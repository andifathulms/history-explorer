---
title: The man who could not be khan
drafted_from: manz-1989
phase: formation
---

Timur was born into a minor Turkicised Mongol clan in the western Chagatai lands
and was not a descendant of Chinggis Khan, which under the political rules of
post-Mongol Central Asia meant he could never legitimately hold the title of
khan. He never claimed it. He ruled for thirty-five years as *amir*, married a
Chinggisid princess to style himself *gurkan* — royal son-in-law — and kept a
puppet khan of the correct descent at his court for the whole period.

That constraint is the most revealing thing about him, and Manz's point: an
empire assembled by a man of extraordinary capacity who was structurally barred
from the only legitimacy his world recognised, and who therefore built his
following out of personal loyalty, plunder and an unbroken run of victories
rather than out of an office.

The campaigns went everywhere: Iran, the Caucasus, the Golden Horde (whose
capital he sacked twice, damaging the northern trade routes permanently and
indirectly benefiting Moscow), Delhi in 1398, Damascus and Baghdad, and the
Ottomans at Ankara in 1402. He was preparing to invade Ming China when he died in
1405.

Almost none of it held. The empire fractured among his descendants within a
generation, and its lasting product was not territorial. The Timurid courts at
Herat and Samarkand — Ulugh Beg's observatory, the painting workshops, the
architecture — set the cultural standard that the Ottomans, Safavids and Mughals
all worked from. Babur, who founded the Mughal empire, was his descendant.
