title: What the Castilians saw

In the autumn of 1404 an embassy from Henry III of Castile reached Samarkand,
and one of the ambassadors kept a journal. Clavijo is the only Western
eyewitness of Timur's court, he arrived at the exact top of it, and he had no
literary ambitions whatever — which is why the account is useful. He writes down
what he was shown, what it cost and how long he was kept waiting.

He arrived into a festival. Timur had ordered every trade in the city —
merchants, cooks, butchers, bakers, shoemakers, all of them — to come out of
Samarkand, pitch their tents on the plain where the horde was camped, sell their
goods there instead of in the city, and each trade to perform a game and walk it
through the camp for the amusement of the army. So they did: the skinners and
butchers came dressed as lions and foxes, there were masquerades on a
carpeted ground, two of Timur's grandsons were married, and there was wine and
fruit for everybody.

On the same ground he had a great number of gallows set up, and announced that
in this festival he knew how to be merciful and kind to some and severe to
others.

The first was the chief magistrate of Samarkand, the greatest officer in the
whole country, whom Timur had left in charge of the city when he departed six
years and eleven months earlier. He had neglected his duties. He was hanged and
his goods confiscated, and Clavijo notes that the punishment of so great a man
caused terror among the people. A councillor who interceded for him was offered
his life for four hundred thousand bezants of silver; he handed over everything
he had, was tortured for more, had no more, and was hung up by the feet until he
died. Another man had been left with three thousand horses and could not produce
all of them; he was hanged, although he offered to produce six thousand if given
time. Traders who had sold meat above its worth were executed and others fined.
Clavijo records the convention without comment: a great man is hanged, the
meaner sort are beheaded.

That is a working description of what the empire ran on. There is no
institution in it. There is a man who went away for seven years, came back, and
audited his officials personally, and whose audit had one penalty.

The other thing Clavijo watched was the building. Timur had put up a mosque in
honour of his wife's mother, and it was the most respected in the city; when it
was finished he decided the entrance was too low, and had the whole thing pulled
down. To get it rebuilt faster he took charge of one part of the work himself
and set his mirzas over the other. By then he could not walk or ride and went
about in a litter, and he came out to the site every day and stayed a while. The
work stopped when the snow began to fall.

The ambassadors, meanwhile, could not get an audience. On the first of November
they went as ordered and waited from morning until noon, and were told he could
not speak to them that day because he was occupied with his grandson, the king
of India, who was going home. On the Saturday he did not come out of his tent,
being ill; they waited until noon and were sent away. On the Sunday they waited
a long time and were told to go back to their lodgings, because the lord would
not see them.

He was seventy, he was building a mosque he had already built once, and he was
four months from setting out to invade China. The Castilians went home without
their answer.
