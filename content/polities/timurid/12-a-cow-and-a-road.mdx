The empire ended in 1507. One Timurid got away, and he wrote down what it was
like — which is why the Timurid afterlife is the only one in this period that
can be read in the first person.

## The boy with a province

*In the month of Ramadan of the year 899, and in the twelfth year of my age, I
became ruler in the country of Farghana.*

That is how the *Baburnama* opens. Babur was a great-great-great-grandson of
Timur on his father's side and a descendant of Chinggis Khan on his mother's,
which by the arithmetic of that world made him one of the best-connected
landless men in Asia. He took Samarkand twice and lost it twice, lost Ferghana,
and spent his early twenties moving between mountain valleys with a few hundred
men.

## What that was actually like

The book does not dramatise it, which is why it lands.

At Dikh-kat, in the hills, he records that he constantly made excursions among
the mountains round about, and generally went barefoot, and from doing this so
much his feet became so that rock and stone made no difference to them. On one
of these walks, between the afternoon and evening prayers, somebody saw a cow
going down a narrow ill-defined road. *I wonder which way that road will be
going*, Babur said. *Keep your eye on that cow; don't lose the cow till you know
where the road comes out.* One of his companions made a joke: if the cow loses
her way, what becomes of us?

In the winter several of his soldiers asked leave to go home to Andijan, since
there were no raids to be made. His beg pressed him to send something of his own
wear to his brother by them. He sent his ermine cap.

And then the sentence the whole memoir turns on: *It passed through my mind that
to wander from mountain to mountain, homeless and houseless, without country or
abiding-place, had nothing to recommend it.*

That is a man in his early twenties who has been a king since he was eleven,
deciding that this is no way to live.

## What he did about it

He took Kabul, held it for twenty years, and in 1526 went down into India and
won at Panipat. The dynasty he founded ruled northern India, in one condition or
another, until the British deposed the last of them after the Mutiny of 1857.

It never called itself Mughal; Europe did. It called itself Gurkani — from
*gurkan*, the son-in-law, the title Timur had adopted because he could not
lawfully call himself khan. The apology of a man who was not a Chinggisid became
the name of an empire in a country he never saw.

## And the book

Browne notes what happened to it afterwards, and the trail is itself a small
history of the dynasty. Babur wrote in Chaghatay Turkish. His grandson Akbar had
it translated into Persian by one of his generals in 1589. That Persian version
is what Leyden and Erskine worked from for the first English translation in
1826, and the Turkish original was not printed until 1857 — the year the empire
it describes the founding of came to an end.

A prince who could not hold a valley in Ferghana produced the only autobiography
of its kind written by any ruler in the Islamic world before the modern period,
and three centuries of his descendants had it read to them in a language he did
not write it in.
