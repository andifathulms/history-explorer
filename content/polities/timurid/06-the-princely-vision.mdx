title: The princely vision

The Timurid court style is the most widely inherited thing in this corpus that
is not a religion or an alphabet, and it was made largely at Herat and Samarkand
between about 1410 and 1500 by workshops the dynasty paid for directly.

Lentz and Lowry's argument is that this was a programme rather than an accident
of wealth. The *kitabkhana* — the book house — was a court institution that
employed calligraphers, illuminators, painters, binders, and also designers
whose drawings turn up executed in tile, in metal, in textile and in
architectural ornament. It produced a coherent visual language across media
because the same people were designing for all of them, on salary, for a patron
who cared. The point of it was princely: to display a kind of cultivated
sovereignty that could be recognised by anyone in the eastern Islamic world.

The names are worth having. Bihzad, working at Herat under Sultan Husayn
Bayqara, is the painter against whom Persian miniature was measured for two
centuries afterwards, and his figures are the first in the tradition that behave
like individuals doing something rather than like types arranged in a
composition. Mir Ali Shir Nava'i, Husayn Bayqara's companion and vizier, ran
much of the patronage — and did something else with consequences beyond art: he
wrote major poetry in Chagatai Turkish and argued in prose, at length and
polemically, that Turkish was capable of everything Persian could do. Courts had
written Persian as a matter of course. After Nava'i there was a literary Turkic
tradition with a canon to point at.

Then the workshops dispersed, and the dispersal is why this page matters to
three others. Bihzad went to Tabriz and worked for the Safavids, who took the
Herat school as their starting point. Timurid architectural forms — the double
dome, the four-iwan court, the garden divided in four — are the vocabulary of
Safavid Isfahan and of Mughal India, where Babur laid out Timurid gardens from
memory and his descendants built the Taj Mahal out of the same grammar. The
Ottoman court took the book arts and the ceramic ornament.

This site records elsewhere, repeatedly, that political weight and cultural
output come apart. The Timurids are the largest case of it in the corpus and
also the strangest, because here the output was the thing that lasted: the
empire was gone by 1507, and three of the four largest Muslim states of the
following two centuries were still working in a visual language its workshops
had assembled.
