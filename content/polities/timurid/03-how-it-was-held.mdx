---
title: A government without a monopoly of force
drafted_from: manz-2007

---

Manz's later book asks a question about Shahrukh's Iran that applies to most of
the polities on this site, and it is worth setting out here because the Timurid
case states it most clearly: how did a government that did not monopolise force
control a society it could not administer directly, and why was the arrangement
stable for decades at a time?

The Timurid answer was that the government did not try to be the only power. The
dynasty and its military servitors were one set of actors. City elites — the
notable families of Herat, Shiraz, Yazd, who supplied the officials and held the
local standing — were another, with their own resources and their own continuity
across changes of ruler. The religious classes, both the ulama and the Sufi
shaykhs whose followings were large enough to matter politically, were a third.
Provincial rulers who acknowledged the Timurids and governed as they liked were
a fourth.

Rule consisted of holding those constituencies in a relationship, mostly by
distributing things: appointments, revenue assignments, endowments, marriages,
tax remissions. It broke down when there was nothing left to distribute or when
a succession forced everyone to choose.

This is why the institutions on this page code the way they do and why the codes
are misleading on their own. `tribal-levy` and `land-grant` are both true of the
army. `appanage` is true of the succession, in the strong sense: Timur assigned
his sons and grandsons territories as personal patrimony and they fought over
them at every death, which is the same coding this corpus carries for the
Seljuks, the Mongols, the Ghurids and both Turkmen confederations. What none of
the four fields can express is that the state's stability came from a
negotiated relationship with people who were not in it, and that the negotiation
was the government's principal activity.

It is also the answer to a question the corpus keeps posing about polities that
look chaotic in the political narrative and produce extraordinary things anyway.
Ulugh Beg's observatory and Bihzad's workshop were funded by a state that was
losing control of its provinces, and the two facts are not in tension: the
patronage was part of how the relationship was held.
