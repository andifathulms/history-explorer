Timur died in 1405 having designated a grandson, and the empire went into four
years of war among sons and grandsons. Shahrukh, Timur's fourth son, won it, and
then ruled for thirty-eight years — longer than his father, and over a state
that looked nothing like his father's.

He governed from Herat rather than Samarkand, and that is the substantive
change. The centre of the empire
moved off the steppe frontier and into Khurasan, and the polity stopped being a
Central Asian conquest machine with Iranian provinces and became an Iranian
state with a Central Asian dynasty. Samarkand went to his son Ulugh Beg as a
viceroyalty.

The rest followed. Shahrukh stopped campaigning annually; he fought the Kara
Koyunlu in the west three times and otherwise defended what he had. He
patronised the ulama and the Sufi orders heavily, presented himself as a Muslim
sovereign ruling by the sharia rather than by the Mongol *yasa*, and dropped the
puppet khan his father had needed. That is a real change in what the dynasty
claimed to be, made in one reign, and it is easy to miss because the family kept
Timur's name for everything else.

His wife Gawhar Shad is one of the substantial figures of the period in her own
right: she endowed the mosque at Mashhad that still carries her name and a
mosque and madrasa complex at Herat, and she was politically active in the
succession disputes for decades after his death, until she was executed at about
eighty.

What Shahrukh did not fix was the succession, and the reason is on the previous
page. The appanage system that had produced the war of 1405 was still in place
in 1447, because it was not a policy but the structure of the family's claim to
its territories.
