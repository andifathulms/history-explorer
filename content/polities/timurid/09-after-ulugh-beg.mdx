Ulugh Beg was murdered in 1449 on the orders of his own son, and the eastern
half of the empire never reassembled. The fifty-eight years between that and the
Uzbek conquest are the part of Timurid history nobody tells, and they contain the
best court the dynasty ever had.

## The scramble

The pattern is monotonous and it is worth seeing in one place, because it is
what appanage succession does when there is nothing left to conquer.

Abd al-Latif murdered his father and was killed himself within months. Abu Sa'id,
a grandson of Timur's son Miranshah, had been attached to Ulugh Beg's court, was
thrown into prison by the parricide, escaped through the negligence of the
sentries to Bukhara, and came out of the wreckage holding much of Iran for two
decades. Mirkhwand calls him supreme among the princes of the House of Timur in
high enterprise, lofty rank and perfect discernment, and says the lands from
Turkistan to Mazandaran reached the height of their prosperity under him. He was
captured and executed by Uzun Hasan of the Ak Koyunlu in 1469.

Meanwhile the cousins fought each other. One of them occupied Herat, defeated a
cousin, was preparing to crush him at Astarabad, and was suddenly attacked by
Jahanshah of the Kara Koyunlu; he abandoned Herat, fled, came back when the
invader withdrew, and was then beaten by Abu Sa'id. He died on the road from
Damghan to Mashhad in 1458, and his cousin and rival was killed the same year.

Every one of those men was a great-grandson of Timur.

## Herat

What was left after 1469 was Khurasan, and Sultan Husayn Bayqara ruled it from
Herat for thirty-seven years.

By any political measure it is a rump: a province, hemmed in by Turkmen
confederations to the west and Uzbeks to the north, spending its revenues on a
capital instead of an army. By any other measure it is the summit of the whole
dynasty. Husayn was a serious patron and a writer himself, in Turki as well as
Persian. His friend and minister Ali Shir Nava'i made Chaghatay Turkish into a
literary language capable of everything Persian could do, and argued the case in
print. Jami was at that court. So was Bihzad.

## What the fifty-eight years were

It is tempting to call this a decline with a cultural consolation prize, and
that gets it backwards.

The Timurid state after 1449 could not defend Khurasan, could not recover
Transoxiana, and could not keep its princes from killing each other. It also
paid for the workshops out of which the visual language of the next three
empires came, and for a court where two literary languages were being worked on
at once by men who knew each other.

Those are not two separate stories. A prince who cannot expand and cannot be
displaced has revenue and no use for it, and time, and rivals to outshine who
are doing the same thing in the next city. The Timurid golden age is what
happens when a dynasty stops being able to conquer and has not yet stopped being
rich.
