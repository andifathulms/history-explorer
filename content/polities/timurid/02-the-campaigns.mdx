---
title: What the campaigns were for
drafted_from: manz-1989
phase: expansion
---

The list is long and the pattern in it is more interesting than the extent. Iran
and the Caucasus repeatedly from the 1380s. The Golden Horde, whose capital he
sacked twice, in 1391 and 1395. India in 1398, where Delhi was taken and its
population massacred. Syria in 1400, where Aleppo and Damascus were sacked and
the craftsmen of Damascus were deported to Samarkand. The Ottomans at Ankara in
1402, where he destroyed the army and captured the sultan. He died in 1405 on
the road to invade Ming China.

Almost none of it was annexed. Timur took submissions, plunder and craftsmen,
installed or reinstalled local rulers, and went home; when the submission lapsed
he came back and did it again, which is why Iran appears in the list four times.
Manz's account treats this not as a failure to consolidate but as the operating
method of a polity whose income was campaign proceeds and whose cohesion was
the expectation of the next one. The campaign proceeds were not what an army
happened to pick up along the way. They were structural income, budgeted for,
and the empire could not stop earning them without coming apart.

Two of the campaigns changed other people's histories more than his own. Sacking
Sarai and New Sarai wrecked the northern caravan route across the steppe and
broke the Golden Horde's grip on its Russian tributaries, which is a substantial
part of why Moscow rose in the following century, and entirely incidental to
anything Timur intended.
And Ankara removed the Ottoman state for a decade, restored the Anatolian
emirates the Ottomans had absorbed, and postponed the fall of Constantinople by
fifty years.

He was also, throughout, building. The plunder went into Samarkand: the Bibi
Khanum mosque, the Gur-i Amir, the Shah-i Zinda, and gardens ringing the city
named after the places he had taken. Deported craftsmen built them. It is the
most direct relationship between conquest and architecture anywhere in this
corpus, and it is a straightforward transfer — Delhi and Damascus paid for
Samarkand.
