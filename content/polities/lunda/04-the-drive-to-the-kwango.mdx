While one arm of Lunda's expansion pushed east toward the copper of Shaba,
another pushed in the opposite direction entirely - west, toward the
Kwango river and the slave markets of Angola, in a campaign whose motives
were tied openly to trade rather than only to territory.

## Trading partners who wanted no guns

Contact with the Angolan slave-trading network reached the Lunda core by
the 1670s at the latest, carried by Imbangala merchants running caravans
loaded with European cloth, beads and crockery - goods the Lunda
aristocracy prized as luxury items and new emblems of status, but not
goods that changed how Lunda fought. The Lunda rejected the firearms the
Imbangala offered in trade and kept their faith in their own broadsword,
the mpok, throughout the whole period of westward expansion described here.
What Lunda exported in return was people: captives taken first from the
Sala Mpasu, Kete, Kanincin and Kanyok on the empire's own margins, and
later Mbwela captives brought in from northern Zambia and eastern Angola
as well.

## Small kingdoms planted on the way

Shortly after 1700, Lunda expeditions began moving into eastern Angola,
where the Lwena country became the first target - a leader named Ciniama
established one of the resulting states by superimposing Lunda rule on
societies that already had deep matrilineal genealogies of their own,
first arbitrating local disputes before local people themselves came to
credit him with real supernatural power. Further west, similar
arrangements produced Chokwe, Minungo and Songo chiefdoms; among the
Shinje, an existing kingdom was overrun outright rather than merely
overlaid. By about 1750, Lunda chiefs held ground on the eastern bank of
the Kwango itself.

## A three-tiered order imposed on the Kongo pattern

At the Kwango, Lunda expeditions found kingdoms structured on the Kongo
model - the Yaka among them - and overran them, driving many of their
people to flee rather than submit. But the conquerors did not dismantle
what they found. Lunda governors were installed above or alongside the
existing local rulers, producing a political order split, by design, into
three tiers - central Lunda officials, absorbed local chiefs, and the
population beneath both - with even the empire's own founding ideology
now dividing conquerors from conquered rather than uniting them. The
Yaka's own paramount, a title called kiamfu, held nominal authority that,
in practice, could not fully control the men beneath him: one group broke
away to form an independent state of the Pelende, while another,
the Sonde-Luwa, emigrated further east and founded chiefdoms entirely of
their own. The northern Kwango filled, over the following century, with
refugees fleeing Yaka slave raids - a displacement severe enough that its
demographic mark on the region was still visible generations later.
