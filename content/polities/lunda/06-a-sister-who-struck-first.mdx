Verney Lovett Cameron, the first European to cross equatorial Africa coast
to coast, passed through Ulunda in 1874 and 1875 and found a court in the
middle of a crisis nobody outside the region had recorded before. The
reigning Mwata Yamvo had died about a year earlier. His successor, by every
account Cameron collected along the way, governed with a cruelty that
outran even the ordinary standard of the office.

## A ruler who studied his own subjects' deaths

That successor's habits went beyond the routine mutilations - cutting off
noses, lips and ears - that Cameron's informants described as unremarkable
punishment. His curiosity extended, on at least one occasion, to
vivisection: he had a pregnant woman killed to satisfy it. His sister, who
was also his own principal wife, objected on grounds nobody could call
abstract - she reasoned that as a woman herself, she might be next. She
gathered a party loyal to her and moved to kill him in his own hut at
night.

## Deposed, and hunting for an army to take it back

The coup succeeded, or nearly did. By the time Cameron's party crossed
into country closer to Urua, they learned the deposed Mwata Yamvo was
"stealing through the country" some miles off, making his own way toward
his kinsman Kasongo - the ruler of Urua, a state Cameron describes as
belonging to the same family line as Ulunda's own royal house - to beg
help retaking his throne. His sister and a brother now held it in his
place. A Kazembe encountered further along the route had already left
his own post to pay respects to whichever Mwata Yamvo currently held
Mussumba, the empire's capital - a courtesy visit made necessary, in the
middle of the 1870s, by exactly the kind of succession violence the
office's own positional-succession ideal was supposed to make unthinkable.

## What the crisis actually touched

None of this, on the evidence Cameron collected, broke the empire outright.
Kazembe still answered to Mussumba; tribute still moved along the routes
his account describes; Kasongo's own court still treated the Mwata Yamvo
as a peer worth courting rather than a rival worth ignoring. What had
changed was the office's own security at the center - a throne that, a
century after Naweej's own organizing reign, could be won or lost by
whichever sibling struck first, in a court a passing European stranger
could hear the news of within days of crossing the border.
