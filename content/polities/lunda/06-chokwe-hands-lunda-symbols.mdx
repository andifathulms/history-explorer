The Chokwe people, absorbed into the Lunda system during the westward
expansion toward the Kwango, occupied an unusual place in the empire they
had been conquered into: valued not chiefly for tribute or manpower, but
for skills the conquerors themselves largely lacked. Central Lunda had
comparatively little tradition of metalworking or fine carving of its own.
The Chokwe, by contrast, were accomplished metallurgists, hunters and,
above all, carvers - and the empire's aristocracy came to depend on
Chokwe hands for both the weapons and the ceremonial objects that
displayed Lunda authority to the rest of the empire.

## Portuguese motifs, carried inland

The objects that survive from this exchange carry a visible record of
where Lunda's trade actually reached. Chokwe carving from the second half
of the eighteenth century shows stylistic borrowing from Portuguese
Renaissance and Rococo decoration - motifs that could only have traveled
inland along the same caravan routes that carried European cloth, beads
and crockery to the Lunda aristocracy in exchange for captives. A wooden
stool or ceremonial object carved in the Lunda heartland could therefore
carry, in its ornament, a visual record of a trading relationship that
stretched all the way to the Atlantic coast.

## Symbols that outran the empire's own administration

Lunda's political reach was loose at its edges, run through tribute and
kinship fictions rather than direct administration - but its symbolic
reach traveled further and more thoroughly than its armies ever
administered directly. The horned cap that marked status at the imperial
court at Mussumba is found, in the archaeological and ethnographic record,
across the entire distance from the Kwango river to the Lualaba - a piece
of material culture that crossed political boundaries the empire's own
tribute-collectors never fully closed.
