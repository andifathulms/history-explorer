By the close of the eighteenth century the eastern kingdom Lunda's own
conquering generals had planted on the Luapula river a hundred years
earlier had grown into something the empire's own founders at Mussumba
could not have anticipated: a state wealthy and organized enough to
negotiate with a European colonial power on close to its own terms.

## A mission that came away impressed

A Portuguese expedition led by Francisco de Lacerda e Almeida, traveling
from the colonial ports of Sena and Tete on the lower Zambezi, reached the
kazembe's court in 1798 and stayed into 1799. What Lacerda's mission found
was not a distant vassal barely worth the journey, but a kingdom whose
military strength impressed the visitors directly, and whose rulers still
practiced the same severe methods of coercing tribute from their own
subjects that had built the state in the first place.

## Tribute becomes trade

The Lacerda mission opened a period of steadily intensifying commercial
contact between the kazembe's court and the Portuguese trading network of
the lower Zambezi. What began, in the 1790s, as a fairly conventional
tribute relationship - the eastern kingdom sending regular payments toward
Mussumba, the imperial center that had planted it there in the first
place - had shifted, within a single generation, into something closer to
an exchange of gifts between equals. Distance from the capital, and the
kazembe's own direct access to a European trading partner that Mussumba
itself had no comparable relationship with, was steadily converting formal
subordination into practical independence.

## An empire drawn on one map

By 1800, taken as a whole, Lunda's conquests and the parallel expansion of
its Luba neighbors had brought a broadly common political culture to the
entire savanna stretching from the Kwango river in the west to the Lualaba
in the east. Lunda horned caps, a mark of status at Mussumba, were by then
recognizable across that whole distance. An institution called mukanda,
practiced among the Pende since at least the 1650s, had spread with Lunda
expansion into new territory in turn. None of this made Lunda a single,
tightly centralized state in the way its own founding household model
implied - the kazembe's own growing autonomy from Mussumba was proof
enough of that - but it had, within a single century, given an enormous
stretch of Central Africa a shared vocabulary of rule that neither Lunda
nor Luba had possessed on their own before either empire began.
