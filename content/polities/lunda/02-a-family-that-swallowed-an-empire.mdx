title: A family that swallowed an empire

Lunda's central institution was an idea about time itself: that a title,
once created, kept the same identity forever regardless of who held it.
When a new office-holder took up a position - whether the emperor's own
throne or a minor district headship - he took on his predecessor's name,
his predecessor's wives, his predecessor's children and other relatives,
and was understood, in a real and operative sense, to become his
predecessor rather than merely to replace him. Five generations after a
title's creation, the system still worked exactly as it had at the start,
because time, in the sense of a title's own genealogy, had simply stopped
passing.

## Kinship as an administrative tool

This idea, which scholars call positional succession, did more than
explain how office worked - it explained how an empire could be run as a
single family. Every "son" and "nephew" in the resulting web owed
obedience to every "father" and "uncle"; every "grandson" was understood
as an ally to his "grandfather," regardless of any blood relationship
that had actually existed between the specific people holding those
titles. A newly conquered chief could be absorbed into this structure
simply by being given a kinship position - a marriage alliance making him
a "son-in-law," say - and a stable, permanently defined place in the
empire came with it automatically. Positional succession was not a
metaphor decorating Lunda politics. It was the actual mechanism through
which a war-conquering empire stretching from the Kwango river to beyond
the Luapula, incorporating peoples who spoke different languages and had
never previously answered to any Rund chief, was administered as though
it were one extended household.

## An empire that ran on tribute and slaves

The household model did not make Lunda gentle. By about 1700 Lunda war
expeditions had already subdued the populations immediately around the
empire's core and incorporated them by exactly this method, before moving
outward to found subsidiary kingdoms across a vast east-west arc. Officials
called tukwata roamed the countryside with military detachments to force
tribute out of district rulers who might otherwise have kept it for
themselves, and the empire's economy depended openly on slave-raiding -
captives taken in conquered territory were put to work cultivating fields
around the capital, growing cassava on plantations along the major roads,
and eventually serving as porters for the caravan trade that connected
Lunda to Angola. A family, in the Lunda idiom - but a family that thrived,
in the plainest sense, on war.
