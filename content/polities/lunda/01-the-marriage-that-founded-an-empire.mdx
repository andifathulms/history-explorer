title: The marriage that founded an empire

Nothing about the land the Lunda empire eventually grew from marked it out
for the role. The Rund territories lay in country with better-watered,
more densely settled and more agriculturally advanced neighbors to the
north, on trade routes that ran through other people's land before they
reached the Rund at all. What the Rund had, instead of geography, was a
story about how their own ruling family began - and an unusual capacity to
turn strangers into kin.

## A chief who gave up his own office

The earliest tradition names a chief called Nkond, who for reasons the
story never quite explains left his own office to his daughter, Rweej,
rather than to a son. Rweej went on to marry a hunter named Cibinda
Ilunga, who had traveled from the Luba court to the east - and, according
to the tradition collected by the Portuguese explorer Dias de Carvalho in
the 1880s, Rweej then abandoned her own office in her husband's favor,
letting him organize the Rund court on the Luba model he already knew.

The hunter-who-marries-the-chief's-daughter is a common device in the
region's founding traditions, repeated with variations by several
neighboring peoples, and the version collected from the Rund themselves is
not straightforwardly the whole story. Scholars examining the political
institutions that Cibinda Ilunga supposedly imported have found nearly as
much Rund influence on later Luba practice as the reverse - a sign that
whatever happened between the two courts, it was not simply one culture's
institutions transplanted wholesale into the other's territory.

## Three accounts of what came next

Even the succession that followed the founding marriage survives in more
than one version. Carvalho's account gives power, after an initial
conquest, to Rweej's own son by Cibinda Ilunga, a man named Yav - whose
name became the title, Mwant Yav, every emperor after him would carry -
and then to Yav's brother Naweej, remembered as the empire's real
organizer. A more recently collected tradition instead holds that Rweej
herself was sterile, and gave her husband a second wife, Kamonga, to bear
the heir in her place - an account that explains why the Lunda court kept
two permanent female offices ever after: the Swana Mulunda, embodying
Rweej's own founding role, and the Rukonkesh, the queen-mother descended
from the role Kamonga had played.

Neither version can be dated by the traditions that carry it. What can be
dated, loosely, is the point at which outside sources first register a
Rund state substantial enough to be worth mentioning at all - by 1680, a
chiefdom built on a marriage nobody can date had already become something
the wider region had reason to notice.
