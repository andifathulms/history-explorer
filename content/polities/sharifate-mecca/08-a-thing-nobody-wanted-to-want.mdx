In the summer of 1918 a British political officer sat in a house in central
Arabia, in weather his thermometer put at over ninety, and listened to Ibn
Sa'ud's court discuss the sharif of Mecca. He wrote it down that evening. Eight
years later the man he was travelling with was crowned in the Great Mosque at
Mecca as king of the Hejaz.

The conversation is the best surviving statement of the case against the
sharifate, made by the people who were shortly going to end it, before they knew
they would.

## The argument

A kinsman of Ibn Sa'ud's led it — a man whose own hereditary claim to the
Wahhabi throne was, as the officer noted drily, at least as good as the one he
was attacking — and he made two points.

The first was dynastic. Husayn belonged to the 'Abadila clan, which owed its
present position to the Turks; the Dhawi Zayd, his relatives, were the senior
branch. On that reckoning Husayn was not even the rightful sharif, let alone
king of anything.

The second was about blood, and it is ugly, and it should be quoted rather than
paraphrased because it is what was actually said. The Ashraf, he argued, were of
much-mixed blood — through their negress, Circassian, Turkish and other mothers
— and could not claim to be pure Arabs like the people of Najd. How, then, could
Husayn claim to be king of the Arabs?

Nine centuries of the sharifate's own strategy is embedded in that accusation.
The office had survived by marrying into every power that came through the
Hejaz, taking the subsidy of every sovereign who wanted his name in the sermon,
and being useful to Egypt and to Istanbul in turn. The households of Mecca were
cosmopolitan because Mecca was. And in 1918, in a room in central Arabia, that
was the charge: not that they had failed, but that they had been in contact with
everybody.

## What the British thought they were doing

The officer answered, and his answer is the other half of the epitaph.

Britain, he explained, had not made Husayn king of the Arabs and could not stop
him calling himself whatever he liked. British recognition of his kingship of
the Hejaz had a specific cause: when the Ottoman sultan declared war and
proclaimed a jihad, Britain needed — for the satisfaction of millions of Indian
Muslim subjects — a recognised Muslim leader to raise a revolt in its favour
against the Turks.

That is the sharifate's last transaction, described by the buyer. What was
purchased in 1916 was exactly what had been purchased since the tenth century: a
Hasanid at Mecca whose recognition made a claim look lawful to Muslims elsewhere.
The only novelty is the elsewhere. Previous customers had wanted the sermon read
for the benefit of Cairo or Istanbul; this one wanted it for the benefit of
Lucknow and Lahore.

## The disclaimer

Then Ibn Sa'ud spoke, and said he coveted no title and no territory outside
Najd. As for Mecca he had no desire whatever to be its master. All that
interested him there was the House of God, and only in that he was obliged as a
Muslim to visit it once in his life, for ten days at the most. The forts and
palaces his ancestors had built at Mecca, Medina and Ta'if merely reminded him
of an episode in the past history of Najd, and he had no wish to reoccupy them.

He was crowned in the Great Mosque on the eighth of January, 1926.

There is no reason to read the 1918 statement as a lie. It reads like a man
declining a burden he had no use for, in a year when taking it was not
available. What changed was not his appetite but the position: the sharif
overreached, the subsidies stopped, the caliphate claim failed, and the Hejaz
turned out to be there for the taking.

## What was abolished

The office was not transferred. It was ended.

Nine and a half centuries of sharifate had rested on a qualification nobody
could confer — descent from the Prophet through Hasan — which is precisely why
every overlord from the Fatimids to the Ottomans had appointed sharifs rather
than governors. The new king of the Hejaz was not a Hasanid and did not pretend
to be. He did not need to be: his claim to the holy cities was custodial and
doctrinal rather than genealogical, and a state built on that argument has no
use for a family whose whole standing is its ancestry.

So the sharifate ends without a successor in the office, and that is not an
accident of the record. It is what happens when the thing being replaced was
never a job in the first place.
