title: The nine years of being a king

Husayn ibn Ali became sharif in 1908, appointed from Istanbul in the ordinary
way, into an empire that had just had a constitutional revolution and a
government that intended to centralise. The Hejaz railway had reached Medina the
same year. Both facts pointed the same direction: the distance the sharifate had
lived on was closing.

In June 1916, with British money, British arms and an undertaking about Arab
independence whose terms are still argued about, he declared a revolt against
the Ottoman empire. The campaign that followed is the one Western readers know
through T. E. Lawrence, and its military significance was modest and its
political significance was not. Husayn took the title king of the Hejaz.

That is the break. For nine hundred and forty-eight years the sharifate's method
had been to serve whoever was strongest, take the subsidy, keep the office and
outlast the sovereign. Husayn stopped being a client and became a principal, and
in 1924, after the Turkish republic abolished the caliphate, he claimed that too.

The nine years went badly. The British subsidy stopped after the war and the
promises were not what he had understood them to be; the caliphate claim was
rejected almost everywhere and cost him support in India and Egypt; and in the
Najd the second Saudi state's successor under Abd al-Aziz ibn Saud had spent
twenty years taking central Arabia. In 1924 Ibn Saud invaded the Hejaz. Ta'if
fell, then Mecca, then Jeddah after a siege. Husayn abdicated in favour of his
son Ali, who abdicated in 1925 and left.

The office was not filled again. Ibn Saud took the title of king of the Hejaz
himself and, in 1932, made it part of a state named after his family — which is
the answer to a question this page has been asking throughout, about whether the
qualification could be conferred. It could not, so it was abolished.

This record has no edges. It is not the successor of any polity in this corpus
and none of them succeeded it, which for a polity that dealt with nearly every
Islamic state on this site across nine centuries is a peculiar result and an
honest one: the sharifate was continuously somebody's client and never anybody's
heir. Hard rule 7 says a polity with no edge is complete. This is what that looks
like at its most extreme.

Husayn's sons were made kings of Iraq and Transjordan by the British. The Iraqi
branch was killed in 1958. The Jordanian one is still there, and its head is
still a descendant of Qatada ibn Idris, who took Mecca in 1201.
