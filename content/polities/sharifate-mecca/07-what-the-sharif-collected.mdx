Burckhardt reached Mecca in 1814, passing as a Muslim, in the year the Egyptian
army was taking the Hijaz back from the Wahhabis and the last sharif to govern it
independently was about to be removed. He stayed for months and wrote down what
the government had actually consisted of, which is not what the title suggests.

<Figure id="burckhardt" />

It consisted of customs. At Yanbu, which had been annexed to the sharifate,
Ghalib kept a vizier and a guard of fifty or sixty men, and Burckhardt's summary
of that officer's authority is that he appears to have had little beyond
collecting the customs. The Arabs of the town were left to the government of
their own shaikhs and enjoyed considerably more liberty than the people of Mecca
and Jeddah. And when a man of Yanbu was unjustly treated, he went out to his
relations in the desert — the Juhayna, a tribe Burckhardt says was not to be
trifled with — who retaliated on the sharif's people and the sharif's caravans
until the matter was compromised.

<Figure id="juhayna" />

That is a state whose subjects have an appeal mechanism consisting of their
cousins robbing the government until it settles.

The revenue was supposed to be shared with the Ottoman pasha of Jeddah. Ghalib
put the whole of the Yanbu customs into his own treasury. When the town was
walled after the first Wahhabi attack in 1802, the sharif ordered the work and
made the townspeople pay the entire cost of it.

Then the Wahhabis took the Hijaz. Burckhardt sets out the sequence
matter-of-factly: Saud reduced the two great Bedouin tribes of the north, which
was made easy by the hatred between them; Yanbu al-Nakhl took a Wahhabi garrison;
Mecca fell and Ghalib submitted; Yanbu held out some months more and then
capitulated and adopted the creed. The Wahhabis did not garrison it. The sharif's
governor stayed in place. What changed was that the Wahhabi tax-collectors
arrived — and the inhabitants, who except for customs duties had never been
subject to any imposts at all, found the new government pressed on them very
heavily.

That sentence is the most damaging thing in the book about the old regime, and
Burckhardt does not seem to intend it as praise. A sharifate that had governed
the holy cities for eight hundred and fifty years had never established direct
taxation over a town in its own province. It taxed goods passing through, and the
pilgrimage, and it did not reach further because it could not.

<Figure id="arafat-plan" />

The other half of the picture is what that bought. At Arafat in 1814 Burckhardt
watched the tents pitched: the two pashas' encampments, and beside them, rivalling
them in beauty and far exceeding the tents of a sharif of the reigning house, the
tents of a Mecca merchant named Jaylani. Anywhere else in the East, he remarks, a
merchant would as soon buy a rope for his own neck as display his wealth in front
of a pasha. The Meccans had not learned to be frightened, because under Sharif
Ghalib extortion had seldom been applied to individuals — and they were now
relying on Muhammad Ali's promise that he would respect their property.

<Figure id="arafat-camp" />

They should not have. But the observation stands as the best account there is of
what the sharifate was for. It was not a strong government and had never tried to
be one. It was a light, predatory, customs-collecting authority over a city whose
business was pilgrims, which left the tribes alone because it had to and left the
merchants alone because the merchants were the revenue — and which lasted nearly
a millennium on exactly that, until two neighbouring powers with real armies
arrived within a decade of each other and neither of them could see the point of
it.
