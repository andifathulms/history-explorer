The Hejaz grows almost nothing, and the sharifate of Mecca is the purest case
anywhere of a state financed by other states.

What it had of its own was Jeddah, the port where Red Sea traffic for Mecca
landed, and the sharifs took a share of its customs. That is why the Mamluk
seizure of the revenue in the fifteenth century mattered so much, and why Cairo's
capture of the Jeddah customs is the moment the Yemeni alternative closed.

The rest was transfers. The *surra*, an annual purse sent from Cairo and later
from Istanbul for the upkeep of the holy cities and the stipends of their
inhabitants. Endowments established for Mecca and Medina by rulers and rich
individuals from Morocco to Bengal, whose revenues flowed to the Hejaz in
perpetuity. Grain shipments from Egypt, without which the city could not be fed.
And direct gifts from any sovereign who wanted something.

## What they wanted

The sermon.

The khutba at Mecca names a sovereign, and it is heard by pilgrims from every
part of the Muslim world in the one place they all come to. That makes it the
single most valuable piece of publicity in Islam, and the sharifs were the people
who decided whose name was in it.

Once you know that, a great many transactions elsewhere resolve into the same
one, seen from the other side of the table. A Hafsid claimant to the caliphate in
Tunis could point to the sharif's acknowledgement and to little else. A Sulayhid
client of Cairo controlled the appointment of the amir of Mecca, which is the
high-water mark of the whole Fatimid claim. The Rasulids of Ta'izz and the
Mamluks of Cairo competed over whose kiswa reached the Ka'ba first. And in 1517
the guardianship passed to Selim, which is a large part of what the Ottoman
sultans were afterwards.

A small family in a barren valley, with no army and no crops, spent nine
centuries auctioning the only thing it had, and was paid by whoever won.
