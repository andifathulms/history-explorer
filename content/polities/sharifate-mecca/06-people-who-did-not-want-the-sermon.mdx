The first Saudi state took Mecca in 1803, and what arrived was not another
overlord.

Every previous conqueror of the Hejaz had wanted the same thing: the khutba, the
title, the pilgrimage running, and a sharif in place to make it lawful. The
Wahhabi movement wanted none of it. It regarded the veneration of tombs as
idolatry and demolished the domed shrines in Mecca and, later, at Medina. It
regarded the endowments and the stipends as corruption. It regarded the Ottoman
sultan's claim as worthless and stopped the Egyptian and Syrian pilgrim caravans
from arriving at all, on the grounds that those who came with them were not
Muslims in the relevant sense.

<Figure id="turaif" />

For a polity whose entire revenue was other people's reverence, this was not a
change of master. It was the removal of the market.

<Figure id="muhammad-ali" />

The Ottomans could not answer it themselves and subcontracted the problem to
Muhammad Ali of Egypt, whose son Ibrahim Pasha took the Hejaz back between 1811
and 1818 with an army equipped on European lines and destroyed the Saudi capital
at Diriyah. The sharifate was restored, the caravans resumed, and the nineteenth
century was spent under an Egyptian and then an Ottoman administration that was
progressively less distant — the telegraph reached Mecca, a garrison was
stationed, the Suez Canal made the Hejaz reachable in days, and the autonomy the
sharifs had enjoyed because they were far away eroded with the travel time.

<Figure id="hejaz-railway" />

The argument that began in 1803 was never settled in the sharifate's favour. It
was suspended for a century, and when it resumed the sharifs had lost the empire
that had suspended it.
