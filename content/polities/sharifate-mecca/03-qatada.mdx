In 1201 a Hasanid chief from Yanbu on the Red Sea coast named Qatada ibn Idris
took Mecca from the line then holding it. His descendants held the sharifate for
the following seven hundred and twenty-four years.

<Figure id="yanbu-1914" />

Seven hundred and twenty-four years in one office is a length of tenure with
very few parallels anywhere, and it was accomplished through the Ayyubids, the
Rasulids, the
Mamluks, four centuries of Ottomans, a Wahhabi occupation, an Egyptian
reconquest and a world war. Every sharif of Mecca between Qatada and the end was
one of his descendants, including Husayn ibn Ali, who declared the Arab Revolt
in 1916 — and the Hashemite kings of Jordan today are of the same line.

The method is visible in the thirteenth century and does not change afterwards.
Qatada is reported to have declined an invitation to Baghdad on the grounds that
he did not trust the caliph's safe-conduct, and to have written a poem about it;
his successors dealt with the Ayyubids in Egypt and the Rasulids in Yemen at the
same time, playing each against the other, and took presents from both. When
Baybars made the pilgrimage in 1269 and asserted Mamluk suzerainty, the sharif
accepted it and kept his position. When the Rasulids sent a kiswa first, the
sharif hung it.

<Figure id="baybars-panther" />

The internal politics were violent throughout. Qatada's family was large and the
office was worth having, so the standard pattern is two or three cousins with
rival claims, each with a patron in Cairo or Ta'izz or later Istanbul, and a
sharifate that changed hands by assassination and by imperial decree in roughly
equal proportions. Meloy's study of the later medieval period is largely an
account of how those factions used the Egyptian connection, and how Cairo used
them.

What the family understood, and what almost nobody in a comparable position
ever understands, is that the object was never to win. A sharif
who defeated his overlord would have had to defend the Hejaz, which cannot be
done. A sharif who lost gracefully and remained necessary kept the office.
