The sharifate's early decades under Fatimid overlordship produced one
episode dramatic enough to survive in detail: a Meccan sharif who
briefly let himself be proclaimed rival caliph to his own Fatimid
overlord, and lived to regret it.

## A rebellion that needed a caliph of its own

Around 1010 a revolt broke out in Palestine against the Fatimid
caliph al-Hakim, led by the Bedouin chief Mufarraj and his son Hasan
of the Jarrah family, after a Fatimid official close to the court had
been put to death at a rival's request and fled to join them in fear
for his own life. The rebels defeated and killed the Fatimid general
sent to put them down, took Ramla, and - having gone this far against
the caliph in Cairo - decided they needed a caliph of their own to
legitimize the revolt. They sent for Husayn ibn Ja'far, the Sharif of
Mecca, and invited him to assume the caliphate himself.

## A crown accepted, and then abandoned by the men who offered it

Husayn agreed readily. He left a deputy to govern Mecca in his
absence, joined Mufarraj's rebel army, and was formally saluted as
"Commander of the Faithful" - a Hasanid sharif, for a brief season,
holding the same title as the Fatimid and Abbasid caliphs both. It did
not last. Hakim wrote to Hasan and Mufarraj offering them estates and
gifts to abandon the revolt, and they took the offer, breaking with
the very sharif they had just proclaimed caliph. The dispute that
followed between the rebels and their discarded figurehead was bitter
enough that Husayn left them outright and returned to Mecca, his
brief caliphate over almost as soon as it had been declared.

<Figure id="hakim-minaret" />

## A reconciliation bought with a name on the coinage

Hakim sent an army to expel Hasan and his followers from Ramla soon
after, and eventually pardoned Hasan at his father's intercession,
granting him an estate in Egypt - though Mufarraj himself was later
poisoned on the caliph's own orders regardless. Husayn's own
"anti-caliphate" at Mecca continued in a kind of suspended defiance
for two more years before he asked to be reconciled with Hakim; once
the reconciliation was granted, he put Hakim's name back on Mecca's
coinage and had it restored to the Friday sermon - the same
submission that had defined the sharifate since its founding, resumed
exactly where it had briefly, ambitiously, and unsuccessfully been
set aside.
