Aparajitavarman — a name meaning "the unconquered" — came to the
Pallava throne around 875, late enough in the dynasty's own history
that the kingdom he inherited was already a smaller and less secure
one than Narasimhavarman's had been two centuries before. He allied
with the Western Ganga king Prithivipathi I against the Pandya king
Varaguna II, winning a battle at Sripurambiyam around 880 so decisive
that a Ganga inscription credits the victory with making Aparajita's
own title finally meaningful — his ally, the record says, "entered
heaven by sacrificing his own life" to secure it.

## A rising neighbor tests an aging kingdom

The victory over the Pandyas did nothing to answer a more immediate
threat. The Chola king Aditya I, son of Vijayalaya, had spent years
making steady inroads into Pallava territory even as Aparajita
celebrated his own southern triumph, pressing the Pallava kingdom from
a direction its own king had little strength left to contest.

## An ending recorded by the victor

Late in the ninth century — the source places it in Aditya's own
final years, working backward from his son Parantaka I's securely
dated 907 accession — the Chola army defeated Aparajita in battle and
annexed the whole of Tondaimandalam outright. A Chola inscription, the
Tiruvalangadu plates, records the moment from the winning side: Aditya
"having conquered in battle the Pallava with his brilliant army,
though (he was) Aparajita (the unconquered) of his queen... accomplished
his object in this direction too" — a pointed irony the Chola scribes
did not let pass unremarked, naming the defeated king's own title in
the same breath as his defeat. The kingdom of the Pallavas, in the
source's own summary, "passed into the hands of the Cholas with the
death of Aparajita" — a dynasty that had once sacked a rival's capital
in triumph ending, four centuries later, as the conquered territory
in someone else's own inscription.
