The Pallava kingdom that emerges from the darkness after its
Prakrit-charter era has a real founder of its own second act:
Simhavishnu, called Avanisimha, whose reign around the last quarter of
the sixth century opens what the dynasty's own later inscriptions
treat as a fresh beginning rather than a simple continuation.

## A king known only through his own son's praise

No inscription survives in Simhavishnu's own name - what is known of
him comes from later charters praising his memory and from a relief
carved at Mahabalipuram showing him with his queens beside a matching
image of his son. The Velurpalayam grant credits him with seizing the
Chola country along the Kaveri delta and defeating a coalition
including the Kalabhras, Malavas, Pandyas and even the king of Ceylon
- extending Pallava authority further into the Tamil south than any
predecessor had managed. His own devotion to Vishnu, embedded in his
name, sat alongside a religious tolerance typical of the age, when
Vishnu and Shiva were still commonly honored in the same temple rather
than by separate sects.

## A Jain king persuaded to change his faith

Simhavishnu's son Mahendravarman I, who took the throne around 600,
was by tradition raised a Jain and given, in his early years, to
persecuting other faiths - including, according to the hagiography of
the Shaiva saint Appar, Appar himself, whom Mahendravarman is said to
have tortured before Appar's own survival of the ordeal converted the
king to Shaivism. Whatever the literal truth of the story, an
inscription from Mahendravarman's own reign describes him as having
"turned back from hostile conduct to the worship of the linga," and
the conversion left a permanent mark: he became the dynasty's most
prolific builder of rock-cut cave temples, excavating shrines across
the Tamil country at Vallam, Mahendravadi, Dalavanur, Siyyamangalam
and Pallavaram, and built at least one Vishnu shrine alongside his
newfound Shaivism, evidence that his own conversion never hardened
into sectarian exclusivity.

## A king who wrote his own satire

Mahendravarman was, by the evidence of his own inscriptions and a
surviving manuscript, a genuine polymath - credited with composing
the Sanskrit farce Mattavilasa Prahasana, a satire of a drunken
Kapalika ascetic that survives complete and offers a rare direct
window into the religious life of his own court at Kanchipuram; with
authorship of a treatise on painting referenced in his own Mamandur
inscription; and, on the strength of a musical inscription at
Kudumiyamalai closely resembling his own style, with a serious
interest in music theory as well. It was also under Mahendravarman
that the long Pallava-Chalukya rivalry began in earnest: the Chalukya
king Pulakeshin II seized the Vengi region from Pallava control early
in the century and, in the course of his own southern campaigns, drove
deep enough into Pallava territory that his own Aihole inscription
boasts of having made "the splendour of the Lord of the Pallavas...
vanish behind the walls of Kanchipuram" - though a separate Pallava
record credits Mahendravarman with a victory over the same enemy at
Pullalur, suggesting a war fought to no clean decision in his own
lifetime, and left instead for his son Narasimhavarman to finish.
