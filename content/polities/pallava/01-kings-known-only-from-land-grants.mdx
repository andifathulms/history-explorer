title: Kings known only from land grants

The earliest Pallava kings of Kanchi survive in the historical record
only through a handful of copper-plate charters written in Prakrit,
undated in any known era and placed in time only by the shape of their
own lettering — palaeography rather than any inscribed year. These
charters record little beyond genealogy and the details of the grants
themselves: land given to individuals, the names of a donor's father,
grandfather and great-grandfather, nothing of politics or war. From
this thin evidence a tentative order of succession can be reconstructed
beginning with a king named Bappa Deva, though the source itself
concedes that "the actual order of succession... is uncertain."

## A dynasty glimpsed through a rival's own inscription

The clearest fixed point in this early darkness comes not from a
Pallava record at all but from the Allahabad pillar inscription of the
Gupta emperor Samudragupta, which names a contemporary Pallava king of
Kanchi, Vishnugopa, ruling somewhere along the Krishna river around the
middle of the fourth century. Taken together with the Prakrit charters'
own palaeographic dating, this places the dynasty's earliest attested
kings somewhere in the range of the third to fourth centuries — a
period the source styles simply "the age of the kings of the Prakrit
charters, circa A.D. 200-350."

## A slow shift from grant records to genealogy

Not until the seventh century did Pallava charters begin recording the
exploits of a donor's royal ancestors alongside the grant itself,
finally giving historians something closer to a connected political
narrative rather than a bare list of names. Everything before that
point remains, in the source's own assessment, more useful for
constructing a family tree than for writing an actual history of what
these early kings did or how they ruled.
