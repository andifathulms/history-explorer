By the time Narasimhavarman I succeeded his father Mahendravarman I
around 630, the Pallavas had already suffered a serious humiliation at
Chalukya hands: Pulakeshin II of Vatapi had advanced deep into Pallava
territory during Mahendravarman's own reign, forcing "the lord of the
Pallavas to retreat behind the walls of Kanchipuram" itself. Early in
his own reign Narasimhavarman faced a further Chalukya thrust that
reached to within twenty miles of his own capital before he crushed
it decisively in a string of battles fought near Kanchi.

## A raid deep into enemy territory

Having repulsed the invaders, Narasimhavarman did not stop at defense.
He entrusted a counter-invasion into Chalukya territory to his general
Siru-Tonda, also known as Paranjoti, who marched on Vatapi itself,
captured the Chalukya capital, and returned to Kanchi loaded with
spoils. A Pallava inscription at Vatapi, mutilated but still legible,
records the achievement directly, and later Pallava charters credit
Narasimhavarman with carrying home "the pillar of victory standing in
the centre of Vatapi" — a trophy taken from the enemy's own capital in
answer to the humiliation his father had suffered a generation before.
Contemporary and later inscriptions style him Vatapikonda —
"Vatapi-taker" — in commemoration of the feat.

## A reign remembered for more than one conquest

Narasimhavarman's reign extended well beyond this single campaign: an
intervention in Ceylonese affairs recorded in the Mahavamsa, the
beginning of the rock-cut excavation work at Mamallapuram that still
bears his own title Mahamalla in its modern name, and a visit from a
Chinese Buddhist pilgrim to Kanchipuram itself, evidence of the city's
standing as a destination worth a foreign traveler's own journey.
Taken together, the source treats his reign as the high point of the
entire Pallava line — a kingdom "considerably enlarged" under a king
whose military reputation rested on avenging, rather than merely
defending against, the dynasty's most dangerous rival.
