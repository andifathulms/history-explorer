The most consequential thing to survive the Dynasty of Isin was not a
building, a treaty or even the law code the previous chapters describe. It
was a list — and the list itself argues, in its own construction, for
exactly the legitimacy the dynasty that compiled it needed.

## What the Sumerian King List actually does

The Sumerian King List presents Mesopotamian kingship as a single object
that descended, unbroken, from a moment before the flood down through a
sequence of cities — Kish, then Uruk, then Ur, and on through further
cities and dynasties — each holding "kingship" as a kind of movable office
that passed from one city to the next whenever the gods judged the time
had come, never split, never held by two cities at once, always
legitimately somewhere. Michalowski's argument is that the document
surviving to the modern reader took the particular shape it has because it
was compiled, or substantially reworked, at the court of Isin itself.

That framing does specific work for whoever compiled it. A list built to
show kingship as a single, continuous, transferable office, arriving at
its own present moment, is a list built to answer one question: which city
holds it now. Composed or finalised under an Isin king, the list answers
that question in exactly the way the Isin court needed answered.

## An improvised throne, made to look inevitable

The dynasty's own founding was nothing like the orderly succession the
King List's structure implies. Ishbi-Erra was a supply officer who chose
not to return to a fallen city and built a kingdom out of what he happened
to be holding when the opportunity presented itself — the ordinary shape
of a governor an empire can no longer remove, not the kind of founding a
king wants remembered as the reason he rules.

The King List does not remember it that way. Read as Michalowski reads it,
the list quietly converts Ishbi-Erra's improvisation into the latest
transfer of an office that had been passing legitimately from city to
city since before the flood — the same office Kish held, then Uruk, then
Ur, now simply residing, as it always eventually had to reside somewhere,
at Isin. A text that looks like disinterested chronology is doing the
specific political work of making an opportunist's holding look like
destiny.

## What outlasted the argument

The list's political usefulness to Isin's own kings ended when Isin's own
kingship did. Its usefulness as a chronological framework did not: copies
and versions of the King List continued to be made in scribal schools for
centuries afterward, read by scribes who had no stake in whether Isin's
own claim to the office had ever been deserved, using a document built to
flatter one vanished dynasty's legitimacy as the basic timeline for
everything that had supposedly happened before it.
