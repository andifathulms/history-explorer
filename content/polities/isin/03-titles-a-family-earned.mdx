Ishme-Dagan, the fourth king of Ishbi-Erra's line, ruled the fullest
version of the kingdom his great-grandfather had improvised. His own
inscriptions claim, in addition to King of Isin and King of Sumer and
Akkad, the title Lord of Erech, and record favor shown to the temples
of Nippur, Ur and Eridu in turn - a claim to authority over every major
<Figure id="ishme-dagan-brick" />

Sumerian religious center, not just the new capital. Bricks bearing
his name and titles, recovered at Nippur in quantity, show building
work substantial enough to back up the claim rather than merely assert
it.

## A king who married a goddess

Ishme-Dagan went further than his predecessors in another direction as
well, styling himself the "beloved spouse" of Ninni, the great goddess
of Erech - a claim to divine consort status the kings of Isin had
inherited as a practice from the earlier Dynasty of Ur, and one
Ishme-Dagan's own son Libit-Ishtar continued after him. Divine rank
and real administrative reach reinforced each other: a king secure
enough in his own legitimacy to claim marriage to a goddess was also,
in this case, a king whose officials' presence is independently
attested at every major city of the south.

## A peak that was already, quietly, an ending

Ishme-Dagan reigned twenty years, closing out the ninety-four years his
own great-grandfather's direct line had held the throne without
interruption. His son Libit-Ishtar succeeded him and reigned eleven
years more - but it was during or immediately after Libit-Ishtar's own
reign that a local governor at Larsa, a city the dynasty had controlled
through its own priestly appointments at neighboring Ur, broke away and
declared himself king in his own right. The most fully titled reign in
the dynasty's history and the moment its slow fragmentation began
turn out, in the sources, to sit right next to each other.
