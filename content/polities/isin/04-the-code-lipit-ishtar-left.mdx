Libit-Ishtar, Ishme-Dagan's son and successor, is remembered for one thing
above everything else his eleven-year reign accomplished: a collection of
laws issued in his own name, roughly a century and a half before Hammurabi
issued the collection that would eventually eclipse it in fame.

## What the code actually is

The Lipit-Ishtar code survives in the same three-part structure later
Mesopotamian law collections would keep: a prologue in which the king
explains why the gods chose him to establish justice, a body of specific
conditional provisions — if a man does this, then this is the
consequence — covering slavery, inheritance, land tenure, marriage and
the hire of boats and oxen, and an epilogue invoking blessings on whoever
respects the king's work and curses on whoever defaces it. Roth's edition
prints it beside the law collection of Ur-Nammu, two centuries older
still, and beside Hammurabi's, a century and a half younger, and the family
resemblance across all three is immediate: this is a genre with an
established form by the time Libit-Ishtar's scribes sat down to draft it,
not an innovation of his own devising.

## What it was actually for

Like the other collections in Roth's volume, the Lipit-Ishtar code was
almost certainly not a functioning law book in the sense a modern reader
would recognise — no surviving Mesopotamian court record cites it as the
authority for a specific verdict, in this period or any other. What it did,
and did successfully, was declare in permanent, public form that the king
had set the kingdom's affairs in order: a royal claim to justice made
concrete in the same genre a predecessor and a successor both used to make
the identical claim.

## Outliving the dynasty that produced it

The code's actual influence shows up not in courtrooms but in schools.
Copies of the Lipit-Ishtar prologue and provisions continued to be made and
studied by scribal students for centuries after the Dynasty of Isin itself
had ceased to exist, alongside the law of Ur-Nammu and, eventually,
Hammurabi's own — the same three-stage transmission this region's legal
writing shows across a thousand years and multiple languages, in which
Libit-Ishtar's is neither the first entry nor the last, but a genuine link
in the middle of the chain.

A dynasty two-thirds of the way through its own existence, its throne about
to pass out of the founder's family entirely, produced a text that would
still be copied by students long after every other trace of the family had
gone.
