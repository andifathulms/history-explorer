The kingdom of Sumer and Akkad did not die with the fall of Ur; the
throne simply moved. Ibbi-Sin, Ur's last king, had sent one of his own
officers, Ishbi-Erra, north to buy grain during a famine that was
already loosening his grip on the kingdom. While Ishbi-Erra was away
from the capital, an Elamite army overran Ur itself and carried Ibbi-Sin
off in captivity - and rather than return to a throne that no longer
existed, Ishbi-Erra used the resources already in his hands to found a
kingdom of his own at Isin, drove the Elamite garrison back out of Ur,
and took up the old royal title, King of Sumer and Akkad, that his
former master had just lost.

## A dynasty rather than an accident

What might have been a single opportunist's lucky escape became,
instead, a durable royal house. Ishbi-Erra ruled for thirty-two years
and firmly established his own family on the throne, and four of his
direct descendants succeeded him in turn without incident, their
combined reigns spanning ninety-four years. Each claimed, and by the
evidence of building inscriptions at Nippur, Ur and Eridu actually
exercised, real authority over the old Sumerian heartland - not a
government in exile, but the kingdom of Sumer and Akkad genuinely
continuing under a new address.

## A famine that ended one kingdom and began another

The irony the sources preserve is that Ishbi-Erra's entire rise began
with a supply mission, not a battle - he was never recorded fighting
his way to the throne of Ur, only failing to return to a city that had
already fallen behind him. What he built afterward, a hundred miles
away at Isin, would outlast the catastrophe that made it possible by
more than two centuries.
