---
title: A throne without a family
drafted_from: king-1910

---

After Libit-Ishtar, the throne of Isin passed out of Ishbi-Erra's own
family entirely. What followed is not one story but two running side by
side: a capital that kept its old titles and a kingdom that stopped being
able to guarantee who would hold them next.

## The titles outlasted the line

Ur-Ninib, whose relationship to the preceding kings the record does not
state, took the throne after Libit-Ishtar and held it for twenty-eight
years; three of his own descendants followed him in turn, and between them
the new line held Isin for sixty-one years. They styled themselves with
the same full royal titulature — King of Isin, King of Sumer and Akkad —
that Ishbi-Erra's own descendants had claimed, and nothing in the record
suggests any of Isin's subjects treated the new family's kingship as less
legitimate for not descending from the founder. A title, once established,
turned out not to require the specific bloodline that had first claimed
it.

## Larsa stops waiting

It was during this same period that the break at Larsa, already visible
under Libit-Ishtar, took permanent root. Gungunum, governing the city on
Isin's own former authority, had stopped issuing his inscriptions in
Isin's name and begun asserting the old royal claim to Sumer and Akkad in
his own right.
His successors at Larsa spent the following decades steadily converting
that assertion into territory, taking cities and canal rights that had
once been unambiguously Isin's to tax, while Isin's own new ruling family
held its capital and its titles without, on the evidence available, doing
much to reverse Larsa's steady encroachment.

## Five kings, thirty-six years, no family

Ur-Ninib's own line ended after sixty-one years, and what followed was
worse than a change of dynasty: thirty-six and a half years in which five
kings, unrelated to each other so far as the record shows, held the
throne of Isin in rapid and unexplained succession. No source states why
each reign ended when it did or how the next king came to the throne
after it; the pattern itself is the evidence, and what it is evidence of
is a kingdom that had stopped being able to produce, or at least to keep,
an heir capable of holding the position for long.

A capital that still called itself the seat of Sumer and Akkad, ruled in
these decades by a rotating cast of kings with no evident claim on each
other, while a rival forty miles away steadily annexed the territory the
title was supposed to describe: that is the condition Sin-magir inherited
when stability finally returned, and what he and his son made of it is
where the dynasty's story actually ends.
