Only in the dynasty's final generation, after the thirty-six and a half
years of rapid, unrelated kings described in the previous chapter, did the
throne of Isin settle again. Sin-magir and his son Damik-ilishu between
them held it for thirty-four comparatively stable years — the longest run
of orderly succession the dynasty had managed since Ishbi-Erra's own
direct line ended — and it was this final, quieter generation that closed
the dynasty out altogether.

## A question the sources cannot settle

Exactly how the dynasty ended is one of the genuinely unresolved
questions in the record. Later date-formulas commemorate a capture of
the city of Isin - one crediting Rim-Sin, king of Larsa, another
crediting Sin-muballit, a king of Babylon and Hammurabi's own father -
and it has been tempting to read one or both of these captures as the
dynasty's actual end, under Damik-ilishu himself. But the chronology
does not hold up cleanly under scrutiny: Rim-Sin's own reign can be
shown to extend well into the time of Hammurabi's son Samsu-iluna, far
too late for him to have also captured Isin in Damik-ilishu's own
generation, and the balance of evidence instead points to these
recorded captures belonging to a later struggle for the same city
between Larsa and Babylon - one that changed hands three times in
less than eleven years - fought a generation or more after the
Dynasty of Isin itself had already ended by some other, unrecorded
means.

## A kingdom that outlived its own dynasty's memory

What can be said with confidence is only the shape of the outcome: a
line that had held the old royal title of Sumer and Akkad for two and
a quarter centuries disappeared from the king list after Damik-ilishu,
and the city of Larsa - once merely one governorship within Isin's
own kingdom - inherited the contest for supremacy in the south that
Isin itself had won at the very start of the story, from a different
rival, under very different circumstances.
