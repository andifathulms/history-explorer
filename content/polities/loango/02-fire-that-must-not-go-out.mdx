Loango's kingship traced its own origin to a family of smiths, and its
central ritual reflected that origin directly: at his coronation, each
Maloango lit a sacred fire called the ntufia, which was kept burning for
the entire length of his reign and extinguished only at his death. Torches
lit from the same flame were carried out ceremonially to every provincial
capital and, from there, on to individual lineage homes - a single fire,
physically distributed across the whole kingdom, standing in for the
king's own authority reaching every household that answered to him.

## A king who answered for the rain

The king's second great ritual duty concerned the rains the kingdom's
farmers depended on. Each year, at the start of the rainy season, the
capital held a festival in which the people first thanked the king for his
protection over the past year and then asked him directly to bring the
rain the coming season required. At the festival's climax, the king stood
on his own throne and shot an arrow into the sky - and, as one witness to
the ceremony recorded, "there is great rejoicing on that day, because
sometimes they would have rain." The honesty of that qualification is
itself telling: nobody pretended the ritual controlled the weather with
certainty, only that the king's own performance of it was what the
kingdom's own cosmology required before the season could properly begin.

## Ritual authority as the whole of the office

Like several neighboring kingdoms in the region, the Maloango's function
was overwhelmingly ritual rather than directly administrative - a pattern
that made the sacred fire and the rainmaking rite not decoration around
an otherwise secular monarchy, but close to the substance of what made a
king a king at all. It is exactly this kind of authority, resting on
performance and legitimacy rather than on direct coercive power, that
would prove most vulnerable once a different kind of power - wealth
earned through trade rather than descent or ritual office - began
accumulating outside the throne's own control.
