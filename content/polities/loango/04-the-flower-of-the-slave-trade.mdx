---
title: The flower of the slave trade
drafted_from: ogot-1992

Portugal held an early monopoly over trade on this stretch of coast, but
lost it after the Dutch arrived following 1598 and pushed the Portuguese
out of Loango entirely, developing rival trading centers of their own
further north along the coast in the process. What had been, through most
of the sixteenth century, a trade in ivory, dyewoods and palm-cloth began
shifting decisively toward captives after about 1670, and by the 1660s
English and French ships had joined the Dutch in hard competition for a
trade that would not fully wind down until close to 1850.

## Numbers that tell their own story

The scale of the change is traceable in the numbers that survive. Around
1639, the Loango coast - Loango itself, together with the nearby ports of
Malemba and Cabinda - exported perhaps a hundred captives a year. By the
period 1762 to 1778 that figure had risen to roughly twelve thousand five
hundred annually, and between 1780 and 1790 it reached thirteen to
fourteen thousand a year. Slaving captains developed their own
preferences within this trade, favoring captives drawn from Loango and
the coast further south as, in one trader's own description, "the flower
of the slave trade, soft and quiet, used to servitude... always satisfied
with their lot" - a characterization that reveals rather more about the
traders' own self-serving mythology than about the people they were
buying and selling.

## A catchment reaching to the heart of the continent

Ships' account books from the period identify three groups among the
people sold at Loango: the Mayombe, the Vili's own immediate neighbors;
the Monteques, Teke people from Malebo Pool; and the Quibangues, Bubangi
and other river peoples from as far as the Congo and Ubangi rivers - a
catchment area reaching deep into the interior of the continent by the
trade's own peak. The Vili of Loango themselves are almost never recorded
among those actually sold into the external trade; the kingdom that ran
the network profited from selling others rather than from being sold
itself.

## A trade that outgrew the kingdom that started it

Around 1780, Loango lost its position as the region's leading port to
Apomande, on Cape López, and to Malemba and Cabinda - the respective ports
of two smaller states, Kakongo and Ngoyo, both of them former vassals of
the Maloango. The very trade that had made Loango's own name known across
three continents ended by enriching neighboring ports the kingdom had
once ruled over, at Loango's own expense.
