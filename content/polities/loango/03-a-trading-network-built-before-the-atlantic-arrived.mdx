---
title: A trading network built before the Atlantic arrived
drafted_from: ogot-1992
phase: peak

Long before any European ship anchored off Loango's own coast, the
kingdom already ran one of the most complex economies in the region -
food-crop farming combined with diversified craft production that
included the weaving of palm-cloth for clothing and, just as importantly,
for use as currency, alongside the working of ivory, copper and redwood
and the production of salt. These goods moved through local markets, above
all the one at Buali, the capital, and reached buyers well beyond the
kingdom's own borders through a trading network the Vili themselves had
built and maintained.

## A partnership that carried goods to the Pool

That network rested on an alliance between the Vili chiefs of Loango and
the chiefs of neighboring peoples, and it reached remarkably far: as far
as the Gabon estuary, into Kunyi country, and all the way to Malebo Pool,
the trading hub controlled by the Teke. None of this depended on European
demand to exist. When Portuguese, and later Dutch, English and French
traders began arriving on the coast in numbers, they were tapping into
commercial relationships the Vili had already built for their own reasons,
not creating a market where none had existed - the Atlantic trade gave
Loango's own existing economy a new intensity and a new external
dimension, but it did not invent the economy it drew on.

## A kingdom already known by name

The kingdom's reach extended along the coast as well as inland. Loango's
own influence was felt, at least intermittently, as far south as Cape
Sainte-Catherine and possibly Cape López - a range broad enough that
sixteenth-century voyagers passing this stretch of coast could reasonably
expect to hear the Mani Loango's name from more than one direction. A
kingdom this well integrated into regional trade before outside contact
was, in a real sense, prepared for the Atlantic economy years before it
ever arrived - equipped with markets, a currency, and trading partnerships
that the new Atlantic traffic would exploit rather than replace.
