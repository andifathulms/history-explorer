The first visible crack in Loango's own royal succession came in 1701,
when the reigning king died without a settled heir in place. A young
female relative, only twenty-five years old, took power as regent, ruling
through her own council while the kingdom went without a confirmed king
for more than a year before one was finally chosen. In a state whose
wealth depended so heavily on trade, the delay mattered in practical
terms as much as symbolic ones: the regency council's own members were,
inevitably, also the heads of the kingdom's leading trading firms, and
royal authority itself was already beginning to be renegotiated in terms
of who actually controlled commerce rather than who merely held the
sacred title.

## A throne with nobody left to inherit it

Whatever stability the 1701 crisis eventually restored did not last. By
around 1750, the royal bloodline itself died out entirely - not a
disputed succession between rival claimants from the same family, but the
literal end of the family whose right to the ntufia and the rainmaking
rite had never previously been in question. Six factions contended for a
throne that no one among them could claim by the ordinary rules of
descent, and the deadlock was broken only when the kitomi, the kingdom's
own senior religious official, imposed a compromise candidate with no
strong hereditary claim at all - a king installed to end a crisis rather
than because anyone believed he was owed the position.

## A compromise that could not hold either

The arrangement bought time rather than stability. The new royal line the
kitomi had installed suffered its own schism not long afterward, and by
1787 - the last year any source can name a king actually taking office -
the throne itself effectively stopped being filled. What followed was not
conquest, and no single dated event marks it: simply an office that had
existed continuously since before written records of the kingdom began,
now left standing vacant for close to two centuries, kept alive only as
an institution nobody currently occupied rather than one that had been
formally abolished.
