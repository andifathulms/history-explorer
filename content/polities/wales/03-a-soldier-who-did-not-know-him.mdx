The peace of Montgomery did not outlast the decade that followed it.
War with Edward I - harder and more determined than any English king
before him had brought to Wales - broke out again in the early 1280s,
and by the autumn of 1282 Llywelyn found himself facing the whole
weight of a king determined to finish what earlier reigns had left
undone. Edward moved on North Wales in person, undertook the
conquest of Anglesey with the help of the Cinque Ports fleet, and set
about shutting Llywelyn and his brother Dafydd up in their own
mountain stronghold of Eryri - a strategy slow to complete but hard
to answer, for all that Welsh resistance won real successes against
it along the way.

To break the pressure building on Gwynedd, Llywelyn resolved to
carry the war south himself, hoping to raise a diversion in a region
where resistance had begun to flag. In November he appeared in the
highlands of Builth, winning over the local Welsh tenants easily
enough, but the royal castle there - freshly rebuilt and held by John
Giffard's Shropshire levies - proved a harder target. It was in the
course of that siege that the last native Prince of Wales met his
end.

## Struck down by a lance

He did not die at the head of his army in open battle. Hastening back
from some private errand to rejoin the troops holding the north bank
of the river Irfon against an English attack, Llywelyn found himself
separated from his own men in an unregarded corner of the field.
Stephen Frankton, an English soldier who had no idea who he was
fighting, struck him down with a lance; it was only afterward, when
the body was recovered, that anyone realized whom Frankton had
killed. The date was the 11th of December, 1282.

His head was cut off, sent to Edward, displayed before the English
army in Anglesey, and forwarded on to London for the crowd there to
see. His body, once certain ecclesiastical objections had been
satisfied, was buried at the abbey of Cwm Hir, where no trace of the
grave now survives. His wife Eleanor had already died in childbirth
that June; their only child, an infant daughter named Gwenllian,
fell into English hands and spent the whole of her life afterward as
a nun at Sempringham, with no heir of her own to carry the line
forward. Wales could continue fighting without Llywelyn for a few
months more, under Dafydd's own leadership, but as the source itself
records, only Llywelyn had been able to give life to the cause at
all - and with him gone, it had no one left who could.
