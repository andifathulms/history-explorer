Gwynedd, like most Welsh kingdoms, had no settled rule of undivided
succession, and Llywelyn ap Gruffudd shared his own inheritance with
two brothers, Owain and Dafydd. A quarrel over how the kingdom should
be partitioned - probably provoked by demands made on Dafydd's own
behalf - came to a head in the summer of 1255, and rather than let
the dispute fester, the three brothers agreed to settle it by arms.

Llywelyn met their combined forces at Bryn Derwin, in the mountain
pass between Arfon and Eifionydd. The battle did not last long. In
the space of a single hour he broke both armies and took Owain and
Dafydd prisoner, ending the partition question as decisively as any
treaty could have.

## Sole lord of Anglesey and Snowdonia

The victory gave Llywelyn what no division of Gwynedd ever could
have: full and undivided authority over Anglesey and Snowdonia, the
heartland of native Welsh power, held without a rival claim from
within his own family for the rest of his life. It was, in the
account of the source itself, a lordship "only wrested from him with
life itself" - the foundation on which everything Llywelyn achieved
afterward, from the wider conquest of Powys and the south to the
recognition an English king would eventually be forced to grant him,
was built.
