Bryn Derwin gave Llywelyn the standing to act as leader of Wales as a
whole, and over the following decade he used it. English weakness
during the baronial wars against Henry III gave him room to extend
his own authority across Powys and much of the south, and even the
collapse of Simon de Montfort's cause - Llywelyn's own occasional
ally - did him no lasting harm. He had, by that point, built a power
that could stand on its own regardless of who held the upper hand in
England, and it became clear to the English government that no
ordinary settlement would satisfy a prince who had won so much on his
own account.

Eleven years of intermittent war ended, in the autumn of 1267, in
formal negotiation at Shrewsbury. Henry III and the papal legate
Ottobuono spent a month working out terms, and on the 25th of
September they agreed to conditions that met Llywelyn's own demands
in full. Four days later the prince himself crossed to Rhyd Chwima,
the ford of Montgomery, and there did homage and swore fealty to
Henry - not as a defeated rebel accepting terms, but as a ruler whose
claims the treaty recognized essentially whole.

## A title no Welsh ruler had held from an English crown

The Treaty of Montgomery gave Llywelyn what Lloyd's own account calls
a remarkable triumph: formal recognition, by charter, as Prince of
Wales - a title no native Welsh ruler had previously extracted from
an English king, rather than merely claimed for himself. Ottobuono,
delighted at the reconciliation, described Llywelyn as a "great and
puissant member of the English realm" in the document recording the
settlement's own terms. It was the high point of everything Llywelyn
had built since Bryn Derwin: a principality whose existence, and
whose prince's own title, the crown that had spent generations trying
to subdue Wales now formally acknowledged.
