Pusuwan held the Qara Khitai throne for fourteen years, longer than
either of the two emperors she ruled between, and the Liao shi records
her downfall in as much detail as it gives any single event in the
dynasty's whole reign in the west - because the men who finally killed
her were the same family that had made her empire's conquests possible
in the first place.

## The regent

When Yilie died in 1155 leaving a son too young to rule, the regency
passed to his own younger sister, Pusuwan, who took the title Chengtian
Taihou and reigned under her own era-name, Chongfu, for fourteen
years - the longest single tenure of anyone who ever held the Qara
Khitai throne apart from Yelü Dashi himself. She had married Xiao
Duolu, son of Xiao Wa-li-la, the general who had led Yelü Dashi's own
cavalry divisions at the founding of the empire in Central Asia and
whose family's loyalty had underwritten every gurkhan's authority
since.

## The affair the general would not tolerate

Pusuwan took her husband's own younger brother, Pugu Dishali, as a
lover, and when the affair threatened exposure she had her husband
Xiao Duolu murdered to clear the way - after first, with what the
sources treat as a particularly pointed insult, granting him the
honorary title Prince of Dongping. It was a miscalculation. Xiao
Wa-li-la, the murdered man's own father and the dynasty's most senior
surviving general, surrounded the palace with troops rather than accept
the killing of his son. Pusuwan and her lover were both shot to death
with arrows.

## What survived her

The dynasty did not merely survive the killing of a reigning gurkhan by
its own senior general - it settled immediately, without a further
succession crisis, on Yilie's second son, Zhilugu, who took the throne
under the era-name Tianxi and, by the Liao shi's own count, held it for
the longest reign of any Qara Khitai ruler after the founder - the
Chinese and Persian chronicles differ by several years on exactly when
that reign ended, which is a dating quarrel rather than a competing
account of the reign itself. What Xiao Wa-li-la's intervention actually
protected was not one woman's authority but the principle that had let
two of them hold it in the first place: that the throne belonged to
Yelü Dashi's own bloodline, male or female, regent or empress, and that
the loyalty the conquest generation owed it did not depend on the sex
of whoever currently held it. Zhilugu's own reign would end
differently - not at the hands of a general defending the dynasty, but
at the hands of a refugee it had sheltered.
