---
title: How to rule what you cannot outnumber
drafted_from: biran-2005
phase: aside
---

The census of the Qara Khitai heartland records something like eighty-four
thousand households. That is a small people holding suzerainty over
Transoxiana, the Semirechye and the Tarim, and everything characteristic about
how they governed follows from it.

They ruled indirectly and almost entirely through people who were already
there. The Karakhanids kept their thrones at Samarkand and Kashgar, the
Khwarazmshahs kept Khwarazm, the Buddhist Uyghur kingdom of Qocho kept the
Tarim, and each remitted tribute and received a Qara Khitai resident to make
sure of it. There was no attempt to settle Khitans on the land, no
redistribution of estates, and — this is the part the Muslim sources
consistently notice — no interference in religion.

The rulers were Buddhist. Their subjects were overwhelmingly Muslim, with
Nestorian Christian, Buddhist and Jewish communities among them. Mosques
functioned, qadis sat, the law was applied, and the Friday sermon was preached
for local rulers rather than for the gurkhan. Muslim writers who had every
reason to describe infidel rule as a calamity mostly describe it as
unobjectionable, and several are notably warmer than that.

Set that against the corpus. This site's Almohad page has a caliphate resting
on an infallible mahdi; its Fatimid page has a state whose foreign policy was a
missionary organisation; its Abbasid page has the mihna, an examination of
judges on a point of doctrine. The Qara Khitai are the polity here that had no
religious programme at all — not tolerance as a principle argued for, but the
practical indifference of a ruling group too small to impose anything and too
sensible to try.

Two further oddities belong in the same chapter. They kept Chinese imperial
forms nobody around them could read, which was a claim addressed backwards to a
dynasty that no longer existed rather than outwards to anyone present. And the
throne twice passed to women who ruled in their own right — Dashi's widow as
regent for her son, and later his daughter Pusuwan as gurkhan — which almost
nothing else in this corpus records, and which the Muslim chroniclers report
with less comment than a reader might expect.
