The empire lasted about eighty-five years and left three things: a reputation
the Mongols spent, a name for China in half the languages of Eurasia, and a
figure Europe waited four hundred years for.

## Cathay

The Khitan ruled the northern Chinese frontier for two centuries, and everyone
who reached China across the steppe reached it through them, so *Khitan* became
the word for the country beyond.

It went west with the Qara Khitai and stayed. Russian still calls China *Kitai*.
Mongolian, Turkic and Persian all carry versions of it. It reached Latin
Christendom through the Mongol-era travellers as *Cathay*, and it was still
being used as though it named a country distinct from China three hundred years
later — which is why Portuguese and Jesuit expeditions went looking for Cathay
after Europeans were already living in Canton.

A dynasty that had ceased to exist in China in 1125 gave the country its name in
the language of everyone who approached it from the west, and the name outlived
every state that ever used it.

## Prester John

The other export was a rumour.

Word travelled west after Qatwan that a great king from the far east, not a
Muslim, had destroyed a Muslim empire in an enormous battle beyond Persia.
Within a few years chroniclers in the Latin kingdoms were reporting a Christian
priest-king in the east who would come to the aid of the Crusader states, and
the letters, maps and expectations attached to that figure ran for four hundred
years — attaching themselves in turn to the Mongols, and eventually to
Ethiopia.

The scholarship generally traces the story to news of 1141. Europe spent four
centuries waiting for rescue from a Buddhist Khitan who had never heard of it.

## The reputation the Mongols inherited

The most consequential legacy is the one that is hardest to see, because it
looks like a Mongol achievement.

For eighty years the Qara Khitai had governed Transoxiana and the Tarim by
leaving everything alone: local dynasties on their thrones, a resident to
collect the tribute, mosques open, qadis sitting, no settlement of Khitans on
the land and no interference in religion. Muslim writers who had every reason to
call infidel rule a calamity mostly describe it as unobjectionable.

Then Kuchlug reversed it in seven years, and when Jebe's army arrived in 1218
proclaiming the restoration of religious freedom, the oasis populations treated
a pagan Mongol advance as a deliverance.

The Mongols got Transoxiana's approaches without a battle, and they got
something more useful: a demonstration, in front of the whole Muslim east, that
they were the ones who let people pray. They traded on that reputation across
the entire conquest. It was earned, in this case, by being less bad than a
usurper who had wrecked an arrangement somebody else had built.

## The women

One thing the Muslim chroniclers record with notably little comment, and it is
worth setting down.

The throne passed twice to women ruling in their own right: Yelü Dashi's widow
as regent for their son, and later his daughter Pusuwan as gurkhan. Both issued
orders, both were obeyed, and the Persian and Arabic historians who describe
them do so in the ordinary register they use for any sovereign.

For a Buddhist steppe dynasty exercising suzerainty over the Muslim heartland of
Central Asia, that is the most quietly remarkable fact about it, and nobody at
the time appears to have thought it worth an argument.
