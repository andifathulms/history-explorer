---
title: The day Transoxiana stopped being Muslim-ruled
drafted_from: biran-2005
phase: peak
---

In September 1141, on the Qatwan steppe north of Samarkand, the Qara Khitai
destroyed the army of Sanjar, the last great Seljuk sultan. It was the heaviest
defeat any Muslim power suffered before the Mongols, and it is why this dynasty
turns up so often as somebody else's overlord.

The consequences were immediate and structural. Transoxiana — Bukhara,
Samarkand, the country the Samanids and Karakhanids had made — became tributary
to a non-Muslim power for the first time since the Arab conquests. The
Karakhanids continued to reign and began to remit. The Khwarazmshahs became
tributaries too and stayed that way for two generations. Seljuk authority east
of Khurasan never recovered.

The other consequence happened in Europe and is one of the strangest in
medieval history. Word travelled west that a great king from the far east, not a Muslim,
had destroyed a Muslim empire in a huge battle beyond Persia. Within a few
years chroniclers in the Latin kingdoms were reporting a Christian priest-king
in the east — Prester John — who would come to the aid of the Crusader states,
and the letters and expectations attached to that figure ran for four hundred
years. The scholarship generally traces the story to news of Qatwan.

What the victory did not produce is an occupation. The Qara Khitai took
tribute, kept a resident to collect it, left the local dynasties on their
thrones, and did not garrison the cities or interfere with their religion. That
is the subject of the next chapter, and it is the reason this empire is
remembered by its subjects less bitterly than almost anyone else who beat them.
