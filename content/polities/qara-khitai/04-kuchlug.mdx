Kuchlug was a prince of the Naiman, a confederation on the western Mongolian
steppe that Chinggis Khan broke in 1204. He fled west, was received at the Qara
Khitai court, married the gurkhan's daughter, and in 1211 took the throne from
his father-in-law.

Then he did the one thing his predecessors had never done. Kuchlug — raised
Nestorian Christian, converted to Buddhism, and on any account a man in a hurry
— began persecuting his Muslim subjects: closing mosques and madrasas in the
Tarim oases, requiring conversion to Buddhism or Christianity, and reportedly
having the imam of Khotan crucified on the door of his own school. Eighty years
of a religious policy that had made an infidel suzerainty tolerable were
reversed in about seven.

The Mongols arrived in 1218 under Jebe with instructions that are the point of
this chapter: proclaim the restoration of religious freedom. The oasis
populations of the Tarim, faced with a choice between a Buddhist persecutor and
a pagan army, treated the Mongol advance as a deliverance and largely did not
resist it. Kuchlug was hunted into the Pamirs and killed. The empire ended
without a battle worth the name.

Two things this leaves for the rest of the site. The Mongols acquired
Transoxiana's approaches cheaply and with a reputation for religious tolerance
they then traded on across the whole conquest — a reputation earned in this
particular case by being less bad than a usurper. And Khwarazm, which had spent
the previous decade throwing off Qara Khitai suzerainty, discovered in the same
year that the buffer between it and the steppe was gone. Its page describes
what came next.

The record types this ending `conquest`, which is accurate and thin. A polity
that had governed by not asking anything of its subjects was destroyed by a
ruler who asked, and finished off by an army its subjects preferred.
