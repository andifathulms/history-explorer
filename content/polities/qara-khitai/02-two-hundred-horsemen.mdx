The Liao dynastic history contains a short account of the man who refounded it
two thousand miles away, and Bretschneider translated it in full. It is the
Qara Khitai founding as told by the Chinese historians of the state Yelü Dashi
walked out of, and it is better than any summary of it.

## The man

> He was a descendant in the eighth generation of Tai-tsu. He was well versed in
> the literature of the Liao, as well as in that of the Chinese; and besides
> this he was a clever horseman and skilful archer. In 1115 he received the
> degree of shi, and was admitted a member of the Hanlin academy.

A Khitan prince of the imperial line with a Chinese doctorate and a seat in the
national academy, who could also shoot from the saddle. That combination is the
whole of what he did next.

## The answer to the emperor

When the Jurchen broke the Liao, the emperor Tianzuo abandoned his throne and
took to roving about. Dashi assembled the dignitaries and put a prince of the
imperial family on the throne instead. The emperor, when he heard, had the
regent put to death and reprimanded Dashi for raising a rival while the
legitimate emperor was still alive.

Dashi answered him:

> Even at the time when the empire was still in full vigour, your majesty had
> not been able once to repulse the enemy, but had fled far away, abandoning the
> people in their calamities; and had I then placed Shun on the throne, the
> reigning family would still have been descendants of our ancestor Tai-tsu. Has
> it not been the more dignified course now thus to act, instead of imploring
> the enemy for mercy?

*To this the emperor had nothing to reply. He entertained Ta-shi with a feast and
pardoned him.*

Dashi did not feel safe. He killed two of the court dignitaries, declared
himself sovereign, and left by night at the head of two hundred well-armed
horsemen, heading north.

## What two hundred horsemen accumulate

Three days out, across the Etsina river, an Ongut chief gave him four hundred
horses, twenty camels and about a thousand sheep. He went on west to a city
called Ketun and rested, and there he assembled the chiefs of seven cities and
eighteen tribes, whose names the Liao history lists.

They raised more than ten thousand horsemen. He appointed officers and armed
them.

In the second month of 1121 he sacrificed a black bull and a white horse to
heaven, to earth and to his ancestors, put his troops in order and set out west.

## The letter to the Uyghurs

Before he moved he had written to the Uyghur king, and the letter is a small
masterpiece of the diplomatic use of history.

He reminded the king that his own ancestor, the first Liao emperor, had passed
through Uyghur country and sent word to the king's ancestor: *do you still
cherish the remembrance of your ancient country? I shall occupy it for you if
you wish to return.* The Uyghur had thanked him and declined, saying his people
had been away more than ten generations, were quite happy where they were, and
had no desire to go back.

*This proves that we have been for a long time on good terms with your people.*
Therefore: I intend to advance west, I ask permission to pass through your
country, do not entertain suspicion.

The king came out to meet him, spent three days entertaining him, gave him six
hundred horses, a hundred camels and three thousand sheep, handed over sons and
grandsons as hostages, declared himself a vassal, and escorted him to the
border of his realm.

That exchange cost nothing and bought a safe corridor, remounts and a client —
using an offer his great-great-grandfather had made three hundred years earlier
and which had been refused.

## Ten thousand li

From there, the history says, he vanquished everyone he met and kept the peace
with those who surrendered voluntarily, and the army advanced ten thousand li,
overthrowing kingdoms and taking camels, horses, cattle, sheep and goods beyond
counting.

At Samarkand he met the combined army of all the western kingdoms, a hundred
thousand men. What he said to his troops before it is the last thing in the
Chinese account worth quoting, and it is a professional's assessment:

> We see before us a numerous host, but it wants an able commander. When they
> are attacked in front, the rear will fail to come to the rescue, and there can
> be no doubt our army will gain the victory.

He split into three: twenty-five hundred against the right flank, twenty-five
hundred against the left, and the main body at the centre, all going in
together. The enemy army was destroyed, and for ten li the ground was covered
with bodies.

He rested at Samarkand for ninety days.
