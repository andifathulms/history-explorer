---
title: A monastery summoned to court
drafted_from: bell-1924

Buddhism in its Lamaist form had spent the twelfth and thirteenth
centuries steadily extending its hold over a Tibetan population
already inclined toward spirits and mysticism, and one monastery in
particular had built a reputation substantial enough to reach beyond
Tibet's own borders. Sakya, in the province of Tsang, was the seat of
a lineage of teachers whose learning and religious authority had grown
large enough that a Mongol prince - a grandson of the empire's own
founder - summoned its High Priest to his court.

## An invitation, not a summons to surrender

The distinction mattered. This was not a defeated ruler being dragged
before a conqueror; it was a patron seeking a teacher, in a pattern
the Mongol court would repeat with other religious traditions across
its own vast territory. The Sakya hierarch went, and the relationship
that began there - a spiritual teacher attached to a secular ruler
powerful enough to protect and promote him - would eventually be
inherited by his own successor and carried to the very top of the
Mongol world.

## A liberal reputation, recorded even by outsiders

What survives of the character of these early Sakya teachers suggests
men more interested in genuine religious instruction than in factional
dominance. One is recorded as having been "very liberal as regards
religious opinions," encouraging everyone he met "to do his best and
to be earnest in his creed, whatever it might be" - an attitude that
served the relationship well as it deepened from one Mongol prince's
private patronage into something considerably larger.
