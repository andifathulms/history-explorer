title: Sovereignty given, not taken

The relationship between Sakya and the Mongol court reached its fullest
expression under Khubilai Khan, the man who would go on to become the
first Mongol emperor of China. In 1270, having invited the Sakya
hierarch of the period to his own court much as his relative had once
done, Khubilai became a convert to Lamaism himself - and in the same
gesture, gave the sovereignty of all Tibet to his religious teacher.
Thus began, in Tibet, the rule of the priest-kings.

## A kingdom that required no army to win

What makes this transfer of power unusual is that it was not really a
conquest at all: no Tibetan army was defeated and no Tibetan capital
was besieged to bring it about. A foreign emperor simply decided that
religious authority and secular rule over Tibet belonged together, and
handed both to the same institution. The Sakya hierarchy did not seize
the country; the country was given to it, by decree, from outside.

## Seventy-five years under a foreign patron's shadow

The arrangement held for roughly seventy-five years, from about 1270
to 1345, a period the record describes as one in which Indian
religious teachers continued to make their way into Tibet and the
Tantric form of Buddhism strengthened its hold on the country. But
the dependency ran in only one direction. The Sakya hierarchs owed
their own political authority entirely to Mongol backing, a debt that
would prove costly the moment that backing could no longer be relied
upon.
