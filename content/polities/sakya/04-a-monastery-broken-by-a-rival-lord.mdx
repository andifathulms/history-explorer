The power of Sakya did not survive the loosening of Mongol authority
that produced it. King Changchub Gyaltsen, a Tibetan lord operating
outside the Sakya hierarchy's own religious establishment, brought
nearly the whole of Tibet under his own personal sway and broke the
Sakya priest-kings' hold on secular power outright. What he built in
its place is remembered in Tibetan tradition as a Second Monarchy - a
deliberate echo of the old imperial line that Langdarma's assassination
had ended centuries before.

## Keeping the religion, discarding the administration

Changchub Gyaltsen's own break with the Sakya regime was selective
rather than total. He continued to promote Buddhism much as his
predecessors had, showing no interest in undoing the religious revival
the Sakya period had overseen. What he did discard were the
specifically Chinese and Mongol administrative habits the Sakya
priest-kings had imported along with their political authority - the
foreign machinery of governance, rather than the foreign religion that
had spread through it, was what the new dynasty chose to reject.

## A martial vigor that did not fully return

Even so, the record is candid that something had changed permanently
in the transition. Tibet's own military vigor was, by this account,
not what it had once been, and Chinese and Mongol influence continued
to be felt in the country regardless of who held nominal power at
Sakya or anywhere else. The dynasty that replaced Sakya's priest-kings
still depended, in some measure, on the same larger power to its east
that had made the Sakya hierarchy's own rise possible in the first
place - proof that ending one arrangement of dependency did not mean
escaping dependency altogether.
