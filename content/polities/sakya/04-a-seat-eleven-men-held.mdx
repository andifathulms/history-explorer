The Yuan dynasty's own official history kept a list of every man who
held the title Ti shih - Imperial Preceptor - the seat Phagpa had first
occupied and the office through which the Mongol court actually
governed Tibet. It runs, without a gap in the record even when there
was a gap in the succession, from 1280 to 1330: eleven appointments in
fifty years, each one confirmed from Beijing rather than simply
inherited.

## A title, not a birthright

Phagpa was succeeded by his own brother Rinchen, who died in 1282 and
was followed by Dharmapala Rakshita, dead within four years. Yeshe
Rinchen held the seat for nine years, to 1295 - the longest single
tenure of the whole run - and after him the office changed hands seven
more times in the next thirty-five years, several appointees dying
within a year or two of taking it up. It was not a hereditary
succession dressed up as an appointment: every Ti shih needed a fresh
imperial decree and a fresh seal, and the Yuan shih records the decree
each time exactly as it recorded the death that made the next one
necessary.

## The seat filled by an outsider

The clearest proof that Beijing, not Sakya, controlled the office came
when the arrangement broke down from the inside. At one point the
Sakya throne's own senior administrator - the figure Tibetan sources
call the Great Administrator - was taken to China in what amounted to
exile, and the vacant Ti shih seat was filled not by a member of the
Sakya family at all but by his own chaplain, a lama named Dragpa Odzer
who belonged to no Sakya lineage whatsoever. The arrangement that
supposedly gave "all Tibet" to one monastic house had, at its actual
moment of crisis, no difficulty replacing that house's own
representative with someone outside it entirely - proof, as much as
any battle would have been, of where the real authority sat.

## Fifty years, eleven men, one office

What the list actually shows is an institution running exactly as
designed: a religious lineage supplying candidates, an imperial court
approving them one at a time, and a political arrangement that
depended entirely on Mongol confirmation continuing to be granted.
Every letter the Ti shih issued in this period opened with the same
formula - by order of the Emperor - because that, and not the Sakya
family's own religious prestige, was the actual source of the
authority behind it. When Yuan confirmation itself became worth less,
in the decades after 1330, the office it had propped up did not long
outlast it.
