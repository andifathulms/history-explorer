Religious patronage gave the Sakya hierarchy its authority, but
running an actual country required administrative machinery, and the
machinery the Mongols brought with them was one built for the steppe
rather than for a settled, monastic society. In 1268, agricultural
estates across central Tibet were reorganized around the household as
the basic unit of account - the first time the region had been
counted this systematically, and in a currency of tens rather than
anything native to Tibetan administrative tradition.

## A hierarchy built entirely on multiples of ten

The system climbed in fixed steps: fifty households made one
"horse-head" unit, two of those made a century, ten centuries made a
chiliarchy, and ten chiliarchies made a myriarchy - ten thousand
households, in principle, under one administrative head. Thirteen such
myriarchies eventually covered the country, though the sources that
describe the system suggest the real number in practice sometimes
exceeded that figure. It was the same decimal logic the Mongols had
used to organize their own armies and subject peoples everywhere else
in their empire, applied here to tax rolls and land registers instead
of soldiers.

## A province too small to qualify, made one anyway

Tibet itself was organized into three circuits - U, Tsang and Ngari -
each in principle large enough to contain ten myriarchies, which
would in turn have made a full province under the standard imperial
scheme. Tibet's actual population fell short of that threshold. Rather
than deny the country provincial status on a technicality, Khubilai
Khan decreed that Tibet would be considered one province regardless,
out of respect for its religious character - a formal exception made
for the very country whose religion had converted him in the first
place.
