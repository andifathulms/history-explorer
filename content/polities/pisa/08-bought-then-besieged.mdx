Gabriello Maria Visconti, unpopular and short of money, agreed in
September 1405 to sell the citadel of Pisa and two outlying castles to
Florence for 206,000 golden florins. On September 12 the Florentine envoy
Gino Capponi took formal possession of the citadel - only for the Pisans
themselves to rise as one, storm it back from the Florentine garrison,
and send an imperious demand to Florence for the return of the castles
they had already been promised in exchange. Florence refused. What
followed was not a negotiation but a siege: from March 1406 a Florentine
army lay before the city, blockading it by land while a chain of forts
along the Arno kept Sicilian grain ships from reaching Pisa's own port.

## A city that turned to Milan, Naples and Burgundy in turn

Facing starvation, the Pisans offered their city outright to King
Ladislaus of Naples, who refused it; appealed to the King of France, whose
envoy told Florence to leave "his city of Pisa" alone and was informed in
reply that Florence had already bought it and fair; and, as a last resort,
appealed to the Duke of Burgundy, whose own messenger's arrogant demand
that the siege be lifted ended with the Florentines throwing him in the
river. None of it changed anything outside the walls. Inside them, the city's
two ruling factions - the ghibelline Agnello and the guelph Gambacorti -
had briefly set aside years of exile and feuding at the Elders' own
insistence, only for Giovanni Gambacorti to have his rival murdered within
days of being recalled and seize personal lordship of the city in the
run-up to the siege itself.

## Terms bought with someone else's hunger

It was Gambacorti who, in the end, opened secret talks with Gino Capponi.
His own price was generous: fifty thousand florins, the islands of Giglio
and Capraia, Florentine citizenship and tax exemption for himself and his
heirs, a castle for his brother, an archbishopric for his cousin - along
with an assurance that the Elders would keep their nominal authority over
Pisa's own finances and that no citizen would be robbed or insulted.
Florence agreed. On October 9, 1406, the Florentines entered a city so
reduced by famine that soldiers who tossed bread to onlookers reportedly
watched some of them die from eating too much, too fast, after months
without it. The Republic's own flag went up in place of Pisa's, three
centuries of independent government ended in an afternoon, and Pisa's own
harbours at Leghorn had already, months earlier, passed into the hands of
a French garrison at Genoa - cutting the fallen Republic off from the sea
that had made it.
