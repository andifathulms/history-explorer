title: A city sold twice over

The fourteenth century gave Pisa one genuine respite in Piero Gambacorti,
elected Captain and Defender of the Commune and the People in 1369 after
years of factional exile and imperial interference. A capable and
popular ruler, Gambacorti tried to use his own standing to broker a
federation among the Italian states themselves - an attempt to settle
disputes between them by arbitration rather than by the wandering
mercenary companies that had come to infest the peninsula. Delegates from
across Italy actually met in Pisa in October 1388 to discuss it.

## A trusted secretary who was not to be trusted

That peace conference was also Gambacorti's undoing. Giovan Galeazzo
Visconti, Duke of Milan, bribed Gambacorti's own long-trusted secretary,
old Jacopo d'Appiano, through d'Appiano's son, who was already in
Visconti's service. When the son arrived in Pisa at the head of Lombard
cavalry, the father dropped all pretence: he had one of Gambacorti's
closest friends murdered, and when Gambacorti himself came out to help
restore order he was struck down along with his two sons. Jacopo
d'Appiano made himself Lord of Pisa in the same stroke that killed the
man who had trusted him longest.

## Sold for two hundred thousand florins

D'Appiano's own rule did not outlive him by much. His son Gherardo,
succeeding him in 1398, buckled under a mixture of threats and bribes
from the same Gian Galeazzo Visconti his father had once outmanoeuvred,
and sold the city of Pisa outright to the Duke of Milan for two hundred
thousand golden florins - keeping only Piombino, a scatter of castles, and
the island of Elba for himself. Pisans who begged to be allowed to buy
back their own city at a higher price were flatly refused; the sale
stood. When Gian Galeazzo died in 1402 he left Pisa to an illegitimate
son, Gabriello Maria Visconti, whose own unpopularity and financial
desperation would, within three years, put the city up for sale a second
time - this time to Florence.
