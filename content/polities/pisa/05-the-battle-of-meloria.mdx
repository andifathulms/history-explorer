---
title: The Battle of Meloria

In August 1282 a Pisan fleet sailed insolently to the mouth of Genoa's own
harbour, taunting the Genoese to come out and fight; the Genoese, their
ships not ready, made excuses and let them go. The Pisans took the refusal
as cowardice. Two years later, in 1284, they armed seventy galleys and
repeated the insult, then withdrew to their own port to wait, daring the
Genoese to follow. This time Genoa answered. Under the command of Oberto
Doria, a Genoese fleet the chroniclers put at over a hundred sail entered
Pisan waters that August, and the Pisans, some eighty-three galleys in
all, put out under their own Podestà, Alberto Morosini, and their
admiral, Ugolino della Gherardesca, to meet them off a rock in sight of
their own harbour called Meloria.

## An omen no one heeded

Contemporary writers record that as the fleet's standard was blessed
before departure, its cross and ball fell from the mast - an evil omen the
Pisans chose to ignore as they sailed out shouting for battle. The fight
that followed off Meloria was long and hard-fought, and by its end the
Genoese had won completely. Villani, writing in Florence, put the Pisan
losses at sixteen thousand dead or captured and forty galleys taken;
Sismondi's own tally, drawn from other chroniclers, gives five thousand
dead, eleven thousand prisoners and twenty-eight ships lost. The two
counts do not agree, and no reconciling them would be honest - what both
agree on is that the loss was total, and that there was scarcely a house
in Pisa that had not lost a son, husband or father either to the sea or to
a Genoese prison. From that defeat, contemporaries said, Pisa never
recovered her former power; the proverb that followed put it more
bluntly still - "he who would see Pisa must go to Genoa."

## An admiral who did not go down with his fleet

Blame settled quickly on Ugolino della Gherardesca himself, who was said
to have fled once the Podestà's own galley was captured and to have been
the first to bring the news of the disaster home. Yet the same Council
that might have condemned him instead named him Podestà and Captain of
the Forces within days, apparently gambling that his standing at Florence
and Lucca might keep Pisa's guelph neighbours from combining against a
city that could no longer defend itself at sea.
