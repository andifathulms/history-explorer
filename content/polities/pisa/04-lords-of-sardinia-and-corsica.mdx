By the middle years of the thirteenth century Pisa stood, on paper, at
the height of everything its own crusading fleets had built. The
Florentine chronicler Giovanni Villani, no friend to Pisa, could still
write that in 1282 "there were more powerful and rich citizens in Pisa
than in any other city in Italy": judges from the Sardinian giudicati of
Gallura and Arborea kept court there, the Pisans were "lords of Sardinia,
of Corsica, and of Elba," their fleets held command of the sea, and in the
Crusader port of Acre they counted among the most powerful of all the
Italian trading nations.

## A rivalry that had never really cooled

That wealth had never gone unchallenged. The struggle with Genoa that
would eventually break Pisa had opened as far back as 1119, in a dispute
over a papal privilege, and had never really been resolved since: the
Tyrrhenian Sea, Pisan and Genoese writers alike understood, was too
narrow a stage for two mistresses. In Acre itself Pisan merchants used
their influence among the city's own burghers to drive out their Genoese
rivals by fire and sword in the early 1280s, and when the Genoese
retaliated with a raiding fleet the Pisans answered with open contempt,
sailing to the mouth of Genoa's own harbour to jeer at ships they
considered too cowardly to fight.

## A greatness resting on no natural foundation

What Pisa's own wealth concealed was a structural weakness Genoa did not
share. Genoa's harbour and its mountain-locked hinterland left its own
citizens no livelihood but the sea and no choice but unity of purpose in
defending it; a whole seafaring population, from one end of the Ligurian
coast to the other, stood ready to man new fleets the moment old ones
were lost. Pisa had no such natural anchor. Its own port lay on a silting
river mouth, its hinterland pulled it constantly into land wars with
Lucca and Florence, and its maritime power had always been, in effect, an
artificial creation - immensely profitable while ships and sailors held
out, but with no deep reserve behind it if they were ever lost all at
once.
