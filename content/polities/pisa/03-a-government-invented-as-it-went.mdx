Pisa's consulate was never designed; it accumulated. Its most direct
ancestor was the Visconte, a hereditary deputy of the Countess Matilda who
shared the city's government with the Bishop and whose office, like so
many in early medieval Italy, was eventually adopted by its holders as
their own family name. Beneath and alongside him stood a Commune
Consilium of "wise men" advising the Bishop, and a general Colloquium
Civitatis in which the whole citizen body could, in theory, be summoned by
the ringing of the cathedral bells - the ancestor of what would later
become a formal Parlamentum.

## A commission that never quite stopped being temporary

Even the consulship itself began as nothing grander than a balia: a
short-term commission appointed for one specific task, most often to
command a fleet, and dissolved once that task was done. Only after a long
series of such ad hoc appointments did the office harden into something
permanent. The associated merchant families who filled it were not, in
any strict sense, elected representatives of the wider population; they
were the same armatori who had always run the city's trade and shipping,
and the mass of ordinary Pisans no more expected a voice in choosing
consuls than they expected a voice in the maritime ventures of the
individual shipowners who employed them. Private enterprise and public
authority were, for most of the twelfth century, barely distinguishable:
even the fleets that sailed on the great crusading expeditions were
largely fitted out and crewed by private citizens acting, in the eyes of
outsiders, as though they represented the Commune itself.

## A cathedral standing in for a constitution

What held this improvised structure together was not any single office
but the cathedral itself - described by contemporaries as the true home
and fortress of the Pisan people, the place where spoils of victory were
dedicated, where the consuls heard citizens' grievances, and where, each
year on the Vigil of the Assumption, the towns of Pisa's own contado sent
wax candles in token of their subjection. The city's unity, in other
words, was expressed in stone and ritual well before it was expressed in
any settled office of state - a fitting emblem for a republic that spent
its first century governing itself without ever quite deciding, in
writing, how.
