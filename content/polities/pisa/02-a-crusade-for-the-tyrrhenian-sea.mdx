A century after Mogahid's expulsion, Saracen corsairs still held the
Balearic Islands, raiding the coasts of Catalonia and southern France and
holding Majorca so full of Christian captives that, by 1113, Pisan
commerce in the western Mediterranean had become intolerable. The Pisans
resolved to end it, and found allies in the Counts of Barcelona and
Montpellier and the Viscount of Narbonne; because their enemies were also
enemies of the Cross, the enterprise received the Pope's own blessing.

## An archbishop who led the fleet in person

On Easter Sunday, Archbishop Pietro Moriconi climbed into the pulpit of
the Pisan Duomo and preached the crusade himself, promising to lead the
Republic's own fleet as his predecessor Daibert had once threatened to do.
Twelve consuls were elected for the campaign alone, and the Archbishop
travelled to Rome to receive the cross directly from Pope Paschal II, who
gave the expedition a red banner - the origin of the white-cross-on-red
arms Pisa carries to this day. That a churchman, rather than any lay
magistrate, headed the embassy and commanded the fleet says as much about
the young Commune's own government as it does about the crusade: the
Archbishop was still, by feudal right as much as by holy orders, the
foremost lord in Pisa, and the noblest consular families of the city were
still formally his vassals.

## Fifty days at the walls of Palma

The campaign dragged into 1114 and 1115, culminating in a siege of the
Balearic capital. When the besieged offered generous terms - the freeing
of every captive, an enormous indemnity - the allied counts were ready to
accept, but the Pisans, backed by the bishops and clergy who had sailed
with them, refused to leave a nest of pirates standing and pressed the
siege through the winter. The outer walls fell in February 1115, the
second circuit weeks later, and the citadel itself on the third of April.
More than fifty thousand of the defenders are said to have been killed and
some thirty thousand Christian captives freed; the plunder included gold
and silver church ornaments looted years earlier from Spain and Provence,
and the ruler who had held the island died during the siege, his
successor carried off to Pisa in chains along with his own family.

## A city that had led, not merely fought

The victory did more than clear the sea lanes: it left Pisa, for a
season, at the head of a genuinely international Christian coalition,
commanding counts and bishops from three different lands in a campaign
its own Archbishop had preached and led. Within a few years Pisan
colonies and trading quarters stretched from Sardinia and Corsica to the
Crusader ports of the Levant, and the city's merchants counted themselves,
without much exaggeration, among the wealthiest in Italy.
