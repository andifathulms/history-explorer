Florentine rule brought Pisa little but decline. Cut off from its own
port and shipping trade, its chief citizens periodically ordered into
supervised exile after a plot to hand the city to a mercenary captain was
uncovered, Pisa within forty years had lost much of its population to
abandoned fields and marsh fever. When a French army under Charles VIII
of France marched through the city on its way to Naples in 1494, an
orator named Orlandi caught the King's own mantle and begged him to
restore Pisa's liberty - and the single word "liberty," the only one the
watching crowd could make out of his petition, was enough. Florence's
lions and lilies were torn down from the public buildings within hours;
by one contemporary's account, a dead republic woke to life in a moment.

## A liberty three different powers tried to sell out from under it

Charles's own commanders had no real interest in Pisan freedom for its
own sake - keeping Pisa and Leghorn out of Florentine hands made Charles,
in effect, master of the coast from Marseilles to Naples, and only the
Pisans' own tearful pleading, and the bribes of soldiers left unpaid, kept
him from formally handing the city back at all. When he finally did sign
away Pisa's citadel to Florence at Asti in 1495, its own garrison
commander defied the order and sold it to the Pisans directly for cash
instead, most of it quietly supplied by Milan, Venice and Genoa - each of
which wanted Pisa for its own reasons and none of which, once they fell
to quarrelling among themselves, was willing to actually defend it.

## Fifteen years, three sieges, and a river that would not be moved

Left to fend for itself, the revived Republic withstood three separate
Florentine sieges over the next decade and a half. During one of them, in
1503, Florence tried to starve the city out for good by digging two great
canals meant to divert the Arno away from Pisa entirely; Leonardo da
Vinci himself inspected the works, which employed some eight thousand
labourers, before a flood washed the central dam away and the scheme was
abandoned. Florence turned instead to blockade: fortified camps at the
mouths of the Arno, the Serchio and the Fiume Morto choked off what little
still reached the city by water. Hunger did what the canals could not.
On June 8, 1509, the Florentines re-entered Pisa, and this time there was
no third revival to follow: the city's own independent government, first
attested in a bishop's charter four centuries earlier, did not return
until Italy itself was unified.
