Pisa's own communal government did not begin with a charter or a single
founding act. It grew, slowly and almost by accident, out of an existing
association of shipowners and merchant families - the armatori - who ran
their fleets, punished their own crews, made their own private treaties
with foreign powers, and fought their own private wars in Sardinia and
along the Tuscan coast, long before any magistracy existed to represent
them collectively. Until the Empire recognized the Commune's existence,
that association was, strictly speaking, illegal; it survived only because
the Bishop of Pisa and the Marquises of Tuscany tolerated it, and because
its own members were, in practice, the only people capable of directing
the ambitions of their fellow citizens.

## An insult that had to be avenged

In 1004 a Muslim fleet sailed up the Arno and sacked a quarter of the city
itself - the first clearly attested episode in two centuries of Pisan
naval warfare. The following year the Pisans struck back at Reggio and
won, and for the next decade their merchant adventurers and those of Genoa
kept the western Mediterranean's Saracen raiders at bay largely through
opportunistic strikes rather than any settled campaign.

## Mogahid's fortress of living men

That changed in 1015, when a Saracen warlord named Mogahid, freshly
installed as ruler of a puppet caliphate on the Balearic Islands, landed
in Sardinia with a thousand horse and more than a hundred ships and
overran the island from its coasts to its mountain interior. Pisan and
Genoese fleets sailed to Sardinia's relief, and when Mogahid withdrew
before them he returned the next year with reinforcements and a vicious
answer for the islanders who had failed to stop him: he forced them to
build him a fortress and then walled the labourers alive into its own
masonry. His men, however, had grown weary of a war that paid no
plunder, and when the combined Pisan and Genoese fleets reappeared in May
1016 he chose to evacuate rather than fight again. The Italians caught his
ships as they embarked; a storm finished what the battle had begun; his
brother, his son and, by some accounts, a favourite wife were taken
prisoner, and though Mogahid himself escaped to Spain, he never again set
foot in Sardinia.

## Magistrates with a name at last

Formal communal government took another eighty years to harden into
something with a name. An imperial diploma of 1081 still describes Pisan
self-government only as an assembly summoned "by the sound of bells," with
no consuls mentioned at all; a Concordia negotiated by Bishop Daibert
around 1090 shows a council of "wise men" advising him but still no
consulate. It was Daibert's own second Concordia, in 1094, that at last
named sitting consuls and appealed to their authority directly - the first
solid evidence that authority in Pisa had passed from the bishop's own
feudal officials to magistrates the associated merchant families chose
among themselves.
