The Kushans are the far end of a collision that started three thousand
kilometres away, and the Chinese histories preserve the first half of it because
the Han empire caused it.

## The Yuezhi

The Yuezhi held the Gansu corridor, between the Chinese frontier and the
Taklamakan, until Modu's Xiongnu broke them in the 170s BC and drove them west.
Their king's skull, the Chinese sources say, was made into a drinking cup.

They went across the Tarim, were pushed on again by another people, and ended
up in Bactria — the country north of the Hindu Kush that Alexander had
conquered, that the Seleucids had held, and where a Greek kingdom had been
running independently for about a century. In the 130s BC they took it.

That is the migration Zhang Qian was sent to find. The Han envoy who reached
them around 129 BC was proposing an alliance against the Xiongnu and found a
people who had been settled in Bactria for a decade, were prosperous, and had no
intention of marching three thousand kilometres back the way they had come.

## Five tribes, and then one

The Chinese account says the Yuezhi in Bactria were divided into five
principalities, each under a *yabgu*, and that after about a century the
principality of Guishuang absorbed the other four and its ruler made himself
king.

*Guishuang* is the Chinese for Kushan. That is the founding, and it comes to us
because a Han court historian was keeping track of the peoples beyond the Tarim
for reasons of frontier security.

Kujula Kadphises, the ruler in question, then took the country south of the
Hindu Kush — Kabul, Gandhara, the Indus — and his successors went on into the
Ganges plain.

## What they walked into

Bactria and Gandhara in the first century BC were the most thoroughly mixed
territory on earth.

Greek settlement from Alexander's colonies, still speaking Greek and striking
Greek-legend coins. Iranian populations and Iranian religion. Indian
populations, Buddhist and Brahmanical, on the southern side of the passes.
Saka and Parthian rulers who had come through in the intervening century. And
the trade of four directions crossing at the same passes.

A steppe people arriving in that took over an apparatus rather than a country.
The Kushans kept the Greek alphabet — adapted, with an extra letter, to write
their own Iranian language — because it was the writing already in use for
administration and coinage.

Their coins say the rest. The earliest issues copy the types of whoever had been
minting before: Kujula's coins imitate Augustus and imitate the last Greek king
of the region. A dynasty establishing itself on somebody else's currency does
not begin by asserting a new identity. It begins by being recognisable.

## Why the dates are so bad

Almost everything above is approximate, and the reason is worth stating.

There is no Kushan chronicle. There is no king list from inside the empire. The
dating rests on coins, on inscriptions using a regnal era whose starting point
is not known, on the Chinese notices, and on cross-references to Roman and
Parthian material.

The accession of Kanishka — the most important date in the whole subject — has
been placed by serious scholars anywhere between about AD 78 and AD 230. The
current consensus favours about 127, on the strength of an inscription found in
1993, and it is a consensus rather than a settled fact.

An empire that ran from the Aral Sea to Benares, minted gold to the Roman
standard and carried Buddhism to China cannot be dated to within a century.
