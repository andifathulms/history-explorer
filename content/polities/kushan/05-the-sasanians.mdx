The Kushan empire was broken by the state that replaced the Parthians in Iran,
and the evidence for it is a rock inscription in three languages that lists the
provinces of somebody else's empire as though they were now provinces of this
one.

## The Naqsh-e Rustam inscription

Shapur I, who ruled Iran from about 240, had his titles and dominions cut on the
Ka'ba-ye Zartosht at Naqsh-e Rustam, in Middle Persian, Parthian and Greek.

The list of what he holds includes Sind, Sakastan, Turan, and — the phrase that
matters — the Kushanshahr as far as Peshawar, and as far as Kashgar, Sogdiana
and Tashkent.

Some of that is claim rather than possession. What is not in doubt is that the
Sasanians took the Kushan territories north and west of the Indus in the middle
of the third century, and governed them through princes of their own house who
struck coins as Kushanshahs — Kushano-Sasanian, in the modern term: Sasanian
rulers using Kushan titles and Kushan coin types over Kushan territory.

## The other end

While that was happening from the west, the eastern part of the empire was
coming apart on its own.

Kushan rule in the Gangetic plain shrinks through the third century; local
dynasties reappear in the sources; the coinage debases sharply, from good gold
to base metal, which is the ordinary signature of a state that has lost either
its bullion supply or its ability to enforce a standard.

A rump Kushan kingdom persisted in the north-west into the fourth century and
was finally cleared away by the Kidarites and then the Hephthalites — steppe
peoples arriving by the same route the Yuezhi had used four hundred years
earlier, and doing to the Kushans exactly what the Kushans had done to the Greek
kingdom of Bactria.

## Why the end is so vague

Because the sources that let us see the beginning were Chinese, and the Han
empire fell apart in the same decades.

The Chinese notices that recorded the Yuezhi migration, the five principalities
and the rise of Guishuang were written because a unified Chinese state was
managing a western frontier and needed the intelligence. After the Han collapse
in 220 there is no such state and no such interest for a very long time, and the
Kushans go dark from that side at precisely the moment they start losing.

From the Iranian side there is Shapur's inscription and the Kushano-Sasanian
coins, which say who won and nothing about how. From the Indian side there is
almost nothing: no chronicle, no dated king list, and inscriptions in an era
whose starting point is disputed.

So an empire whose beginning is dated by a Chinese envoy's report and whose end
is dated by a Persian king's boast has, in between, no history from its own hand
at all — which is a fair description of what it is like to hold a junction. You
appear in everybody's records except your own.
