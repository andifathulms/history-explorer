Kushan political history is thin — the chronology of the kings is still argued
over, and the date of Kanishka's accession has been placed anywhere across a
century. The coins carry most of the weight.

They are trilingual in effect: Greek script writing the Iranian Bactrian
language, with Kharosthi on some issues, and a pantheon on the reverses that
includes Greek deities, Iranian ones, Shiva, and the Buddha — the Buddha's first
appearances in human form on coinage are Kushan. A single reign issues gold with
Iranian fire gods and gold with Buddhist iconography, and the gold standard
matches Roman aurei closely enough to indicate the trade the empire lived on.

<Figure id="ku-huvishka" />

This is not syncretism as a philosophy. It is a state advertising itself in
every currency of legitimacy available to the populations it taxed and traded
with, which is what an empire straddling Bactria, Gandhara and the Ganges plain
had to do.

The transmission happened along the same routes. Buddhism moved north-west out
of India through Kushan territory into the Tarim oases, and from there to China,
where it arrives in the Han period at the far end of the road the Han had opened
to buy horses. The Gandharan sculptural tradition, which gave the Buddha a
Hellenistic face, is the residue of Alexander's colonists three centuries
earlier.

A steppe people, a Greek settlement, an Indian religion and a Chinese trade
route meet inside one government's territory, and the meeting is the empire.
