At Peshawar around 402, a Chinese monk was shown the largest building he had
seen anywhere, and was told the story of why it was there.

## The prophecy

Faxian records that when the Buddha was travelling in that country he told
Ananda that after his *parinirvana* there would be a king named Kanishka who
would build a tope on that spot.

Kanishka was duly born. One day, riding out to look about him, he came on a
little herd-boy making a tope by the roadside — Sakra, the ruler of the gods, in
disguise, put there to give the king the idea. The king asked what he was
making. *I am making a tope for Buddha.* The king said, very good.

And then, directly over the boy's tope, he reared another, more than four
hundred cubits high and adorned with layers of all the precious substances.

> Of all the topes and temples which the travellers saw in their journeyings,
> there was not one comparable to this.

The little tope, the story ends, came out from the side of the great one, more
than three feet high.

## What the story is doing

Read it as a claim about legitimacy and it is exactly the right story for this
dynasty.

The Kushans were a steppe people ruling Buddhists, Greeks, Iranians and Indians
who had no reason to accept them. What the tale supplies is a prophecy: the
Buddha named this king personally, five hundred years in advance, and the god
Sakra came down to make sure he thought of it.

And the detail that makes it work is the boy's tope emerging from the side. The
king's enormous structure does not replace the small one; it contains it, and
the small one is still visible. That is a very precise image of what the Kushan
empire was doing with the traditions it had come to govern.

## The bowl

Faxian also records what else was at Purushapura: the Buddha's own alms-bowl.

And he records that a king of the Yuezhi had come with an army to take it, and
could not — the elephants he had brought could not move it, so he built a tope
and a monastery there instead and left a guard, and made offerings.

A Kushan king failing to carry off the relic and endowing a monastery on the
spot instead is a story about the limits of conquest told by the people being
conquered, and the Kushans evidently let it stand.

## What the empire actually was

At its height it ran from the Aral Sea and the Oxus across the Hindu Kush,
through Gandhara and the Punjab, down the Ganges to Benares — and the point of
it is not the extent but the junction.

Four commercial systems met inside one government's territory: the Chinese silk
route through the Tarim, the Iranian roads to Mesopotamia, the Indian trunk
routes to the Gangetic plain, and the sea route from the Indus mouths to
Egypt. Kushan gold was struck to a weight that matched the Roman aureus closely
enough to circulate against it, which is what a state does when a great deal of
Roman gold is arriving.

Kanishka's own titles on his coins say the same thing without a word of
narrative: *shaonanoshao*, king of kings, which is Iranian; *maharaja*, which is
Indian; *devaputra*, son of god, which is a translation of the Chinese *tianzi*.
