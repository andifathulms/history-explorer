Kujula Kadphises died around 85 AD, past eighty years old, and left the
Kushan throne to his son, generally known to history simply as Kadphises
II. The new king inherited an ambition to match his father's conquests, and
aimed it first at an adversary his father had never had to face directly:
Han China, at the height of its own reassertion of control over the
Central Asian oasis routes.

## An insult, answered with a defeat

For thirty years, from 73 to 102 AD, the Chinese general Ban Chao had led
campaigns that reopened the roads north and south of the Taklamakan and
brought kingdom after kingdom - Khotan, Kashgar, Kuche, Kharachar - back
under Chinese suzerainty. Kadphises II, who considered himself the equal
of the Chinese emperor rather than a subordinate power on his frontier,
responded to this advancing authority in 90 AD by demanding a Han princess
in marriage. Ban Chao read the demand as the insult it was clearly intended
to be, arrested the envoy who delivered it, and sent him home.

Kadphises II answered the rebuff by sending an army: seventy thousand
cavalry under his viceroy Xie, across the Pamirs by a pass some fourteen
thousand feet high. The mountains did more damage to the force than any
Chinese army needed to. What emerged into the plains beyond was so
shattered by the crossing that Ban Chao destroyed it without difficulty.
Kadphises II was compelled to pay tribute to the Han court - the one clear
military defeat in an otherwise unbroken record of Kushan success.

## Compensation, found elsewhere

The check on his ambitions in the direction of China did not end them. Over
the following decade Kadphises II turned the same energy against India
instead, extending Kushan rule across the whole of the north-west and down
the Ganges as far as Benares - a conquest apparently significant enough
that an Indian embassy appeared in Rome in 99 AD to announce it to the
emperor Trajan, at the furthest reach either empire's diplomacy stretched
toward the other.

## What the gold said

It was Kadphises II, not his father, who put Kushan coinage on a gold
standard for the first time - striking an abundant issue of aurei that
matched the weight of their Roman prototypes closely enough to circulate
alongside them, at exactly the period Roman gold was arriving in quantity
to pay for Indian silk, spices and gems. His own coin legends call him
*basileus basileon*, king of kings, a title his father had never claimed
for himself. A ruler who had just been humbled on his eastern frontier by
an empire he considered his equal made sure that everywhere else his money
travelled, it said otherwise.

Kadphises II reigned for perhaps thirty-five or forty years, into the 120s
AD, before the throne passed to Kanishka - the successor whose name would
outlast every other Kushan king's by a margin his own father's failed
Chinese marriage proposal could never have predicted.
