For four hundred years after his death the Buddha was not shown. Then, in
Kushan territory, he was — and what he looked like was Greek.

## Aniconism, and the end of it

Early Buddhist art represents the Buddha by absence: an empty throne, a parasol,
a pair of footprints, a wheel, the Bodhi tree with nobody under it. The reliefs
at Sanchi and Bharhut are full of crowds worshipping a space.

In the first and second centuries AD that stops, in two places at once —
Gandhara in the north-west, and Mathura on the Jumna. Both are Kushan.

The Gandharan Buddha wears a himation in heavy classical folds, has a
straight nose and wavy hair gathered in a topknot that is treated like the hair
on a Greek god, and stands in a contrapposto. The workshops that produced him
were working in a Hellenistic idiom that had been in Bactria and Gandhara since
Alexander's colonists, applied to an Indian subject by sculptors serving a
Central Asian dynasty.

Kanishka's coins carry the earliest images of the Buddha in human form on any
coinage, labelled in Greek letters: *BODDO*.

## Why it happened there

Nobody knows, and the honest answers are all partial.

One is that a devotional Buddhism developing towards the Mahayana wanted an
object to venerate, and the anthropomorphic image met a demand rather than
creating one. Another is simply that the Gandharan workshops had the technique
and the habit of representing gods as men, and were commissioned. A third, which
the Kushan coins support, is that a dynasty putting Greek, Iranian, Hindu and
Buddhist deities on its own money in human form had already normalised the
practice.

What is certain is the direction it travelled afterwards. The Gandharan and
Mathuran images are the ancestors of every Buddha figure from Bamiyan to Nara —
the seated Buddha of the Tarim caves, of Longmen and Yungang, of Korea and
Japan, descends by a traceable line of copying from statues made in the Kushan
north-west.

## The road

The other inheritance is the route, and the Kushans held every stage of it.

Buddhism moved out of India across the Hindu Kush, along the oasis chain of the
Tarim — Khotan, Kucha, Turfan — and into China, arriving at the Han court in the
first century AD. The translators who did the work at Luoyang in the second
century included men from Parthia, from Sogdiana, and from the Yuezhi
themselves: the names given them in the Chinese sources begin with a syllable
identifying their origin, and *Zhi* marks a Yuezhi.

So the first substantial translations of Buddhist scripture into Chinese were
made partly by subjects of this empire, along a road this empire policed, in a
period this empire's own chronology cannot fix.

## What is left of them

Almost nothing above ground, and an enormous amount underground.

The great tope at Peshawar that Faxian called the finest he had seen anywhere
was excavated in 1908 and found to contain a reliquary casket with an
inscription naming Kanishka. The Gandharan sculpture is in museums by the
thousand. The Bactrian language, written in Greek letters, was largely
unreadable until documents began surfacing in the 1990s.

And the dynastic sanctuary at Rabatak, found in 1993, gave a genealogy and a
list of conquests in Kanishka's own words — the first Kushan king to speak
directly rather than through a coin legend, seventeen centuries after he
stopped.
