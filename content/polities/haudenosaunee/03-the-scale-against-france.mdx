For as long as the French and English colonies in North America
remained close to evenly matched, Morgan credits the Iroquois with
deciding the contest between them. "The enmity and power of the
Ho-de'-no-sau-nee were sufficient to turn the scale against" France, he
writes, and France "must chiefly ascribe the final overthrow of her
magnificent schemes of colonization in the northern part of America" to
this one Indian confederacy - a judgment resting on a century of
Iroquois raids into French Canada and a standing refusal to let French
intimidation replace the "covenant chain" of friendship the League had
kept with the Dutch and, after 1664, the English.

## A sixth nation at the fire

The confederacy's capacity to absorb outsiders on its own terms showed
again in 1715, when the Tuscarora, driven from North Carolina by a
catastrophic war with English colonists, turned north on the strength
of a claimed common origin in the Iroquoian language family. They were
settled on land ceded from the Oneida and admitted as a constituent
nation - what the English had called the Five Nations became, from that
point, the Six Nations in common usage - though the original five kept
their fifty sachemships to themselves and never seated a Tuscarora among
the sachems of the council.

## A long peace

The decades after that admission, Morgan records, were the calmest the
confederacy had known: "from the close of the French war until the
commencement of the American Revolution, was a time of general peace."
It was the last such peace the League would see. The Revolution that
followed put a system built for external supremacy to a test it had
never faced before - an alliance it could not present a single united
answer to.
