The League's first encounter with Europeans was a defeat. In 1609, a
Mohawk war party of about two hundred met Samuel de Champlain on the
shore of the lake he named for himself, allied against them with the
Huron and Adirondack; it was the first time the Iroquois heard a
firearm, and Morgan records that its "marvellous power" carried the
field against them outright. The French alliance with the Iroquois'
traditional enemies hardened into what Morgan calls an uncompromising
and inveterate enmity that never cooled until Britain's conquest of
Canada a century and a half later.

## A new era at Fort Orange

The balance turned in 1615, when the Dutch built a trading post at what
became Albany. Friendly relations with the Dutch, later inherited by the
English as a "covenant chain," opened a fur trade that brought the
Iroquois firearms of their own - and with firearms came what Morgan
describes as their rapid elevation to supremacy over every neighboring
Indian nation. The Iroquois had already, by his account, grown into "a
populous and powerful confederacy" holding ground no other Indian
people east of the Mississippi had matched; muskets gave that standing
teeth.

## The Neutral Nation and the Erie broken in turn

The campaigns that followed were fast and, by Morgan's account, close
to annihilating. In 1643 the Iroquois drove the Neutral Nation from the
Niagara peninsula and settled its mouth themselves. Ten years later, in
1653, war against the Erie - who held the south shore of Lake Erie east
to the Genesee - came near to exterminating that nation outright,
leaving the confederacy in possession of the whole of western New York
and the northern edge of Ohio. By around 1670 the Huron and Adirondack
had been dispersed and subjugated as well, and Iroquois colonial
villages stood on the north shore of Lake Ontario to hold ground the
League had never occupied before European contact - conquest built,
each time, on the muskets that had first defeated them.
