Before the League, the five nations of present-day New York were separate
and often hostile bands, drawn from a common stock but split by rivalry
into open warfare of unknown duration. Tradition traces the project of a
confederacy to the Onondaga, among whom an Onondaga lawgiver, Dekanawida -
"Da-ga-no-we'-da" in Morgan's rendering - first proposed a compact that
could let five warring nations "elevate themselves to a general
supremacy" together rather than destroy one another apart.

## A sachem too dangerous to refuse

The obstacle, as the Iroquois told it, was Tododaho himself: an Onondaga
sachem of such terrible power that snakes grew from his hair and anyone
who met his angry look fell dead. Tododaho resisted the new order, since
accepting it meant giving up absolute power to sit as one equal among
many. A Mohawk sachem, Hiawatha - "Ha-yo-went'-ha," the man who combs -
is remembered as the one who calmed him, combing the snakes from his
hair and clearing the way for his consent. In tribute, the first of the
fifty sachemships created by the League was named for Tododaho himself
and marked with honors beyond the other forty-nine, though bound by the
same equal vote in council as every other seat.

## Fifty seats, five nations

The compact settled at a council fire on the northern shore of Onondaga
lake, after a debate tradition says ran many days, created fifty
permanent sachemships distributed among the Mohawk, Onondaga, Seneca,
Oneida and Cayuga. Each nation kept its own council for local affairs,
while the sachems together formed the supreme council of the League; the
Onondaga, who had named the fifty original titles, were called
name-bearers, and the Seneca, as hereditary doorkeepers of what the
Iroquois called their common Long House, guarded its western entrance.
No source can fix the year this happened - the Iroquois' own traditions
reach back to a remote and unrecorded past, and the clearest outside
clue, the circumstances of first Dutch contact on the Hudson, only
suggested to later observers that the League had already stood for
roughly a century by 1609.
