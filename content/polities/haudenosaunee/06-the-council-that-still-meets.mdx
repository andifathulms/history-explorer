Political independence was gone, but the League's own forms were not.
At intervals of one or two years the scattered fragments of the Six
Nations still assembled in general council, summoned and conducted
exactly as in the days of their own sovereignty, to raise up sachems
with the ancient ceremonies whenever death or deposition left a title
vacant. Every nation but the Oneida and Tuscarora still held, in the
main, to the ancient faith and its festivals - and around 1800 that
faith itself was reshaped by a man born to trouble it least: a Seneca
sachem named Handsome Lake, who had spent the better part of his life
a drunkard, and who, given up for dead during a long illness, said
three spirits sent by the Great Spirit had appeared to him, restored
him to health with berries from their own hands, and commissioned him
to preach against the intemperance then threatening the Iroquois with
outright extinction.

## Doctrines carried from village to village

Handsome Lake did not discard the old religion; he wrapped a new
temperance reformation inside it, holding over the disobedient the
threat of a hell he claimed to have toured under his spirit guides'
protection, and offering the virtuous a heaven barred to every white
man. He carried the doctrine from nation to nation for the rest of his
life, exempting only the already-Christianized Oneida, and won enough
converts that discord gave way, by report, to sobriety and industry
across whole communities. He died at Onondaga in 1815, on one of his
own pastoral visits, and was succeeded - the only person the Iroquois
ever formally raised up to the office - by his own grandson, Sosehawa,
who preached the same doctrine for upwards of thirty years afterward
and still lived at Tonawanda where he had been born.

## A recitation at Tonawanda, October 1848

The doctrine survived less as scripture than as recitation. At the
Mourning and Religious councils the League still convened, a portion
of two or three days was set aside for Sosehawa to repeat, word for
word, the instructions Handsome Lake claimed to have received - and
one such recitation, delivered at a general Mourning council at
Tonawanda across the four days from the 4th through the 6th of
October, 1848, before Mohawk, Onondaga and Seneca delegates and the
"children" nations of Oneida, Cayuga and Tuscarora, was translated
from Sosehawa's own words by Ely S. Parker, the Seneca sachem who
worked with Lewis Henry Morgan to record the confederacy's own account
of itself. Stripped of its treaties
and its territory, the confederacy that had once held the balance
between France and England went on raising up its sachems by the old
forms and gathering, nation by scattered nation, to hear a dead man's
revelation recited by a living one.
