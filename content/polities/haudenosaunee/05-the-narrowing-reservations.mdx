The peace of 1783 left each of the Six Nations to face the tide of
settlement pressing west across New York on its own. The Mohawk, who
had abandoned their country during the war, resettled permanently on
land the British Crown secured for them at Grand River in the Niagara
peninsula and near Kingston. The Oneida, despite having fought for the
American side, fared little better: their lands were ceded away in a
long series of negotiations until only a single small reservation
remained, and the nation itself split three ways, a few hundred
staying on at Oneida Castle, a larger band settling at Green Bay in
Wisconsin, and another crossing into Canada on the river Thames.

## A nation scattered abroad

The Cayuga suffered worse than either. Hemmed in on every side within
twelve years of the first white settler's house going up in their own
county, they surrendered their whole domain by 1795 and abandoned even
their last reservation around 1800, scattering to Green Bay, to Grand
River, and in the largest number to Sandusky in Ohio, from which the
government later removed them again, into the Indian Territory west
of the Mississippi. Only the Onondaga, granting away their lands by
treaty but keeping the valley around their own ancient council fire,
came through the dispossession relatively whole. The Tuscarora settled
on land the Seneca themselves gave them near the Niagara river. By the
middle of the nineteenth century the Six Nations, once masters of the
country between the Hudson and Lake Erie, held only scattered
fragments of it: three small Seneca reservations at Tonawanda,
Cattaraugus and Allegany, a remnant of Oneida land, the Onondaga
valley, the Tuscarora tract, and the reserves in Canada, where the
descendants of every nation but the Oneida and Tuscarora had, between
them, recreated something like a miniature of the ancient League.

## The Ogden Land Company

The Seneca's own three reservations drew a particular predator. A
land speculation company held the "preemptive right" to whatever
Seneca land might eventually be sold, and for fourteen years pursued
its purchase by fraud, bribery and intrigue that a contemporary
account judged worse than the treaties that had dispossessed the
Cherokee in Georgia. Two treaties, in 1838 and 1842, were forced on
the Seneca by these means; one of the four reservations, the
forty-nine-thousand-acre Buffalo tract bordering the growing city of
that name and worth, by one estimate, over a million dollars, passed
to the company for roughly a tenth of that sum. By the time of the
last census the remaining Seneca in western New York numbered a little
over twenty-seven hundred, living on annuity income the state paid
from the proceeds of a century of land sales, on three reservations
that together would not have covered one of the state's own smaller
counties.
