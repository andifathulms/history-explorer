Muhammad of Ghor did not accept the defeat at Tarain as final. He
returned the following year, 1192, with a fresh army and met
Prithviraja - again at the head of an immense confederate host, its
numbers swollen by contingents from princes across Northern India - on
the same field. This time the result reversed completely. A charge of
twelve thousand well-armed Ghurid horsemen broke the confederate line
outright, demonstrating, as Smith puts it, "the inability of a mob of
Indian militia to stand the onset of trained cavalry." Prithviraja was
taken prisoner and executed in cold blood; Ajmer itself, his own
capital, was put to the sword or sold into slavery when the Ghurid
army reached it.

## A single year, both directions

The two battles of Tarain, fought exactly a year apart on the same
ground, bracket the entire arc of the Chahamana dynasty's own
independence: the first proved a united Northern India could still
turn back a Ghurid invasion outright, and the second proved that
victory had bought nothing durable, because the same confederate
structure that had won in 1191 simply could not survive contact with
a properly organized cavalry charge in 1192. Prithviraja's own defeat
opened the Ghurid conquest of Delhi and the wider Gangetic plain in
earnest - the campaign that would, within two years, end the
Gahadavala kingdom of Kanauj as well, and within little more than a
decade give Northern India the sultanate whose own slave-general
founders would outlast every Rajput power that had once contested
Ajmer, Kanauj and Delhi among themselves.
