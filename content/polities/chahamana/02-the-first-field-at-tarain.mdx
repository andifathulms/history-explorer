Vigraha-raja's own nephew Prithviraja - Prithviraj, or Rai Pithora -
became, in Smith's own phrase, "the popular hero of Northern India,"
his exploits in love and war still the subject of bardic verse
centuries after his death. The romantic side of that reputation rested
on his abduction, around 1175, of the daughter of Jaichand, the
Gaharwar king of Kanauj - a match Smith notes was "not unwilling" on
the princess's own part, whatever obstacle her father's rivalry with
Prithviraja had placed in the way. The military side rested on real
achievement: in 1182 Prithviraja defeated the Chandela king Parmal and
captured Mahoba, and by the 1180s his own standing was secure enough
that Northern India's rival powers looked to him as their natural
leader against a new and growing threat from beyond the Indus.

## A confederacy that actually worked

That threat was Muhammad of Ghor, already undisputed master of most
of the Punjab, whose advance did what decades of mutual rivalry
between Chahamana, Chandela, Gahadavala and other Rajput powers had
never managed: it forced them into a genuine common cause. In 1191
Prithviraja led the resulting confederacy against the invading Ghurid
army at Tarain, between Thanesar and Karnal, and won - a defeat severe
enough to force Muhammad's own forces back across the Indus entirely.
It was the last real Hindu military victory over the Ghurid advance,
and it made Prithviraja, for one year, the undisputed hero not just of
song but of Northern India's actual military balance - a triumph that
gave no warning of how completely it would be reversed within twelve
months.
