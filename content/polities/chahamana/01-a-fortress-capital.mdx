The Chahamanas - Chauhans, in the vernacular - were, like the
Pratiharas, one of the four Rajput clans a well-known legend traced to
a purifying fire-pit at Mount Abu, alongside the Paramaras and
Chaulukyas. For generations their own power was based at Sambhar,
also called Shakambhari, in the heart of Rajputana. That changed
around 1100, when a chief named Ajayadeva Chauhan founded a new
capital at Ajmer - coins struck in his own name and that of his queen
Somaladevi survive as evidence of the new seat's early standing.
Ajmer's own position, defensible and central to the trade routes
crossing eastern Rajputana, would anchor Chahamana power for the rest
of the dynasty's independent history.

## Vigraha-raja's own reach

The dynasty's own territorial reach grew across the twelfth century.
Vigraha-raja IV, reigning in the century's middle decades, extended
his ancestral dominions considerably - later legend credited him with
conquering Delhi outright from its Tomara rulers, though Smith judges
the claim erroneous; Delhi's own Tomara dynasty, founded around the
building of its Red Fort a century earlier, was not in fact displaced
by the Chahamanas at this point. What is certain is that Vigraha-raja
was a patron of real distinction: inscriptions recovered from Ajmer's
own principal mosque preserve substantial portions of two Sanskrit
and Prakrit dramas from his court, one composed in his own honour and
the other, by tradition, his personal composition. It was a court
confident enough in its own standing to commission theater - and it
would produce, within a generation, the dynasty's single most famous
king.
