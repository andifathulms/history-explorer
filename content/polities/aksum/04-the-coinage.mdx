Aksumite coinage is the best evidence the kingdom left about itself, and this
chapter is about it as evidence rather than as money.

It was struck in gold, silver and bronze from the late third century until some
point in the seventh. The legends are in Greek on the early issues — the language
of the Red Sea trade — and shift to Ge'ez later. The coins have been found in
Arabia, in Egypt, and in India, at Karnataka and in a hoard in the south, which
is the physical trace of the Red Sea and Indian Ocean route the kingdom lived on.

## Why gold matters

Very few states minted a gold coinage. On the common contemporary reckoning
there were three in the world the Mediterranean knew: Rome, Persia and Aksum.

That is not a statement about wealth alone. Issuing gold means having gold,
having a trade that requires denominations that large, and having a stamp that
merchants outside your territory will take at face value rather than by weight.
The third condition is the political one and it is the reason Mani's list in the
first chapter includes this kingdom.

The silver and bronze issues were for domestic use and several carry an unusual
feature: inlaid gold on the design, applied to a base-metal coin, which is a
technique nobody else in the period was using in quantity.

## Dating a conversion from a die

The religious change is legible in the same series and this is the part that
makes the coinage more than an economic indicator.

The early issues carry a disc and crescent above the king's head. From
part-way through Ezana's reign they carry a cross. The inscriptions shift in the
same reign from invoking Mahrem to invoking the Lord of Heaven, and the third
chapter is about what that did.

Nobody in Aksum wrote the conversion down in a form that survives. It is dated
from the money.

That is worth holding onto, because it is the condition of everything known
about this kingdom. Most ancient states are reconstructed from paperwork —
delivery receipts, provincial files, temple accounts. Aksum left none of it.
What it left is coins, royal inscriptions and very large stones, and every
statement anybody makes about how it worked is built out of those three
things.

## When it stops

The coinage ends in the seventh century, and the reason is the same reason
everything else about the kingdom ends: the Red Sea reoriented after the Arab
conquests, and the trade that had required gold denominations went
elsewhere.

For a kingdom whose best evidence is its money, the end of the money is the end
of the evidence. Aksum goes on for roughly another three hundred years, and
almost nothing is known about any of them.
