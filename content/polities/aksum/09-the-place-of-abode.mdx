Four hundred years after Aksum stopped being the capital of anything, a scribe
called Isaac finished a book which asserted that God lived there.

The *Kebra Nagast* — the Glory of Kings — was assembled at Aksum in the early
fourteenth century. Budge summarises what its compiler believed he was doing,
from Isaac's own colophons, and the third of the three propositions is the one
that concerns this kingdom:

> That the God of Israel had transferred His place of abode on earth from
> Jerusalem to Aksum, the ecclesiastical and political capital of Ethiopia.

Isaac says he toiled at it *for the glory of the kingdom of Ethiopia, and for the
going forth of the heavenly Zion, and for the glory of the King of Ethiopia*.

## What the claim required

That the Ark of the Covenant had been taken from Jerusalem to Aksum by Menelik,
Solomon's firstborn son by the queen of Sheba, and had stayed there.

Isaac is entirely untroubled by how Menelik obtained it. He glories in it, on the
ground that the removal was manifestly God's will: God had judged that the Jews
were no longer worthy custodians of the Ark in which His presence was, and the
Ark itself wished to go. Ethiopia, in the words of the psalm, had stretched out
her hands unto God, and He had gone to her.

That is a very large claim to make about a provincial town in the northern
highlands, and it was made at the moment the town had least going for it
politically.

## When it was made, and why

The political centre of Christian Ethiopia had moved south out of Aksum
centuries earlier. The Zagwe ruled from Lasta and built the churches at Lalibela.
Then in 1270 a dynasty took the throne claiming descent from Solomon through
Menelik, and it is that dynasty's need that the book serves — Isaac's own
tradition held that God had permitted the Solomonic line to be broken and the
Zagwe to reign, and had then restored it.

So a book compiled at Aksum argued that the legitimacy of a dynasty ruling from
elsewhere depended on a relic held at Aksum, in a church at Aksum, under the care
of the clergy of Aksum.

## What that bought the town

Six hundred years of being the ceremonial centre of a state that was governed
from somewhere else.

Ethiopian emperors went to Aksum to be crowned, at the church of Maryam Tsion,
long after there was nothing else there. The chapel beside it is where the Ark is
said to be kept, watched by a single guardian monk who does not leave and who
alone may see it. The claim has never been withdrawn and is not subject to
inspection.

## A city that outlived its own state twice over

The stelae were already ancient and already fallen when Isaac was writing — a
row of granite monoliths carved to look like storeyed buildings, raised by a
pagan dynasty for its own dead, standing among the churches of the Christian
kingdom that succeeded it.

Aksum's political existence lasted perhaps seven hundred years. Its existence as
the place where Ethiopian kingship was authorised has now lasted a thousand more,
on the strength of a book written by a scribe who believed that the God of Israel
had moved house.
