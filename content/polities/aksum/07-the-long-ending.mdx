This record's `ended.type` is `gradual absorption` with an empty `by` and a date
of 940, and every part of that needs qualifying.

No conqueror is recorded. No battle ends it. The date is a convention. And
`gradual absorption` implies absorption into something, which is not what
happened either — the state contracted, moved inland, and turned into a different
polity in the same highlands under a different dynasty.

## What actually went wrong

The sea closed.

The Arab conquests of the 630s and 640s took Egypt, the Levant and eventually the
whole northern Red Sea littoral. Aksum was not invaded — the early Islamic
tradition remembers the kingdom as the place that sheltered the Prophet's
followers when they fled Mecca, and treats it as exempt from jihad on that
account, which is a remarkable piece of diplomacy for a Christian state to have
achieved in the seventh century.

But the trade it had lived on now ran between Muslim ports under Muslim
arrangements, and Aksum was not one of them. Adulis stops being an international
harbour. The coinage stops in the same century. This record's `revenue_basis`
reads `trade-toll, tribute`, and the first of those two values simply ceased to
be available.

What was left was the plateau: an agricultural kingdom with no port, cut off from
the Mediterranean world it had been a member of, and with a church whose head
still came from Alexandria and now came from a Muslim-ruled Alexandria.

## The three hundred years

The record's span runs to 940 and almost nothing is known about the last three
centuries of it.

The turning point on this record for the 640s is marked `contested: true`, and
the reason is that the causal chain — the conquests reoriented the trade, the
trade loss killed the state — is a reconstruction rather than a report. Nobody in
Aksum wrote it down. What can be observed is that the coins stop, the inscriptions
stop, the capital declines archaeologically, and the political centre of the
Ethiopian highlands moves south over the following centuries.

Phillipson's whole argument, and the reason this record cites him against
munro-hay-1991 in places, is that this is a transformation rather than a collapse
— that the continuity into medieval Christian Ethiopia is stronger than the older
accounts allowed, and that the search for a moment of fall is the wrong shape of
question.

## Gudit

Ethiopian tradition supplies a moment anyway, and this record does not enter it.

The tradition names a queen — Gudit, or Yodit — who is said to have destroyed
Aksum around the end of the tenth century, burned the churches, and killed the
royal line. She appears in later Ethiopian chronicles and in an Arabic letter of
the period referring to a queen who was attacking the Christian kingdom. Almost
nothing about her can be established and the date of 940 on this record's span
comes from that neighbourhood of tradition.

So the span's end is a convention drawn from a story, and the last chapter says
so rather than presenting a year that looks like the others on this site.

## What came after

The Zagwe dynasty, from roughly the twelfth century, ruling from further south
and building the rock-hewn churches at Lalibela. Then the Solomonic dynasty from
1270, which claimed descent from Solomon and Sheba and ruled, with interruptions,
until 1974.

Neither is in this corpus, which is why this record's `successor_claims` field is
null rather than zero — the data comment says nobody has looked, and that is
accurate. Whether the Zagwe or the Solomonic kings asserted *this kingdom's*
legitimacy in the sense coding rule 1 requires is a real question that the two
sources on this record do not address.

What is not in question is that the church, the script, the liturgical language
and the highland Christian state all continued without a break. This record ends
and the thing it was does not, which is the least satisfying kind of ending for a
site organised around polities and is what the evidence supports.
