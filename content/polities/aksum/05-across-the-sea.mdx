In 525 Kaleb of Aksum crossed the Red Sea with a fleet, destroyed the kingdom of
Himyar in south-western Arabia, and killed its king.

Himyar was a Jewish kingdom in the Yemeni highlands, and it had been persecuting
the Christian community at Najran. The invasion was mounted in response, with
Byzantine encouragement and, on some accounts, Byzantine ships. It ended
Himyar.

For about half a century afterwards an African kingdom held both shores of the
southern Red Sea.

## What the sources are

Unusually good, and unusually strange.

Bowersock's book reconstructs the war largely from two inscriptions carved on a throne at
Adulis, a monument that no longer exists and is known only because a sixth-century
Egyptian merchant called Cosmas Indicopleustes was asked by the local governor to
copy the texts before an expedition and did so, and included them in a book about
why the world is flat.

Beside that there is Syriac hagiography about the martyrs of Najran, Sabaean
inscriptions from the Himyarite side, a Greek diplomatic account, and Ethiopian
tradition. Five bodies of evidence in five languages about a war between two
kingdoms that most narratives of late antiquity do not mention.

## The high-water mark

That was the furthest Aksum ever reached and the last thing it did that the
wider world troubled to write down.

An Aksumite viceroy named Abraha eventually made himself independent in Arabia
and ruled there for decades; Islamic tradition remembers him for an expedition
against Mecca with an elephant, in a year the tradition calls the Year of the
Elephant. Whether that expedition happened as
described is argued about and is not settled.

The occupation ended when a Sasanian expedition took Yemen in the 570s, and
Aksum never crossed the water again.

## The reign that mattered less

Kaleb took Aksum further than any other king and is not the reign the kingdom is
remembered for, which is worth being explicit about.

Ezana's reign produced the conversion, the inscriptions, the change on the
coinage and a church that is still standing. Kaleb's produced a successful
overseas expedition that held for fifty years and left nothing behind on either
shore: no Aksumite institution in Arabia, no lasting church, no dynasty. Abraha's
viceroyalty went its own way and was then taken by Persia.

Reach and consequence come apart here about as cleanly as they ever do.

Kaleb is said to have abdicated, become a monk, and sent his crown to Jerusalem.
That is the kind of detail the Ethiopian tradition preserves and the external
sources do not, and it should be read as tradition.
