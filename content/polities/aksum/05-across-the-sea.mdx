In 525 Kaleb of Aksum crossed the Red Sea with a fleet, destroyed the kingdom of
Himyar in south-western Arabia, and killed its king.

This corpus carries Himyar as a record in its South Arabia region and that record
ends here. Its own chapters describe a Jewish kingdom in the Yemeni highlands
that had been persecuting the Christian community at Najran, and an Aksumite
invasion mounted in response with Byzantine encouragement and, on some accounts,
Byzantine ships.

For about half a century afterwards an African kingdom held both shores of the
southern Red Sea.

## What the sources are

Unusually good, and unusually strange.

Bowersock's book — this chapter's source, and already cited on the Himyar record
— reconstructs the war largely from two inscriptions carved on a throne at
Adulis, a monument that no longer exists and is known only because a sixth-century
Egyptian merchant called Cosmas Indicopleustes was asked by the local governor to
copy the texts before an expedition and did so, and included them in a book about
why the world is flat.

Beside that there is Syriac hagiography about the martyrs of Najran, Sabaean
inscriptions from the Himyarite side, a Greek diplomatic account, and Ethiopian
tradition. Five bodies of evidence in five languages about a war between two
kingdoms that most narratives of late antiquity do not mention.

## The high-water mark

This is the furthest this record reaches and the last thing it does that the
wider world wrote down.

An Aksumite viceroy named Abraha eventually made himself independent in Arabia
and ruled there for decades; Islamic tradition remembers him for an expedition
against Mecca with an elephant, in a year the tradition calls the Year of the
Elephant. The Himyar record in this corpus discusses that episode and declines to
adjudicate it, which is the right treatment and not repeated here.

The occupation ended when a Sasanian expedition took Yemen in the 570s. This
corpus's Sasanian record carries the reach and this one carries the loss.

## The shape of the reign

Kaleb is not this record's `peak` ruler. Ezana is, and the reason is worth being
explicit about, because the reach argument would give it to Kaleb.

The site's `peak` field names the reign a record is organised around rather than
the maximum extent, and for a polity with no `reach_km2` figure at all there is
no extent to organise around. What Ezana's reign has is the conversion, the
inscriptions, the coinage change and the church, all of which are still running
consequences. What Kaleb's has is a successful overseas expedition that lasted
fifty years and left nothing behind on either shore.

Kaleb is said to have abdicated and become a monk, and to have sent his crown to
Jerusalem. That is the kind of detail the Ethiopian tradition preserves and the
external sources do not, and this record notes it as tradition rather than
entering it anywhere.
