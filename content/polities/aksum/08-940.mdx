Aksum has no fall. No conqueror is recorded, no battle ends it, and the date
usually given is a convention standing in for a process nobody described.

## Gudit

Ethiopian tradition supplies a moment anyway.

It names a queen — Gudit, or Yodit — said to have destroyed Aksum around the end
of the tenth century, burned the churches and killed the royal line. She appears
in later Ethiopian chronicles, and an Arabic letter of the period refers to a
queen attacking the Christian kingdom. Almost nothing about her can be
established, and the conventional end date comes from that neighbourhood of
tradition rather than from anything datable.

So the year Aksum ends is a number drawn from a story, and it is better to say so
than to print it as though it were the same kind of date as its beginning.

## What came after

The Zagwe dynasty, from roughly the twelfth century, ruling from further south
and building the rock-hewn churches at Lalibela. Then the Solomonic dynasty from
1270, which claimed descent from Solomon and Sheba and ruled, with interruptions,
until 1974.

Whether either of them claimed to be continuing *Aksum* — as against ruling the
same highlands and the same church — is a real question, and the sources here do
not settle it. What is not in question is that the church, the script, the
liturgical language and the highland Christian state all carried on without a
break. The kingdom ends and the thing it was does not, which is the least
satisfying kind of ending and the one the evidence supports.
