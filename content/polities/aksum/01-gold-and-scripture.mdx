title: A coinage and an alphabet

Aksum minted gold, and almost nobody did. In the third century there were three
powers issuing a gold coinage in the world the Mediterranean knew — Rome, Persia,
and Aksum — which is the single clearest indicator of what it was: a state
controlling the Red Sea end of the trade in ivory, incense and African goods
moving between the Mediterranean and India, with a port at Adulis and a capital
in the highlands behind it.

Ezana converted to Christianity around the 330s, roughly contemporary with
Constantine, and the coinage changed from a disc-and-crescent to a cross, which
is how the conversion is dated. The church that resulted was and remains
Miaphysite, aligned with Alexandria rather than Constantinople, and it survived
the Islamic conquest of Egypt as an isolated Christian polity in the highlands.

The Ge'ez script is counted on this page as a descendant script under the coding
rules. It derives from South Arabian letterforms, but under Aksum it acquired
vowel marking, becoming a syllabary rather than a consonantal alphabet — an
adaptation substantial enough that the rules count it, and it remains the script
of Amharic and Tigrinya today.

The kingdom declined from the seventh century as the Red Sea trade shifted and
the Islamic conquests reoriented the routes. No conqueror is recorded, and the
site types the ending `gradual absorption` accordingly.
