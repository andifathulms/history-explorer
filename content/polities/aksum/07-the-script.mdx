Aksum's most durable act was not a conquest. It was a change made to a set of
letters in the fourth century, and it is being used to type this sentence's
Ethiopian equivalent today.

## What was adapted, and by whom

The Ge'ez script descends from the South Arabian alphabet — the same family the
Sabaean inscriptions across the Red Sea are written in. That derivation happened
before Aksum's own history begins, and belongs to whoever carried the letters
over the water.

What happened under Aksum, in the fourth century and apparently in the same
generation as the conversion to Christianity, is that the script acquired
systematic vowel marking. Each consonant sign was modified into a set of seven
forms, one for each vowel, by adding strokes and loops and by changing the shape
of the base letter.

That turned a consonantal alphabet into a syllabary. It is a different kind of
act from the one that usually spreads a writing system: Hittite scribes took
Mesopotamian cuneiform and wrote their own language in it, Urartian scribes did
the same with Assyrian cuneiform, and the Cyrillic workshops took Greek letters
for Slavonic. Each of those borrowed a system and left it standing. The Aksumite
change rebuilt the system itself — the inherited consonantal skeleton is South
Arabian, and the vowels are Aksum's.

## What it is still doing

Ge'ez as a spoken language died. The script did not.

<Figure id="a-garima" />

It is the script of Amharic, of Tigrinya, of Tigre, and of Ge'ez itself as a
liturgical language, and it is read and written daily by tens of millions of
people. It is one of a very small number of writing systems in use today that
belongs neither to the Phoenician line nor to the Chinese one, and the shape it
has — a consonant with its vowel built into the letterform — was given to it in
this kingdom, in the fourth century, and has not been given up since.
