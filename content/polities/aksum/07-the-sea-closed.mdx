Aksum was a port and a plateau, and in the seventh century it stopped being a
port.

The Arab conquests of the 630s and 640s took Egypt, the Levant and eventually
the whole northern Red Sea littoral. Aksum itself was not invaded — early Islamic
tradition remembers the kingdom as the place that sheltered the Prophet's
followers when they fled Mecca, and treats it as exempt from jihad on that
account, which is a remarkable piece of diplomacy for a Christian state to have
achieved in the seventh century.

But the trade Aksum had lived on now ran between Muslim ports under Muslim
arrangements, and Aksum was not one of them. Adulis stopped being an
international harbour. The coinage stopped in the same century. A kingdom that
had lived on tolls and tribute lost the first of those two outright.

What was left was the plateau: an agricultural kingdom with no port, cut off from
the Mediterranean world it had been a member of, and with a church whose head
still came from Alexandria and now came from a Muslim-ruled Alexandria.

## The three hundred years

Almost nothing is known about the last three centuries.

The causal chain above — the conquests reoriented the trade, and the loss of the
trade killed the state — is a reconstruction rather than a report. Nobody in
Aksum wrote it down. What can be observed is that the coins stop, the
inscriptions stop, the capital declines archaeologically, and the political
centre of the Ethiopian highlands moves south over the following centuries.

Phillipson's argument, and the reason he is cited here against the older account,
is that this is a transformation rather than a collapse — that the continuity
into medieval Christian Ethiopia is stronger than earlier historians allowed, and
that the search for a moment of fall is the wrong shape of question.
