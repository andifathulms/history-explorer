At Aksum, in a field, there is a granite monolith lying broken in five pieces. It
is about thirty-three metres long and weighs something in the order of five
hundred tonnes. It was carved to look like a multi-storey building — a false door
at the base, rows of beam-ends and windows up the shaft, a curved top — and it
was quarried four kilometres away and dragged to the site.

It fell, almost certainly while being raised, and was never re-erected.

<Figure id="a-remhai" />

It is the largest single piece of stone anybody in the ancient world attempted to
stand upright. The Egyptian obelisks are smaller. The unfinished obelisk at Aswan
is bigger and was never moved.

## What that is evidence of

The stelae are pre-Christian, they mark royal graves, and there are more than a
hundred and forty of them of which the six or seven largest are the carved
architectural ones. The one still standing is twenty-four metres. Another, taken
to Rome by Mussolini's forces in 1937, was returned in 2005 and re-erected in
2008.

For a state that left no administrative documents, they are the institutional
record. Quarrying, transporting and raising a five-hundred-tonne monolith
requires a labour force that can be assembled and fed, an engineering tradition
that has done it repeatedly at increasing scale, and a political authority that
can commit resources on that order to a grave.

Ezana's inscriptions name regiments, which suggests a standing force rather than
a levy, and that is an inference from a handful of names. The stelae are the
harder evidence for the same underlying claim: whatever Aksum's arrangements
were, the state could direct organised labour at scale, and it did so for six or
seven generations running.

## And what it is not evidence of

Not how the throne passed, which nobody knows. Not the fiscal machinery beyond
the customs and the tribute. Not who was buried under most of them.

Phillipson is careful about the limits. The excavated tombs under the great stelae were
robbed in antiquity. The names on the king lists cannot be securely matched to
the monuments. What survives is the fact of the effort and almost nothing about
the people who commanded it.

## The turn

Then the stelae stop.

They stop in the fourth century, at about the point of Ezana's conversion, and
the royal burials move to churches. That transition — from monolith to
basilica, both of them the biggest structure anyone in the region was building —
is the clearest single statement of what Ezana's conversion did to the state, and
it is legible in the ground before any text has to be consulted.
