This record's `descendant_scripts` count is 1 and the item is the vocalised Ge'ez
syllabary. It is one of the few non-zero entries on that axis in the corpus, and
the reasoning behind it is worth setting out, because the corpus has spent
several pages explaining why other scripts do *not* count here.

## What was adapted, and by whom

The Ge'ez script descends from the South Arabian alphabet — the same family the
Sabaean inscriptions this corpus's Saba and Himyar records describe are written
in. That derivation happened before this record starts and belongs to whoever
carried it across the Red Sea.

What happened under Aksum, in the fourth century and apparently in the same
generation as the conversion, is that the script acquired systematic vowel
marking. Each consonant sign was modified into a set of seven forms, one for each
vowel, by adding strokes, loops and changes of shape to the base letter.

That turned a consonantal alphabet into a syllabary, and coding rule 3 counts
exactly this: *an existing script extended with new letters or conventions to
write a language it did not previously write* — or, as here, to write it in a way
the parent system could not. It is the same kind of claim the Old Babylonian
record carries for Hittite cuneiform.

The count sits on this record and not on a South Arabian one because the
vocalisation is the Aksumite act. The inherited consonantal skeleton is the
parent's.

## Why the other cases went the other way

This corpus has four records where a script claim lands on somebody else's page,
and they are worth naming beside this one because the difference is the whole
substance of rule 3.

Hittite cuneiform is counted on the Old Babylonian record. Elamite cuneiform is
counted on a Mesopotamian one. Urartian cuneiform is counted on the Neo-Assyrian
record. Cyrillic is counted on the Byzantine record, although the workshop was at
Preslav in Bulgaria — which the First Bulgarian record calls the most
counter-intuitive zero on this site.

In every one of those four the adapting polity took a neighbour's writing system
and used it for its own language. The parent script's owner gets the count,
because rule 3 asks whose system was adapted.

Here the parent is South Arabian and its owner is not in this corpus, and the
adaptation — the vocalisation — is a change to the system itself rather than a
borrowing of it. So the count stays where the work was done, which is the outcome
the Bulgarian page would have preferred and did not get.

## What it is still doing

Ge'ez as a spoken language died. The script did not.

It is the script of Amharic, of Tigrinya, of Tigre, and of Ge'ez itself as a
liturgical language, and it is read and written daily by tens of millions of
people. It is one of a very small number of writing systems in use today that is
neither alphabetic in the Phoenician line nor derived from Chinese, and the shape
it has — a consonant with a vowel built into the letterform — was given to it in
this kingdom in the fourth century.

The Nabataean record in this corpus carries the Arabic alphabet as its single
influence count and observes that the polity scores near-nothing on every other
axis. This record is the same shape: no reach figure, three turning points, one
coded institution that is an inference, and a writing system that outlived the
state by eleven hundred years and is being typed in now.
