title: Gold, and a change of symbol
drafted_from: munro-hay-1991
phase: peak

Aksumite coinage is the best evidence the kingdom left about itself. It was
struck in gold, silver and bronze from the late third century, with legends
initially in Greek — the language of the Red Sea trade — and later in Ge'ez, and
it circulated as far as India, where Aksumite coins have been found.

The gold is the significant part. Very few states minted it: Rome, Persia and
Aksum, on the common contemporary reckoning, and issuing a gold coinage means
having gold, a trade that requires it, and a state whose stamp people will accept
at face value.

The religious change is legible in the same series. Early issues carry a disc and
crescent, the pre-Christian symbol; from Ezana's reign they carry a cross. Nobody
had to write the conversion down for it to be dated — it is on the money, and the
inscriptions shift in the same reign from invoking the god Mahrem to invoking the
Lord of Heaven.

The Ge'ez script counted on this page belongs to the same period. It derives from
South Arabian letterforms, but under Aksum it acquired systematic vowel marking,
turning a consonantal alphabet into a syllabary — an adaptation substantial
enough that the coding rules count it, and one still in use for Amharic and
Tigrinya seventeen centuries later.
