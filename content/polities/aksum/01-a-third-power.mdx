Some time in the third century the Persian prophet Mani listed the great
kingdoms of the world. There were four: Rome, Persia, Aksum, and China.

Two of those four are among the largest records in this corpus. One is on the
other side of Asia. The fourth is a highland kingdom in what is now northern
Ethiopia and Eritrea, and a reader who has not met it before is entitled to ask
what it was doing in that company.

## A port and a plateau

The answer is a combination almost nowhere else had.

Aksum sits at about two thousand metres on the northern Ethiopian plateau —
good agricultural country, reliable rainfall, defensible, and cool enough to be
healthy. Adulis, its port, sits on the Red Sea about a hundred and seventy
kilometres away and eight hundred metres of descent below.

The plateau fed the state and the port connected it to everything. The Periplus
of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek merchant's handbook of the first century, has
Adulis exporting ivory, rhinoceros horn, tortoiseshell and obsidian, and
importing cloth, glass, brass, iron and wine, with a note that the king — the
Periplus calls him Zoskales — is careful about his money and knows Greek
literature.

That is the earliest external description of the polity on this record and it is
already describing a state with a customs regime and a literate ruler.

## The route

What made a small highland kingdom one of Mani's four is where the goods were
going.

The Red Sea was the Mediterranean's route to India. Roman ships from Egypt ran
down it with the monsoon; African ivory, incense and gold came up it; and Aksum
sat at the point where the African interior's goods met the sea lane. This
record's `revenue_basis` reads `trade-toll, tribute` for that reason — the same
first value this corpus gives Palmyra, Characene, Derbend and the Nabataeans, and
here at the largest scale any of them reached.

The inland half of the field is the tribute. Ezana's campaign inscriptions,
which are the closest thing this kingdom left to an administrative record, list
what came back from expeditions against neighbouring peoples: cattle by the
thousand, sheep, captives, and the submission of named rulers.

## Two languages, in stone

The kingdom wrote in Ge'ez and in Greek and frequently in both at once. Ezana's
inscriptions survive in versions in Ge'ez in its own script, Ge'ez in South
Arabian letters, and Greek — the same text, three ways, on the same monument.

Greek because the customers were Greek-speaking. Ge'ez because the population
was. A state that puts both on a royal monument is telling two audiences the
same thing, and the sixth chapter is about what happened to the second of those
scripts under Aksumite hands.
