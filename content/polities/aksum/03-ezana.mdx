Ezana ruled somewhere around the middle of the fourth century and this record
names him as its peak, which for a kingdom with no reach figure and no
administrative archive means something specific: he is the reign the evidence
converges on.

His inscriptions survive in Ge'ez, in Greek and in Ge'ez written with South
Arabian letters. His coins survive in quantity. And the thing that happened
during his reign can be dated from the money without anybody having written it
down.

## The change of symbol

The early Aksumite gold carries a disc and a crescent above the king's head — the
pre-Christian symbol, on issues from the late third century onward.

From part-way through Ezana's reign the same coinage carries a cross.

The inscriptions change in the same reign and in the same direction. The earlier
ones open by invoking Mahrem, the unconquered war god whom the Greek versions
render as Ares, and describe the king as his son. The later ones invoke the Lord
of Heaven, who has given the king power, and end with a formula about the might
of the Lord of All.

This record's `legitimation` field carries `divine-sanction` across the whole
span, and the point of the field is exactly this. The structure of the claim did
not change. The king's right came from above before the conversion and after it;
what changed was the identity of the power above, and the formulae stayed
recognisably the same shape while the name in them was replaced.

Very few records in this corpus can show a legitimation field's content turning
over inside one reign with the evidence in two independent media.

## Frumentius

The story of how it happened comes from Rufinus, writing in Latin around 400,
who says he had it from the brother of the man involved.

Two young Syrians from Tyre, Frumentius and Aedesius, were on a ship that put in
on the Red Sea coast; the crew were killed and the boys were taken to the
Aksumite court, where they were kept and given positions. Frumentius eventually
went to Alexandria, asked Athanasius to send a bishop for the Christians in
Aksum, and was told to go and be one himself.

Whatever the story is worth, the institutional result is not in doubt and it is
the most consequential thing in this record. The Ethiopian church took its head
from Alexandria and went on doing so for sixteen hundred years — the *abun* was
an Egyptian appointed by the Coptic patriarch until 1959. That made it
Miaphysite, aligned with Egypt and Armenia and Syria rather than with
Constantinople and Rome, and it is why this record's `religions_carried` count is
Miaphysite Christianity and not simply Christianity.

The alignment is also why the church survived. When Egypt became Muslim in the
640s, the Alexandrian patriarchate continued under Muslim rule and went on
consecrating bishops for a Christian kingdom in the Ethiopian highlands that no
Muslim power ever conquered. Two Christian establishments cut off from the rest
of Christendom, each supplying something the other needed, for a thousand years.

## The order of events

One thing is worth stating plainly because the sequence is unusual.

Ezana's conversion is roughly contemporary with Constantine's and earlier than
the Christianisation of most of the Roman west, earlier than Ireland, earlier
than the Franks by a century and a half, and earlier than the Rus by six hundred
and fifty years. This corpus's Armenian record has the earlier claim and the two
are usually the pair that gets cited.

Aksum is not usually in the list at all, which is a fact about who has written
the lists.
