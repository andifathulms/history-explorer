Around 518 a merchant from Alexandria was at Adulis, the Aksumite port on the
Red Sea, when the governor asked him to do a piece of clerical work. The king was
preparing to invade Himyar and had written down for copies of two Greek
inscriptions standing outside the town.

The merchant and a colleague named Menas went and copied them. One set went to
the governor. They kept a duplicate, and twenty-five years later the merchant —
by then a monk, writing a book to prove that the earth is a flat rectangle shaped
like the Tabernacle — printed it. That is the only reason the text exists.

## What was standing there

Cosmas describes the objects with a surveyor's care, because he had measured
them.

At the entrance to the town, on the western road, was a marble chair about two
and a half cubits high, of the shape he calls a bishop's throne, cut with Greek
characters. Behind it stood a slab of basanite three cubits high, quadrangular,
rising to a point at the top so that the sides sloped down like a lambda. The
slab had fallen over behind the chair and its lower part was broken away. Both
were covered in Greek.

On the back of the chair were carved figures of Hercules and Mercury. His
companion Menas thought they stood for strength and wealth. Cosmas preferred to
take Hercules as strength and Mercury as speech, on the authority of the Acts of
the Apostles, where the people of Lystra call Barnabas Jupiter and Paul Mercury
because Paul was the chief speaker.

The inscription on the slab was Ptolemaic — a king of Egypt recording an
expedition into Asia with elephants from the Troglodytes and Ethiopia. The one on
the chair was Aksumite, and it is the only extended contemporary account of an
Aksumite king's campaigns that survives in any language.

## Why the king wanted a copy

Because he was about to do the same thing, and the stone said it had been done
before.

The king was Kaleb, whom Cosmas calls Elesbaan, and he was fitting out an
expedition across the Gulf against the Jewish king of Himyar, who had been
killing the Christians of Najran. That war is the high point of Aksumite power:
an African kingdom crossing the Red Sea, destroying an Arabian one, and governing
it through a client for half a century.

The governor of Adulis was ordered to send up copies of the inscriptions. The
inference is plain enough and Cosmas does not draw it: a king preparing an
overseas campaign wanted the text of the local monument recording the reach of
earlier rulers, and the traders who happened to be in port were told to transcribe
it.

## The other thing he was told about

Cosmas also records how the kingdom paid for itself, and he is careful to say that
this part is not his own observation.

Every other year the king of the Aksumites sent agents through the governor of
Agau into the country called Sasu, on the edge of the gold-bearing land, to
bargain for gold; some five hundred other traders travelled with them. They took
oxen, lumps of salt and iron. At the mining district they camped and fenced the
camp with a great hedge of thorns, slaughtered the oxen, cut them up, and laid the
pieces of meat on top of the thorns along with the salt and the iron.

> Then come the natives bringing gold in nuggets like peas, called tancharas, and
> lay one or two or more of these upon what pleases them — the pieces of flesh or
> the salt or the iron, and then they retire to some distance off. Then the owner
> of the meat approaches, and if he is satisfied he takes the gold away, and upon
> seeing this its owner comes and takes the flesh or the salt or the iron. If,
> however, he is not satisfied, he leaves the gold.

Business was done that way, Cosmas says, because the languages were different and
interpreters were hardly to be found. The expedition stayed five days or
thereabouts, and went home armed, because the tribes on the road knew they were
carrying gold. Going and returning took six months.

## Two kinds of evidence

Both halves of the chapter are what a merchant notices, and they are worth
separating.

The inscriptions Cosmas measured and copied himself, and his transcript has been
the basis of everything written about early Aksumite conquests ever since. The
gold trade he had from the men who made the journey, and the silent exchange he
describes is a story told about distant peoples in a great many places and
periods, including by Herodotus about the Atlantic coast. It may be exactly right.
It is not the same order of testimony.

What is not in doubt is the shape of the operation: a kingdom in the highlands,
reaching the sea at one port, sending a state-organised caravan of five hundred
men on a six-month round trip every second year to buy gold with salt, iron and
beef, and using the proceeds to put an army across the Red Sea.
