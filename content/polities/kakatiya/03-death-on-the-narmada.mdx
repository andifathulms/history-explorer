Prataparudra inherited a kingdom already tested by decades of war and
his own grandmother's contested rule, and spent his first years on the
throne, in 1303, fighting off the earliest Delhi Sultanate raid into
Telangana. The army 'Ala-ud-Din Khalji sent under Malik Fakhr-ud-Din
and Jhaju reached almost to Warangal itself before the Kakatiya forces
checked it at Upparapalli and drove it back in what the source calls a
crushing defeat — a genuine victory, but one that only postponed the
reckoning.

## A second army, and a first tribute

'Ala-ud-Din did not forget the humiliation. Once his northern and
western frontiers were secure, he sent a far larger force under Malik
Na'ib Kafur, which reached the Kakatiya capital in January 1310 and
settled in for a prolonged siege. This time Prataparudra sued for
peace rather than risk another battle, and Malik Na'ib accepted on
hard terms: Prataparudra would hand over his accumulated wealth
outright and pay an annual tribute in money, elephants and horses
going forward. A kingdom that had checked one Delhi army in the field
became, without losing a single battle in this second round, a
tributary of the very power it had beaten seven years before.

## A tribute that stopped, and an army that returned

The tribute did not last. As Delhi's own control over the Deccan
weakened amid a rapid succession of new sultans, Prataparudra simply
stopped paying. Ghiyas-ud-Din Tughluq responded by sending his son
Ulugh Khan — the future Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq — with a large
army that besieged Warangal for six months. Caught without an adequate
garrison or enough stored grain to withstand a long siege, Prataparudra
fought as best he could, but when his hastily gathered provisions ran
out and his troops began to starve, he opened the gates and surrendered
himself and his family.

## A captive who never reached Delhi

Ulugh Khan judged it too dangerous to leave the fallen king anywhere
in Telangana, where his presence might spark a revolt, and sent him
toward Delhi under armed escort. Prataparudra never arrived. He died
on the banks of the Narmada river partway through the journey in 1323.
One chronicler dismisses the death in a single hostile line without
explaining it; a later grant, by contrast, states that Prataparudra
"departed to the world of Gods by his own desire" — language the
source reads as pointing to suicide, or a killing carried out at the
fallen king's own request, rather than death from illness on the road.
A proud monarch who had once driven a Delhi army from his own capital
in open battle chose, it seems, not to see the inside of the one that
had finally beaten him.
