In 1199 Georgian forces under Tamar's generals, the Zakarid brothers — Armenian
nobles in Georgian service, who governed the Armenian territories the kingdom
took — captured Ani from the last Shaddadid emir.

That ending is worth pausing on, because it is almost the only time in a
thousand years that one Caucasian state ends another. Everything else in the
isthmus is ended from outside it: the Bagratid kingdom by Byzantium, Derbend by
the Seljuks, the Shirvanshahs by the Safavids, Iberia and Armenia and Albania by
the Sasanians. The Caucasian polities are neighbours who intermarry, raid,
shelter each other's exiles and quarrel about passes, and hardly ever finish one
another. What finishes them arrives.

The city changed hands afterwards more or less continuously — Georgian,
Mongol, Turkmen, Ottoman — and was finished by earthquakes and by the shifting
of the trade routes rather than by a conquest. What is on the site today is a
ruined city on a closed border in a military zone, with a cathedral, a mosque,
several churches and a great deal of wall.

The dynasty invented no script, propagated no religion, and left nobody claiming
to be its heir. What it has instead is a position: a Kurdish emirate governing an
Armenian capital under a Turkish sultanate, ended by a Georgian queen, and a
family that left it in the twelfth century and took Egypt.

That is a fair description of the Caucasus. Nothing in it inherits from anything
else in it. Everything in it connects to somewhere else.
