Alp Arslan took Ani in 1064 and sacked it. The Armenian nobility dispersed
westward, and out of that dispersal came, eventually, the Armenian kingdom of
Cilicia.

In 1072 the Seljuks handed Ani to a Shaddadid. Manuchihr ibn Shavur took the
city and his descendants held it, with interruptions, until 1199 — a hundred and
twenty-six years of a Kurdish Muslim dynasty governing the royal capital of
Christian Armenia.

<Figure id="manuchihr-mosque" />

The arrangement worked better than the sentence suggests. The Shaddadids of Ani
governed an overwhelmingly Armenian city; they used the walls the Bagratids had
built and added to them, minted coins, maintained the churches, and appear in
Armenian sources as rulers to be complained about rather than as an occupation
to be resisted. Manuchihr built the mosque that still stands on the edge of the
gorge with his name on it, beside a cathedral that was fifty years older than
his family's arrival.

<Figure id="manuchihr-vault" />

The Ganja half of the house did not last as long. The Seljuks took Ganja itself
in 1075 and the senior line ended there. So the dynasty's story begins in Arran
and finishes in Armenia, because its centre of gravity moved when its own
capital was taken and it was handed somebody else's.

The mechanism behind that is the ordinary one for a family that holds territory
as property: divide it among the members, let the branches diverge, and end up
with what are effectively separate polities. Usually this is how a dynasty
destroys itself. Here it is what saved it. The branch that was given the ruined
city outlived the branch that kept the good one by a hundred and twenty-four
years.
