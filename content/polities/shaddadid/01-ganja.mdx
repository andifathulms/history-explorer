The Shaddadids were Kurds, and they appear in Arran — the country between the
Kura and the Araxes, which is now western Azerbaijan — in the middle of the
tenth century, when the Sallarid dynasty that had been holding it was failing.

Muhammad ibn Shaddad established himself at Dvin around 951. His sons took Ganja
in 971, and Ganja was the seat for the following century. Fadl I, who ruled from
985 to 1031, is the substantial figure: he held Arran against the Byzantines and
the Georgians and the Rus raiders coming up the Kura from the Caspian, and he
built the bridge over the Araxes at Khudaferin, whose piers are still in the
river.

<Figure id="fadl-coin" />

Kurdish dynasties of this period are rarer in the histories than they were on the
ground. The Marwanids in the Jazira were another and stayed Kurdish; the Ayyubids
were Kurdish by origin and Arabised within a generation. All three came out of
the same collapse in the same century — the Abbasid provincial system failing,
and local men with local followings keeping what they were already holding — and
none of them is usually described as part of one phenomenon, because the three
are filed in three separate scholarly literatures.

The Shaddadid position was between things and always had been: Muslim rulers over
a largely Armenian and Albanian Christian population, in country the Byzantines
claimed, next to a Georgian kingdom that was rising, with the Shirvanshahs and
Derbend to the east and Baghdad's authority to the south becoming theoretical.
They managed it the way small dynasties in that position always do — by
acknowledging whoever was nearest and strongest, and by marrying into whoever
was next.

<Figure id="khudaferin" />

Then the Seljuks arrived, and the family got a second life in an unexpected
place.
