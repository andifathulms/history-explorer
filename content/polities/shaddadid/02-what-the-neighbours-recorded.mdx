---
title: What the neighbours recorded
drafted_from: aristakes-bedrosian
phase: expansion
---

The Shaddadids left no chronicle of their own. What survives of their rise comes
from the people they were rising at the expense of, and the Armenian who wrote
the fullest contemporary account of the eleventh century mentions them exactly
three times — which is itself the most informative thing about them.

The first mention is a relationship. Aristakes calls the Shaddadid ruler
Apusuar — Abu'l-Aswar Shavur — and identifies him in a single clause as the man
who held Dvin and Ganja and was the son-in-law of Ashot, king of Armenia. That
is the whole of the Shaddadid method in eight words. A Kurdish Muslim family
holding two cities in a Christian country had married into the Bagratid royal
house, and the Armenian historian records it as an ordinary fact about who
somebody was, not as a scandal.

The second is a war, and it is being fought against the Byzantines rather than
against the Armenians. After Ani was surrendered to Constantinople in 1045, the
emperor's lieutenant subdued the country and then marched on Dvin; Abu'l-Aswar
fought him at the city gate, and Aristakes says an inestimable number of men were
killed there — including Vahram, the great prince of Armenia, and his son, which
caused the Armenians great mourning. Vahram had been one of the nobles who put
the last Bagratid king on the throne and was ignored when it mattered. He died
outside a Shaddadid city, in Byzantine service, fighting the emir who had married
into his own royal family.

The third is a raid. In 1055 troops came into Armenia under the Seljuk sultan's
name, though Aristakes notes that some say they were Abu'l-Aswar's. People fled
out of the open country towards Ani and not all of them got inside, because night
fell and the gates were shut. The troops came in the dark, seized the gates, put
swords to work, and went home with booty and captives.

Set the three together and the shape of the expansion is visible without a single
Shaddadid document. While the Armenian kingdom was being dismantled — by its own
nobles selling their fortresses, by a catholicos selling the capital, by a
Byzantine administration that garrisoned the cities and then quarrelled with the
church — the family at Dvin and Ganja was doing none of those things. It was
marrying into the royal house, holding its walls against the empire that had
bought the kingdom, and taking what was outside the walls when it could. In 1045
Ani belonged to the Bagratids. In 1064 the Seljuks stormed it. By 1072 it was
Shaddadid, and stayed Shaddadid for most of the next century and a quarter.

There is a reason a dynasty like this appears in other people's books only when
it raids. A house that survives by marriage, by paying whoever is currently
strongest and by not being worth a campaign does not generate the kind of event a
chronicler records. It generates continuity, which is invisible until you notice
that everyone who was writing about them is gone and they are still there.
