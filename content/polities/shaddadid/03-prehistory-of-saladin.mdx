---
title: Where Saladin's family came from
drafted_from: minorsky-1953
phase: aside
---

Minorsky's volume has three essays. Two are on the Shaddadids of Ganja and of
Ani. The third is called "Prehistory of Saladin", and it is the reason this page
matters to several others on this site.

This corpus's Ayyubid record opens in Egypt, with a Kurdish officer in the
service of Nur al-Din of Damascus, sent south on his uncle's staff. It says the
family was Kurdish and moves on, because that is where the Ayyubid story is
usually taken to begin.

It began here. Saladin's grandfather Shadhi came from Dvin, in the Armenian
borderlands of Shaddadid Arran — the town Muhammad ibn Shaddad had established
himself in around 951 — and the family was of the Rawadiya, a Kurdish group
associated with that country. Shadhi took his sons out of the Caucasus to Iraq
in the early twelfth century; one of them, Ayyub, ended up commanding a fortress
at Tikrit for the Seljuk governor, and the other, Shirkuh, took service with
Zangi. Their descendants took Egypt.

Minorsky's essay works out what can be established about that migration from the
Arabic and Persian sources, and the answer is: a Kurdish military family from a
frontier zone, in the service of a local dynasty, moving south when the local
dynasty stopped being able to employ them, and finding better employment with
the Turkish military households that were then assembling in Iraq and Syria.

It is an ordinary career and it is worth having on this site because of where it
ends. The man who took Jerusalem in 1187, whose dynasty this corpus records
across Egypt, Syria, the Jazira and Yemen, and whose family's arrival is a
turning point on four pages, came from a Kurdish town in the Caucasus that
belonged to the emirate on this page.

The corpus does not carry an edge for it, and should not. Coding rule 1 is
explicit that dynastic descent on its own is not a claim: being descended from a
place or a service is a genealogy, and genealogies are not successions. What it
is is a chapter, and the chapter is here because a reader working down the
Ayyubid page would never otherwise learn that the answer to where the family came
from is two thousand kilometres north-east of anything that page describes.
