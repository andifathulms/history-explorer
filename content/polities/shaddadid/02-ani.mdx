---
title: A Kurdish emir in the Armenian capital
drafted_from: minorsky-1953
phase: peak
---

Alp Arslan took Ani in 1064 and sacked it. This site's Bagratid Armenia record
ends with that, and with the dispersal of the Armenian nobility westward that
eventually produced the Cilician kingdom.

In 1072 the Seljuks handed Ani to a Shaddadid. Manuchihr ibn Shavur took the
city and his descendants held it, with interruptions, until 1199 — a hundred and
twenty-six years of a Kurdish Muslim dynasty governing the royal capital of
Christian Armenia.

The arrangement worked better than the sentence suggests. The Shaddadids of Ani
governed an overwhelmingly Armenian city; they used the walls the Bagratids had
built and added to them, minted coins, maintained the churches, and appear in
Armenian sources as rulers to be complained about rather than as an occupation
to be resisted. Manuchihr built the mosque that still stands on the edge of the
gorge with his name on it, beside a cathedral that was fifty years older than
his family's arrival.

The Ganja half of the house did not last as long. The Seljuks took Ganja itself
in 1075 and the senior line ended there, which is why this record's span runs on
the junior branch — a page that begins in Arran and ends in Armenia because the
family's centre of gravity moved when its capital was taken and it was given
somebody else's.

The site codes the succession `appanage` on that basis, and it is the ordinary
case: territory held as family property, divided among members, with the
branches diverging until they are effectively separate polities. What is unusual
is that in this instance the division is what preserved the dynasty. The branch
that got the ruined city outlived the branch that kept the good one by a hundred
and twenty-four years.
