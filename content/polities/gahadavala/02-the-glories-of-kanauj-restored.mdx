Chandradeva's grandson Govindachandra held the throne for at least
half a century, his reign attested between 1104 and 1155 by land
grants numbering close to sixty - more than survive from any other
Gahadavala king - and by coins distributed widely enough to trace the
real extent of his authority. Smith reads this evidence plainly:
Govindachandra "succeeded to a large extent in restoring the glories
of Kanauj," rebuilding a genuine regional power on the foundation his
grandfather's conquest had only sketched out. The court held both
Kanauj and Varanasi, and one of his own land grants from Oudh, dated
to 1129, records a special levy called turushka danda - a tax raised
specifically to fund resistance against Muhammadan raiders already
probing the kingdom's own frontier, proof that even at its own
strongest the Gahadavala state was organizing its finances around a
threat it could see coming decades before that threat became fatal.

## A restored throne, an unfinished contest

What Govindachandra's reign did not do was settle, once and for all,
the older question the Pratiharas and Palas had spent three centuries
fighting over - which power would hold Kanauj permanently. His own
restoration was real, but it was also, in retrospect, a last full
flowering rather than a new equilibrium: the same turushka danda that
funded his own defenses shows a kingdom already budgeting for a war it
expected to keep fighting, against an enemy whose own raids into India
were only going to grow more frequent, not less, in the generations
after his death.
