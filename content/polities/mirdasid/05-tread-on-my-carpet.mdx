title: Tread on my carpet

Alp Arslan crossed the Euphrates in 1070 and besieged Aleppo, and Ibn Khallikan
records what he demanded and what was done about it. The demand was one sentence
and the answer took place at night.

## A thing that had not happened before

Ibn Khallikan quotes al-Ma'muni's history for the significance of the crossing
itself, and it is worth taking literally:

> It is said that neither in ancient nor in Islamic times did any Turkish king,
> prior to Alp Arslan, cross the Euphrates.

Turks had been in Syria for a century — as slave soldiers, as mercenaries, as
governors, latterly as the Turkmen bands the Mirdasids had hired and could not
dismiss. What had never happened was a Turkish *sovereign* bringing his own army
over the river.

Aleppo at that moment was held by Mahmud ibn Nasr ibn Salih ibn Mirdas al-Kilabi
— three generations from the Bedouin chief who had taken the city.

## The terms

Negotiations opened, and Alp Arslan's condition was not territorial. It was
physical.

Mahmud, he said, *should come and tread on his carpet* — should stand on the
sultan's rug — if he wished for peace.

That is the whole of it. No tribute is mentioned, no cession, no garrison. The
sultan of the largest empire in the Islamic world wanted the emir of Aleppo to
walk into his tent and be seen doing it.

So Mahmud went, at night, and he took his mother with him.

Alp Arslan received them both with great kindness, put robes of honour on them,
sent them back to their city — and decamped.

## What was actually transacted

Read one way this is a humiliation, and the night and the mother are the
evidence: a man going in the dark, with the woman whose lineage was half his
claim, to stand on a carpet.

Read another way it is the cheapest settlement any Syrian ruler got out of the
Seljuk advance. Aleppo was not besieged to a finish, not sacked, not garrisoned
and not given to somebody else. The Mirdasids kept it for another decade. What
they surrendered was the proposition that they were nobody's subjects — and by
1070 that proposition was not worth defending, because the Turkmen who were
eating their countryside had been invited in by Mahmud himself.

The court that Alp Arslan's grandfather's people had once served as hired bows
now required its emir to come and be looked at. Both sides understood the
ceremony exactly, which is why there is no fighting in the account and why the
robes of honour matter as much as the carpet.

## Afterwards

Alp Arslan went on north, and in the following year fought the battle at
Manzikert that took Anatolia off the Byzantines.

Aleppo's own reckoning came later and from a different direction. Mahmud died in
1075; his sons quarrelled; and in 1080 the city passed out of the family
altogether — handed over, not stormed, which by then was how things in northern
Syria were done.
