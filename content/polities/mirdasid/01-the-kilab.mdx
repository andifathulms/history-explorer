The Banu Kilab were an Arab tribe of northern Syria who had been in the country
around Aleppo since well before the Hamdanids and had spent the tenth century
being hired by whoever held the city. They supplied the Hamdanid cavalry. When
the Hamdanids failed they supplied the Fatimids, and when it suited them they
supplied the Byzantines, and none of these employments was exclusive or
permanent.

What they had not done was hold Aleppo. Kennedy's account of the tenth and
eleventh centuries in the Jazira and Syria makes the point that the bedouin
confederations of this period were not an interruption of settled government but
a form of it — powers with real revenue, real diplomacy and no city — and that
the recurring question is whether a tribe could take a walled town without
ceasing to be a tribe.

Salih ibn Mirdas answered it. He was imprisoned by Mansur ibn Lu'lu', the man
then holding Aleppo, escaped in 1014, raised the Kilab, beat Mansur in the field
and forced recognition as paramount emir of the countryside. He then spent ten
years taking the forts — al-Rahba on the Euphrates first — that made the city
untenable without him. He besieged Aleppo in late 1024 and was inside it by the
new year.

The dynasty is named for his father, which is the usual Arab practice and is a
small piece of evidence for the argument: nobody had heard of Mirdas, and the
name was the tribe's, not a house's.
