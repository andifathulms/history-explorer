---
title: The price of the khutba
drafted_from: zakkar-1971

Aleppo in the eleventh century sat between two empires that both wanted it and
neither of which could hold it cheaply. The Fatimids were in Egypt and southern
Syria; the Byzantines had come back to northern Syria under Nikephoros Phokas
and held Antioch, sixty miles away. The Mirdasid emirate existed in the space
between, and its foreign policy was to be worth more to each of them as a buffer
than as a province.

The instrument was the khutba — the Friday sermon, which named whichever
sovereign the city acknowledged. Naming the Fatimid imam bought Cairo's
recognition and Cairo's money. Naming nobody, or renaming, was the standing
threat. Zakkar's judgement on the Kilab is that they served whoever paid most
and, in a crisis, would sell their employer to the highest bidder, and it is
worth reading that as a description of how the emirate was financed rather than
as a complaint about tribal character. A polity with no army it did not have to
feed and no revenue base larger than one city has a short list of assets, and
its position was the largest of them.

Salih forgot this once and it killed him. In 1029 he took a Mirdasid army south
into Palestine against the Fatimids, in alliance with other bedouin, and was
destroyed at al-Uqhuwana near Lake Tiberias. His sons Nasr and Thimal inherited
half the territory and the whole lesson. For the fifty years that followed, the
Mirdasids fought their neighbours, their cousins and their own tribe, and never
again fought an empire for land.

They fought each other constantly. Nasr and Thimal divided the emirate, then
took it from each other; Thimal lost Aleppo to a Fatimid governor and got it
back; nephews and brothers held it in turn, and on at least three occasions the
city changed hands without a siege because the Kilab outside had decided which
Mirdasid they preferred.
