---
title: Thimal's peace
drafted_from: zakkar-1971

Mu'izz al-Dawla Thimal held Aleppo, with interruptions, for most of thirty years,
and the stretch from about 1050 to 1057 is the emirate working as designed. He
was recognised by the Fatimids, left alone by the Byzantines, and at peace with
his own tribe, and Aleppo went into a building boom — walls, markets, and the
kind of construction a city only undertakes when it expects to keep what it puts
up.

The arrangement Thimal ran is the answer to the question the first chapter
asked, and it is not the one either the tribe or the city would have chosen. The
Mirdasids did not govern Aleppo the way the Hamdanids had. They lived largely
outside it with the Kilab, drew the revenue, and left the city's day-to-day
administration to its own notables and its own judges — a division of labour
that was stable while the emir was strong and ambiguous the moment he was not.
His wife Alawiyya bint Waththab, a Kilabi noblewoman, held al-Rahba in her own
right and negotiated with Cairo directly.

The emirate's ceiling is visible in what Thimal did with his good years, which
was nothing. There was no expansion, no institution founded, no army built that
did not disperse at harvest. A polity whose income is its own strategic
awkwardness has no reason to become anything else and no way to survive the
awkwardness ending.
