Eleventh-century Aleppo, like Damascus, had an ahdath: an armed militia raised
from the quarters of the city, commanded by a ra'is drawn from the local
notables, and answering to neither the emir nor the caliph. It policed the
markets, took sides in factional fighting, opened and closed the gates, and on
more than one occasion decided who ruled.

<Figure id="mi-gate" />

The ordinary categories for a medieval army all describe how a ruler raised a
force — a tribal levy, a grant of land for service, purchased slaves,
conscription. None of them fits, because the ahdath was not raised by a ruler.
It was the armed expression of a city that had learned, across the collapse of
Hamdanid rule and four decades of Fatimid, Byzantine and bedouin claimants,
that it would need to hold its own walls between regimes. Calling it a state
levy would be wrong, and calling it a following owed to some chief would be
wrong too. The emirate's own soldiers were the Kilab; the
city's soldiers were something else entirely.

The ahdath is what the eleventh century in Syria has instead of a state. Aleppo
and Damascus both spent this period being fought over by outsiders whose armies
came and went, and both cities retained a permanent, local, non-dynastic armed
body that outlasted every claimant. When Nur al-Din takes Damascus in 1154 the
gates are opened from the inside, and the reason there is an inside to open
them is a hundred and fifty years of this.

It ends when the Turkish military households arrive with soldiers they own. The
Zangids and after them the Ayyubids had no use for an armed citizenry and
suppressed it, and the militia disappears from the record in the twelfth
century. Every polity that holds Aleppo or Damascus after 1128 raised its army
by purchase or by revenue assignment, and none of them let a city keep weapons.
