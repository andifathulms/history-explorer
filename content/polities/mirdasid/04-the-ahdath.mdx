---
title: The city's own soldiers
drafted_from: zakkar-1971
phase: aside
---

Eleventh-century Aleppo, like Damascus, had an ahdath: an armed militia raised
from the quarters of the city, commanded by a ra'is drawn from the local
notables, and answering to neither the emir nor the caliph. It policed the
markets, took sides in factional fighting, opened and closed the gates, and on
more than one occasion decided who ruled.

This site's institutions vocabulary has no value for it, and that is deliberate
rather than an oversight. `tribal-levy`, `land-grant`, `slave-soldier`,
`conscript` and the rest all describe how a ruler raised a force. The ahdath was
not raised by a ruler. It was the armed expression of a city that had learned,
across the collapse of Hamdanid rule and four decades of Fatimid, Byzantine and
bedouin claimants, that it would need to hold its own walls between regimes.
Coding it as `conscript` would say a state levied it; coding it as `client-levy`
would say a chief owed it. Both are false, and the field is coded `tribal-levy`
for the Kilab alone, with this chapter carrying the rest.

The corpus reason to notice it is that the ahdath is what the eleventh century
in Syria has instead of a state. Aleppo and Damascus both spent this period
being fought over by outsiders whose armies came and went, and both cities
retained a permanent, local, non-dynastic armed body that outlasted every
claimant. When Nur al-Din takes Damascus in 1154 the gates are opened from the
inside, and the reason there is an inside to open them is a hundred and fifty
years of this.

It ends when the Turkish military households arrive with soldiers they own. The
Zangids and after them the Ayyubids had no use for an armed citizenry and
suppressed it, and the militia disappears from the record in the twelfth
century. Every polity that holds Aleppo or Damascus after 1128 raised its army
by purchase or by revenue assignment, and none of them let a city keep weapons.
