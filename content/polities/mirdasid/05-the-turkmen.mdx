title: The Turkmen

From the 1060s the emirate's problem stopped being empires and became migration.
Turkmen bands moving west ahead of and alongside the Seljuk advance arrived in
northern Syria in numbers, and they were not an army that could be bought off
once. They raided the countryside the Kilab grazed, took service with whichever
Mirdasid was paying, and left when the pay stopped. Mahmud ibn Nasr used them to
take Aleppo from his uncle and then could not get rid of them.

In 1070 he appealed to the Seljuk sultan Alp Arslan to control the Turkmen he
had invited in. Alp Arslan came the following spring, on his way to the campaign
that ended at Manzikert, and after a short siege Mahmud submitted. The khutba at
Aleppo passed from the Fatimid imam in Cairo to the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad
and the Seljuk sultan.

Wilson's argument about this period is that reading it as two invasions —
Turkish from the east, Frankish from the west — arriving at a passive Syria gets
the causation backwards, and the Mirdasid case is his kind of example: the
Turkmen were in Aleppo because an Aleppan emir hired them, and the Seljuk sultan
was at the walls because that emir wrote to him.

What the submission cost was the emirate's entire business. For fifty years the
Mirdasids had been paid to be the thing between Cairo and Constantinople. Once
Aleppo read the khutba for Baghdad and a Seljuk sultan had beaten the Byzantine
emperor in the field, there were no longer two customers, and a bedouin emirate
holding one city had nothing else to sell.
