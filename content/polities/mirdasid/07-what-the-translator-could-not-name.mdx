title: What the translator could not name

The Mirdasids handed Aleppo over in 1080 and the city carried on without them,
as it had carried on before them.

Over the next seventy years it belonged in turn to the Uqaylids, to the Seljuk
Tutush, to Tutush's son Ridwan, to a succession of Turkish officers, to Ilghazi
the Artuqid, to his nephew Balak, to Zangi, and finally to Nur al-Din. Some of
those transfers were sieges. Most were negotiations, and the party being
negotiated with was frequently not an army at all.

## The word in the footnote

Ibn al-Qalanisi keeps mentioning the *ahdath* of Aleppo, and when H. A. R. Gibb
came to translate him in 1932 he had to stop and admit that he did not know
exactly what to call them.

His note says it is difficult to assign a precise meaning to the word; that it
may be applied loosely to young men in general, to apprentices, or even to the
town rabble; and that certain passages nevertheless make it clear that they
were a definite military organisation, for which he settles on "armed bands" or
*gens d'armes*.

That footnote is the afterlife of the Mirdasid emirate in one paragraph. The
institution that repeatedly decided who governed Aleppo has no stable name in
the sources, no founder, no charter and no formal place in anybody's
government, and a great Arabist working from the fullest contemporary chronicle
could not pin it down beyond a guess and a hedge.

## Why it is hard to name

Because it was not part of the state. Every category the period offers
describes a force a ruler raised — a tribal levy, a grant of land in return for
service, purchased slaves, conscription. The ahdath was the armed expression of
a city that had learned it would be governed by strangers and had better be
able to choose which ones.

That is why it survived every dynasty. The Kilab who put the Mirdasids in
Aleppo were gone by 1080. The Uqaylids lasted five years, Ridwan and his
officers a generation, the Artuqids months at a time. The militia of the
quarters was there throughout, opening and closing gates, and in 1154 the same
institution in Damascus let Nur al-Din in over the wall.

## What the dynasty is worth

The Mirdasids ruled Aleppo for fifty-five years and left almost nothing inside
its walls that made them hard to replace, which is the judgement their own
ending delivers.

But the thing they governed through — a city with its own armed body, its own
notables, its own opinion about who should hold the citadel, and the ability to
act on it — outlasted them by a century and a half and settled the fate of both
of Syria's great cities in the end. The eleventh century in Syria is usually
written as a list of dynasties passing through. It reads better as the history
of two towns deciding, repeatedly, whom to let in.
