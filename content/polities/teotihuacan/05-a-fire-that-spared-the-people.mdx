Sometime in the 600s or 700s, the physical apparatus of the Teotihuacan
state was destroyed by fire. Temples and elite residences, concentrated
especially along the central section of the city's own great avenue,
were burned and their idols deliberately smashed - a pattern of
destruction archaeologists describe as selective rather than
indiscriminate, aimed precisely at the buildings and objects that
represented state authority rather than at the city as a whole.

## Insiders, outsiders, or both

Who carried out the burning remains genuinely disputed. One reading
holds that only people with intimate knowledge of the city's own sacred
architecture could have known exactly what to target and how to
desecrate it most effectively - the act of insiders settling an old
account, much as the earlier burning of the Feathered Serpent Pyramid
may have been. Another reading holds that centuries of decline had
allowed surrounding societies to grow strong enough, in population and
organization, to overwhelm a capital that had lost the will or the
leadership to defend itself, with any dissident insiders who joined them
playing a supporting rather than a leading role. The evidence does not
clearly favour either account over the other.

## A city that outlived its own empire

What the fire did not do was empty the city outright. A substantial
population, perhaps forty thousand people, remained or resettled at
Teotihuacan afterward, and the site has remained continuously
significant, if never again as a capital, down to the present day. What
ended was not the place but its politics: Teotihuacan would go on being
a town, sometimes a fairly important one, for the rest of Mesoamerican
history, but it never again commanded the kind of regional authority,
tribute, or prestige that had let its own rulers raise, without any need
to explain themselves in writing, the largest religious monuments the
Americas had yet seen.
