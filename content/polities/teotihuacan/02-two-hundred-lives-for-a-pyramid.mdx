Around 200 CE, the dedication of the temple honouring the Feathered
Serpent within the Ciudadela precinct was marked with a mass killing.
Excavations in the late 1980s uncovered roughly two hundred sacrificial
victims buried as part of the pyramid's own construction, many dressed
in military costume and equipped with weapons, arranged beneath and
around the structure in patterns archaeologists connect to the
Mesoamerican calendar and creation symbolism rather than to any random
disposal of bodies. Two large looted pits beneath the pyramid and at the
foot of its stairway may once have held the remains of Teotihuacan's own
rulers, accompanied in death by these followers.

## A war god as real as the warriors who served him

The scale of the sacrifice fits a broader pattern archaeology has
uncovered across the city: war was not merely symbolic decoration at
Teotihuacan but a genuine organizing force in its own political life.
Clay figurines found throughout the city, once puzzling for their
contorted poses, are now understood to depict warriors poised to throw
a spear, shield in hand. Armies drawn from common citizens as well as
the elite, fighting in disciplined formation with dart-throwing
weapons, most plausibly explain how a city without any obvious natural
fortress commanded the loyalty, or the fear, of neighbouring
settlements it would otherwise have had every reason to resist.

## A reputation that outlasted the reality

Teotihuacan's own military reputation eventually travelled further than
its armies plausibly ever marched. Distant Maya cities as far away as
Tikal and Kaminaljuyú adopted Teotihuacan-derived war symbolism and
weaponry into their own art and ritual, evidence less of direct conquest
- which the distances involved make unlikely - than of a prestige so
potent that foreign elites found it worth borrowing on its own terms,
regardless of whether Teotihuacan's own soldiers had ever set foot in
their territory.
