Sometime in the first or second century before the common era, the
scattered population of the Basin of Mexico began concentrating in a
single place in its semi-arid northeastern reaches. The move was
remarkably fast: within a couple of centuries the settlement covered
some twenty square kilometres and held sixty to eighty thousand people,
a scale no earlier Mesoamerican community had approached. Modern
archaeology calls the city Teotihuacan - a name given to it centuries
later by the Aztecs, who found its ruins already ancient and its
builders' own name for it already lost.

## Pyramids built to need no inscription

What the newly concentrated population built first were monuments on a
scale meant to be self-explanatory. The Pyramid of the Sun, begun early
in the city's growth, rivals in footprint the largest pyramids of
Egypt. Within a few generations it was joined by the Pyramid of the
Moon and, further south, a walled sixteen-hectare precinct called the
Ciudadela enclosing a third great structure honouring the Feathered
Serpent. All of it was laid out along a single five-kilometre avenue,
later named the Avenue of the Dead, under one coordinated city plan
imposed across nearly the whole settlement - a degree of central control
matched by few other Mesoamerican cities before or after it.

## Rulers who left buildings instead of names

No inscription found at Teotihuacan names a king, records a conquest, or
commemorates a dynasty the way contemporary rulers elsewhere in
Mesoamerica did on carved stelae. What survives instead is architecture:
structures on a scale that made verbal or pictorial boasting
unnecessary, built by rulers modern archaeologists infer were few in
number, individually powerful, and confident enough in their own
authority that they needed no monument to say so directly. Possible
royal tombs are suspected beneath both the Sun and Moon pyramids, though
looting in antiquity has left their contents, and the identities of
whoever built them, unrecoverable.
