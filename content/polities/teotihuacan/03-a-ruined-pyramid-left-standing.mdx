The Feathered Serpent Pyramid did not stand unaltered for long.
Excavation of its front face found that the pyramid and the temple atop
it had been deliberately burned in an intense fire, its modelled clay
walls smashed and used as fill for a stepped platform later built to
cover the ruin. What is striking is what the builders chose not to do:
rather than burying the damaged structure under a grander replacement,
in the way earlier Teotihuacan monuments had regularly been enlarged
and rebuilt, they left the burned ruin exposed behind the new
platform - a visible scar rather than a concealed one.

## A warning left standing on purpose

Archaeologists read the decision to leave the damage visible as
deliberate: a reminder, aimed at any future ruler tempted to repeat the
scale of the pyramid's own dedication sacrifice, of what excess had
already cost one regime. Whether the reaction came quickly, from elites
appalled at the killing of their own high-status kin, or only after a
succession of autocratic rulers had continued the same pattern for a
century or more before a weaker one allowed revolt to succeed, the
archaeological sequence cannot yet say with confidence.

## A city that may have chosen to be ruled differently

What followed, across the centuries from roughly 250 to 600 or 700 CE,
was a city that kept building on a large scale but stopped building new
pyramids to individual glory. Effort shifted instead to architecturally
substantial apartment compounds that eventually housed nearly the whole
population, from the lowest social rank to the highest, within
standardized residential units rather than grand individual monuments.
Whether this reflected a genuinely new theory of collective governance
or a restoration of an older one that a period of powerful individual
rulers had only temporarily displaced, the shift in what the city's own
labour and resources were spent on is unmistakable in the archaeological
record itself.
