A fragment of black diorite six and a half centimetres thick, less than half
a metre across, sits in a museum in Palermo, broken out of the middle of a
slab that was once more than two metres high and seven feet long. It is
inscribed on both faces with a continuous table of years, running from
kings before the unification through the Fifth Dynasty, and it is the
oldest annalistic record that survives from anywhere in Egypt. What it
shows is not a narrative history. It is a filing system, and the filing
system is the discovery.

## A rectangle for every year

Each king's reign is laid out as a horizontal register: his name across
the top, and beneath it a row of small rectangles, one per year, each
containing the events the scribes judged worth recording and, at the
bottom, a measurement — probably the height of that year's Nile flood, in
cubits, palms and fingers.

In the First and Second Dynasties the rectangles are small and the
entries are sparse: a "Worship of Horus," the "Birth of Anubis," a
"Feast of Sokar," repeated with only small variation year after year.
One entry, for a king whose own name is lost from the break in the stone,
records simply *Union of the Two Lands* and *Circuit of the Wall* — the
only surviving contemporary notice, however terse, of the event the whole
period is named for.

## Why the state needed a name for a year

Egypt had no era count and no numbered calendar running continuously
across reigns. What it had instead was a year-name: each year was
remembered and dated by whichever event the scribes chose to put in its
rectangle, so that a document could be dated "the Year of the Battle and
Smiting of the Northerners" or "the Year of the Feast of Desher" the way a
later state would date something to a regnal year number.

That system had a structural problem, and the Palermo Stone shows the
solution developing in real time across its own registers. As the
dynasties go on, the event chosen to name a year is more and more often the
biennial count of cattle, land and gold — the state's own fiscal
assessment — until by the Fifth Dynasty almost nothing else is used. A
year with no count in it has to be called "the Year after the Nth
Occurrence," which is a description with no content of its own except its
distance from the last time the state counted what it owned. The count
had become the clock.

## What a filing system this old is evidence of

The Palermo Stone was carved in the Fifth Dynasty, compiling annals that
by then ran back seven hundred years. That in itself says something:
whatever the fate of any particular year's original record, the
information in it — who was king, how high the river rose, when the count
was taken — had been kept, copied and judged worth compiling into one
permanent monument long after anyone alive could have remembered the
events firsthand.

A kingdom that tracks its own tax base by fixed biennial counts, and names
its years after doing so, is not simply a court staging its authority for
onlookers the way a smiting scene or a row of five hundred subsidiary
graves does. It is a bureaucracy keeping its own books, for reasons that
have nothing to do with anyone watching — and it had been doing so since
close to the unification itself.
