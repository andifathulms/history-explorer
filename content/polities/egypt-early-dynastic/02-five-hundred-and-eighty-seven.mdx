The kings of the First Dynasty were buried at Abydos, in the desert above
the Nile, in tombs that were not built to hold one body.

Djer's tomb is surrounded by rows of subsidiary graves — three hundred and
eighteen of them around the tomb itself, and a further two hundred and
sixty-nine around his separate funerary enclosure closer to the cultivation.
Djet's tomb has a hundred and seventy-four subsidiary burials, and his own
enclosure another hundred and sixty-one. These were not later additions and
they were not accidents of a crowded cemetery. They were dug, filled and
covered at the same time as the king's own burial, in a single planned
operation, and the people in them — most of them young adults, men and
women both, buried with a modest set of grave goods each and a small stela
giving a name and sometimes a title — died when their king did.

## What the numbers are evidence of

Nobody wrote down why. There is no inscription that explains the practice,
justifies it, or even names it as a thing being done; it is known entirely
from the graves themselves, laid out around the royal tomb in exact rows
like a rank of soldiers, each dug to the same plan.

What the numbers show is reach rather than cruelty in the abstract. A king
who could compel — or at least stage the appearance of compelling — several
hundred of his own household to die with him, in an orderly single
operation, on one day, was demonstrating a kind of authority no inscription
could have claimed as convincingly. It is the same kind of proof Narmer's
palette offers: the state was proving what it could do by doing it, not by
writing a law that said it could.

## Then it stopped

The practice is heaviest under Djer, lighter under his successors, and
by the middle of the dynasty it is gone. No First Dynasty king after
roughly the fourth reign is known to have taken retainers into death with
him, and no Second Dynasty tomb shows the practice at all.

Nothing survives to say whether it was abandoned by royal decision, by
quiet resistance from the households asked to supply the dead, or simply
because a court that had proved the point once did not need to keep proving
it. What is certain is the direction: an early Egyptian kingship secured
its household's compliance by the most absolute demonstration available,
and within a few generations had stopped needing to. Later Egyptian
funerary practice replaced the retainers with painted or modeled substitutes
— servant statuettes, and eventually the shabti figures that fill Middle
and New Kingdom tombs by the hundred — doing symbolically what these tombs
at Abydos had once done in fact.
