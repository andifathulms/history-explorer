The object the whole period is usually explained through is a piece of
ceremonial siltstone the size of a large dinner plate, carved on both faces,
found at Hierakonpolis in 1898. It shows a king wearing the tall white crown
of Upper Egypt on one side and the crown of the north on the other, above the
serekh — the palace-façade frame that holds a king's Horus name — reading
Narmer. On one face he grips an enemy by the hair and raises a mace over him.
On the other, two men lead animals with impossibly long intertwined necks,
and at the bottom a bull breaks down a walled town with its horns and tramples
a man underfoot.

## What it is evidence of

Whether the palette records a real campaign, a single ceremony, or a much
longer process of unification compressed by later memory into one king and
one image, is genuinely disputed, and the disagreement is not about whether
Egypt became one kingdom around this time — nobody doubts that — but about
whether this specific object is a news report or a liturgy.

What is not disputed is easier to state. Before Narmer, Upper and Lower Egypt
— the long river valley and the fan-shaped Delta at its mouth, different
terrain, different pottery, different local kingships as far as the evidence
goes — are two things. After him, for the whole of the rest of Egyptian
history with only a handful of interruptions, they are one thing, ruled from
one place, by one man who wears both crowns and is titled *nesu-bity*, he of
the sedge and the bee, the plant of the south and the insect of the north
named in the same breath.

## The Menes question

Later Egyptian tradition, and the Greek writers who drew on Egyptian
priestly records, name the first king of the unified state Menes. Whether
Menes is Narmer under a different name, or Narmer's son and immediate
successor Hor-Aha, or a composite figure standing in for several early
kings whose individual reigns had blurred together by the time anyone wrote
them down, cannot be settled from the evidence available. What can be said
is that a king-list tradition running two and a half thousand years after
the events it describes agreed on one thing the archaeology also shows
independently: something happened here that later Egyptians treated as the
beginning of their own history, not as one event among others in it.

## What there was to govern

None of this made a state by itself. A king who could put his name on a
palette was not yet a king who could feed, arm and account for a kingdom
five hundred miles long.

That had to be built, in the next two centuries, out of nothing that
existed anywhere else to copy. There was no earlier Egyptian kingdom to
inherit an administration from. What there was, instead, was a river that
flooded every year on a schedule regular enough to plan around, a
population concentrated in a narrow band a few miles wide for its entire
length, and a king whose Horus name was now written, stamped, and sealed
onto pots, labels and tomb goods from the First Cataract to the
Mediterranean. Everything the next two centuries produced — the burial of
the king's own household with him, a calendar built from tax assessments,
a title so sophisticated a child could hold it and a kingdom that would
split before it would settle a succession — starts from that name on that
palette, whoever exactly he was.
