Nynetjer, the third king of the Second Dynasty, reigned for decades — the
Palermo Stone's entries for him run through counts in the double digits —
and somewhere in the course of that long reign he did something no king
before him is recorded doing. He divided the administration of Egypt in
two.

## What the evidence actually shows

No source states why. Later tradition, filtered through Manetho centuries
afterward, attaches a famine to the reign; some modern readings prefer a
continuing rebellion the unified administration could not settle; neither
can be confirmed from anything contemporary. What can be said is narrower
and stranger: a kingdom that had been governed as one thing since Narmer
was, for some part of Nynetjer's own reign or in its immediate aftermath,
run as two.

The kings who follow him are barely kings at all, in the sense the First
Dynasty would have recognised. Weneg is known mainly from objects found at
Saqqara, in the north, which is not proof he never held the south but is
exactly the pattern a partly divided administration would leave behind.
Sened is not attested in any contemporary source whatsoever — his existence
rests entirely on later king-lists, and even those disagree about where to
put him. Manetho's own list for this stretch of the dynasty includes names
no monument has ever confirmed, which either means the monuments have not
survived or means the later compilers were padding a period they could not
actually reconstruct, and there is no way from here to tell which.

## What two centuries of stability had not settled

This is the point worth sitting with rather than passing over on the way
to the war that ends the period. For two hundred years, since Den first
wore both crowns as a single object, nothing had forced Egypt to answer
the question of what held the two halves of the kingdom together once the
king holding it was gone, incapable, or dividing it himself. Retainer
burial had answered how far a king's authority over his own household
reached, and the Palermo Stone had answered how a state counted what it
taxed. Neither institution had anything to say about succession, because
succession within one family, from a strong king to a strong heir, had
simply worked often enough that the question never had to be asked out
loud.

Nynetjer's division asked it. The unattested and half-attested kings who
follow him are the answer working itself out in real time, in a record too
thin to show the working but clear enough to show the result: a kingdom
whose unity had never rested on anything written down was not, it turned
out, automatically self-restoring once it came apart. Something more
would have to happen before it was whole again.
