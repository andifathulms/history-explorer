Den ruled for close to fifty years, longer than any king before him, and by
most measures — the range of his titles, the reach of his officials, the
sophistication of the objects buried with him — his reign is the high point
of the First Dynasty.

## A king before he could rule

He may not have started as king in name. Several sources, including a seal
impression naming a "king's mother" alongside him, point to Merneith — his
mother, and possibly a widow of the previous king — governing in his
minority, with a tomb of her own at Abydos built to the same scale and
enclosed in the same royal cemetery as the men's. Whether she is counted as
a ruling queen in her own right or as a regent holding the office open for
her son cannot be settled from what survives, but either reading means the
same thing about the institution: a First Dynasty kingship could be held
open across a minority by a woman, and the kingdom did not come apart while
it waited for him to grow into it.

## What he added to the title

Den is the first king shown wearing the double crown, the White Crown of
the south fitted inside the Red Crown of the north into a single object,
and the first to carry the full title *nesu-bity*, King of Upper and Lower
Egypt, rather than the two halves of it invoked separately. Two centuries
after the unification, the office finally had a single piece of regalia
and a single formula to match the single kingdom it had always claimed to
be.

An ivory label from his tomb, small enough to have been tied to a
container of oil, shows him raising a mace over a kneeling foreign captive
and names the scene *the first time of smiting the East* — a formula that
would be repeated, with the same verb and the same gesture, on royal
monuments for the next three thousand years. Whatever the actual campaign
behind it amounted to, it is the ancestor of an image every later Egyptian
king would have carved of himself doing the same thing to somebody.

## What the administration looked like

The same reign produces the clearest evidence yet of a working
bureaucracy rather than a court staging its own authority. Seal
impressions from his tomb list an increasingly elaborate hierarchy of
officials and offices — treasurers, seal-bearers, estate managers — and
inscribed jar labels record deliveries of oil and wine keyed to specific
years, in the same year-naming convention the Palermo Stone would later
compile in full. A tomb inscription datable to around his reign gives the
title of a physician, one of the earliest such titles known from anywhere.

None of it looks improvised. A state that could hold the throne open
during a minority, put a standard crown and title on record, keep a
dated year-by-year account of its own transactions, and staff itself with
named specialists in medicine as well as revenue, was no longer running on
the personal authority of one man in the way the unification itself had.
It was running on an office that had outlasted several kings already and
was visibly built to outlast the next one too.
