Al-Mundhir ibn al-Harith succeeded his father in 569 and inherited both halves
of the position: the frontier command and the patronage of a church the imperial
government was trying to suppress. Relations with Constantinople deteriorated
across the 570s — there was a period when the subsidy stopped and the frontier
went unguarded, and a Roman campaign that failed with each side blaming the
other.

In 580 he went to Constantinople and was received with honour. Two years later
he was arrested and exiled to Sicily. His son was taken shortly afterwards, and
the emperor Maurice divided the following among fifteen chiefs, none of them
supreme.

That is the ending, and this site types it `fragmentation` with `by:
[byzantine]`, which is an awkward combination and is meant to be. The polity was
not conquered, did not collapse and was not replaced by a successor. Its patron
took it apart on purpose, having decided that the instrument it had built in 529
had become more dangerous than the problem it was built for. The vocabulary can
describe a state coming apart and a state being conquered; it has no clean way
to say that a state was disassembled by the power that assembled it.

What that decision cost is one of the standing questions of the period, and it
should be stated carefully rather than dramatically. Fifty-four years later the
Roman army was destroyed at the Yarmuk, in exactly the country the phylarchate
had held, by an Arab force from the peninsula. Later Arabic tradition puts a
Jafnid, Jabala ibn al-Ayham, on the Roman side of that battle. It is a very old
argument that the empire had dismantled its own Arab frontier and could not
reconstruct it in time, and it is not provable: the Roman east in the 630s had
just survived twenty-six years of Persian war and plague, and there is more than
one thing wrong with it.

What is certain is narrower. Rome had spent eighty years building an Arab
political authority in Syria — a court, a subsidy, a church, a poetry — and then
ended it. Sixty years after that, Syria was ruled from Damascus by an Arab
dynasty that used the same steppe residences, patronised the same kind of
court, and made Jabiya a centre again.
