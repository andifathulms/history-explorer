The Christological controversies of the fifth and sixth centuries had left the
eastern provinces divided between the Chalcedonian settlement the imperial
court held to and the Miaphysite position most of Syria and Egypt held to. The
imperial government persecuted the Miaphysites intermittently, and by the 540s
the confession was running out of bishops: without bishops there are no
ordinations, and without ordinations a church expires within a generation.

<Figure id="theodora" />

Al-Harith was Miaphysite, and he asked the empress Theodora for bishops. He got
them. Jacob Baradaeus was consecrated and spent decades travelling the east in
disguise ordaining clergy, and the hierarchy he built is the ancestor of the
Syriac Orthodox Church, which still exists. A body of Christians survived as a
separate church because an Arab client king asked the emperor's wife for a
favour.

<Figure id="resafa-arch" />

The Jafnids also built. Churches and shrines along the frontier, endowments at
Rusafa — the pilgrimage centre of Saint Sergius, the soldier-saint whose cult
the frontier Arabs took up in numbers — and structures at Nitl and elsewhere
that excavation has attached to them. The point is not that the Jafnids were
Christian — plenty of client dynasties were — but that they propagated a
particular confession among populations that had not held it, and endowed the
institutions that kept it there.

<Figure id="resafa-building" />

It also explains part of what happened in 582. A client who is the political
patron of the confession the imperial court is trying to suppress is a client
with an independent constituency, and the Jafnid phylarchs used it — al-Mundhir
convened Miaphysite bishops and intervened in their disputes, which is the
behaviour of a sovereign rather than a subsidised ally. Rome noticed.
