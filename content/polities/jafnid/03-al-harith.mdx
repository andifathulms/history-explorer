In 529 Justinian did something Rome had not done before: he put all the Arab
allies of the eastern provinces under one man. Al-Harith ibn Jabala — Arethas to
the Greek sources — was made supreme phylarch and given the rank of patrician,
the highest dignity ever conferred on a foreign ally, and he held it for forty
years.

The reason was al-Hira. The Nasrid al-Mundhir had been raiding Roman Syria for
decades with Persian backing, reaching as far as Antioch's hinterland, and the
separate Roman phylarchs could not coordinate against him. A single command
could. In 554 al-Harith met him in battle and killed him, and the Nasrid threat
that the phylarchate had been assembled to answer was finished.

<Figure id="qasr-panorama" />

Al-Harith is a substantial figure in the Greek sources and they are not
flattering. Procopius accuses him of treachery at the battle of Callinicum in
531 — the standard charge against an allied commander when a Roman general
loses — and Fisher's reading of these passages is that they say more about how
Roman writers handled Arab success than about what happened. The same
historian's account is the only one we have.

The court that al-Harith and his son ran is the part that outlasted them. It
patronised Arabic poetry — the pre-Islamic poets name Jafnid patrons, and the
tradition of the Arab warrior-court with its poets and its wine and its
generosity is substantially a memory of this one. It built and endowed churches
across the frontier. And the later Umayyad caliphs, ruling the same country from
the same Golan, took the model: a court in the Syrian steppe, Arabic verse,
audience halls out in the country, and Jabiya as a centre. The Umayyad caliphate
begins in 661. The kind of court it kept was assembled here first.
