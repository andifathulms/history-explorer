---
title: Jafnid, not Ghassanid
drafted_from: fisher-2011
phase: aside
---

Almost everything written about this polity calls it Ghassanid, and this page
does not. The reason is Fisher's, and it is a claim about what kind of object a
polity is, which makes it this site's question too.

Ghassan is a confederacy — a large tribal grouping that later Arabic genealogy
traces to the Azd and brings north out of Yemen. The Jafnids are a family: the
descendants of Jafna, who were the people Rome actually dealt with. Later Arabic
tradition merged the two, so that the family became the royal house of the
confederacy and the confederacy's history became the family's, running back to
the third century.

Fisher's argument is that the merger obscures how the arrangement worked. Rome's
agreements were personal. Justinian appointed al-Harith, not Ghassan; the rank
and the subsidy were his; and when Maurice wanted the arrangement to stop, he
arrested a man and the following dispersed. A polity that can be ended by
detaining one person is not a nation with a dynasty at its head. It is a
dynasty with a following, and the difference is visible in 582.

This matters to the corpus beyond the label. This site's spans and edges assume
polities are things that persist independently of individuals — that is what
lets a page say a dynasty ruled for two hundred years. The Jafnid case is at the
edge of that assumption, and the span field shows the strain: it opens as a
range between a treaty and an appointment, and closes as a range between a
deposition and a battle fifty-four years later that a different tradition
attributes to the same house.

Two further consequences follow, and both are recorded rather than smoothed
over. The `capitals` entry for Jabiya carries a comment saying the court moved.
And `last` is null, because the line after 582 is disputed and the man later
tradition puts at Yarmuk is not enterable as a fact on that authority.
