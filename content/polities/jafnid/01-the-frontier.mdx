title: Hiring the frontier
drafted_from: fisher-2011
phase: formation

Rome and Sasanian Iran shared a border that ran, for much of its length, through
country neither of them could garrison: the steppe between Syria and the
Euphrates, and the desert south of it. Both empires solved the problem the same
way. They paid Arab dynasties to hold it.

On the Persian side this was the Nasrid family at al-Hira on the lower
Euphrates, whose confederacy later tradition calls Lakhm. On the Roman side,
from about 502, it was the Jafnids. The arrangement gave the empire cavalry that
could operate where regular troops could not, intelligence about the interior,
and — most importantly — a counterpart who could answer raids in kind, because
the only effective response to a raid from the steppe was a raid back, and
imperial armies were not built for it.

What the Jafnids got was money, rank and recognition. Fisher's point about the
money is that it was not a bribe: the subsidies were the ordinary machinery by
which Rome retained allies, paid in the same way as to any *foederatus*, and
this site codes the polity's revenue basis as `tribute` because that is the
vocabulary's word for revenue received from another polity, while noting that
the word implies a relationship this was not.

The frontier they held was not empty and it was not only military. It carried
the caravan traffic from Arabia, the pilgrim traffic to shrines like Sergiopolis
at Rusafa, and a settled and semi-settled Arab population that had been there
for centuries and was increasingly Christian. The Jafnids were the political
expression of that world rather than an army stationed in it.
