In 539 the Persian king wanted a war and needed somebody else to start it.
Procopius describes the mechanism without any evident sense that it requires
apology, and it is the clearest account there is of what the Jafnids had become
by the middle of the century.

Khusrau conferred with al-Mundhir of al-Hira on the matter and commanded him to
provide causes for war.

Al-Mundhir accordingly brought a complaint against al-Harith — that al-Harith
was doing him violence over boundary lines — attacked him in a time of peace,
and began raiding Roman territory on that pretext.

## The argument that was true

When challenged, al-Mundhir made a legal case which Procopius, no friend of his,
concedes.

He was not breaking the treaty between Persia and Rome, he said, because neither
of them had included him in it.

*And this was true.* No mention of Saracens was ever made in the treaties, on
the ground that they were covered by the words Persians and Romans.

Both Arab kings were therefore in the same position, and it is worth stating it
from the Jafnid side because it is usually described from the other. Al-Harith
was a king by Roman grant, commanding Roman-allied clans on Roman territory, and
he did not exist in the document that governed war and peace between the two
empires. He could be attacked without a treaty being broken. He could attack
without a treaty being broken. Everything he did was deniable by the people who
had told him to do it.

## What they were arguing about

The disputed ground was called Strata and lay south of Palmyra. Procopius, who
seems to have taken some care over this, describes it: no water anywhere, no
trees, no crops, burnt dry by the sun, used for grazing a few flocks.

Justinian sent two men to arbitrate. Summus, formerly commander of the troops in
Palestine, held that Rome should not surrender the country. Strategius, the
patrician who ran the imperial treasury, begged the emperor not to do the
Persians the favour of handing them a pretext for a war they already wanted —
over a small piece of ground of absolutely no account, unproductive and unfit
for crops.

Justinian took the matter under consideration, and a long time was spent on the
question.

## What the episode did to the position

Nothing on the ground. Everything to the standing of the office.

Al-Harith had been made a king in order to be the equal of the man on the other
side. What the Strata affair demonstrated was the limit of that equality: the
two kings could be set at each other by their patrons at any time, on any
pretext, over anything, and neither empire needed to explain itself afterwards
because neither had ever put them in writing.

The war came in 540 and Khusrau sacked Antioch. Al-Harith went on serving,
killed al-Mundhir fourteen years later, and remained the most successful client
Rome had on that frontier.

Forty years after his death the empire arrested his grandson, broke the
confederation into fifteen pieces, and stopped paying.
