---
title: How it was known
drafted_from: procopius-dewing

Procopius spent twenty years insinuating that al-Harith was a traitor, and then
recorded the thing that proved he was not. The proof is the worst sentence in the
Jafnid record.

The insinuations run through the *Wars*. Callinicum in 531 was lost because the
Arab wing gave way; the Assyrian campaign of 541 failed because al-Harith went
off with the plunder and sent no word back; whenever a Roman general is
embarrassed, the phylarch is nearby. Procopius is the only continuous source for
any of it, and he never quite states the charge — he arranges the facts so that a
reader will make it for him.

Then, under the year 545, he describes something that happened without either
empire in it. Rome and Persia had just concluded a five-year truce. Al-Harith and
al-Mundhir went to war with each other anyway, unaided by the Romans or the
Persians, on their own account. Al-Mundhir caught one of al-Harith's sons in a
sudden raid while the young man was out pasturing horses, and sacrificed him
immediately to Aphrodite — the Greek name Procopius uses for al-Uzza.

And then Procopius writes the clause: *and from this it was known that Arethas
was not betraying the Romans to the Persians.*

That is the whole of his acquittal and it is delivered in a subordinate clause.
The evidence that the supreme phylarch of the Roman east was loyal is not
anything he did for Rome over forty years — not the patriciate, not the
campaigns, not killing al-Mundhir at Chalcis nine years later. It is that the
Persians' client killed his son on an altar, which he would not have done to a
man who was working for him.

The rest of the notice is brief. The two of them met in a general engagement
afterwards, al-Harith's forces won overwhelmingly, and he came close to capturing
two of al-Mundhir's sons alive but did not. *Such, then, was the course of events
among the Saracens.*

Two things follow, and the second is the more important.

The first is that the two client dynasties fought their own war during an
armistice between the empires that employed them. Procopius records elsewhere
that no treaty between Rome and Persia ever mentioned the Arabs, on the theory
that they were covered by the names of the principals — which al-Mundhir had once
pointed out meant that nothing in such a treaty bound him. The clients had a
quarrel of their own and a calendar of their own.

The second is what counts as proof in a Greek historian's account of an Arab
ally. Forty years of service produce a permanent suspicion; one atrocity
committed against him by the other side produces a grudging clause. It is not
that Procopius disbelieved al-Harith. It is that nothing al-Harith could do would
have settled the question, and the thing that settled it was done to him.
