The Jafnid kingdom exists because of a problem on the other side of the
frontier, and Procopius — who was there, as secretary to Belisarius — states the
problem and the solution in consecutive sentences.

The problem was al-Mundhir of al-Hira. He held the position of king and ruled
alone over all the Arabs on the Persian side, which meant he could bring his
whole force against any point of the Roman frontier he chose. Against that,
Procopius says, neither a Roman *dux* commanding a district's troops nor any of
the phylarchs — the Arab leaders allied to Rome — was strong enough to stand,
because the Roman forces were parcelled out among the districts and no single
piece of them was a match for him.

## The concession

So Justinian put al-Harith son of Jabala, who ruled the Arabs of Roman Arabia,
in command of as many clans as possible, and bestowed on him the dignity of
king.

Procopius adds five words that carry the whole significance of the act: *a thing
which among the Romans had never before been done*.

Rome had used allied Arab leaders on this frontier for centuries and had always
given them Roman ranks — phylarch, patrician, *gloriosissimus* — offices in the
imperial hierarchy, held at the emperor's pleasure, over a defined district.
What it had never done was recognise one of them as a king, because a king is
not an office in somebody else's system.

It did it now, and it did it for a purely operational reason: to concentrate
enough Arab force under one command to face the man across the desert.

## What the grant produced

A great deal, and none of it what was intended.

Al-Harith held the position for forty years. He fought Persia repeatedly,
eventually killed al-Mundhir in battle in 554, and made the Jafnid house the
paramount Arab power on the Roman side of the steppe. He also used the standing
the royal title gave him for purposes that had nothing to do with the frontier
— most consequentially, going to Constantinople and obtaining the consecration
of bishops for the anti-Chalcedonian church the empire was persecuting.

That is the recurring hazard of the arrangement. A client given enough authority
to be useful has enough authority to have his own policy, and the Jafnids' own
policy was a church their patron regarded as heretical.

## The judgement Rome could not make

There is one more sentence in the passage and it is the most revealing thing
Procopius wrote about the man.

Al-Mundhir, he says, went on hurting the Romans just as much as before, if not
more — since al-Harith was either extremely unfortunate in every raid and every
engagement, or else turned traitor as fast as he could. *For as yet we know
nothing certain about him.*

A serving Roman officer, writing about the empire's own senior Arab commander in
the middle of his career, cannot tell whether the man is unlucky or a traitor,
and says so in print. That uncertainty is not a failure of evidence. It is the
permanent condition of a frontier client: he is judged entirely on results, he
operates where nobody from the capital can see, and the difference between bad
luck and bad faith is invisible from Constantinople.
