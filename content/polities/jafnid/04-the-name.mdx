---
title: Jafnid, not Ghassanid
drafted_from: fisher-2011
phase: aside
---

Almost everything written about this dynasty calls it Ghassanid, and that name
is avoided here. The reason is Fisher's, and it is a claim about what kind of
object we are looking at.

Ghassan is a confederacy — a large tribal grouping that later Arabic genealogy
traces to the Azd and brings north out of Yemen. The Jafnids are a family: the
descendants of Jafna, who were the people Rome actually dealt with. Later Arabic
tradition merged the two, so that the family became the royal house of the
confederacy and the confederacy's history became the family's, running back to
the third century.

Fisher's argument is that the merger obscures how the arrangement worked. Rome's
agreements were personal. Justinian appointed al-Harith, not Ghassan; the rank
and the subsidy were his; and when Maurice wanted the arrangement to stop, he
arrested a man and the following dispersed. A polity that can be ended by
detaining one person is not a nation with a dynasty at its head. It is a
dynasty with a following, and the difference is visible in 582.

It matters beyond the label. Writing history at all assumes that polities
persist independently of the individuals running them — that is what licenses a
sentence like *the dynasty ruled for two hundred years*. The Jafnid case sits
right at the edge of that assumption. Its beginning is a range between a treaty
and an appointment; its end is a range between a deposition and a battle
fifty-four years later that a different tradition attributes to the same house.
What is being dated is not obviously one object.

Two further consequences follow from that, and neither should be smoothed
over. Exactly when the court moved to Jabiya is not securely fixed. And who
actually held the position last is not securely known either: the line after
582 is disputed, and the man later tradition places at Yarmuk rests on that
tradition's own authority rather than on anything closer to the event.
