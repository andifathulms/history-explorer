What the Jafnids left was not a state, a church or a successor. It was a list of
names, and the list travelled a very long way.

Nine centuries after the confederation was broken up, a court historian in
Yemen sat down to write the official history of the Rasulid sultans, and opened
it with a genealogy. Before reaching any Rasulid he goes through Saba and his
sons, the great dam at Marib, the dream that warned of its collapse, the
dispersal of the Azd — and then Jafna going north with Ghassan, the Ghassanid
princes of the Syrian frontier, and their last king.

Only after that does he arrive at the soldier in Egypt whom Saladin sent to
Yemen.

## The list itself

It is worth looking at what the genealogy actually consists of, because it is
almost entirely epithets.

The first of the princes of Ghassan was Mazin son of Azd, called the Gatherer
Together of Ghassan and the Slayer of Famine. Then his son Tha'laba, called the
Provision of Travel. Then Imru' al-Qays, surnamed Buhlul; then Haritha; then
Amir, who was designated Water of the Sky, or as some say Water of the
Rain-Cloud; then Imran, who was a diviner; then his brother Amr, surnamed the
Little Tatterer. Then Jafna. Then a run of Hariths distinguished only by
nicknames — Harith the Greater, Harith the Halter, Harith the Lesser.

And at the end: Jabala son of al-Ayham son of Jabala. *And he was the last of
their princes. And God knows best.*

## The numbers do not agree

The compiler is candid about how much of this is guesswork.

The number of their princes, he writes, was thirty sovereigns. It has been said
thirty-two sovereigns, and even thirty-six sovereigns. And the period of their
rule was six hundred and sixteen years — though in some history, he adds, the
duration of their rule was one thousand six hundred years.

A range of six hundred to sixteen hundred years is not a chronology. It is a
statement that nobody knew, and that the not-knowing did not matter for the
purpose the list was serving.

## What the purpose was

A dynasty of Turkmen soldiers ruling Arabs in Yemen needed an Arab ancestry, and
the most prestigious available were the Ghassanid kings of the Roman frontier —
famous, extinct, sufficiently distant that nobody could check, and attached at
the far end to the kingdom at Marib.

So the Jafnid afterlife is a pedigree. Not a claim by anybody with a plausible
connection, but a name kept in circulation for nine hundred years because it was
the best thing an ambitious house could say about itself, in a country two
thousand kilometres from Jabiya, in a book whose author was not sure whether the
dynasty had lasted six centuries or sixteen.

The empire that made them a kingdom, and then unmade it in a single arrest,
would have found that outcome incomprehensible. It is also the only part of them
that outlived the seventh century.
