The Jafnids ruled because an emperor said they did, and they never pretended
otherwise.

Al-Harith was phylarch and patrician because Justinian made him so. The rank was
conferred, announced and displayed, and the family's standing among the Arabs of
the frontier rested visibly on Constantinople's recognition of it. They did not
claim to be kings in their own right, they did not claim descent from anyone who
had been, and they did not claim that God had chosen them. They held an office,
and the office had an owner.

## A common arrangement, rarely named

It is easy to read that as weakness, or as a colonial relationship dressed up.
It is neither. Holding authority by grant from a sovereign you do not claim to be
is one of the most durable ways power has been arranged, and once you start
noticing it, it is everywhere.

The kings of Caucasian Iberia were invested by Rome, which acquired the right by
treaty in 298. The kings of Greater Armenia were nominated by Parthia and crowned
by Rome under the settlement of AD 63 — a throne with two owners, which worked
for longer than it had any right to. A Hujrid phylarch held his position from
Himyar. A Shaddadid branch held Ani on a Seljuk diploma. The sharifs of Mecca
were qualified by descent from the Prophet and appointed by whoever happened to
hold Egypt.

And some of the most powerful men of the medieval world chose the arrangement
deliberately. Timur ruled for thirty-five years beside a Chinggisid puppet,
because descent barred him from the title himself and he wanted the authority
more than the name. Hasan Buzurg had done the same with Ilkhanid figureheads a
generation earlier. Karim Khan Zand refused the title of shah outright and
governed as deputy for a Safavid infant.

## Why it lasts

What these have in common is that the ruler's right to rule is portable and
revocable, and both of those turn out to be advantages.

Portable, because a grant can be made to a family that has no local claim at all
— which is precisely why frontier powers used it. Rome did not need the Jafnids
to be legitimate among the Arabs before appointing them; the appointment was what
made them so.

Revocable, because it gives the conferring power a lever short of war. The
Jafnids did not fall in a battle. In 582 Maurice deposed the phylarch and broke
the confederation up deliberately, dividing the following among fifteen chiefs,
and the frontier Rome had spent a century assembling went with it. A family that
had risen by grant fell by cancellation, which is the risk built into the whole
arrangement, and the reason those who could afford a different claim usually made
one.
