Krishna III, who came to the Rashtrakuta throne in 939, inherited an
empire already the dominant power of the Deccan, but it was his own
campaign against the Cholas that would make his reign the dynasty's
own high point. After early Rashtrakuta gains in Chola territory, the
Chola king Parantaka I rallied his forces and sent an army under his
own crown prince, Rajaditya, to drive the invaders back.

## A battle decided by one man's death

The two armies met in 949 at Takkolam in the North Arcot district. By
the Rashtrakuta's own account, the Cholas fought with such initial
force that "none dared to counter-attack" — until Krishna's own
brother-in-law, the Ganga king Butuga, rallied the Rashtrakuta line,
charged directly at Rajaditya's own elephant, killed the animal beneath
him, and climbed into the howdah to kill the crown prince himself on
the spot. The Chola army, disheartened by the sudden loss of its own
commander, broke and fled, leaving the field to the Rashtrakutas. In
reward for the feat, Krishna granted Butuga governorship over nearly
the whole of the Bombay Karnatak.

## A conquest pressed to its furthest point

With the Chola army broken, Krishna pushed the advantage as far as it
would go, defeating both the Kerala and Pandya kings in turn and
occupying Ramesvaram itself, where he built temples — named for himself
— as permanent monuments to a conquest no local ruler could tear down.
Even the king of Ceylon, the source notes, sent his own submission
rather than risk the same fate. For a time Krishna held effective
possession of a large part of the Chola kingdom, a reach south of the
Krishna river no Rashtrakuta king before or after him would match.

## An overlordship no predecessor had achieved

The source's own judgment on Krishna's reign is unambiguous: "no other
king in ancient India was ever overlord of the entire Deccan in so
complete a sense of the term as was Krishna in c. A.D. 965." Where
earlier Rashtrakuta kings had taken Kanchi without ever breaking Chola
power at its own southern reach, Krishna alone combined both — a single
reign in which the empire Dantidurga had founded two centuries earlier
reached the furthest extent it would ever hold.
