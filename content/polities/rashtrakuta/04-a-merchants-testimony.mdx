Arab writers who never set foot far from the coast still had a clear
picture of who ruled the Deccan's interior. They called the
Rashtrakuta emperor the Balhara - a rendering of Vallabha, a title
every king of the dynasty bore - and treated his kingdom as one of the
major powers of the age. Sulaiman, a merchant writing around 850,
recorded a principle he had observed of Indian kingship generally but
applied it, in the Rashtrakuta case, to a ruler whose reach he had
seen for himself: "when a king subdues a neighbouring state in India,
he places over it a man belonging to the family of the fallen prince,"
governing thereafter as a tributary. Of the Rashtrakuta emperor
specifically, Sulaiman's own verdict was blunter still - that every
neighbouring prince, "though master in his own state," was made to
pay him homage. Al-Mas'udi, writing later in the same tradition,
described the Balhara's own army in similarly plain terms: "his
horses and elephants are innumerable."

## No flattery, a historian concludes

Altekar, writing in the twentieth century with the epigraphic record
open before him, treats the merchant's judgment as sober reporting
rather than a traveller's exaggeration: "the observation of Sulaiman,
that the Rashtrakutas were the most feared and powerful rulers of
India, is no flattery, but a mere statement of facts." The empire
Dantidurga had founded from a position of feudatory obscurity had, by
the ninth century, become one a passing foreign merchant could not
help but name first among its neighbours.
