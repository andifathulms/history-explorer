Krishna III's own successors could not hold what he had built. His
brother Khottiga, who inherited the throne in 967, saw the Rashtrakuta
capital itself briefly occupied by an invading Paramara army - a
humiliation the empire had never suffered even at the height of its
wars with the Cholas. When Karkka II succeeded Khottiga in September
972, the damage to Rashtrakuta prestige was already done, and a
charter issued within a month of his own accession, full of
conventional praise for victories that existed, the source notes dryly,
"only in the fancy of the grateful poet," could not disguise how
exposed the empire had become.

## A small lord with a long memory

The man who took advantage of this weakness was Taila II, ruler of a
minor fief at Bagewadi who had served loyally as an ordinary feudatory
under Krishna III. Taila believed himself a direct descendant of the
imperial Chalukya family of Badami — the same dynasty the Rashtrakutas
had overthrown two centuries before — and both his own lineage and his
marriage to a Rashtrakuta princess convinced him he had as much claim
to rule the Deccan as the family currently holding it. His mother's own
Chedi relatives, embittered by unprovoked Rashtrakuta attacks under
Krishna III, gave him further backing.

## A weak king with no one left to trust

Karkka's own position was already fragile before Taila moved against
him. He had overridden the succession claims of Indra, grandson of
Krishna III, alienating Indra's powerful maternal uncle; his own
ministers were, by his enemies' account, vicious and widely resented;
and when Taila finally attacked at the end of 973, Karkka found himself
without reliable allies even inside his own capital.

## A dynasty ends where it began

The battle was fierce and decisive. Taila's own records admit the
fighting was sanguinary before he secured "the sovereignty of the
world," and Karkka's two chief advisers were killed on the field.
Karkka himself escaped, carving out a small principality in Mysore
where he continued, with a certain defiance, to use the full imperial
titles of a throne he no longer held, ruling there until 991. Taila
marched on to occupy Manyakheta itself, restoring Chalukya power on
the very ground from which Dantidurga's own family had displaced his
ancestors two centuries earlier — a debt of two hundred years, settled
in a single afternoon.
