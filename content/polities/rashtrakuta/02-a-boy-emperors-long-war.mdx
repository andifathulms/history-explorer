Govinda III, who succeeded his father Dhruva and ruled from 793 to
814, pushed the empire Dantidurga had founded to a reach it had never
held before - defeating enemies "from Kanauj to Cape Comorin on a
hundred different battlefields," in the source's own account, and
annexing Malwa as an imperial viceroyalty. When he died in 814 he left
the throne to his son Sarva, a boy of thirteen or fourteen who took
the title Amoghavarsha - "unfailing rainer of blessings" - and would
go on to reign for more than sixty-four years, one of the longest
securely dated reigns of any monarch anywhere.

## A kingdom that nearly ended before it properly began

The regency arranged by Govinda III, under Amoghavarsha's cousin
Karkka, held for barely two years before a serious rebellion broke
out around 817. Contemporary records blame "deceitful Kali" for
turning feudatories, ministers and relatives against the boy emperor;
whatever the cause, the revolt spread until the young Amoghavarsha
was driven to flee the country entirely and Rashtrakuta power
collapsed for a time. Karkka fought the rebels back and restored his
ward to the throne by May 821, but only after Vijayaditya II, the
Chalukya king of Vengi whom Govinda III had once deposed, joined the
revolt and claimed to have fought the Rashtrakutas in a hundred and
eight battles over twelve years. Rashtrakuta records themselves admit
that Amoghavarsha had to "raise afresh the glory of his house, which
had sunken deep into the Chalukya ocean."

## Three fronts, on and off, for thirty years

The kingdom Amoghavarsha spent the rest of his reign defending had to
be defended on every side at once. He drove the Vengi Chalukyas back
at Vingavalli around 830, only to lose the province again to a rival
general within fifteen years and never recover it. His own cousins,
ruling as loyal Rashtrakuta viceroys in Gujarat for two generations,
turned on him after 835 in a war against his own family that dragged
on for about twenty-five years before ending in reconciliation. The
Ganga kings of the south rebelled and were driven back more than once,
until Amoghavarsha finally settled the enmity around 860 by marrying
his own daughter to a Ganga prince. Unlike his father and grandfather,
Amoghavarsha was never a celebrated general in his own right - but the
empire he had so nearly lost as a boy of seventeen was still standing,
intact in its essentials, when he handed it to his own son more than
sixty years later.
