---
title: The two who won
drafted_from: ibn-al-qalanisi

For about five years in the 1120s the most dangerous men in the Levant were two
Artuqid cousins, and between them they destroyed a Frankish army, captured the
count of Edessa and captured the king of Jerusalem.

Then one died of an illness in a village and the other was killed, and it
stopped as suddenly as it had started.

## Ilghazi

Ilghazi held Mardin and, from 1118, Aleppo. In June 1119 the Turkmen came to
him from every quarter — Ibn al-Qalanisi's phrase is that they gathered like
lions seeking their prey and gerfalcons hovering over their victims — and he
took them against the prince of Antioch, who had marched out with everything
the principality could raise.

It took less than an hour. The Franks were left on the ground in one prostrate
mass, and their prince with them. It was the heaviest defeat a crusader state
suffered before Hattin, seventy years later.

He did not take Antioch. His Turkmen dispersed with the plunder, which is what
Turkmen did, and there was no army left in the field to follow the victory
with. This is the recurring shape of Artuqid power: they could assemble a
devastating force and could not keep one.

Ilghazi died of an illness in a village called al-Fuhul in Diyar Bakr in
November 1122. His sons Sulayman and Timurtash took his lands, held together
for a while, and then quarrelled, and neither would give way.

## Balak

His nephew Balak had the better run.

In the autumn of 1122 he fell on the Franks of Edessa at Saruj and took their
count Joscelin prisoner along with his cousin and a number of their leaders.
The following April, King Baldwin came against him near Manzara and Balak
defeated and captured him too, with his principal knights, and put him in the
castle of Kharput with Joscelin.

Then Aleppo. In May 1123 he came down on the city, burned its crops, blockaded
it and took possession of it.

He was holding the king of Jerusalem, the count of Edessa, the bishop of
al-Bara and the second city of Syria at the same moment, and he had done it in
eight months.

The prisoners then broke out of Kharput by a stratagem and seized the castle
itself. Balak marched back, besieged his own citadel until he had it again, put
a proper garrison in it, and told them to be more careful.

## Why it did not last

Nothing was built on any of it.

The victories were won with tribal cavalry that came for plunder and left with
it. Aleppo was held for a moment and not integrated. The prisoners were worth
ransoms rather than territory. And the moment the two men were gone, the
family's own division — three branches that never reunited — meant that no
single Artuqid could put that kind of force in the field again.

What the dynasty took from the decade was a reputation, and then the sense to
stop trying. For the next two hundred and eighty years the Artuqids of Mardin
acknowledged whoever was strongest and were left alone, which is a duller and
far more successful policy than the one that made them briefly famous.
