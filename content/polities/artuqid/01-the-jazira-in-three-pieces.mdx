---
title: The Jazira, in three pieces
drafted_from: hillenbrand-1999
phase: formation
---

Artuq ibn Eksuk was a Turkmen commander in Seljuk service who was made governor
of Jerusalem in 1086. His sons did not keep Jerusalem — the First Crusade took
it in 1099 — but they kept what they had taken in the meantime, which was the
Jazira: the country between the upper Tigris and Euphrates, with Mardin, Hisn
Kayfa, Amid and Harput.

They then divided it, permanently, into three branches that never reunited and
frequently fought. This site codes appanage on the record for the same reason
it does on the Seljuk and Danishmendid pages, and here the consequence is the
opposite of the usual one: the division did not destroy the dynasty, it
outlasted every neighbour that could have absorbed it whole. The Hisn Kayfa
branch fell to the Ayyubids in 1232 and the Harput branch to the Seljuks of Rum
in 1234; Mardin went on until 1409.

The middle of that is the interesting part. Mardin survived by being
successively a Seljuk vassal, a Zangid vassal, an Ayyubid vassal, a Mongol
vassal — Qara Arslan submitted to Hulegu — and then a vassal of whoever held
Iraq, for two hundred and seventy-five years, on a rock above a plain that
everybody wanted to cross and nobody wanted to garrison. It is the same
technique this corpus records for the Bavands in the Elburz and the Karamanids
in the Taurus, applied on a smaller and more exposed piece of ground.

Their one moment of being a great power was 1119. Ilghazi took an army against
the principality of Antioch and destroyed it at a place the Franks afterwards
called the Field of Blood, killing the prince and most of the knighthood of the
principality — the heaviest defeat a crusader state suffered before Hattin
seventy years later. He then could not follow it up: his Turkmen dispersed with
the plunder, as Turkmen did, and Antioch was not taken.

That is why the counter-crusade in this corpus belongs to the Zangids and the
Ayyubids and not to the people who won this battle. Winning is a matter of
cavalry; keeping is a matter of an army that stays.
