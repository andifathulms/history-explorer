Ibn Khallikan was compiling his dictionary of eminent men in the middle of the
thirteenth century, and when he came to record how the Ayyubid sultan al-Kamil
took Amid and Hisn Kayfa, he wrote down whom he took them from.

The prince was al-Malik al-Mas'ud Rukn al-Din Mawdud, son of al-Malik al-Salih
Mahmud, son of Nur al-Din Muhammad, son of Fakhr al-Din Qara Arslan, son of
Rukn al-Dawla Da'ud, son of Nur al-Dawla Sukman, son of Artuq.

Nine names, ending in the Turkmen officer whose son Sukman held Jerusalem for
the Seljuks before the family went east and took the rock at Mardin. Ibn
Khallikan gives the whole chain and then adds, in the manner of a careful man,
that he had already dealt with Artuq, the ancestor of this dynasty, earlier in
the book.

## The date, from a well-informed man

Then he does the thing that makes him useful. He says he learned from a native
of Amid, a well-informed man, that Amid was surrendered to al-Kamil on the 19th
of Dhu al-Hijja of that year — October 1232. On the 20th, the sultan's son
entered the city. On the first day of Muharram following, the sultan entered it
himself.

Three dates, a day apart, for the end of a branch of a dynasty that had lasted
a hundred and thirty years. They are given in a paragraph about somebody else's
conquests, in a list that continues immediately to Damascus, to Yemen, to Mecca
and to the ten kings riding in al-Kamil's train.

That is the Artuqid afterlife in its exact proportion. The family that had
destroyed the army of Antioch and held the king of Jerusalem in a dungeon ends
as a genealogy in a sentence about somebody else's year.

## What was left at Mardin

The Hisn Kayfa branch went in 1232 and the Harput branch to the Seljuks of Rum.
The branch on the rock at Mardin outlasted them both by a hundred and
seventy-seven years, acknowledging Ayyubids, Mongols, Ilkhans and whoever else
came through, until the Kara Koyunlu — a Turkmen confederation that had no use
for tributaries — took the city in 1409.

Nothing of the dynasty went on afterwards except its buildings, which is more
than most of the states that outranked it can say. The bridge is still there.
The book of machines is still copied and studied. And the pedigree survives
because a judge in Damascus, writing about a sultan who had just taken their
city, thought it worth recording who exactly had lost it.
