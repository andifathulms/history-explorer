The Artuqids built a bridge over the Batman river, in the hill country east of
Diyarbakır, and it is still standing. It went up in the 1140s, it is a single
stone arch, and the span is a little under forty-one metres.

<Figure id="malabadi-bridge" />

That figure is worth pausing on. It is among the widest stone
arches built anywhere before the modern period — wider than any Roman arch, and
not exceeded in Europe for centuries. It was raised by a minor Turkmen dynasty
whose political history, as the previous chapter says, is a list of the
overlords it acknowledged.

The engineering is legible in the shape. The arch is not a half-circle: it is a
segmental arch on a rising approach, with chambers built into the haunches to
take weight out of the structure where weight does no work, and side rooms in
the piers where travellers could shelter. It was built to carry a road across a
river that floods, in a country where the alternative was a ford that is
impassable for part of the year.

Set it beside the chapter after next and a pattern appears. The Artuqids also
paid for al-Jazari's book of machines — pumps, crankshafts, gearing, water
clocks — written at Amid for the ruler of the same family a few decades later.
A dynasty of no military weight in a poor province produced, within about sixty
years, one of the widest stone spans in the pre-modern world and the best
surviving medieval treatise on mechanism.

That is not a coincidence and the connection is water. The Jazira is a country
between two rivers where everything depends on getting water lifted, moved and
crossed: irrigation off the Tigris and Euphrates, mills, and roads that have to
survive a flood season. A ruling house in that country with money to spend and
no prospect of spending it on conquest spent it on hydraulic works, and the
people it employed were the best in the field because the field was where the
demand was.

Measured by territory, army or anything they exported, the Artuqids barely
register. What they are worth knowing about is what they built — the bridge,
and, next, the book.
