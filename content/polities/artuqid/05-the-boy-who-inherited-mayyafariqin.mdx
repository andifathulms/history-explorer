Ibn Khallikan came from this region and was writing about the men who took it
apart. He tracks the Artuqids the way he tracks everyone — by genealogy — and the
genealogies are what show the shape of the decline.

## 1185: a minority

In the summer of 1185 Saladin was in the north, trying and failing to get Khilat,
and he turned aside to besiege Mayyafariqin.

Ibn Khallikan explains why that city and why then. Qutb al-Din Ilghazi ibn Alpi
ibn Kartash ibn Ghazi ibn Ortoq, its lord, had died and left it to his son Husam
al-Din Baluk Arslan, *a mere boy* — and this circumstance, he writes, *had
encouraged Saladin to undertake its siege*.

The place was taken at the end of August, and not by assault. Saladin succeeded
by a stratagem in persuading the garrison to capitulate and hand it over.

That is the whole condition of the dynasty in one sentence. The Artuqids held
strong cities in difficult country and were extremely hard to remove by force. A
minority was the opening, and their neighbours had learned to watch for one.

## 1232: seven generations, and Amid

Half a century later the same thing happened on a larger scale, and Ibn Khallikan
was close enough to it to have been passing through.

In 1229, he says, he went through Harran and found al-Kamil established there with
the Egyptian army. Three years afterwards al-Kamil took the field with a large
force and took Amid, Hisn Kayfa and other cities of the region from their Artuqid
lord — whom Ibn Khallikan names in full:

> al-Malik al-Mas'ud Rukn al-Din Mawdud, the son of al-Malik al-Salih Mahmud, the
> son of Nur al-Din Muhammad, the son of Fakhr al-Din Kara Arslan, the son of
> Rukn al-Dawla Dawud, the son of Nur al-Dawla Sokman, the son of Ortoq.

Seven generations back to the founder, recited in the sentence that records the
loss of the dynasty's principal city. He then adds where he got the date: from a
well-informed native of Amid, who told him the city was surrendered on 19
Dhu'l-Hijja — October 1232 — and that al-Kamil's son entered the next day and
al-Kamil himself a fortnight later.

The Ayyubid did not have to besiege it either.

## Why the dynasty did not end

What is striking about both episodes is how little either changed.

The Artuqids lost Mayyafariqin in 1185 and Amid in 1232 and went on existing.
They kept Mardin, and they kept it for another century and three quarters, through
Ayyubid overlordship, through the Mongols, through the Ilkhanate and past it.

This is not a dynasty that was destroyed. It is one that was serially demoted —
from an independent power that had beaten the Franks in the field to a lord of one
citadel who paid whoever was currently strongest and was left alone because
removing him was more trouble than it was worth.

The genealogies are the evidence for that reading, and they cut both ways. A
family that can be named seven generations deep in a chronicle is a family that
mattered enough to be tracked. It is also, by the point Ibn Khallikan is writing,
a family whose principal remaining asset *is* the genealogy — the claim to be the
descendants of Ortoq, holding what is left of Ortoq's country, on somebody else's
sufferance.
