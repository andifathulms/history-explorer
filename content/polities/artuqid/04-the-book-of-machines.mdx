title: The book of machines

In 1206, at Amid, an engineer named al-Jazari finished a book for his patron,
the Artuqid ruler Nasr al-Din Mahmud, whose father and grandfather he had also
served. It is called *al-Jami bayn al-ilm wa'l-amal* — the compendium of theory
and practice — and it is usually known as the Book of Knowledge of Ingenious
Mechanical Devices.

It describes about fifty machines, with instructions for building them and
drawings of the parts. Water clocks and candle clocks, including one with a
figure that drops a ball onto a cymbal on the hour. Automatic vessels for
serving drink. A waitress automaton. A hand-washing device with a flush
mechanism. Fountains whose patterns change on a cycle. And, less decoratively,
the water-raising machines the region actually needed: a crank-driven pump, a
twin-cylinder suction pump with a genuine crankshaft, and a segmental gear.

Historians of technology treat several of those as the first surviving
descriptions anywhere. The simpler fact is who paid for it. The book was
commissioned, paid for and preserved by a minor Turkmen dynasty in the Jazira
whose political history is a list of the overlords it acknowledged, in the
century between the crusade it defeated and the Mongols it submitted to.

The same thing keeps happening in that century and it is worth stating plainly.
The Sultanate of Rum's cultural peak came while it was a Mongol tributary. The
Ilkhanate produced the first world history after destroying the caliphate. The
Ma'munids of Khwarazm held al-Biruni and Ibn Sina for fifteen years and lasted
twenty-two. The Kakuyids gave Ibn Sina the only settled decade he ever had. The
relationship between how much power a court had and what got made there is much
weaker than any account organised around power will suggest.

The Artuqids left no measurable trace of any other kind. They also have, on the
strength of one patron in one small city, a permanent claim on the history of
mechanical engineering.
