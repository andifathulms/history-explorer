The first chapter says the Mardin branch outlasted its two siblings by nearly
two centuries as a vassal of whoever held Iraq. This chapter is about how, and
about what that is worth in a corpus that ranks polities.

The list of overlords is the point. Mardin acknowledged, in order: the Great
Seljuks; the Zangids of the previous pages in this region; Saladin and the
Ayyubids after him; the Rum Seljuks briefly; the Khwarazmshahs in their last
years; the Mongols under Hulegu, to whom Qara Arslan submitted in person; the
Ilkhanate for as long as it existed; and finally the Kara Koyunlu, who ended
the line in 1409. That is nine sovereignties across two hundred and
seventy-five years, and the Artuqids outlasted every one of them in Mardin.

The technique was not clever diplomacy. It was a rock. Mardin sits on a scarp
above the Mesopotamian plain with a citadel on the summit, in a position that
can be blockaded and starved but is expensive to storm, above a plain that
every army crossing between Anatolia and Iraq wants to move through quickly. A
power heading somewhere else prefers a tributary on that rock to a siege, and
the Artuqids made sure that preference was always available.

It is the same calculation the Bavands made in the Elburz, the Nizaris in the
Alborz, the Karamanids in the Taurus and the imams of Oman behind the Hajar.
Mardin is the version on the smallest and most exposed ground, which is why it
required the most consistent submission.

The end came from the direction that had never been an overlord. The Kara
Koyunlu were a Turkmen confederation competing for pasture rather than a state
collecting tribute, and a confederation has no use for a tributary. They took
Mardin in 1409 and the dynasty stopped.

The dynasty's end is usually given as a range, 1232 to 1409, because the three
branches ended separately — Hisn Kayfa to the Ayyubids, Harput to the Seljuks
of Rum, Mardin to the Black Sheep — and the sources date the dynasty by
whichever branch is meant.

On duration the Artuqids outlast most of the empires of their world. On every
other measure they register nothing at all. They held a small province by
conceding to everybody, and spent the quiet that bought on a bridge and a book,
which is a defensible way to use three centuries and leaves almost no trace in
any account organised around power.
