Herod had ten wives and a great many children and he killed three of his sons.
Alexander and Aristobulus, the two by Mariamme the Hasmonean, were strangled at
Sebaste in 7 BC. Antipater, the eldest, was executed five days before Herod's own
death. The Augustan joke Macrobius preserves — that it is better to be Herod's
pig than his son — depends on the pig being safe because a Jew would not eat it.

He rewrote his will at least six times. The last version divided the kingdom
between three surviving sons: Archelaus to have Judaea, Samaria and Idumaea with
the royal title, Antipas Galilee and Peraea, Philip the territories north-east of
the Sea of Galilee.

## Ratified elsewhere

It did not take effect on his death. It went to Rome.

Archelaus and Antipas both sailed to argue it before Augustus. A delegation of
fifty Judaeans went too, and asked for something neither brother had proposed: no
king at all, and incorporation into the province of Syria under Roman governors,
on the grounds that they had had enough of this family. Josephus says eight
thousand Jews of Rome turned out to support them.

Augustus heard all of it and issued a settlement that was mostly the will with
alterations. The three sons got their territories. Archelaus got Judaea but not
the title of king — he was made ethnarch, with the kingship held out as a
possibility if he behaved.

That is why this record's `succession_rule` reads `nomination`. The king
designated, in writing, repeatedly. And the designation was an application rather
than a decision, because the office had been conferred by Rome and only Rome
could confer it again. Coding rule 7 takes the rule in force, and the rule in
force had two steps.

Meanwhile, in the months the hearing took, the country rose. There were risings
in Judaea, Galilee and Peraea, a former royal slave and a shepherd both declared
themselves king, the Jerusalem garrison was besieged, and Varus came down from
Syria with two legions and crucified two thousand people restoring order.

## Ten years

Archelaus lasted until AD 6, when a joint delegation of Jews and Samaritans —
who agreed about very little else — went to Rome to complain about him. Augustus
deposed him, exiled him to Gaul, and annexed Judaea, Samaria and Idumaea as a
Roman province under a prefect.

The assessment for taxation that followed is the census of Quirinius, and it
produced the revolt of Judas the Galilean. Josephus treats that movement, and
the philosophy he says Judas founded, as the seed of the war sixty years later.

So the Judaean core of this kingdom was a client kingdom for forty-three years
and a province for the rest, with one interruption: Agrippa I, Herod's grandson,
held the whole of his grandfather's territory from AD 41 to 44 because he had
been in Rome and had been useful to Claudius on the day Caligula was murdered.
He died suddenly at Caesarea after three years, in a scene Josephus and the Acts
of the Apostles both describe and describe similarly, and it reverted.

This record's `ended.type` is `gradual absorption` for that reason. Three
absorptions across eighty-six years, each an administrative act taken on the
death or removal of a client, and no campaign fought for any of them.
