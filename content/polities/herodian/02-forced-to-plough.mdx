Herod's kingdom grew the way a client king's kingdom grows: the emperor gave him
places, and expected results.

Josephus describes one of the transfers and what followed, and it is the clearest
statement anywhere of what Rome thought a client was for.

Augustus took Trachonitis — the lava country south-east of Damascus, broken,
defensible and inhabited by people who lived by raiding their neighbours — away
from its previous holder and added it to Herod's territory.

The inhabitants, Josephus writes, now *had not power to rob, but were forced to
plough the land and to live quietly, which was a thing they did not like*; and
when they did take the trouble, the ground did not produce much fruit for them
anyway.

## The reputation

Herod would not let them go back to raiding, and Josephus says this procured him
a great reputation for his care.

<Figure id="lajat2" />

That sentence is the transaction in miniature. Rome had a lawless district on a
provincial frontier and no wish to garrison it. It handed the district to a
king who would suppress the raiding at his own expense, using his own troops,
and who would be commended for it. What Herod got was territory and standing.
What Augustus got was a quiet border and no legion tied down.

<Figure id="herod-map" />

## What happened when he left

The arrangement's weakness showed the moment he was out of the country.

Herod sailed for Rome — it was the voyage on which he went to accuse his son
Alexander and commend another son to Caesar's protection — and the Trachonites
put about a report that he was dead, revolted, and went back to robbing their
neighbours.

His commanders put them down in his absence. About forty of the principal
raiders, frightened by what had happened to those already taken, left the
country altogether and went into Arabia, where the Nabataean minister Syllaeus
took them in and gave them a stronghold to live in.

Josephus adds the detail that explains why: Syllaeus had wanted to marry Herod's
sister Salome and had been refused.

## The shape of the thing

So the expansion of Herod's kingdom looks like this. An emperor reassigns a
district. The new king stops the inhabitants living the way they have always
lived and makes them farm ground that will not carry them. They wait for him to
be away, spread a rumour that he is dead, and start again. The survivors go over
the border to a neighbouring court, which shelters them out of a private
grievance about a marriage.

None of it is conquest and all of it is work. A client kingdom was a
subcontract, and Herod was good at it — which is why he kept being given more,
and why what he did with the money is the part worth looking at.
