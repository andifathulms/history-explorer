In the winter of 40 BC the Roman senate voted Herod king of the Jews. Antony
proposed it, Octavian supported it, the vote was unanimous, and the two of them
walked him up to the Capitol between them to sacrifice and to deposit the decree
in the treasury.

<Figure id="augustus-veii" />

He held none of the territory. The Parthians had overrun Syria and installed a
Hasmonean in Jerusalem; Herod had escaped with his family to Masada and then to
Rome to ask for help for his brother-in-law, and had come away with a kingship
he had not asked for and would have to conquer. It took him three years.

<Figure id="masada" />

## A right conferred, and nothing else

Herod's claim to rule rested on a grant from an external sovereign, and on
nothing else at all.

Consider what was not available to him. Descent from the old royal line was
out: he was an Idumaean commoner whose grandfather had been a temple official
at best, whose family had been Jewish for two generations because a
Hasmonean king conquered Idumaea and imposed the law, and whose opponents
said for fifty years that this did not count. Priestly sanction was out — he
was not a priest and could not be, and the high priesthood he appointed and
removed at will. A claim on some predecessor's own title was out — he took
none as his ground. And conquest describes how he got Jerusalem, not why he
was entitled to it in the first place; he had been king for three years
before the siege.

What he had was the vote. The Latin formula for what it made him was *rex socius
et amicus populi Romani*, a king who was an ally and friend of the Roman people,
and Herod's account of himself never left it.

It is the same arrangement the Arab client kings of late antiquity held five
centuries later — the Jafnids on the Roman frontier, the Nasrids on the Persian
— and it has the same weakness in every case. A right conferred can be
withdrawn, and its holder spends his reign making sure that the sovereign who
conferred it does not want to.

This is not the first record entered with the value; the Nasrid page came first.
It is the case the argument should have been made from.

## The other half of the title

*King of the Jews*, and Herod meant both words.

He was a Roman client and he was also the ruler of a Jewish kingdom, and
Richardson's book makes its argument in its subtitle: the two were a single
arrangement rather than a contradiction managed. Herod built temples to Augustus
at Caesarea, Sebaste and Panias, in the parts of his kingdom that were not
Jewish, and did not put one anywhere that was. He struck coins with no human
image for circulation in Judaea. He rebuilt the Temple on a scale nobody had
attempted and did not enter the parts of it he was not entitled to enter. He
sent his sons to Rome to be educated and married a Hasmonean.

<Figure id="herod-coin" />

It is a career of holding two audiences at once, and the reason it is legible in
this much detail is Josephus, who used Nicolaus of Damascus — Herod's own court
historian — and then wrote against him. Very few ancient states are visible
through a hostile source drawing on an official one.
