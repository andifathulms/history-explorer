The list is unreasonable for a kingdom this size. Caesarea Maritima, a city and
an artificial deep-water harbour built into open sea with hydraulic concrete
shipped from Italy. Sebaste, a rebuilt Samaria. Masada, Herodium, Machaerus and
a chain of desert fortress-palaces with cisterns, baths and swimming pools.
Jericho's winter palaces. Aqueducts. The Antonia. Public building at Athens,
Rhodes, Antioch, Berytus, Damascus and Nicopolis, and a subsidy to the Olympic
games.

And the Temple, begun about 20 BC: the Second Temple rebuilt on an artificial
platform that doubled the enclosure to something like fourteen hectares, the
largest sacred precinct in the Roman empire. The work outlasted him by decades
and was finished about six years before Titus burned it. Its retaining wall is
still standing and is the most visited object anything in this corpus produced.

## What paid for it

This record's `revenue_basis` carries three values, which is more than most
pages here, and two of them are unusual enough to justify the space.

`land-tax` is ordinary: an agricultural kingdom taxed agriculturally, and
Josephus preserves the figures the sons' territories were assessed at when the
kingdom was divided.

`monopoly` is the balsam. The groves at Jericho and Ein Gedi produced a resin
that grew nowhere else in the world, was used in medicine and perfume, and was
worth extraordinary sums; the kings held them as a royal possession. Cleopatra
had extracted them from Herod and leased them back to him, which tells you what
they were worth to both of them.

`mining` is the strangest line in the accounts. Josephus has Augustus grant
Herod the lease of the copper mines of Cyprus at half the revenue. The king of
Judaea drew income from extraction in a province he did not rule, by the gift of
the emperor who had made him king — which is this record's whole arrangement
compressed into a bookkeeping entry.

## What it was for

Richardson's reading is that the building programme is the argument. A client
king is retained for as long as he is more useful than a province, and Herod
spent his reign making the case in stone: harbours and roads that served Roman
trade, fortresses that held a difficult frontier, cities named for the emperor,
and a Temple that kept the population of his own kingdom occupied with something
other than him.

It worked for thirty-three years, which is longer than most eastern client
kingdoms managed, and it is the same calculation the Nabataean record in this
region describes from the other side of the Dead Sea. Both kingdoms lasted as
long as they were worth more to Rome intact than converted.

Ten years after Herod died, Judaea was a province.
