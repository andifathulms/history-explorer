Herod's kingdom was not inherited by anybody. It was broken into pieces at his
death and the pieces were afterwards handed round among his descendants by
Roman emperors, individually, by letter, as favours — and taken back the same
way.

Josephus records one such transaction as a matter of routine administration, and
the routineness is the point.

In the twelfth year of his reign Claudius bestowed on Agrippa the younger the
tetrarchy that had been Philip's, and Batanaea, and added Trachonitis to it,
with Abila, which had been the tetrarchy of Lysanias. And he took Chalcis away
from him, which Agrippa had governed for four years.

Three districts granted, one withdrawn, in a single sentence, by an emperor who
had never been there.

## What the family did with what it had

The other thing Josephus records in the same passage is what the Herodian house
had become useful for, which was marriages.

Agrippa gave his sister Drusilla to Azizus, king of Emesa — on condition that he
be circumcised. She had previously been promised to Epiphanes, son of King
Antiochus, who had undertaken to come over to the Jewish religion in order to
marry her and then declined to go through with it, and the match had fallen
apart on exactly that point.

He gave his other sister Mariamne to Archelaus son of Helcias, to whom their
father had betrothed her.

And from that marriage, Josephus notes, came a daughter, whose name was Berenice.

## A dynasty as a clearing house

Read those two paragraphs together and the position is exact.

The Herods no longer ruled a kingdom; they held whatever assortment of tetrarchies
the current emperor had assigned them. What they still possessed that nobody
else in the region did was a royal Jewish pedigree combined with complete
familiarity with Rome — which made them the natural intermediaries between the
Jewish population and the empire, and made their daughters valuable to the small
kings of Syria and Commagene, for whom marrying one meant acquiring a connection
at the imperial court.

The price of the connection was circumcision, negotiated in advance, and at
least one prospective bridegroom found it too high once the moment came.

## The end of it

The last of them, Agrippa II, sat with the Roman army outside Jerusalem in 70
and watched the Temple his great-grandfather had built come down. He kept his
districts, died childless around the end of the century, and the family stops.

Herod had spent forty years buying a place for his house in the Roman world,
successfully, by being more useful than any alternative. What his descendants
inherited was the usefulness rather than the kingdom, and when the thing they
were useful for stopped existing — a Jewish population with a Temple, requiring
management — so did they.
