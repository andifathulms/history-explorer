The war began in 66 and Titus took Jerusalem in the summer of 70. Herod's
Temple — begun about 20 BC, finished around AD 64 — burned.

Agrippa II, the last of the family to hold territory, spent the war on the
Roman side. He had tried to prevent it: Josephus gives him a long speech to the
crowd in Jerusalem in 66 arguing that Rome could not be beaten, listing the
nations that had tried, and it is a set piece rather than a transcript but the
position is certainly his. When it failed he sent two thousand cavalry to the
Roman army, was wounded at the siege of Gamla, and was with Titus at Jerusalem.
His sister Berenice became Titus's mistress and nearly his wife.

He kept his northern territories for another twenty-two years and died about 92,
and Rome took them.

## The shape of the record

A dynasty founded by a Roman vote to make Judaea governable spent its last act
watching Rome destroy the thing it had built, from the winning side, and was then
quietly wound up.

## What ended, and what was ended

There are two endings here and they do not coincide.

The dynasty was absorbed the way client dynasties are: a territory at a time, on
the death or removal of its holder, with no campaign fought against it. Agrippa
II kept his districts through the war and after it, and died childless.

The thing that ended in 70 was not the dynasty. It was the Temple, the
priesthood, the pilgrimage and the sacrificial cult — the whole apparatus of a
religion practised in one building — and Judaism afterwards is what was rebuilt
without them. A client kingdom's administrative dissolution and the destruction
of a temple happened in the same country within a generation of each other, and
only one of them is still consequential.

## The pair

The pairing between this dynasty and the Hasmoneans is closer than a
succession. The Hasmonean record's single influence
count is Judaism carried into Idumaea by John Hyrcanus in 112 BC — and that
campaign is why there was a Herod at all. His family were Jews because of it,
and the charge his enemies pressed for fifty years was that being a Jew that way
did not count. The one lasting thing the Hasmoneans did produced the dynasty
that extinguished them, seventy-five years later.

The two houses also gave opposite answers to the same question. The Hasmoneans
had no lineage and wrote themselves a constitution: an assembly of the people
and the priests confirmed Simon and his heirs. Herod had no lineage and got a
Roman vote. Election and appointment, in the same city, a century apart, both
addressed to the identical problem of ruling Judaea without being descended from
anybody entitled to.

Neither answer survived contact with the empire next door, and the Temple
outlasted both dynasties by six years.
