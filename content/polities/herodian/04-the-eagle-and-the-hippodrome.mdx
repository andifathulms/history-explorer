title: The eagle and the hippodrome
drafted_from: josephus-whiston

Josephus gives the last months of Herod's reign at length, and the two scenes he
chooses are the ones the dynasty never lived down.

The first is the eagle. Herod had set a great golden eagle over the principal
gate of the Temple. Two teachers of the law with a following of young men
persuaded their pupils that the image was contrary to the law and that to pull it
down would be a pious act even if it cost them their lives — and while the king
lay ill and a rumour went round that he was dead, they let themselves down from
the roof on ropes in the middle of the day and cut the eagle to pieces with axes
in front of the crowd. The captain of the Temple guard arrested about forty of
them. They did not deny it. Brought before the king, they said they had obeyed a
better law than his and were glad. Herod, carried out on a litter because he
could no longer stand, had the teachers and the men who had actually cut the
image burned alive, and deposed the high priest for having let it happen.

The second is what he ordered from his sickbed. Josephus says Herod knew the
country would celebrate his death, and could not bear it; so he had the notable
men of the whole nation summoned to Jericho, shut them into the hippodrome, and
gave his sister and her husband an instruction: the moment he was dead, they were
to surround the place with soldiers and kill everyone in it, so that every family
in Judaea would be in mourning on the day of his funeral whether it wanted to be
or not.

He died. They let the men go home. Josephus records both halves — the order and
the refusal — and it is the refusal that is the more useful fact, because it says
what the household thought of the instruction.

Between the two scenes Josephus puts the illness, in a catalogue of symptoms so
specific that physicians have been diagnosing it ever since: a slow fever, an
intolerable itching over the whole surface of the body, continual pains in the
bowels, swollen feet, convulsions, difficulty breathing except upright. He was
taken to the warm springs across the Jordan and lowered into a bath of oil, and
came out of it apparently dead, and revived. He tried to stab himself with a
knife he was using to cut an apple and was stopped by a cousin. The cry raised in
the palace at that moment reached his eldest son Antipater in the prison below,
who offered his gaolers money to let him out — and the gaoler reported it, and
Herod, hearing of it, had Antipater executed at once. He died five days later.

Josephus is not an impartial witness of the family and says so about other
things, but he is not inventing the pattern. He has already given the strangling
of the two sons by the Hasmonean princess, and their mother's execution before
them, and Herod's own remorse afterwards. What the last months add is the
direction of travel: a king who could no longer tell a religious protest from a
rebellion, and who assumed at the end that his subjects' grief would have to be
manufactured.

The kingdom he left had temples, harbours, a rebuilt Jerusalem, and a fortress at
every strategic point in the country, and Rome thought well enough of him to
confirm his will in most particulars. Within ten years of his death the Judaean
half of it had been put under a Roman governor at the request of its own
inhabitants.
