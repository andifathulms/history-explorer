Abu Ya'qub Yusuf spent his apprenticeship as a provincial governor before he was
ever a caliph. His father Abd al-Mu'min sent him to Seville in 1159 at the
request of its own shaykhs, and he began by putting down an independent chief
at Tavira and taking Mértola from an Almoravid holdout - the ordinary work of
holding a frontier, done before he had any claim to the succession at all. It
did not go only his way: the following year a Christian king defeated him
outside Seville itself, and a number of Almohad shaykhs and scholars died in
the battle.

## Recalled to be named heir

In 1163 Abu Ya'qub was summoned back to Marrakesh to be recognised as his
father's successor in place of an older brother, and he set out again almost
immediately behind Abd al-Mu'min, who had resolved on one more campaign of his
own. It was the founder's last expedition: he died at Salé that same year. His
own brother Abu Hafs secured the Almohad hierarchy's allegiance to Abu Ya'qub
before the news had even finished spreading, and the new caliph took his
father's throne with no interval for a rival claim to form.

## A reign spent putting out other people's fires

Much of what followed was the same pattern repeated at scale: send Abu Hafs or
a son to deal with whatever had broken loose, and follow if it mattered enough.
The Almohads crushed Ibn Mardanish's Murcia in 1165, campaigned in the Ghomara
mountains two years later, and in 1168 sent their elite troops to relieve a
besieged Badajoz - arriving to learn the garrison had already been saved
without them, its Portuguese besieger captured in the process. In 1177 Abu
Ya'qub banished two of his own viziers; in 1178 his fleet, under a son of the
very Ibn Mardanish he had defeated, raided Lisbon for a considerable haul of
plunder. That same year Abu Hafs, his brother and longtime deputy, died in the
holy war he had spent his career fighting.

## Santarém

Abu Hafs's death removed the man who had carried out most of Abu Ya'qub's own
campaigning, and when the Christian king broke a standing truce not long
afterward, the caliph committed himself personally. He assigned his own sons
provincial commands in Spain and followed them across the strait to lead the
war himself. The siege of Santarém went wrong in a way the sources cannot fully
agree on - Muslim and Christian chroniclers dispute whether treachery or simple
confusion caused the Almohad army to withdraw without their sovereign's
knowledge - but the outcome is not in dispute. Struck by a lance or an arrow
below the navel, Abu Ya'qub Yusuf died under the walls of Santarém on 13 July
1184, and his son Ya'qub succeeded him within days.

Twenty years on the throne left him the caliph who had to govern what his
father conquered rather than conquer it himself - holding Ifriqiya, subduing
Murcia, relieving Badajoz too late to matter - and a death in the field that
handed the empire, at its widest reach, to the son who would win Alarcos and
lose Las Navas de Tolosa in his turn.
