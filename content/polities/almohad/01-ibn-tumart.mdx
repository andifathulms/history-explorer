Ibn Tumart was a Masmuda Berber from the Anti-Atlas who studied in the east,
returned around 1120 preaching an uncompromising doctrine of divine unity —
*tawhid*, from which his followers took the name al-Muwahhidun, the Almohads —
and denounced the Almoravids as anthropomorphists and therefore unbelievers.
Around 1121 he was proclaimed the mahdi, the rightly guided one, by his
followers at Tinmal in the High Atlas.

<Figure id="tinmel2" />

That claim is the difference between this movement and the one it replaced. The
Almoravids were enforcers of Maliki law who never denied the caliphate's
existence. The Almohads declared their founder infallible, restructured Berber
tribal society into a graded hierarchy of the movement, and after Ibn Tumart's
death his successor Abd al-Mu'min took the title of caliph outright. The Maghreb
had produced its own caliphate, on doctrine rather than descent.

They took Marrakesh in 1147, then Ifriqiya, then al-Andalus, and for the first
and only time the whole Islamic west was one polity. Its high point, under
al-Mansur, included a victory over Castile at Alarcos in 1195; its break came at
Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212, after which al-Andalus was progressively lost and
the Maghreb split into the three successor states that shaped North Africa
afterwards.
