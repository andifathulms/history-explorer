title: Two things at once
drafted_from: bennison-2016
phase: aside
---

The Almohad court commissioned the commentaries on Aristotle that carried Greek
philosophy into Latin Europe. The Almohad state abolished the legal status of
Jews and Christians in its territory and forced them to convert or leave. Both
of those are true of the same regime in the same decades, and a page that
reports one without the other is not describing the polity.

The philosophy first. Abu Ya'qub Yusuf, the second Almohad caliph, was seriously
educated and interested in philosophy; Ibn Tufayl, his physician, introduced Ibn
Rushd to him, and — the story is Ibn Rushd's own — the caliph asked him what the
philosophers held about whether the heavens were eternal or created, and he was
too frightened to answer until the caliph began answering it himself. He was
commissioned to write commentaries on Aristotle that would make him
intelligible. He produced them over about twenty-five years, alongside a career
as a qadi in Seville and Cordoba, and they were translated into Latin and Hebrew
within two generations. Latin Christendom called him the Commentator, and the
thirteenth-century argument about Aristotle in the universities of Paris and
Oxford is conducted substantially through his readings.

Now the other thing. The Almohads held that the *dhimma* — the arrangement under
which Jews and Christians paid a tax and were left to their own law, which had
been the settled practice of every Muslim polity on this site for five hundred
years — was not valid. Communities that had been in Morocco and al-Andalus since
before the conquest were required to convert. Synagogues and churches were
closed. Families converted publicly and kept their practice privately, and were
subjected to discriminatory dress and surveillance because the regime did not
believe them. Many left: for Christian Spain, for Egypt, for Provence. Among
them was a Cordoban family whose son, Moses ben Maimon, ended as court physician
in Cairo and wrote the *Guide of the Perplexed* — a work in Arabic, by a
refugee from this polity, arguing with the same Aristotle its caliph was
commissioning commentaries on.

The two facts are not in tension in the way a modern reader expects. The
Almohad position was that truth was single and demonstrable, that the learned
should reason their way to it, and that those who would not accept it had no
standing. Philosophical seriousness and the abolition of religious pluralism
came out of the same conviction.

This site's rating panel for this polity gives a reach figure, a longevity range
and three influence counts. It has no way to record that the same court did
both of these things, or that the two are connected.
