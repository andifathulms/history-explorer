The Almohad caliphate's best day was at Alarcos in 1195, when Ya'qub al-Mansur
beat Alfonso VIII of Castile badly enough to win a truce and a surname. The
story that attached itself to the battle is an exchange of letters, and Ibn
Khallikan preserves both the story and the evidence that it is not true.

## What he had

He tells you where he got it, which is the habit that makes him worth reading.

Towards the end of 1270, in Damascus, he saw a notebook in the handwriting of a
scholar who had been chief of the professors there and had travelled in Spain,
writing down notes on the Almohad administration while he lived in it. Ibn
Khallikan copies a chapter of it whole.

According to that notebook: the truce between Ya'qub and Alfonso ran out; Ya'qub
resolved to cross; he summoned his governors and troops to Salé and then fell
seriously ill, the physicians gave him up, the muster collapsed and he was
carried back to Marrakesh. The nomad tribes, encouraged, spread ruin through the
country; Alfonso did the same in Muslim Spain; and the Almohad army had to be
broken up into detachments and sent east and west to put down the resulting
disorder.

It was at that point — with the caliph dying and his army dispersed — that
Alfonso sent an ambassador to demand the surrender of a frontier fortress, with a
letter drafted in Arabic by a Christian secretary of his named Ibn al-Fakhkhar.

## The letter

It opens with a Christian *basmala*: *In thy name, O God, creator of the heavens
and of the earth! His blessings be upon the Lord Messiah, the Spirit of God, his
word and his eloquent messenger.* Then it gets to business, and the business is
an argument between two commanders of the faithful about which of them is
protecting the Muslims of Spain.

> It cannot escape the attention of whoever is gifted with a penetrating
> intellect that you are the emir of the Hanifite community as I am that of the
> Christian one. You well know how the chiefs of the Spanish Muslims have
> abandoned and deserted each other, how they neglect the care of their subjects
> and how greatly they are inclined to enjoy repose. You know that I make them
> undergo the law of the strongest, expelling them from their abodes, carrying
> their children into captivity and making an example of their men. You have at
> present no excuse to offer for not coming to their assistance.

Then the taunt about the law of retaliation — that God has excused the Muslims
from killing ten Christians for every Muslim slain, *because he knew that you
have among you many men so weak and feeble* that they could not manage it. Then
the accusation: *you procrastinate, year after year, making one step forward and
the other backward. I know not whether you be detained by cowardice or by the
intention of belying the promises made by thy Lord.*

And then the offer, which is the part that makes the letter famous. Since the
sultan apparently cannot find a way across the sea, Alfonso proposes to come to
him: send ships, galleys and transports, and *I may pass over to you with my
bands and fight with you in the place which you like best*. If the sultan wins,
he will have been handed a magnificent present. If Alfonso wins, he will *deserve
to be the emir of the two communities and the sovereign of the two continents*.

Ya'qub tore the letter up, wrote on the back of one of the pieces —

> Return to them; for we shall certainly go unto them with troops which they
> shall not be able to withstand, and we will expel them from their possessions,
> humbled and despicable. My answer you shall see, not hear!

— and added a line of al-Mutanabbi under it: *He has no letters to send but
swords; no other ambassadors than the fivefold host.* He pitched his tents
outside the town that same day, crossed the strait, met the enemy assembled and
prepared, and, in the notebook's words, *made them suffer a most disgraceful
defeat*.

## The problem

Having given all that, Ibn Khallikan keeps reading, and then reports what he
found.

He came across another book, by an Andalusi author, containing the same letter —
attributed to a different Alfonso, addressed to a different sovereign, the
Almoravid Yusuf ibn Tashufin, a full century earlier. The reply given there is in
the same terms as the one he has just quoted. And that author seems to have taken
it from a document in the hand of an Egyptian official who lived long before
Ya'qub was born, which means the message cannot have been sent to Ya'qub at all.

His comment on the contradiction is four words: *God knows where the truth lies!*

He then adds that he met a number of learned Maghribis who thought the date he
had been given was wrong, and sets out their version as well.

## What that is worth

A splendid set piece survives in two versions a hundred years apart, fitted to
whichever Muslim ruler the story needed, and the man transmitting it says so
himself instead of choosing.

The Almohads were the most ideologically explicit regime the Islamic west
produced: a caliphate founded on a doctrine, run by a hierarchy, with the
caliph's authority resting on a claim about right belief. A letter in which a
Christian king addresses the caliph as one commander of the faithful to another
and accuses him of neglecting his own community is exactly the letter that
regime's supporters would have wanted to exist, and exactly the letter its
enemies would have enjoyed inventing.

Alarcos happened. The victory was real, the truce that followed it was real, and
seventeen years later the same coalition of Christian kingdoms destroyed the
Almohad army at Las Navas de Tolosa. What the letters are evidence of is not the
battle but the argument about it, which went on being rewritten for as long as
anybody in either language needed a use for it.
