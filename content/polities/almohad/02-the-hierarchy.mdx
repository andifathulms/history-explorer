title: A movement with ranks
drafted_from: bennison-2016

The Almohads are the most thoroughly organised polity in this site's Islamic
West, and the organisation is what let a mountain movement govern from the
Atlantic to Tripoli.

Ibn Tumart did not simply preach. He built an institution, and the shape of it
is unusual enough to set out. At the top was the Council of Ten, his earliest
companions; below that a council of fifty representing the tribes that had
joined; below that ranked grades of adherents — the *talaba*, students of the
doctrine who were also administrators and political officers, and the *huffaz*,
those who had memorised his teaching. Tribes were enrolled into the movement
collectively and given a place in the order. There were formal
purges — the *tamyiz*, the sorting — in which the doubtful were identified and
killed.

What that produced is a state whose administrative class was a religious cadre,
recruited by examination in the founder's doctrine and posted where it was
needed. The talaba went out as provincial officials, army chaplains and
inspectors, and the doctrine was taught to the troops in Berber as well as
Arabic. This site's Great Seljuk page has Nizam al-Mulk funding madrasas to
produce a scholarly class the state could rely on; the Almohads went further and
made the doctrine itself the qualification for office.

The building programme is the same argument in stone, and it is the most
visible thing they left. The Koutoubia at Marrakesh, the Giralda at Seville, the
unfinished Hassan tower at Rabat, the mosque at Tinmal over Ibn Tumart's tomb:
all within about forty years, all in the same austere idiom of enormous plain
surfaces and geometric brick and stone, all deliberately unlike the ornamented
work of the dynasty they had replaced. The square minaret form they used is the
one this site's Aghlabid page traces to Kairouan in 836.

The cost of building a state on one man's infallibility appears on the last
chapter of this page. A doctrine that qualifies men for office can also
disqualify them, and a caliphate that rests on the mahdi being right cannot
easily survive his being seen to be wrong.
