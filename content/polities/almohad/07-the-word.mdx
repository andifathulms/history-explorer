The caliphate ended in Morocco in 1269 when Zenata pastoralists took Marrakesh.
The word did not end with it. For most of the following century the strongest
state in North Africa still called itself Almohad, still had a body of men in
its capital who were the Almohads, and still transacted its politics through
the question of who was one.

That state was in Tunis. The family that ran it descended from Abu Hafs Umar,
one of the ten companions Ibn Tumart had put at the head of the movement, and
it had kept the mahdist doctrine in 1229 when Marrakesh threw it away. So the
Almohads outlived the Almohad empire by taking the argument east — which is
also how they came to be destroyed by it.

## One night in Tunis, 1250

The founder of the Tunis line died in 1249 and was succeeded by his son
al-Mustansir, who was about twenty. The vizier, a man of the Hintata — one of
the mountain tribes that had sheltered Ibn Tumart in the first place — thought
a boy could be steered.

What stood in his way was the sultan's household troops. Ibn Khaldun describes
them exactly: a regularly organised corps containing nobody but Christian
slaves and Spanish Muslims of good family, who owed their fortunes and their
positions to the last sultan, whose numbers overawed the Almohads and whose
members were taking the best posts of the empire away from them.

The vizier approached the dead sultan's brothers first and got nothing. Then he
approached the son of one of them, swore secret allegiance to him, and on a
morning in August went to the palace gate as though to hold his public
audience, arrested his colleague, and led the Almohad chiefs to the young man's
house to take the oath.

The sultan put his own supporters on horseback and sent his freedman Zafir to
attack. The two forces met at the prayer ground outside the walls and the
Almohads were routed. The vizier was killed there. Zafir went on to the house
of the sultan's uncle, killed him and his son, met the other uncle on the road
with his son and killed them both as well, and took the heads to the sultan.
The houses of the Almohads were given over to pillage and destroyed to their
foundations.

Zafir's enemies then persuaded the sultan that a freedman who had killed his
uncles — whose innocence was perfectly well established — had acted with
extreme illegality, and Zafir fled to the Arabs and lived as an outlaw.

Fifty-five years after Alarcos, the movement that had made a caliphate out of a
doctrine was a faction being cleared out of a capital in a night by a guard of
Christian slaves. Ibn Khaldun, who worked for the family that ordered it, tells
it without comment as a piece of ordinary court business, which by then it was.

## The part that could not be pillaged

The buildings did not care. The Koutoubia at Marrakesh, the Giralda at Seville
and the unfinished tower at Rabat were up before any of this, and two of them
are still the tallest old things in their cities. So were the commentaries: Ibn
Rushd's Aristotle was translated into Latin and Hebrew within two generations
of his death and was being argued over in Paris and Oxford while the Almohad
shaykhs of Tunis were being run down in the street.

An empire founded on the proposition that its founder could not be wrong left
almost nothing that depended on his being right.
