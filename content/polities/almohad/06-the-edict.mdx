In 1229 the Almohad caliph al-Ma'mun crossed from Spain to Morocco to take a
throne that several relatives were also claiming, and to get there he made a
bargain and then issued an order. Between them they finished the thing his
great-grandfather's teacher had founded, and he did it deliberately.

## The bargain

The Christian king put twelve thousand cavalry at his disposal. Ibn Khaldun's
translator sets out the terms in a footnote, from the sources: in return the
king received ten Muslim frontier fortresses, which he named himself; the
sultan undertook to build a church in Marrakesh for the troops, in which they
were to be allowed to ring bells at the hours of prayer; if one of those
soldiers wished to embrace Islam the sultan was obliged to refuse him and send
him back to his corps to be judged; and if a Muslim became a Christian he was
not to be punished in any way.

That is the caliph of the Almohads — the office founded on the proposition
that the ruler of the Almoravids was an unbeliever for a mistake about the
divine attributes — contracting to prevent conversions to Islam in his own
capital.

He landed, destroyed the army of a rival who came out to meet him, and went up
to Marrakesh.

## The shaykhs

There he called the Almohad shaykhs together, recited to them the offences they
had committed, arrested about a hundred of the principal ones and had them put
to death. The author of the *Rawd al-Qirtas*, who exaggerates whenever he
reports numbers, says four thousand six hundred Almohads were executed on that
occasion, that their heads were set on lances around the palace, and that when
a courtier complained of the smell the sultan told him the smell of a dead
traitor displeases only traitors and is agreeable to loyal subjects. He was, the
same author says, a shedder of blood who did not pause at it for the space of a
blink; he also knew the Quran perfectly, and the traditions, and the history of
the ancient Arabs, and philology, and letters, and wrote Arabic with a rare
elegance.

## The order

Then he sent an edict to every city of the empire. The name of the Mahdi was to
be struck off the coinage and out of the Friday prayer. The part of the call to
prayer that was in Berber, which carried a pious remembrance of him, was to be
suppressed. The words inserted into the dawn call — *arise*, and *praise be to
God* — were to go. The custom of calling Ibn Tumart the impeccable imam was
abolished. Other practices introduced by the Mahdi and kept by Abd al-Mu'min
and his descendants went with them, and each time the prohibitions were
renewed, more were added.

Every Almohad was hearing the same thing from every minaret in the west at
once, and what they were hearing was a silence where a name had been.

## What was left

A caliphate is an argument for obedience. Ibn Tumart's had been that he was the
rightly guided one and that the men descended from his companions carried his
authority. In one year the reigning caliph killed the men and cancelled the
argument, and put nothing in their place except being the man in Marrakesh with
a Castilian bodyguard.

The provinces drew the obvious conclusion. Ifriqiya declined to accept the
repudiation and kept the doctrine and the province together. Tlemcen's governor
simply stopped being one. In al-Andalus, Seville transferred its allegiance to
a rebel the moment al-Ma'mun's ship left, and Ibn al-Ahmar was already at
Arjona. Cordoba fell to Castile seven years later.

The empire did not end in 1229. It only stopped being able to say why anyone
should mind.
