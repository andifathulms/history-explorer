James II, deposed in England but still recognized as king by his
Catholic supporters in Ireland, drew up his army along the southern
bank of the river Boyne in the summer of 1690 to block his
son-in-law William's advance on Dublin. William surveyed the position
from the northern bank the day before the battle; while he sat resting
afterward, a Jacobite gun crew fired on his party, and a six-pound
shot struck him on the shoulder, tearing his coat and drawing blood.
He remarked, in Dutch, that it had come near enough, had the wound
dressed, and remained on horseback for three hours more. A garbled
report of his death reached Paris in time for public rejoicing there
before it was corrected.

## Fords found by men who knew the ground

The battle itself, fought on 1 July, turned on a flanking crossing
upriver at Slane, guided by Williamite officers from the Enniskillen
garrisons who knew every ford on the river. While that force drew off
part of the Jacobite line, the main Williamite army - the Blue Dutch
Guards in the lead, drums beating as they entered the water waist-deep
under fire - forced the crossing at Oldbridge directly. Jacobite
cavalry under the Duke of Berwick charged the Dutch guardsmen
repeatedly as they came ashore but could not break them, and by early
afternoon the Jacobite infantry was in full retreat.

## What one river crossing actually settled

James left the field for Dublin and then France, never to command an
army in Ireland again; the war itself dragged on for another year
around Limerick, ending only with that city's surrender the following
October. But the Boyne broke the one realistic chance Jacobite Ireland
had of reversing William's position by a single decisive stroke, and
the anniversary of a battle James did not even stay to finish would go
on being marked in Ireland for centuries after everyone who had
actually fought in it was dead.
