Tyrone held out for two more years after Kinsale, but with his
confederacy broken and no further help likely from Spain, he opened
negotiations for submission early in 1603. Elizabeth herself, in one of
her last acts of state, gave Mountjoy authority to grant surprisingly
generous terms - restoring nearly all of Tyrone's former lands rather
than punishing him further - on the reasoning that a repentant earl
with something to lose made a more reliable subject than a chieftain
with nothing left to protect.

## A submission timed by accident

Elizabeth died on 24 March 1603. Mountjoy learned of it on the 27th,
but his secretary managed to keep the news from becoming public in
Ireland until 5 April - and in the days between, unaware the queen he
was submitting to no longer lived, Tyrone made his way to Sir Garret
Moore's house at Mellifont and knelt at the door of Mountjoy's own
chamber. He remained kneeling, by an eyewitness's account, for the better
part of an hour, performing his penitent submission in stages as he
was called forward, renouncing the name O'Neill, all dependence on
Spain, and every claim over neighbouring lords' lands.

## Tears for the wrong reason, or the right one

Only after the written submission was complete did Tyrone travel on to
Dublin, arriving the day before official word of Elizabeth's death and
James's accession reached the city. When the new king was proclaimed
and the crowds around him shouted for joy, Tyrone, by every account
present, wept openly - grief the onlookers read as remorse for his own
rebellion, though he had submitted everything the war had been fought
over to a monarch who, unknown to him at the time, was no longer even
alive to receive it.
