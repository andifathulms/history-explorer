Gerald FitzGerald, fifteenth Earl of Desmond, had spent years as a
hunted fugitive by the time his rebellion finally collapsed in the
autumn of 1583. Most of his followers had already made their peace
with the crown's forces under the Earl of Ormonde, and Desmond himself
was reduced to hiding with a handful of companions in the woods of
Kerry, his last trusted adviser physically shackled to one of
Ormonde's own men so he could no longer be consulted in private.

## A raid on the wrong household

What finally exposed him was an act of ordinary desperation rather
than any grand betrayal. On 9 November, twenty of his remaining men
raided cattle from Maurice O'Moriarty near Tralee Bay, stripping his
wife and children in the process. The O'Moriartys, with leave from the
local English garrison, traced the stolen herd into the glen of
Glanageenty. Climbing the hillside by moonlight, Owen O'Moriarty spotted
a fire below and, at daybreak, led a party into the hut it came from.

## "Thou hast killed thyself long ago"

Desmond's own companion fled at the first alarm; the earl himself was
caught half-asleep and wounded in the arm before he could be
recognized. "I am the Earl of Desmond," he cried out, "save my life!"
Owen O'Moriarty's reply survives in the record almost exactly as
spoken: "Thou hast killed thyself long ago, and now thou shalt be
prisoner to the Queen's Majesty." A rescue seemed imminent as they
carried him off, so Owen ordered the fatal blow struck rather than
risk losing him - the earl's head was carried first to Ormonde, then
to Elizabeth herself, and displayed on London Bridge as proof that the
last great Geraldine rising was finally, verifiably over. His
forfeited estates across Munster were opened to English settlement in
the plantation that followed, reshaping the province's landholding
more thoroughly than the war itself had.
