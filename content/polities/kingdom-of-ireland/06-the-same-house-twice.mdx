Four years of uneasy peace followed Tyrone's submission - years in
which the earl found himself increasingly hemmed in by English
officials suspicious of his every move, his old authority over
neighbouring lords steadily whittled away by a government no longer
willing to leave any Gaelic lord more power than the letter of his own
patent strictly allowed. In September 1607, a French ship arrived off
the Donegal coast at Rathmullan, in Lough Swilly, sent for Tyrone and
Rory O'Donnell, Earl of Tyrconnell, by arrangement made weeks before.

## A last night at Mellifont

Before he sailed, Tyrone took formal leave of the Lord Justice in
Dublin and spent his final night on Irish soil at Sir Garret Moore's
house at Mellifont - the same house where he had knelt for nearly an
hour four years earlier, submitting to Mountjoy without knowing the
queen he submitted to was already dead. Whether the choice was
deliberate or simply practical, an eyewitness in his own party recorded
it without comment, one house serving as the setting for both the
end of his war and the end of his presence in Ireland altogether.

## Ninety-nine passengers, and an empty title left behind

The ship that carried them from Lough Swilly on 14 September held
some ninety-nine people in all - the two earls, their immediate
families, and a substantial following of kinsmen, retainers and
clergy. Rory O'Donnell left behind his wife, Brigid Fitzgerald, and
took his infant son with him instead; neither man would see Ireland
again. With their departure, no Gaelic lord of any real standing
remained on the island outside the crown's own patents and plantations
- the war Tyrone had fought to a draw at Yellow Ford and lost outright
at Kinsale ended, in effect, not on a battlefield but on the deck of a
ship none of the men aboard expected to be their last sight of home.
