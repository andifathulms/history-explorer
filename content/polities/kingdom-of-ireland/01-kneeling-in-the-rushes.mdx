The kingdom an Irish Parliament had voted into existence in 1541 was,
for its first decades, more a change of style than a change of
government. Henry VIII's own solution for making the new title real
was not conquest but a bargain: Gaelic chiefs would surrender their
lands to the crown and receive them back as English peerages, holding
by patent rather than by the sword or by Brehon custom.

## An earldom for O'Neill

Con O'Neill, head of the most powerful lordship in Ulster, was
persuaded to travel to the English court in 1542 to receive the
earldom the Irish government had chosen for him - Tyrone, since Ulster
itself was a royal title the King would not part with. His written
submission, signed with a mark since he could not read the English it
was drafted in, asked Henry's forgiveness for having "usurped" his own
name and state and begged to be given "what name, state, title, land,
or living it shall please your Highness." A week later he knelt in a
chamber at Greenwich hung with cloth of Arras and was girt as Earl of
Tyrone by the King's own hand, his chaplain translating his thanks
into English for the court. The Four Masters recorded the moment
plainly: O'Neill was created an earl, and enjoined that he should not
be called O'Neill any longer.

The same summer brought Murrough O'Brien to Court, created Earl of
Thomond, and Mac William of Clanricarde, created earl of that name -
each ceremony a repetition of O'Neill's, each chief trading an
Irish title with centuries of standing behind it for an English one
that existed only on paper. The bargain suited the crown far more than
it suited the families who took it: Con O'Neill's own patent, granting
the earldom in remainder to a son whose legitimacy his own kinsman
Shane would later deny outright, planted a succession dispute that
outlasted every man present at Greenwich that day.

## A plantation with a fort at its centre

Where persuasion failed, the government tried a harder instrument.
Sir Edward Bellingham's campaigns against the O'Mores and O'Connors of
Leix and Offaly in the late 1540s were followed, under Mary's own
deputy the Earl of Sussex, by a formal settlement of both territories
as shire ground - the first attempt anywhere in Ireland to replace a
Gaelic lordship wholesale with an English colony rather than merely
extract its submission. The western half of the new Queen's County
was reserved for the O'Mores themselves, each head of a sept made a
landlord holding by knight-service, forbidden unlicensed firearms on
pain of forfeiture and, on a second offense, death; the eastern
district went to English settlers bound to keep arms, speak English,
and hold no more than three hundred acres. Twelve fortified villages,
among them Stradbally and Abbeyleix, were built as satellites to the
royal fort renamed Maryborough after the Queen, with the fort at
Offaly renamed Philipstown after her husband. A church was ordered
built in each settlement within three years.

The old inhabitants never reconciled themselves to it. Donough
O'Connor and the O'Mores' own chief Connell fell into Sussex's hands
within the year and were released only at Kildare and Ormonde's
intercession; both broke their promises of good behaviour as soon as
they were free. The settlement held in outward form regardless,
Leix and Offaly renamed King's and Queen's County - the pattern of
plantation the crown would apply, with far greater violence, to
Munster after the Desmond rebellion's collapse a generation later.
