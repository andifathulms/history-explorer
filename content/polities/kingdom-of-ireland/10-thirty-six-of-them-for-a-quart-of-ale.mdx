The century after the Treaty of Limerick's broken promises settled
into a fixed shape: a Catholic majority excluded by the Penal Laws
from land, arms, and public life, governed by a Protestant Ascendancy
that was itself, on questions of trade and legislation, treated by
Westminster as barely more than a dependency.

## A trade strangled by statute

England's own woollen manufacturers had watched Irish wool exports
grow into a serious competitor, and in 1699, at Parliament's own
request, William III agreed to discourage the trade outright. The
result was industrial ruin: several thousand manufacturers left the
country for Germany, France and Spain, the western and southern
districts were said to be nearly depopulated, and by 1707 the Irish
government could not pay its own garrisons on a debt of less than
£100,000. Only smuggling wool to France, which the government proved
unable to stop, kept the collapse from being total. William Molyneux's
tract asserting Ireland's right to legislate for itself, provoked
chiefly by these restrictions, was formally condemned by the English
Commons and burned by the common hangman - a fate that stood, for a
generation, as a warning against pressing the argument further.

## A tradesman who was not a tradesman

Jonathan Swift, the dean of St Patrick's, took up Molyneux's own
argument in earnest once he had a grievance sharp enough to carry it.
In 1724 the crown, without consulting the Irish Parliament, Privy
Council or Lord Lieutenant, granted an Englishman named Wood a patent
to coin £108,000 in copper halfpence for Ireland - a job arranged
through the mistress of George I, who was to take a share of the
profits. Writing anonymously as a Dublin tradesman, Swift produced a
series of letters warning that the new coin would defraud whoever
touched it, calculating with mock precision that thirty-six of Wood's
halfpence would scarcely purchase a quart of twopenny ale. His famous
Fourth Letter went further, declaring outright allegiance to the King
"not as King of England, but as King of Ireland," and asserting that
"government without the consent of the governed is the very
definition of slavery." A reward of £300 for his identity went
unclaimed; a Dublin grand jury refused twice to indict his printer
despite the presiding judge's open hostility; both Houses of the
Irish Parliament petitioned against the patent; and Walpole's
government, unable to quell the agitation by any other means, was
forced to withdraw it.

## A grievance too large for any one pamphlet to settle

The halfpence themselves were a minor matter - Swift's own admirers
conceded they were probably sound coin - but the affair proved that a
sufficiently unified protest could still make Westminster yield, and
his open citation of Molyneux's banned doctrine kept the argument for
Irish legislative independence alive in print through the decades in
which the Ascendancy Parliament had neither the will nor, under
Poynings' Law, the legal standing to press it directly. The claim
Swift and Molyneux had both raised - that Ireland was a kingdom
entitled to make its own laws - would not be answered until an armed
Volunteer movement gave a later generation of the same Ascendancy the
leverage neither pamphleteer had possessed.
