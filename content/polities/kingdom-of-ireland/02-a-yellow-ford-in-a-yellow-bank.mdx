By 1598 Hugh O'Neill, Earl of Tyrone, had built the most formidable
Gaelic military confederacy Ireland had seen under English rule,
armies trained and drilled in something closer to continental fashion
than the loose warbands of earlier risings. When an English relief
column under the Marshal, Sir Henry Bagenal, marched to resupply the
besieged fort on the Blackwater that August, O'Neill and his ally
Hugh O'Donnell were waiting for them at a crossing the local people
called, for the colour of its bed and banks, the Yellow Ford.

## A column strung out over open ground

The Irish forces, outnumbering the relief column roughly two to one
and fighting in the loose, mobile formation their opponents' close
ranks could not match, harassed the English column from the moment it
crossed the ford. O'Donnell's marksmen used the juniper-covered hills
to pick off the ox teams hauling the army's heaviest gun, which stuck
fast in the mud; Bagenal himself, riding forward to rally his
vanguard, was shot through the brain. The confusion following his
death was made worse by an accidental explosion of gunpowder among
the English ranks.

## A rising the defeat set loose

The rear half of the army held formation well enough to retreat to
Armagh in reasonable order, but the losses - close to two thousand men,
by contemporary count - amounted to the heaviest single defeat English
arms had yet suffered in Ireland. News of the victory did what years
of O'Neill's own diplomacy had not: it drew risings across Munster,
Leinster and Connacht that had been simmering under the surface into
open rebellion almost overnight. What had been a war contained largely
to Ulster became, within weeks of the Yellow Ford, a rising that
threatened the crown's hold on the whole island at once.
