---
title: A night march that lost its way
drafted_from: bagwell-1885

A Spanish expeditionary force landed at Kinsale in the autumn of 1601,
hoping to give Tyrone's rebellion the foreign backing it had sought for
years. Lord Deputy Mountjoy moved quickly to besiege the town before
the Spanish could break out or be properly reinforced, and by December
his own army was itself caught between the garrison inside Kinsale and
the combined forces of Tyrone and O'Donnell converging from the north
- a position Mountjoy himself likened to Cromwell's later one at
Dunbar, holding the sea while the enemy held the land around him.

## Betrayed before the attack began

A coordinated assault was planned for the night of 24 December: the
Irish army would strike Mountjoy's camp while the Spanish garrison
sallied out to join them. The plan was betrayed in advance - one of
Tyrone's own officers let the English commander Carew learn the timing
over a shared bottle of whiskey - and Mountjoy had his men standing to
arms hours before the attack came. Worse for the Irish, their own
chiefs quarrelled over precedence in the march, strung their three
battalions out too far apart to support each other, and O'Donnell's
guides lost their way in the dark entirely.

## Routed at daybreak

Dawn found Tyrone's vanguard alone and exposed within sight of the
English lines, with O'Donnell's own division still wandering somewhere
behind. Mistaking a tactical withdrawal for a retreat, the Irish foot
broke and ran; the English cavalry, given leave to press the advantage,
rode down the fleeing army with almost no resistance. The Irish lost
something close to two thousand men; the English, by Mountjoy's own
count, lost one. Tyrone withdrew north in dejection, the Spanish
garrison surrendered soon after, and the confederate army that had
terrified the English administration for seven years broke apart into
its component clans, never to fight as one force again.
