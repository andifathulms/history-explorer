By September 1649 Oliver Cromwell had landed in Ireland with an army
better supplied and more heavily armed than anything the country had
seen, sent to avenge the massacres of Protestant settlers that had
opened the 1641 rebellion and to finish, once and for all, the
reconquest the Tudors had never quite completed. Drogheda, held for
the royalist cause by Sir Arthur Aston, stood between his army and
the north.

<Figure id="ki-cromwell" />

## A breach carried at the second attempt

Cromwell's siege guns, firing shot far heavier than the town's own
cannon could match, opened a practicable breach within days. The
first assault was thrown back, but Cromwell himself led a second wave
through the gap, and once his infantry gained the inner defences the
defenders were driven back into the Mill-mount, a fortified rise
inside the walls. There, by Cromwell's own account written days
later, he ordered his men to "put all to the sword," forbidding any
quarter to those found still in arms.

<Figure id="ki-laurence" />

## Two days of killing, and a justification offered in the same breath

The slaughter continued through the following day. Soldiers taken in
the two towers that held out longest were, in one case, decimated and
the survivors shipped to Barbados; in the other, spared outright but
transported the same way. Sir Arthur Aston himself was killed,
reportedly with his own wooden leg, in which rumour had wrongly
placed a hoard of gold. Cromwell's own estimate put the dead at close
to two thousand, and he offered, in the same letter that reported the
figure, both an apology and a defence at once: that the severity was
"a righteous judgment of God upon these barbarous wretches," and that
it would "tend to prevent the effusion of blood for the future." The
campaign that followed bore that logic out in its own way - garrison
after garrison surrendered rather than risk what Drogheda had shown
awaited those who did not.
