The government's first attempt to pass a legislative union with Great
Britain, in 1799, failed to carry the Irish House of Commons - a
genuine defeat, not a formality, that showed the independence Grattan
had won in 1782 could still function as more than a name. The
rebellion of the previous year had changed the political weather
enough, though, that a second attempt the following session found a
different reception, helped along by methods a member who sat through
the debates and voted against the measure took care to document in
detail.

## Peerages and cash, itemised

Jonah Barrington, an Irish MP who opposed the Union to the end,
recorded that patronage was distributed with a thoroughness that left
little to chance: offices, honours, and outright payment for
individual votes. By his own reckoning, and the commissioners' records
he had access to, something close to one and a half million pounds of
Irish money passed to members of both Houses of Parliament under the
formal heading of compensation for the loss of their seats and
patronage once Ireland's own Parliament ceased to exist - alongside a
fresh round of peerages for members whose support required a
different kind of reward. Barrington's own judgment was blunt: without
the bribes and the peerages together, a majority against the Union
would have stood.

## A parliament that ended itself

The Act passed. On 1 January 1801 the Kingdom of Ireland's own
Parliament, the body that had declared itself a free nation under
Grattan not two decades before, merged into a new Parliament of the
United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland at Westminster - not
conquered, not overthrown by any foreign army, but voted out of its
own existence by men many of whom had been paid, directly or
indirectly, to cast exactly that vote. The kingdom William Pitt had
promised would come with Catholic emancipation attached did not, in
the end, receive it for another generation; the union itself outlasted
every one of the men who negotiated it into being.
