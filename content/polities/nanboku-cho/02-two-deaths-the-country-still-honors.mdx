Kusunoki Masashige had made it the purpose of his life, from the age of
fifteen, to overthrow the Kamakura shogunate and restore the mikado to
direct power. He fought for Go-Daigo from 1330 onward, survived
repeated sieges by Hōjō forces, and helped drive Ashikaga's own troops
out of Kyoto three separate times once the rebellion began. When his
superiors ignored his advice to pursue and finish Ashikaga's retreating
army while the chance existed, the rebel forces regrouped, returned by
land and sea with an army several times the size of anything Kusunoki
commanded, and forced a battle at Minatogawa, near Hyōgo, that his own
side had no realistic chance of winning.

## An honorable end chosen deliberately

Defeated and with his own tactical counsel disregarded throughout the
campaign, Kusunoki judged that life was no longer honourable to
continue. He said a formal farewell to his wife and infant children,
handed his son Masatsura the sword the mikado himself had once given
him, charged him to follow the soldier's calling and avenge his
father's death, and calmly ended his own life. A hundred and fifty of
his companions, and sixteen of his own relatives, followed his example
rather than survive him.

## A single combat with a hundred men

Nitta Yoshisada's own death, two years later in 1338, matched Kusunoki's
for deliberate courage if not for the scale of the forces against him.
Ambushed in a narrow rice-field path near Fukui by roughly three
thousand of the enemy while marching with only about fifty men of his
own, he refused every suggestion that he flee on horseback. He charged
the ambush with his sword, took an arrow between the eyes, and, rather
than let his enemies claim his head, cut it off himself before he died
- a feat warriors of the age deliberately trained themselves to
perform. His body went unidentified until searchers found, sewn into a
bag beneath a heap of his own men's self-inflicted dead, a letter in
Go-Daigo's own hand naming him commander against the rebels.

## Two names that outlasted every emperor either one served

Neither Kusunoki nor Nitta lived to see the war's outcome, and neither
is remembered chiefly, in later Japanese memory, for which side
ultimately prevailed. A nineteenth-century American teacher living in
Japan, asking his own students and friends which figure in the whole of
their history they considered noblest, found them answer, unanimously,
with Kusunoki's name alone - a devotion to duty and calmness under
certain death that outlived,
in popular memory, the imperial cause both men actually died fighting
for.
