When Ashikaga Takauji installed Kōgen as a rival emperor in Kyoto in
1336, Go-Daigo refused outright to accept that anyone but himself held
the throne. He left the capital carrying the sacred imperial regalia -
the physical objects Japanese political tradition held to be
inseparable from legitimate sovereignty itself - and continued to reign
as what he, and generations of Japanese historians after him, would
insist was the only real emperor in the country.

## A dispute settled, in theory, by two men alone

Nitta Yoshisada, still fighting for Go-Daigo's cause, once tried to end
the whole war with a single gesture. Encamped opposite Ashikaga's own
army, he rode out alone and proposed that the conflict - though it had
arisen from the rivalry of two emperors - be settled by personal combat
between himself and Ashikaga rather than by the deaths of the countless
soldiers caught up in it. Ashikaga's own retainers talked him out of
accepting the challenge, and the war continued exactly as it had been
fighting: an argument over legitimacy conducted at the scale of a
national civil war rather than a duel.

## A court whose whole claim was to be the true one

What made Go-Daigo's own court different from a mere rebel faction, in
the eyes of everyone who continued to fight for it, was precisely its
claim to legitimacy rather than to territory. It held the regalia.
It held the true line of descent, by its own reckoning, from the
sun-goddess herself. It held, in the person of scholars like Kitabatake
Chikafusa, an articulate case for why the throne in Kyoto was a puppet
and its own exiled court the only rightful government Japan actually
had. Whether that claim could survive contact with fifty-six years of
war fought largely by men who had stopped caring which emperor they
were technically dying for was a separate question entirely.
