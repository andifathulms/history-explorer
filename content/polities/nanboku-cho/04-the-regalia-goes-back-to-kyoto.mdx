By 1392 an Ashikaga envoy judged the moment right to end a war that had
already outlived every original participant's stake in it. He persuaded
Go-Kameyama, the reigning Southern emperor, to travel to Kyoto in
person and hand the sacred regalia directly to Go-Komatsu, the Northern
emperor the Ashikaga had backed from the schism's very beginning.

## Terms that promised more than they delivered

The settlement gave Go-Kameyama the honorific title of ex-emperor and
formally declared Go-Komatsu the sole reigning sovereign, with a
further promise that the throne would thereafter alternate between the
two imperial branches in turn. The ceremony itself - abdication and
surrender of the regalia on one side, investiture on the other - was
conducted with full solemnity in one of Kyoto's great temples, and the
fifty-six-year war ended in that single formal exchange.

## Whose victory it actually was

The alternation promised at the settlement never in fact took place;
the Northern line, and the Ashikaga shogunate that had sponsored it
throughout, retained the throne going forward. The plainest verdict on
the whole affair is blunt: the outcome redounded to the glory and power
of the Ashikaga rather than to either imperial branch, a fitting result
for a war the shogunate had done more than anyone to prolong and had
now ended on terms suited entirely to its own advantage.

## A verdict later generations would still argue over

Japanese historians would go on debating for centuries which line had
actually held legitimate authority throughout - the Dai Nihon Shi itself
defended the Southern emperors as the true descendants of the
sun-goddess and dismissed their Northern rivals as usurpers, a judgment
modern Japan came to accept as authoritative even though it was the
Northern line, propped up by Ashikaga arms from the beginning, that
held the actual throne when the fighting finally stopped. The Southern
court's own claim outlived it in the history books far more
comfortably than it had ever managed to on the battlefield.
