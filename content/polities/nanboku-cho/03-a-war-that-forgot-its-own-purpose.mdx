---
title: A war that forgot its own purpose
drafted_from: griffis-1883

The conflict began with a clearly defined stake: which of two claimants
was Japan's rightful emperor. By the time Go-Murakami had succeeded his
father as the Southern line's sovereign and reigned across nearly three
decades of continued fighting, that original question had begun to
matter less and less to the men actually doing the fighting.

## A national free-for-all wearing an old label

What had opened as a war of imperial legitimacy gradually degenerated
into a general melee fought on a national scale for reasons that had
little to do with either emperor. Local strongmen used the cover of
northern or southern allegiance to
seize land from their neighbours, settle old grudges, and expand their
own holdings, regardless of which court's cause they nominally claimed
to serve. Even the rival emperors themselves, by the war's later
decades, took diminishing interest in conflicts that had become,
functionally, private property disputes conducted under an imperial
banner.

## Two lines still standing, and a country largely indifferent to why

By the time Go-Kameyama inherited the Southern claim, most of the
figures who had actually cared about the dynastic question at the
war's outset - Kusunoki, Nitta, Go-Daigo himself - were long dead, and
the war persisted largely from inertia and the accumulated territorial
stakes of two generations of local fighting rather than from any
remaining conviction that the outcome would settle a real
constitutional question. A conflict that began over who held the
sacred regalia had become, in practice, a war almost nobody remembered
starting for that reason.
