---
title: Twenty-three years before the end
drafted_from: hovannisian-1997
phase: aside

Around 405, Mesrop Mashtots devised an alphabet for Armenian, under the
patronage of the king Vramshapuh and the catholicos Sahak. Within about a
generation the Bible had been translated into it, along with a large body of
Greek and Syriac theology, and Armenians had begun writing history in their own
language — Koriwn's life of Mashtots, Agathangelos, Faustus, Movses Khorenatsi,
Eghishe on the revolt of 451.

The Armenian fifth century is called the golden age for that reason, and the
chronology is the thing this chapter is for. The alphabet is made in about 405.
The monarchy is abolished in 428. The literature that has carried Armenian
identity for sixteen hundred years was created in the last twenty-three years of
the kingdom that paid for it, and most of it was written after the kingdom had
gone.

This corpus keeps finding that political weight and cultural output come apart —
it has now said so for the Artuqids, the Marwanids, Trebizond, the Kara
Koyunlu, the Muzaffarids, the Hujrids and Bagratid Ani. This is the extreme
case, and it runs in the direction that matters: the state ended and the
alphabet did not, and every subsequent Armenian polity in this corpus, in
Cilicia and at Ani, wrote in it.

The site's `descendant_scripts` field on this record is null, and the reason is
a genuine dispute rather than an absence. The Armenian tradition credits
Mashtots with the Georgian and Caucasian Albanian alphabets as well; the
Georgian tradition credits its own script to a much earlier king; modern
scholarship is divided and the Albanian case is the best supported of the three.
Coding rule 2 excludes adaptations a source assigns elsewhere, and here the
sources assign them in different directions. Null is the honest reading, and it
is worth noticing that this is the one place in the corpus where three national
traditions disagree about who invented what.
