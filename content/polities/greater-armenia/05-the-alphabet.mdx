title: Twenty-three years before the end
drafted_from: hovannisian-1997

Around 405, Mesrop Mashtots devised an alphabet for Armenian, under the
patronage of the king Vramshapuh and the catholicos Sahak. Within about a
generation the Bible had been translated into it, along with a large body of
Greek and Syriac theology, and Armenians had begun writing history in their own
language — Koriwn's life of Mashtots, Agathangelos, Faustus, Movses Khorenatsi,
Eghishe on the revolt of 451.

The Armenian fifth century is called the golden age for that reason, and the
chronology is the thing this chapter is for. The alphabet is made in about 405.
The monarchy is abolished in 428. The literature that has carried Armenian
identity for sixteen hundred years was created in the last twenty-three years of
the kingdom that paid for it, and most of it was written after the kingdom had
gone.

Political weight and cultural output come apart all the time, and this is the
extreme version of it, running in the direction that matters. The state ended
and the alphabet did not. Every Armenian polity afterwards — at Ani, in Cilicia
— wrote in it, and so does everybody in Yerevan this morning.

There is a dispute about how much else Mashtots made, and it is unusually
tangled. The Armenian tradition credits him with the Georgian and the Caucasian
Albanian alphabets as well. The Georgian tradition credits its own script to a
king centuries earlier. Modern scholarship is divided, and of the two claims the
Albanian one has the better support.

Three national traditions, three alphabets, and no agreement about who invented
which. It is not a question that can be settled from the outside, and the
temptation to settle it is exactly what makes it worth leaving open.
