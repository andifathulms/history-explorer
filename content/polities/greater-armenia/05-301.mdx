Armenia adopted Christianity as the religion of the state under Tiridates III,
converted according to the tradition by Gregory the Illuminator, who had been
imprisoned in a pit for thirteen years first. The conventional date is 301, and
a good deal of modern scholarship prefers something around 314; the difference
matters to the claim of being first only if one is arguing with Rome about it,
which people have been.

<Figure id="khor-virap" />

What is not disputed is the priority in substance. Armenia is the earliest state
adoption of Christianity anywhere, and Iberia followed about a generation later
and Caucasian Albania about the same time — three kingdoms along the Sasanian
empire's northern flank, all Christian within four decades of each other.

<Figure id="etchmiadzin" />

The strategic reading is the obvious one and it is not the whole of it. Christian
Armenia was permanently unreliable to a Sasanian empire that was in the same
decades consolidating Zoroastrianism as a state religion with a state priesthood,
and permanently intelligible to Rome. It aligned the country and it did so at a
cost the Armenians paid for the following three centuries.

The cost is on the chapters that follow, and it is worth naming the shape now. Every
subsequent Sasanian attempt to secure Armenia included pressure on the church:
the abolition of the monarchy in 428, the attempt to impose Zoroastrianism that
produced the revolt of 451, the periodic persecutions. And every subsequent
Byzantine attempt included pressure of a different kind, because the Armenian
church rejected the Council of Chalcedon in the sixth century and was thereafter
heretical in Constantinople's eyes.

A church that made the country legible to one empire made it a target for both.
Four centuries later the Bagratuni kings would draw the obvious conclusion and
take their crown from the caliph in Baghdad instead, on the reasoning that a
Muslim overlord had no opinion about Chalcedon.
