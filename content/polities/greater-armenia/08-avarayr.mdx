---
title: Twenty-three years after the kingdom
drafted_from: elishe-neumann
phase: afterlife
---

The Arsacid kingdom of Armenia was abolished in 428. In 450 the Sasanian court
ordered the country to give up Christianity for Zoroastrianism, and discovered
that abolishing the monarchy had not removed the thing that could refuse.

What refused was the *naxarars*, the noble houses, with the church behind them.
Elishe, who wrote the account within living memory and presents himself as
having been there, states their reasoning as a formula that has been quoted in
Armenian ever since: apostasy they look on as death, and death for God as an
immortal life; slavery on earth they esteem the liberty of their lives, and to be
cast into banishment they call being in secret with God.

Vardan Mamikonian, who commanded, knew perfectly well that the country was
divided — Elishe says the disbelief of the timorous had not escaped him, and
that he knew the undecided spirit of even the men attached to him, and encouraged
the army anyway. The prince of Siwnik had gone over to the Persians with a body
of troops. Vardan replaced the defectors with his own brothers, sons and nephews
and sent each of them out with a force, and summoned everyone else to Artashat.
Elishe then lists the houses that came, name by name — Artsruni, Khorkhoruni,
Palunik, Amatuni, Gnuni, Dimaksian, Arsharuni — which is the point of listing
them: there was no king to command any of them and they came.

His speech before the battle survives in the account. Do not fear the numbers of
the heathen; do not withdraw your necks from the sword of a mortal man; if the
object of our lives is completed by a holy death in battle, let us meet it with a
joyful heart. And earlier in it, the harder sentence, which is about what they
had all been doing for the previous year: *you remember how we outwitted the
tyrant, how we treated him as a simple child, how we accommodated ourselves to
his impious will by seeming apostasy, though in secret we were true to God.*

They met on the plain of Avarayr on 2 June 451. Vardan saw a chosen body of
Persians fall on the Armenian left, attacked the Persian right, scattered it
along with the elephants and drove them back to where they had started, and for
a moment the Persian main body broke and ran from its own camp. The Persian
commander rallied them, ordered the officer riding one of the elephants — which
Elishe says served as a watchtower or a fortified city — to sound the trumpet,
and went into the front rank himself. Vardan was killed on the spot. The fighting
went on all day; the bodies were heaped like fallen cedars and like walls of
stone, and the spears and bows were so broken and the dead of both sides so
mixed that the bodies of the blessed could not be told apart. It was spring, and
the flowering meadows were destroyed by the crowd. Elishe writes that there was
no distinction between victors and vanquished — wherever the brave of either side
met, there were victims.

Then he counts. Of the Mamikonian house, Vardan and a hundred and thirty-three;
of the Khorkhoruni, Khoren and nineteen; of the Palunik, Artak and fifty-seven;
and so through nine houses, two hundred and eighty-seven men with their princes,
and seven hundred and forty others, and one thousand and thirty-six in all. On
the other side, three thousand five hundred and forty-four, including nine men of
distinction — and the Persian commander, counting his own dead and finding the
proportion, was troubled, because the affair had not fallen out as he expected.

Armenia lost the battle. The survivors dispersed into the hills and the castles
they held, and Elishe notes that the number who got away was greater than the
number who fell, and that they went on ruling every district that could not be
taken. The Sasanian court did not raise the religious question again on those
terms, and thirty-odd years later conceded it in writing.

That is the reason a chapter about a battle belongs after the end of a kingdom
rather than inside it. The Armenian state had been gone for twenty-three years
when this happened, and it turned out that the thing which had made Armenia
Armenian was not the state — it was an aristocracy that could still assemble
itself, and a church, and the alphabet that had been cut for it a generation
earlier. Those three survived every subsequent conquest, and 2 June is still
kept.
