For the century after Pompey, Armenia was the standing quarrel between Rome and
Parthia: each installed kings, each removed the other's, and the fighting was
periodic and inconclusive. In AD 63 they settled it at Rhandeia.

The king of Armenia would be a prince of the Arsacid house — that is, a Parthian
royal — nominated at Ctesiphon. He would receive his crown from Rome. Tiridates
travelled to Italy to be crowned by Nero in person, in a ceremony Roman writers
describe at length and evidently enjoyed.

<Figure id="garni-inscription" />

It worked. There was no serious Roman-Parthian war over Armenia for fifty years,
and the arrangement in modified forms governed the kingdom's constitutional
position for three centuries.

<Figure id="garni-temple" />

It is worth being clear about how strange the arrangement is, because it is easy
to read past.

Thrones are normally filled by a rule internal to the country: eldest son,
senior kinsman, division among heirs, nomination by the incumbent, election,
acclamation, or whoever can raise the most men. Armenia's was filled by a treaty
between two foreign empires, which specified both the family the king had to
come from and which capital would choose him and which would crown him.

Client kingship is common enough; a great many small states have had a king
their neighbour approved. What is unusual here is the formality — two great
powers, in writing, agreeing in advance the procedure for filling a third
country's throne, and then keeping to it for three hundred years. Neither of
them thought this odd. Armenia was not a party.

The Armenian nobility went on being Armenian throughout, which is the other half
of the story and the reason the arrangement was survivable. A king nominated in
Ctesiphon and crowned in Rome still had to govern through nakharar houses that
had been there before either empire and would be there after.
