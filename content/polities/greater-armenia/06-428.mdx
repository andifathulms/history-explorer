title: The crown removed and the houses left

Rome and Persia partitioned Armenia by agreement in 387, taking roughly a fifth
and four-fifths respectively, and left an Arsacid king on the Persian side. In
428 the Sasanians abolished that kingship at the request — the sources are
consistent on this — of some of the Armenian nobles themselves, who preferred a
Persian governor to a king of their own who might tax them.

Calling that a conquest is accurate about the agent and misleading about the
mechanism. Nobody stormed Armenia in 428. A foreign empire removed a native
monarchy because a proportion of the native aristocracy asked it to.

What happened next is the whole argument of the Caucasus. The crown was removed
and the nakharar houses were not. They went
on holding their districts, their fortresses and their cavalry, and when the
Sasanians attempted in 449 to impose Zoroastrianism, the houses raised an army
under Vardan Mamikonian and fought at Avarayr in 451. They lost the battle
comprehensively and Vardan was killed. Thirty-three years later, at Nvarsak,
the Sasanians conceded religious freedom and the restoration of the nakharars'
privileges, because the country had proved ungovernable on the other terms.

Armenian tradition treats Avarayr as a victory and the reasoning is sound: the
objective was the church and the church survived.

The country then spent four and a half centuries without a crown — Sasanian,
then Byzantine, then from the 640s under the caliphate, governed throughout by
the same houses under a resident governor and an Armenian prince. In 884 one of
those houses, the Bagratuni, took a crown from Baghdad.

Four hundred and fifty-six years separate the two Armenian kingdoms, and it is
worth being exact about what crossed the gap and what did not.

What crossed: the church, the alphabet, the literature written in it, and the
noble houses in their own valleys with their own fortresses and their own
cavalry. What did not: the state. There was no Armenian kingdom for four and a
half centuries, and there was an Armenia throughout, which is only a paradox if
you assume the two words mean the same thing.
