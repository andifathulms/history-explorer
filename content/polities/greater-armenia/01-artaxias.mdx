Antiochus III lost to Rome at Magnesia in 190 BC, and the Seleucid empire's
authority in its northern provinces went with the battle. Artaxias, the
*strategos* Antiochus had appointed over Armenia, declared himself king.

<Figure id="boundary-stone" />

A deputy left in charge by a distracted overlord is the commonest way a state
begins anywhere — the Tulunids in Egypt, the Rasulids in Yemen, Toghtekin at
Damascus, a governor at Sohar, a hundred others across the following two
thousand years. Artaxias is among the earliest examples anyone can name.

Artaxias founded Artaxata on the Araxes — Plutarch says Hannibal, in exile at
his court, advised on the site — and his dynasty held the Armenian highland for
about a hundred and eighty years. What they governed was not a state in the
Seleucid or Roman sense. Armenia was a country of hereditary noble houses, the
*nakharars*, each holding its own districts with its own fortresses and its own
cavalry, and a king was the senior among them rather than a sovereign above
them.

<Figure id="artaxata" />

That structure is the key to the whole region and Toumanoff's argument is worth
stating plainly: the three Caucasian countries had one social system and three
crowns. The crowns were removed by the Sasanians within a hundred and fifty
years of each other. The system was not, and could not be, because it was not
attached to the crowns. That is why all three countries reappear afterwards as
principalities rather than dissolving into provinces.

It is also why the Bagratuni, who take the Armenian crown in 884, are not a new
dynasty arriving from outside. They are one of these houses, attested here in
the second century BC, waiting four and a half centuries.
