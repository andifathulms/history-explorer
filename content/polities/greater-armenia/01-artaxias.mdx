---
title: A governor who kept the province
drafted_from: hovannisian-1997

Antiochus III lost to Rome at Magnesia in 190 BC, and the Seleucid empire's
authority in its northern provinces went with the battle. Artaxias, the
*strategos* Antiochus had appointed over Armenia, declared himself king.

This corpus has recorded that opening about fifteen times — the Tulunids in
Egypt, the Rasulids in Yemen, the Muzaffarids in Fars, Toghtekin at Damascus,
Umar ibn Rasul, the Ya'ariba governor of Sohar. A deputy left in charge by a
distracted overlord is the single most common way a polity begins on this site,
and this is the oldest instance of it in the corpus by a thousand years.

Artaxias founded Artaxata on the Araxes — Plutarch says Hannibal, in exile at
his court, advised on the site — and his dynasty held the Armenian highland for
about a hundred and eighty years. What they governed was not a state in the
Seleucid or Roman sense. Armenia was a country of hereditary noble houses, the
*nakharars*, each holding its own districts with its own fortresses and its own
cavalry, and a king was the senior among them rather than a sovereign above
them.

That structure is what this site codes `client-levy` for, and it is the same
coding this corpus now carries on four Caucasian records — Greater Armenia,
Iberia, Caucasian Albania and Bagratid Armenia — from one source. Toumanoff's
argument is that the three Caucasian countries had one social system and three
crowns; the crowns were removed by the Sasanians within a hundred and fifty
years of each other and the system was not, which is why all three reappear as
principalities rather than as provinces.

It is also why the Bagratuni, who take the Armenian crown in 884 on the record
six entries over, are not a new dynasty from outside. They are one of these
houses, attested here, waiting four and a half centuries.
