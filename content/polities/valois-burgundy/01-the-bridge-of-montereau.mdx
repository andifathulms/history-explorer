John the Fearless had spent twelve years as the most powerful, and
most hated, man in France, his own rivalry with the House of Orleans
having already produced one assassination - his own hand behind the
murder of the king's brother in 1407. By 1419, weary of the civil war
that had followed and genuinely anxious for peace, he agreed to meet
the dauphin in person on the bridge at Montereau, to settle at last
the terms neither side had honored. "I desire peace at any sacrifice,"
he told the advisers who warned him against going.

He kept the appointment. A wooden barrier had been built across the
bridge; John passed through it with ten unarmed attendants, knelt
before the dauphin, and had begun a courteous exchange when the gate
behind him was quietly reopened. Armed men rushed from concealment
near the river. Tanneguy Duchatel, the plot's chief author, struck
the duke from behind with a hatchet; others followed with swords
before he fell, and a final man knelt to drive a sword the rest of
the way through a body that still showed signs of life. "The
vengeance that had waited twelve years for its opportunity was
satisfied."

## Your brother has assassinated my father

Philip, John's son and heir, was at Ghent when the news reached him.
"Michelle," he told his wife, the French king's own daughter, "your
brother has assassinated my father." He summoned his friends and the
Estates of his provinces to decide his course, and the choice before
him was neither difficult nor slow: an alliance with England against
the French crown whose own heir had just arranged his father's
murder. That alliance - and the English king's own claim, pressed
with Burgundian support, to the throne of France itself - would
define the whole of Philip's reign and the character of the state he
built out of his father's revenge.
