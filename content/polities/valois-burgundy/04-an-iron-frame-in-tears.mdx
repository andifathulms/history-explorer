Philip's final years were spent, by his own choice, at a remove from
the harder business of ruling. Content with the punishment his forces
had already inflicted on the rebellious town of Dinant, he left the
subsequent campaign against Liege to his son and heir Charles, count
of Charolais, and withdrew to a life of quiet religious observance -
the duke who had built the wealthiest court in Europe ending his days
in contemplation rather than in state. In June 1467, while staying at
Hinges, he was struck by a sudden, fatal illness.

Charles received the news at Ghent and left immediately, riding so
hard that he outstripped his own escort and arrived at Bruges alone.
What followed startled everyone who witnessed it. Charles's own
reputation was for a coldness and severity that spared no one, friend
or dependent; at his father's bedside he wept without restraint, a
violence of grief that seemed to those present entirely out of
character for a man of such deliberate and unyielding temperament.
It was not performance. Those who knew him best recognized that the
tears were as genuine as the harshness they were used to seeing from
him instead.

## A different kind of duke

Philip's death made Charles duke of Burgundy in fact as well as in
waiting, and the state he inherited was, on paper, stronger than it
had ever been - richer, wider, and more feared than at any point in
his father's own reign. But the qualities that had built it were not
the qualities Charles brought to ruling it. Where Philip had preferred
negotiation, marriage and purchase to force, Charles preferred force
outright, and the wars he chose to fight in the years that followed
would test the strength his father had spent a lifetime accumulating
against enemies his father had always found some way to avoid.
