The state Philip the Good spent his reign assembling had no single
name that matched its reach. Duke of Burgundy in the kingdom of
France, he was also count of Flanders, Artois and Hainaut, duke of
Brabant and Limburg, and lord of a widening arc of Low Countries
territory acquired by marriage, purchase and inheritance rather than
conquest - lands under the nominal authority of two different crowns,
French and imperial, answering in practice to neither. No other
prince in Europe, sovereign or vassal, commanded a court as wealthy
or as widely admired.

That wealth had a use beyond magnificence for its own sake. Twice
widowed and without a legitimate heir, Philip sent an embassy to
Portugal in 1428 to negotiate marriage with Princess Isabella,
sending with it the painter Jan van Eyck to bring back her likeness
before he committed himself to the match. She landed at Sluys in
November 1429 to a welcome so large the road to her lodgings had to
be cleared by force, and on the 10th of January, 1430, the day of
their wedding, Philip founded a new order of chivalry in her honor:
the Order of the Golden Fleece.

## Founded by a vassal, not a sovereign

No other institution of its kind in Europe was created by a prince
who was not himself a crowned king, and none outshone it in the
prestige it came to carry. The chroniclers who served Philip's court
- Chastellain foremost among them - wrote of it and of the duke's
own magnificence in language meant to rival the courts of kings
outright, and largely succeeded: the Golden Fleece was sought after
by sovereign princes who held no fief of Burgundy at all. It was, in
its way, the clearest possible statement of what Philip had built - a
vassal state whose own duke could found an order of chivalry that a
king might envy, and could not.
