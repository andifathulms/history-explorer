Philip's alliance with England had never been unconditional. In
1434 he warned that if Henry VI refused the peace terms offered by
Charles VII, he would abandon the English cause entirely - his price
named in advance as the towns of Amiens, Ponthieu and a scattering
of other places in the north. The test came the following year, at
a congress so large that all Europe watched it: on the 5th of
August 1435, two cardinals presided over an assembly at Arras that
brought together the English, the French, the Duke of Burgundy,
ambassadors of the Emperor, and delegates from across the continent,
all hoping to end what was already, by then, a war of a century's
standing.

## A defection sold dearly

The French offered to cede Normandy and Aquitaine as English fiefs
if the English king would give up his claim to the French crown
outright; England refused, and the congress broke apart. It was
then that Philip made good on his warning. Bound as he still was by
solemn oaths to England, he hesitated - but on the 21st of September
1435 he signed the Treaty of Arras and went over to the French side.
He did not come cheaply: in exchange for recognizing Charles VII as
king, he secured Auxerre, Macon, Peronne, Montdidier and the towns
of the Somme for himself, along with a release, for his own lifetime
only, from the feudal homage a duke of Burgundy owed the French
crown. Days after the news reached him, the English regent in
France, the Duke of Bedford - by common judgment the one man capable
of holding the English cause together - died at Rouen. Paris, tired
of English occupation, opened its gates to the French crown the
following spring.

## Ghent broken at Gavere

Philip's reign was not spent only in diplomacy. The great cloth
cities of Flanders had never fully accepted Burgundian rule, and in
1452 their leading city, Ghent, rose against him outright. Philip
crushed the revolt on the bloody field of Gavere, a defeat so
complete that it marked the final subjection of the powerful and
turbulent cities of Flanders to Burgundian authority - even as those
same cities' own wealth and sophistication were quietly preparing
them for the different role they would play in Europe a century
afterward.
