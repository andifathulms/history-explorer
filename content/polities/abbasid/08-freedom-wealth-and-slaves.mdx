East of Basra the ground was flats and ditches, and on them rich Basran
contractors worked gangs of black slaves, most of them brought from the East
African coast — the land of the Zanj. The job was to strip off the nitrous
surface soil, both to reach the fertile ground beneath and to recover the
saltpetre in the crust.

Nöldeke's observation about the arrangement is the beginning of everything that
followed. Industrial labour on that scale, in the open country, was rare in the
Islamic world; the work was very hard and the supervision had to be strict; and
the attachment that elsewhere bound a household slave to the family he had grown
up in was entirely absent. What forms instead, among masses of people working
together, is a common sense of grievance and eventually a sense of their own
strength. He compares it to the servile wars of the late Roman republic, and the
comparison is exact.

## What he actually promised

In September 869 a man calling himself Ali ibn Muhammad came into the saltpetre
district under cover of acting as a business agent, and began working on the
gangs. He claimed descent from the Prophet's son-in-law; whether the claim was
true was disputed at the time and cannot be settled now.

What he offered them is the part that gets left out.

He told the slaves how badly they were being treated, and promised that if they
joined him they would have freedom, wealth — *and slaves*. Nöldeke states the
consequence plainly: he did not preach universal equality, but reserved
supremacy for the particular class he was addressing.

The theology was chosen to fit. Being an Alid, he might have been expected to
argue a hereditary right and found a Shi'i movement. Instead he adopted the
doctrine of the Kharijites, the Alids' bitterest opponents, who held that the
office should go to the best man *though he were an Abyssinian slave*. To men in
a ditch, a claim about somebody's grandfather was worth nothing and that
sentence was worth everything. He put a Kharijite verse on his banner and signed
himself on it simply *Ali, son of Muhammad*, with no allusion to the descent he
claimed elsewhere.

## Basra

The rising took southern Iraq for fifteen years. The rebels built a capital
below Basra on a salt flat where nothing grew, called al-Mukhtara, the elect
city — palm-branch and mud huts, sun-dried brick palaces, prisons for the
captives, mosques — on ground so changed by the shifting waters that its site
will probably never be found.

In 871 they took Basra itself, the second commercial city of the empire. The
chief had vowed vengeance on it. His general Ali ibn Aban let a delegation
through to plead for quarter and was replaced for it by a less soft-hearted man.
Inhabitants were reportedly drawn together at appointed places by offers of
safety so that they could be killed more conveniently. The lowest figure the
chroniclers give for the dead is three hundred thousand — a number of the kind
that period produces and not one anybody counted, but nothing in the tradition
suggests a small massacre.

The women and children were taken as slaves. Women of the houses of Ali and of
the reigning Abbasid family were sold to the highest bidder. And, Nöldeke
reports, many of the Zanj received ten slaves or more as their share.

The promise had been freedom, wealth and slaves, and at Basra it was kept in
those three terms exactly.

## Fifteen years

The caliphate could not finish it quickly. Army after army was beaten in night
attacks in country nobody from Baghdad could read: tidal channels, broken
embankments, marsh that had been arable within living memory, and canals around
Basra which one source claims numbered more than a hundred and twenty thousand.

What ended it was al-Muwaffaq, the caliph's brother, who ran the war as a siege:
a new city built opposite the rebel capital, the waterways closed, and the
supplies that traders and bedouin had been carrying in within sight of the army
finally cut. Al-Mukhtara was stormed in 883. How the chief died is unknown —
Nöldeke inclines to suicide, partly because no soldier ever claimed the reward
for killing him. The head reached Baghdad on a pole in November 883.

Thousands of the Zanj surrendered and were pardoned and taken into the caliph's
service, because it would have been senseless to drive them to desperation when
they could be used as soldiers. Others died of thirst in the desert or were
enslaved again by bedouin. A contemporary source describes the ones who entered
imperial service as barbarians who spoke no Arabic and ate carrion, which is
what the winners thought of the men they had just recruited.

## What it did to the caliphate

Fifteen years, the second city of Iraq sacked, the Gulf trade routes closed, the
sugar and saltpetre economy of the south wrecked, and the campaign in the end
conducted and won not by the caliph but by his brother.

The pattern is the one that runs through the whole of this stretch. Authority
worked when a capable man in the family took it and spent everything he had on
it; the office itself supplied legitimacy and very little else. Al-Muwaffaq
saved Iraq for his brother and never became caliph. Within a generation the men
running the empire on the caliph's behalf were doing it without the family
connection, and within two the arrangement had a name.
