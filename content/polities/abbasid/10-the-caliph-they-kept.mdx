In December 945 Ahmad ibn Buya, a Daylami soldier from the Caspian highlands,
entered Baghdad and took the city. He deposed the caliph al-Mustakfi the
following year and had him blinded. He did not abolish the caliphate. He took
the title *amir al-umara*, installed the next Abbasid in the palace on a
pension, and ruled Iraq and western Iran for the rest of his life with a
powerless Sunni caliph in his keeping.

The Buyids were Shi'i. They had every doctrinal reason to end the Abbasid line
and no military reason not to. That they kept it for a hundred and ten years is
the fact that explains what the office had by then become. The caliph's grant
was the currency in which authority was quoted across the whole east — the
Samanids in Bukhara, the Ghaznavids after them, and every governor who wanted
his seizure to read as an appointment. A Buyid amir who destroyed the
institution would have destroyed the thing that made his own rule legible to
everyone outside Iraq, and put nothing in its place.

So the office survived precisely because it had been emptied. It was worth more
as a stamp than as a rival, and a stamp is cheap to keep.

Whether the Buyids ever seriously weighed replacing the Abbasids with an Alid
caliph of their own is contested; the sources that report the deliberation are
later and hostile, and there is no settling it. What is not contested is the
outcome. For over a century the most powerful men in Iraq governed in the name
of a prisoner whose signature they needed and whose authority they had taken.
