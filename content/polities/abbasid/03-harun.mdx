Harun al-Rashid's reign (786–809) is the one everybody has heard of, and the
reputation is a problem for reading it: the *Thousand and One Nights* made him
a figure of legend three centuries later, and the legend has been read
backwards into the reign ever since.

What the reign was, in institutional terms, is the point at which the Abbasid
state was wealthy and coherent enough to be administered by professionals — the
Barmakid viziers, until Harun destroyed them in 803 for reasons the sources
report differently and none of them convincingly.
It is also the point at which
the caliphate was already delegating: Ifriqiya was handed to the Aghlabids as a
hereditary governorship in 800, in exchange for tribute and the khutba. The
empire was being converted into a system of clients while it was still at its
height.

<Figure id="harun-dinar" />

Harun's succession arrangement is what broke it. He divided the inheritance
between two sons — al-Amin in Iraq, al-Ma'mun in Khurasan — with terms
deposited at the Ka'ba, and the arrangement lasted until he died. The fourth
fitna that followed was not a frontier revolt or a sectarian rising. It was the
empire's two halves fighting each other with their own armies, and it ended in
813 with Baghdad besieged and sacked by Khurasani troops, and al-Amin killed.

Al-Ma'mun won, and won as a Khurasani candidate. He stayed in Merv for six
years before returning to a capital that had learned its garrison could be
beaten by the provinces. Every subsequent Abbasid attempt to solve the army
problem starts from that fact.
