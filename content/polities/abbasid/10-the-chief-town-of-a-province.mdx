The siege began in the middle of Muharram 656 — January 1258. Hulagu commanded
the centre in person and camped east of the city; his right lay before the Gate
of the Sultan's Market on the north, his left before the Kalwadha Gate at the
southern end of East Baghdad, and detachments that had crossed the Tigris
upstream came round by Anbar and the Isa canal to take the western side. The
main assault was aimed at the Persian Bastion and the Halbah Gate.

Inside, the city helped. Karkh and the quarter around the shrine of the Imam
Musa were Shi'i, and to demonstrate what they thought of a Sunni caliph they
corresponded with the besiegers.

After about fifty days East Baghdad was stormed. Al-Musta'sim was brought out
of the Kalwadha Gate to stand as a suppliant in front of Hulagu, and was killed
shortly afterwards in the camp near that gate, with his sons; the authorities
differ on the details and agree on the fact. Hulagu moved into the Ma'muniyya
quarter, which the Persian historian of the conquest renders, deliberately, as
the Monkey-house.

The sack lasted forty days. A large part of the population was killed, and a
fire took the caliph's mosque, the shrine of Musa al-Kazim, the tombs of the
caliphs at Rusafa, and most of the streets and houses. Then the army moved on
to the next conquest, Hulagu leaving orders that the mosque and the shrine were
to be rebuilt.

## What the city became

The rest is a list, and the list is the point. From being the real or nominal
capital of Islam, Baghdad became the chief town of the province of Arab Iraq.

Hulagu's descendants held it for not quite a century. Then the Jalayirids,
whose chief made it his residence in 1340. Then Timur, who occupied it in 1393,
stayed two months, and on leaving instructed his lieutenant to rebuild a city
that had mostly fallen into ruin. Then the Turkomans of the Black Sheep from
1411, and the White Sheep who dispossessed them in 1469, and Shah Isma'il's
Persians in 1508, and the Ottomans in 1534, and the Persians again in 1623
through the treachery of a janissary officer, and Murad IV in 1638, after which
it was the seat of a Turkish pasha.

Nine changes of master in four centuries, and not one of them made anything of
it except a provincial capital.

## The last man to see the mosques

Ibn Battuta was in Baghdad in 1327, sixty-nine years after the siege, and he
appears to have been the last writer who saw the three great mosques of the old
capital still standing: al-Mansur's on the west bank, the mosque of Rusafa on
the east, and the Seljuk sultan's mosque a mile from it.

All three have since disappeared entirely, along with every trace of the great
hospital, which in Ibn Battuta's day was already a ruin on the river bank where
the old bridge of boats had crossed.

A city that had been laid out as a circle with the ruler at its centre, and had
grown into the largest thing of its kind west of China, is now recoverable
mostly as a list of names in books, matched up against ground where somebody
would have to dig.
