---
title: Two centuries of translation, and who paid for it
drafted_from: gutas-1998
phase: peak
---

Between the middle of the eighth century and the end of the tenth, very nearly
the whole surviving corpus of Greek secular science was translated into Arabic:
medicine, astronomy, mathematics, physics, botany, alchemy, philosophy, most of
Aristotle, most of Galen, Euclid, Ptolemy. Not the poetry, not the drama, not
the history — the useful books. It is the largest transfer of knowledge between
two languages before the printing press.

The version usually told makes it a story about individuals: a curious caliph,
a famous translator, a house of wisdom. Gutas's argument, which this chapter
follows, is that the individuals are real and the explanation is not. A
two-century programme, employing hundreds of people, funded well enough that
Hunayn ibn Ishaq could be paid by weight for what he produced, is a social
phenomenon with a demand behind it, and the demand was the caliphate's.

Several demands, in fact, and they are worth separating. The court needed
astrology, which needs astronomy, which needs Ptolemy and the mathematics under
him. The administration inherited a Sasanian bureaucratic culture in which
scientific patronage was part of what a king did. Physicians were wanted and
Galen was where medicine was. And there was an ideological argument, which is
the part that makes this Abbasid rather than merely eighth-century: the claim,
promoted in early Abbasid circles, that the sciences had been Persian, that
Alexander had scattered them to the Greeks, and that recovering them in Arabic
was therefore a restoration rather than a borrowing. A dynasty that had come to
power on Khurasani arms and ruled through a Persianate administration had good
reason to find that argument congenial.

Two things follow. The first is that the movement ended, around the close of
the tenth century, and not because anybody suppressed it: the useful books had
been done and the Arabic scientific tradition had outgrown them. The second is
that almost everything later courts in the east had to think with came out of
this. When the Ilkhanate's vizier writes a world history, when the Nizamiyya
madrasas endow a scholarly class, when al-Biruni measures India for a Ghaznavid
patron, the intellectual equipment was assembled in Baghdad on Abbasid money in
these two centuries.
