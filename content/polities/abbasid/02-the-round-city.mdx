The dynasty needed a capital. Kufa had made and unmade caliphs for a century
and could not be trusted; Damascus was the losing side's city; the two
provisional seats the first Abbasids tried had already produced an
insurrection. In 762 al-Mansur chose a site on the west bank of the Tigris, at
the point where the canal system of the Euphrates comes closest to the river,
and built something nobody in Islam had built before.

## Cotton soaked in naphtha

Craftsmen and labourers were brought from Syria, Mosul, the Jibal and
Babylonia, with architects and land surveyors, and four overseers were put over
them. One of the four was Abu Hanifa, the jurist whose school is the oldest of
the four Sunni traditions of law — and what he is credited with on the site is
a piece of administration: instead of counting the bricks one by one, he
measured the stacks with a graduated rod and computed the number.

The plan was laid out on the ground in lines of cinders, and to make the
outline legible they set balls of cotton soaked in naphtha along it and set
them alight. The foundations were dug on the burning line.

## The shape

What went up was a circle. Two great concentric ramparts with a water-filled
ditch outside them, and a third wall inside enclosing the central area — four
gates in each ring, equidistant, so that four highroads ran from the centre
like the spokes of a wheel and passed through five gateways each before
crossing the ditch.

The gates were named for where their roads went: Kufa to the south-west and
Basra to the south-east, both opening on the Sarat canal; Khurasan to the
north-east on the Tigris, at the main bridge of boats; Syria to the north-west,
on the Anbar road. In the centre stood the caliph's palace, called the Golden
Gate, and beside it the great mosque, and nothing else at first.

The Arabic writers say what the point was, and al-Mansur said it himself: the
sovereign should live at the centre of everything and equidistant from all of
it. A circular city with four gates was a novelty in Islam, and probably came
from Persia. Every city the conquerors had founded until then had grown out of
a camp. This one was a diagram.

## What the measurements do not agree about

Nothing of it survives, and the figures in the sources vary in the way figures
do.

Ya'qubi gives five thousand ells from gate to gate, about 2,500 yards, which
makes the outer circle around the ditch nearly 3,200 yards across; the central
area alone was some 2,000 yards, over a mile. Yaqut gives an Arab mile between
gates, which is close enough. Khatib al-Baghdadi reports the caliph
al-Mu'tadid, a century and a half later, pointing out the old limits as
covering two Arab miles in every direction — and elsewhere Khatib passes on a
tradition that would make the city oval and another that halves the diameter,
neither of which anybody else supports.

The bricks are more certain than the plan. They were sun-dried and enormous:
cubes an ell — eighteen inches — on every side, weighing two hundred *ratls*,
and half-bricks eighteen inches square and nine thick at a hundred.

Within fifty years the round city had been besieged by one caliph's army
against another's and was largely a ruin, and Baghdad had spread across the
river and around it into the sprawling two-banked city that is meant when the
name is used. The diagram lasted no time at all. The place it was drawn on has
been a city ever since.
