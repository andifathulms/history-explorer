For three centuries after the Buyids took Baghdad in 945, the caliph was a
sovereign nobody obeyed and everybody needed. Buyid amirs, then Seljuk sultans,
ruled in fact and were invested by him in form. It was not an empty
arrangement — a Seljuk sultan wanted the diploma, and the Ghaznavids and
Khwarazmshahs quarrelled over the wording of theirs — because the caliph was the
only remaining source of a legitimacy that was not simply conquest. A polity that
had lost its army had kept the thing the armies wanted.

There was even a late recovery. From the 1130s the caliphs governed Iraq
directly again as Seljuk power broke up, and al-Nasir (1180–1225) ran a genuine
territorial state with a foreign policy.

Hulegu's army arrived in 1258. Baghdad was besieged and taken, the population
massacred over several days, and al-Musta'sim executed — by report rolled in a
carpet and trampled, so that no royal blood touched the ground. The line
continued in Cairo under Mamluk protection, where an Abbasid caliph was installed
in 1261 and his successors held the title without authority until the Ottoman
conquest of Egypt. That Cairo line is the one successor claim counted on this
page.

The date matters for this site specifically. Every polity in the Iranian
Intermezzo held its authority, on paper, from the institution that ended here —
and all of them had ended before it did. The Khwarazmshahs, the last of them,
were destroyed by the same Mongol advance twenty-seven years earlier. The grant
outlived every dynasty that ever claimed one.
