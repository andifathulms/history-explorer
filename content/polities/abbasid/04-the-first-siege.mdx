Harun died in 809 having divided the empire between two sons. Within three years
one of them was besieging the other inside the city their great-grandfather had
built, and the Round City did not survive it.

Al-Amin held Iraq and the capital. Al-Ma'mun held the east and preferred, Le
Strange notes drily, to remain safely in far-off Khurasan; he sent two generals
instead. Harthama came at Baghdad from the east. Tahir — who would found the
Tahirid dynasty on the strength of what he did here — crossed the Tigris at
Ctesiphon, came up the great Kufa road, and invested the western side.

## What a siege did to a city with no walls

East Baghdad had no town wall at all. Its people built barricades across the
roads where they left the city and used the line of their own houses and garden
walls as the outer defence.

On the west Tahir set up outside the Anbar Gate and began on the suburbs. The
Harbiya quarter was largely brought down by his catapults, and the ruin ran from
the Tigris bank at Baghtiyin round past the Syrian Gate to the Kufa Gate and the
line of the Sarat canal. Fire finished what the catapults began. The great mills
where the two Sarat canals met were partly destroyed. The suburbs along the
Karkhaya canal were laid in ruins.

Then it simply went on. The siege dragged from month to month and the
inhabitants, in Le Strange's phrase, suffered horribly. Zubayda — Harun's widow,
the most famous woman of the age — was driven out of her own palace near the
Qatrabbul Gate and went to join her son inside the Round City.

The methodical part is the worst of it. Tahir moored a fresh bridge of boats
upstream so he could move reinforcements between the two armies. He destroyed
the Old and the New bridges over the Sarat, cutting the highroads out of the
Kufa and Basra gates. When Harthama stormed the Khurasan Gate and took the
eastern half of the city, his first act was to cut the main bridge of boats, so
that the Round City was isolated.

## The end of it

The merchants of West Baghdad began to parley when they saw the defence failing,
and al-Amin's troops deserted in increasing numbers.

He retired with his mother into the Palace of the Golden Gate at the centre of
the Round City, keeping a way out to the river through the Khuld palace and its
gardens. Then Harthama planted catapults in the Mukharrim quarter across the
water, the stones made the Khuld untenable, and its garrison abandoned it.

Al-Amin decided to surrender, and — fearing Harthama less than Tahir — set out
secretly at night to be rowed across to the eastern camp. By accident or by
treachery the boat went over. He swam back to the western bank, where Tahir's
troops took him, and Tahir had him killed at once in a garden near the Anbar
Gate. The head went to Ma'mun in Khurasan as proof that the war was over.

## What it cost

Ma'mun arrived some months later and rebuilt much of the capital. The Round City
never recovered.

He did not live in it. When he was in Baghdad at all he stayed on the east bank,
in the old Barmakid palace below the Mukharrim quarter. His brother al-Mu'tasim
would leave the city altogether for Samarra within twenty years.

So the purpose-built capital lasted fifty years as the thing it was designed to
be. It was not destroyed by an enemy of the dynasty or by a frontier the
caliphate could not hold. It was destroyed by the Abbasid army, on the orders of
an Abbasid caliph, to remove an Abbasid caliph, while the man who ordered it
stayed two thousand miles away and waited for the head to be sent to him.
