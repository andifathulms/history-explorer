title: The soldiers at Samarra
drafted_from: el-hibri-2021

Al-Mu'tasim's solution to an army with local loyalties was an army with none:
Turkish slave soldiers, bought young from the Central Asian steppe, converted,
trained, and attached to the caliph personally rather than to a tribe or a
province. It is one of the most consequential administrative decisions in
Islamic history, and it is the origin of the institution that produces the
Ghaznavids, the Mamluks and eventually the janissaries.

In 836 he moved the whole apparatus sixty miles up the Tigris to a new capital
at Samarra, because Baghdad's population and his new troops could not be kept in
the same city without fighting. Samarra was a caliphal city in a way Baghdad had
stopped being: enormous, planned, and containing almost nobody the caliph had
not brought there.

Within thirty years the guard was choosing the caliph. Between 861 and 870 four
caliphs were raised and destroyed by their own Turkish commanders — a period the
sources call the anarchy at Samarra — and the office never recovered its
independence from the men who held its weapons. The army the caliphs bought to
free themselves from provincial politics became the only politics there was.

The provinces drew the obvious conclusion. The Tahirids were already governing
Khurasan hereditarily; the Saffarids took Sistan without asking; the Samanids
consolidated Transoxiana; the Buyids took Baghdad itself in 945 and kept the
caliph. What was left in each case was the grant and the khutba — the caliph's
name on the coinage and in the Friday prayer, and nothing behind it.
