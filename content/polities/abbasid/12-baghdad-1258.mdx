title: The grant outlived the debtors

An office with no army spent its last three centuries investing the men who
had one — Buyid amirs, then Seljuk sultans, each ruling Iraq and the Iranian
east by force and asking Baghdad to say so in form, because conquest alone
could not make a seizure read as succession. That arrangement, not a standing
army or a governed territory, is what the Abbasid caliphate actually was for
most of its existence, and the arithmetic underneath it is worth doing once,
end to end, before the narrative of how it stopped.

Tahirid, Saffarid, Samanid, Ghaznavid, Seljuk, Khwarazmshah: every dynasty of
consequence in the Iranian east for four hundred years held its authority on
paper from an institution that did not fall until 1258 — and every one of
them had already fallen first. The Khwarazmshahs, the last of them, were
destroyed by the same Mongol advance that would take Baghdad, twenty-seven
years earlier. An office that had not commanded an army of its own since the
ninth century outlived every army that ever claimed to rule in its name.
