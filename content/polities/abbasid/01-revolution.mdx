---
title: What the revolution actually changed
drafted_from: kennedy-2004
phase: formation
---

The Abbasids came to power in 750 on a Khurasani army raised in the name of an
unnamed member of the Prophet's family, and the first thing they did with it was
disappoint everyone who had been imagining a different name. The second was to
have Abu Muslim, who had organised the whole thing, killed in 755.

What changed was the centre of gravity. The Umayyads had ruled Syria-first,
through Syrian tribal levies, facing Byzantium. The Abbasids ruled from Iraq,
through a Khurasani army and a salaried administration, facing east. Baghdad —
founded in 762 on an empty site chosen for its rivers and its distance from
Kufa's politics — was a purpose-built imperial capital in a way Damascus never
was, and within fifty years it was probably the largest city west of China.

The other change was who could hold office. The Umayyad settlement had an Arab
military class at the top of it and everyone else attached sideways as clients.
The Abbasid administration was staffed substantially by Persians, most famously
the Barmakid family of viziers, and it ran on a Sasanian bureaucratic inheritance
that the Umayyads had used but never absorbed. The conquest aristocracy stopped
being the state.

This is the arrangement the rest of this site depends on. Every dynasty in the
Iranian Intermezzo — Tahirid, Saffarid, Samanid, Ghaznavid, Seljuk — holds its
province by an Abbasid grant, or claims to, and names the caliph in the Friday
sermon. That relationship is recorded here as edges running out of this polity
into another region, which is what the succession model is for: they are not
the Abbasids' successors and never claimed to be. They are its delegates,
outlasting the delegation.
