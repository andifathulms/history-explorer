---
title: What the revolution actually changed
drafted_from: kennedy-2004
phase: formation
---

The Abbasids came to power in 750 on a Khurasani army raised in the name of an
unnamed member of the Prophet's family, and the first thing they did with it
was disappoint everyone who had been imagining a different name. The second was
to have Abu Muslim, who had organised the whole thing, killed in 755.

What changed was the centre of gravity. The Umayyads had ruled Syria-first,
through Syrian tribal levies, facing Byzantium. The Abbasids ruled from Iraq,
through a Khurasani army and a salaried administration, facing east. Baghdad —
founded in 762 on an empty site chosen for its rivers and its distance from
Kufa's politics — was a purpose-built imperial capital in a way Damascus never
was, and within fifty years it was probably the largest city west of China.

The other change was who could hold office. The Umayyad settlement had an Arab
military class at the top of it and everyone else attached sideways as clients.
The Abbasid administration was staffed substantially by Persians, most famously
the Barmakid family of viziers, and it ran on a Sasanian bureaucratic
inheritance that the Umayyads had used but never absorbed. The conquest
aristocracy stopped being the state.

It is also the arrangement the next four centuries in the east run on. The
Tahirids, the Saffarids, the Samanids, the Ghaznavids and the Seljuks all hold
their provinces by an Abbasid grant, or claim to, and all name the caliph in
the Friday sermon. None of them is a successor to the Abbasids and none ever
said it was. They are delegates who outlast the delegation, which is a
different and stranger relationship, and it is the one that keeps the office
alive for five hundred years after it stops governing anything.
