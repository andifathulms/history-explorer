In 833, a few months before he died, al-Ma'mun sent instructions to his
governor in Baghdad. Judges, jurists and traditionists were to be examined on a
point of doctrine — whether the Qur'an was created or eternal — and those who
would not affirm that it was created were to be removed from office, refused as
witnesses, and if necessary imprisoned. The examination is called the *mihna*,
and it ran under three caliphs for about fifteen years.

<Figure id="amajur-quran" />

The doctrine is not the point, and the caliphs' opponents understood that
better than most later accounts do. What the *mihna* asserted was jurisdiction:
that the caliph could determine a question of belief and require the religious
professionals to accept his determination. The Umayyads had made the earlier
version of the same claim, styling themselves deputy of God; the *mihna* is
that claim made operational, by an Abbasid, against named people, with
penalties.

It failed, and it failed in the most damaging possible way. Ahmad ibn Hanbal
refused, was flogged and imprisoned, and did not yield, and his refusal made
him the most respected religious figure of the age. Around 848 al-Mutawakkil
abandoned the examination and reversed the policy. No caliph tried again.

What was settled in those fifteen years was not the createdness of the Qur'an —
that question simply stopped being asked at swordpoint. What was settled was
who had the right to answer it. After the *mihna* the definition of Islam
belonged to the scholars, as a class the caliph paid, appointed and could not
overrule, and the caliphate held political authority and a religious title with
the content taken out of it.

So the caliphate began contracting before its territory did. The office lost
something in 848 that it never recovered, and that mattered more in the long
run than the provinces it was about to lose to its own guard. Every later ruler
who sent to Baghdad for a caliphal investiture was buying the political
endorsement of an institution that had already conceded the religious argument.
