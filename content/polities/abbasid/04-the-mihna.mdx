---
title: The last caliph who tried to say what Islam was
drafted_from: crone-hinds-1986
phase: contraction
---

In 833, a few months before he died, al-Ma'mun sent instructions to his governor
in Baghdad. Judges, jurists and traditionists were to be examined on a point of
doctrine — whether the Qur'an was created or eternal — and those who would not
affirm that it was created were to be removed from office, refused as witnesses,
and if necessary imprisoned. The examination is called the *mihna*, and it ran
under three caliphs for about fifteen years.

The doctrine is not the point, and the caliphs' opponents understood that better
than most later accounts do. What the *mihna* asserted was jurisdiction: that
the caliph could determine a question of belief and require the religious
professionals to accept his determination. This site's Umayyad page has the
earlier version of the same claim, where the caliphs styled themselves deputy of
God; the *mihna* is that claim made operational, by an Abbasid, against named
people, with penalties.

It failed, and it failed in the most damaging possible way. Ahmad ibn Hanbal
refused, was flogged and imprisoned, and did not yield, and his refusal made him
the most respected religious figure of the age. Around 848 al-Mutawakkil
abandoned the examination and reversed the policy. No caliph tried again.

What was settled in those fifteen years was not the createdness of the Qur'an —
that question simply stopped being asked at swordpoint. What was settled was who
had the right to answer it. After the *mihna* the definition of Islam belonged
to the scholars, as a class the caliph paid, appointed and could not overrule,
and the caliphate held political authority and a religious title with the
content taken out of it.

That is why this chapter is filed under contraction on a page whose territory
was not shrinking yet. The office lost something in 848 that it never recovered
and that mattered more, in the long run, than the provinces it was about to lose
to its own guard. Every subsequent polity in this corpus that seeks a caliphal
investiture is seeking the political endorsement of an institution that had
already conceded the religious argument.
