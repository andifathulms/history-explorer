In 1055 Tughril Beg entered Baghdad, expelled the Buyids and restored the
caliph — to exactly the position he had held before. The Great Seljuk sultans
were Sunni and presented themselves as the caliphate's protectors, which
changed the theology of the arrangement and nothing about its arithmetic. The
sultan held the army and the provinces; the caliph held the investiture that
made the sultan's holding lawful, and the arrangement ran, with interruptions,
for over a century.

## A second name in the pulpits

That arrangement was tested almost as soon as it was made. In 1058 a Turkish
commander named al-Basasiri, acting for the rival caliphate at Cairo, took
control of Baghdad while Tughril Beg was away putting down a revolt by his
own brother. For the better part of a year the Friday sermon in every mosque
of the Abbasid capital named not al-Qa'im but the Fatimid imam al-Mustansir
as commander of the faithful, and al-Qa'im himself was held as a hostage
downriver at Anah, a bargaining piece in an argument over whose name Baghdad's
own pulpits belonged to. Tughril, once free to deal with it, did not
negotiate with Basasiri at all — he went directly to the officer holding the
caliph and had him released. The Fatimids had reached Baghdad through one
disaffected general and could not keep it: holding Iraq would have needed an
army they had never built that far east. But the arrangement Tughril had made
only three years earlier survived its first real test by the width of one
man's loyalty, not by its own strength.

What is usually missed is what happened when the protector weakened. Seljuk
power fragmented after 1092 and again after 1157, and each time the caliphs
took back not just prestige but ground — tax, garrisons, an actual
administration in Iraq. By the time al-Nasir li-Din Allah came to the throne in
1180 there was something real to govern, and he governed it for forty-five
years, the longest reign in the dynasty.

Al-Nasir is the most interesting Abbasid after al-Ma'mun and the least written
about here until now. He rebuilt a territorial state in Iraq, and he tried to
rebuild the caliph's authority over rulers he could not coerce by reorganising
the *futuwwa* — the urban chivalric associations — into an order with himself
at its head, and inviting princes to join it. It was an attempt to make the
caliphate a network of personal allegiance rather than a fiction of delegation.

It very nearly worked, and then it met Khwarazm. Muhammad II repudiated
al-Nasir in 1217 and marched on Baghdad; the campaign broke in the mountains
before it arrived. Within four years the Khwarazmshah's empire had been
destroyed by the Mongols, and the caliphate had thirty-seven years left.

The point for the next chapter is that what ended in 1258 was not a shadow. It
was a functioning state, smaller than it had been and much larger than it had
been in 945.
