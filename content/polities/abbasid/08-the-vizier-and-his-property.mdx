By the reign of al-Muqtadir, which began in 908, the caliphate had a problem
that no amount of authority could solve: it could not pay the troops, and the
only reliable money left in the empire was money already in the hands of its own
officials.

What it did about that is the clearest picture we have of a state consuming
itself, and Miskawayh, working from the records of the men who ran it, sets it
out in the language of accountancy.

## The riot

Abu Ali al-Khaqani, made vizier without experience and bewildered by the job,
could not pay the stipendiaries and the chief officers in full. So they rioted.
They occupied the Oratory, took most of the officers with them, and abused him
to his face.

Al-Muqtadir ordered him to pay. He excused himself on the grounds of deficient
supplies and reduced revenue — and then said the interesting thing: the money
extracted from Ibn al-Furat, the previous vizier, and from his dependents had
gone into the caliph's *private* treasury, and the treasurer would not release
any of it.

The caliph then ordered half a million dinars paid out of that private treasury
to satisfy the rioters. And appointed a postmaster of Baghdad who was also to be
overseer of the vizier, the army, the officials, the judges and the police —
which is what a state does when it has stopped trusting its own executive.

## Office as a source of extraction

The mechanism this produced is the thing to understand.

A vizier's tenure ended in his arrest and the confiscation of his estate. That
confiscated estate was then a significant part of the next year's revenue. So the
office was worth holding for what could be accumulated in it, and worth ending
for what could be taken back — and everyone involved knew both halves.

Ibn Thawaba, seeing how weak Khaqani was, wrote to the caliph through Umm Musa,
one of the palace women, offering to extract vast sums the vizier had overlooked:
seven hundred thousand dinars from Muhammad ibn Ali al-Madharai and his brother
alone. He was given full powers, and used them to extort with violence, dismiss
the vizier's appointees and install his own. After which, Miskawayh says, various
knaves wrote in through the same channel asking for posts and promising money,
and the vizier was ordered to appoint them, so that the administration was
weakened and the knaves extorted money by every form of violence.

The bureaux were also cooking the books downward. Hamid ibn Abbas had farmed the
revenue of Wasit for four years; the clerks drew up his account at 240,000 dinars
a year plus 24,000 *kurr* of barley for the cavalry, when what he had actually
contracted for was the previous year's register with a slight addition.

## What Mu'nis understood

The sharpest moment is a piece of advice.

Seeing the disorder, al-Muqtadir proposed bringing Ibn al-Furat back as vizier.
Mu'nis the eunuch — the commander who was the real power for most of the reign —
advised against it, and his reason was not administrative. It would cause a
scandal, he said, if the provincial governors learned that the Sultan had
dismissed a vizier and then been compelled to restore him after a few months;
and the Sultan's action would be attributed simply to the desire to seize the
vizier's goods.

Not that it would be *unjust*. That it would be understood. The centre had
reached the point where its provinces read every appointment as a plan to
confiscate somebody, and the man closest to the caliph knew they did.

A government financed by robbing its own servants cannot recruit good servants,
and cannot be believed by the provinces that pay it. Al-Muqtadir was murdered by
his own troops in 932. Within four years the caliphs had handed military and
fiscal power to a commander-in-chief, and within thirteen a Daylami soldier had
walked into Baghdad and taken the city from all of them.
