Opechancanough's death brought the peace that decades of war and
diplomacy never had. His successor, a chief named Necotowance, took a
title that measured exactly how much had changed: not paramount chief
of the Powhatan in his own right, but "King of the Indians," a style
that named a subject rather than a sovereign. In October 1646
Necotowance signed a treaty formalizing what his own new title already
implied - he would hold his authority from the King of England, in
exchange for the colonial assembly's protection from his enemies.

The terms went further than a change of title. Necotowance ceded to
the English outright all the country between the York and James
rivers, from the falls down to Kiquotan, and agreed that any Indian
found within that ceded territory without a messenger's badge could be
killed on sight. What remained to his own people was the land north of
the York, and a hunting ground running from the head of the Yapin to
the old Mannakin town - bounded territory, held on English terms,
rather than the reach of thirty tributary tribes a single emperor had
once commanded. In acknowledgment of the whole arrangement,
Necotowance was to deliver twenty beaver skins to the governor every
year, at the season when the wild geese departed - tribute, from the
people who had once received Captain Smith as a prisoner and decided
his life was theirs to give or take.
