Twenty-two years after the first massacre, Opechancanough struck
again. On the 18th of April, 1644, his warriors fell on the colony a
second time, in an attack some attributed to fresh encroachments on
Indian land and others suspected - improbably - of having been
encouraged by colonists themselves hoping to profit from the chaos of
England's own civil war. Whatever the cause, the first blow was not
followed up. The attackers' own courage failed them after the initial
onslaught, and they withdrew many miles from the settlements, giving
the colonists time to gather their cattle, call an assembly, and
organize a war of reprisal that the first massacre, twenty-two years
earlier, had never provoked.

## Carried by his own warriors

Opechancanough himself was by now nearly a hundred years old, his once
commanding presence worn down to a body his own men had to carry from
place to place in a litter. He could no longer walk, and his eyelids
had to be lifted by attendants for him to see at all. It did not save
him. Sir William Berkeley moved against him with a troop of horse,
surprised him at a distance from his own residence, and brought him
prisoner to Jamestown, where he was treated, by the governor's own
order, with courtesy.

Captivity did not break him. Hearing a crowd gather out of curiosity
to see the famous chief, he had his eyelids raised, summoned Berkeley
to his presence, and told him plainly that had their positions been
reversed, he would never have made a show of his prisoner. About a
fortnight after his capture, one of his own guards shot him in the
back for private revenge. He lingered a while with the wound before
he died at Jamestown - the last paramount chief of a confederacy that
had answered the English colony's own arrival, four decades earlier,
with nothing but the confidence of a man who ruled thirty tribes
outright.
