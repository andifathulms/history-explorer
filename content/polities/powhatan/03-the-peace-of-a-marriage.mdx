title: The peace of a marriage

The peace that finally held was built on an attachment that had
nothing to do with ransom negotiations. John Rolfe had fallen in love
with Pocahontas during her captivity, and she with him; when Rolfe's
intentions reached Powhatan, the old ruler's consent came almost
immediately - within ten days he sent an uncle and two of his own
sons as his deputies to attend and confirm the wedding, held about
the first of April 1614. From that marriage, the English colonists
recorded, came friendly trade and commerce with Powhatan and all his
subjects for the first time since Smith's own departure five years
before - and a secondary peace besides, as the neighbouring
Chickahominy, watching the reconciliation, sent their own messengers
offering to become subjects of the English crown rather than remain
outside the new arrangement.

It was the high point of the confederacy's dealings with the colony:
a marriage confirmed by the paramount ruler himself, a former enemy
tribe volunteering its allegiance, and years of raiding replaced, for
a time, by trade neither side had to force out of the other at
gunpoint. Powhatan never travelled to Jamestown to see the peace his
daughter's marriage had built, remaining at his own residence and
governing his confederacy exactly as he always had - only now without
an English colony demanding satisfaction from him every few months.

## Death, and a peace inherited rather than renewed

Powhatan died in April 1618. His brother Itopatin succeeded him as
the confederacy's nominal paramount chief, and both Itopatin and
another brother, Opechancanough, confirmed the English alliance
Powhatan's own consent had sealed four years earlier. But the peace
that followed was inherited rather than actively renewed, resting now
on a marriage bond whose author was dead and on a confirmation from
men who had not personally negotiated it. Opechancanough, in
particular, held real influence in the confederacy's affairs from
this point far beyond what his brother's nominal title suggested -
and the peace he had only confirmed, rather than made, would prove to
be exactly the kind he judged himself free to end.
