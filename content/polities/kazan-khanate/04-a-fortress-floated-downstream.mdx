The Russians took Kazan in 1552 because of something they did in 1551, and it
was not a battle.

## The problem

Every earlier campaign had failed the same way. An army could reach Kazan by
river in early summer, sit in front of the walls, and then run out of food,
because the country between the frontier and the city could not feed it and the
supply line up the Volga could be cut. Artillery could be floated down but not
kept in the field. Winter sieges were worse: the ice made the approach possible
and the cold made staying impossible.

Six campaigns in twenty-seven years had come home for those reasons.

## The answer

In the winter of 1550–51 a fortress was cut in the forests of the upper Volga far
to the north — walls, towers and gates, framed and fitted and then taken apart —
floated down the river in the spring, and reassembled at the mouth of the
Sviyaga, about twenty-five kilometres upstream from Kazan on the high right bank.

It was standing in roughly four weeks.

Sviyazhsk gave the Russians what they had never had: a garrisoned, provisioned,
artillery-capable base within a day's march of the enemy capital, held through
the winter, on ground the khanate could not retake. It also sat on the right bank
among the Chuvash and Mari, who came in and swore to Moscow within weeks of its
appearing, taking with them the levies that had made up most of the khanate's
infantry.

The khanate surrendered a khan, accepted a Muscovite appointee and gave up the
right bank in a negotiation conducted under the shadow of the new fort. Then, in
1552, it repudiated the settlement and took a Nogai prince as khan instead.

## The siege

The army that came in August 1552 was the largest Moscow had ever put in the
field and it came equipped for a siege rather than a campaign: a hundred and
fifty heavy guns, a corps of musketeers, engineers, and a Danish or German
specialist in mining whom the Russian sources call simply the man who knew about
powder.

The method was the mine. The water supply was cut by blowing the underground
passage the city drew from. Then galleries were driven under the walls over
several weeks and charged, and on 2 October the mines were fired and the assault
went in through the breaches.

The fighting in the streets lasted the day. The khan was taken. The Tatar
population of the city was killed or driven out, and the city was resettled.

## What made the difference

Not numbers, and not courage on either side.

It was that the Russian state had reached the point where it could build a
fortress in one place, move it four hundred miles, and stand it up in a month —
and could keep an army of that size supplied at the end of a river for a season.
That is an administrative capability rather than a military one, and it is the
capability the khanate could not answer and did not have.

The same machine went down the Volga to Astrakhan four years later, and then
across the Urals a generation after that. Kazan is where it was first assembled.
