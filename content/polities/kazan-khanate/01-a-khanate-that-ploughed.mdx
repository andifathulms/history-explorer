The Jochid successor states were mostly hordes: a prince, his clans, and the
pasture they moved between. Kazan was not. It was a city on a river with a stone
citadel, a fair, a tax register and farmers, and that is the fact from which
everything else about it follows — including its destruction.

## Where it came from

Ulugh Muhammad had been khan of the Golden Horde and had lost it. Driven west and
then east by rivals, he arrived on the middle Volga in 1438 with a following and
took Kazan, a town that had grown up near the ruins of the old Volga Bulgar
capital.

What he took over was not a blank steppe. The middle Volga had been a settled
Muslim country since the tenth century: the Volga Bulgars had a state there when
Kiev was pagan, and they had grain, furs, silver and a market that traders came
to from Khwarazm and the Baltic. The Mongol conquest had wrecked it and the
region had recovered under the Horde as a province.

So the khanate was a Chinggisid dynasty imposed on an old settled polity, in the
way the Ilkhans were imposed on Iran — and like the Ilkhans it took the
administration it found.

## What that meant

It meant revenue that did not have to be raided for. Grain from the black-earth
right bank, furs and honey from the forest peoples of the left, and the tolls of
a river fair that sat on the junction of the Volga and the Kama, where the routes
from the Caspian, from Siberia and from Moscow met.

It also meant subjects who were not Tatars. The Mari, the Chuvash, the Udmurt and
the Mordvins were the majority of the population, paid the tribute, and provided
most of the army. They were not Muslims, they were not Chinggisids, and their
loyalty was to whoever was collecting from them — which turned out to matter
enormously in 1551.

And it meant a fixed capital that could be besieged. A horde that is beaten rides
away and re-forms. A khanate whose whole revenue is inside one set of walls is
finished when the walls go.

## The neighbour

Kazan is about seven hundred kilometres from Moscow down a river that runs the
right way.

The Volga system connects the two, and the Oka and the Kama connect the parts,
which meant that an army with boats could reach Kazan from the Russian centre in a
season and that the merchants of both states needed each other continuously.
Herberstein, writing in the 1520s, notes both halves: that the rivers flowing from
Moscow into the Volga made the subjugation of Kazan easier, and so did the
commercial intercourse *which they could not dispense with*.

A state that is your customer, your supplier, your rival for the fur trade and
three weeks away by water is not a state you can ignore. Moscow did not ignore
it for a hundred and fourteen years.
