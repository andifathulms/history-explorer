Seven years after the fall of Kazan an English merchant came up the Volga from
Astrakhan and recorded how long it took and what he saw on the way.

Anthony Jenkinson was the Muscovy Company's agent, returning from Bukhara with a
party of Tatar ambassadors and a hundred Russian gunners provided by the tsar for
safe conduct. He left Astrakhan on 10 June 1559.

> And the eight and twentieth daye of July following, we arriued at the Citie of
> Cazan, hauing beene vppon the waye from Astracan thither, sixe weekes, and
> more, without any refreshing of victuals: for in all that waye there is no
> habitation.

## What that sentence contains

Fifteen hundred kilometres of the greatest river in Europe, between two cities
Russia had just conquered, with nothing on it.

That is not a description of wilderness. It is a description of a corridor that
had been emptied — by the raiding that had gone up and down it for a century and a
half, by the wars of the 1540s and 1550s, and by the collapse of the Nogai hordes
on the left bank, which Jenkinson had watched at Astrakhan the previous year.

A merchant needs provisions and expects to buy them. What he records is that
between the two former khanates there was nobody to buy from, and that he and a
hundred gunners and a Tatar embassy went six weeks on what they carried.

## The city he arrived at

Jenkinson's own notes on Kazan are brief: a fair town with a strong castle,
lately in Tatar hands, now Russian, and being rebuilt. The wooden fortifications
put up after the assault were in the course of being replaced in stone.

He does not record any Tatars in it, and he would have been interested: he was a
man who counted everything, and at Astrakhan he had counted the dead.

## What filled it

The emptiness did not last, and the speed with which it filled is the measure of
what had been suppressing it.

The Volga below Kazan was fortified in stages over the following forty years —
Samara, Saratov, Tsaritsyn, each a garrison town at a crossing or a portage — and
behind the garrisons came peasants, monasteries, salt works and fisheries. The
river became the main artery of Russian trade to the Caspian and Persia, which is
exactly what the English company had come to find, and the reason Jenkinson was
on it in the first place.

He had been sent to look for a route to Asia that did not pass through Ottoman
or Portuguese hands. What he found was a river that Russia had spent a century
and a half unable to use, opened in his own lifetime, and still so raw that
travelling its length meant carrying six weeks of food.
