The city fell on 2 October 1552 and the khanate was not restored. That second
fact is what makes the date matter, because Kazan had been taken before.

## What had happened the other times

Ivan III took it in 1487 and installed a khan. Vasily III's armies took or
besieged it repeatedly and the outcome each time was a change of khan. Even in
1551 the settlement was a Muscovite appointee on a Chinggisid throne with the
khanate's institutions intact.

That was the normal European and steppe practice and everybody understood it: a
defeated state kept its dynasty, its law, its religion and its name, and changed
its patron.

In 1552 Ivan IV did not install anybody. The khan was taken to Moscow, baptised,
given an estate and a Russian title, and died a Russian nobleman. The territory
was annexed, governed by a Muscovite voevoda, and an archbishopric was founded at
Kazan in 1555 with instructions to convert. Ivan added *Tsar of Kazan* to his
title, and it stayed in the Russian imperial style until 1917.

## Why the difference

Because of what the annexation was for.

Taking Kazan removed a rival for the Volga trade and a Crimean ally on the
eastern flank. Keeping Kazan opened the whole river to the Caspian, and Astrakhan
followed within four years. The Volga is one system; a Russian Kazan made a
Russian Astrakhan nearly inevitable and a Russian Siberia possible, because the
Kama leads to the Urals and the Stroganovs were granted their charters on the
Kama fifteen years later.

A client khanate could not deliver that. A province could.

## The wars that followed

The conquest is conventionally dated to the fall of the city, and the people who
lived in the khanate did not experience it that way.

The forest peoples of the left bank — Mari above all — rose against Russian rule
within months and were still rising thirty years later. The risings of 1552–57,
1571–74 and 1581–84 are sometimes counted as three Cheremis wars and are better
understood as one long refusal, put down by fortress-building, punitive columns
and famine. A khanate that had been taken in a single day took a generation to
hold.

## What was left standing

The Tatar population was expelled from the city and forbidden to live inside it,
and settled in a suburb across the lake which became the Tatar quarter and is
still there. Mosques in the city were destroyed; the great one, whose imam is
remembered as having died in the assault, gave its name to the mosque rebuilt on
the Kremlin site in the twenty-first century.

What survived was a language, a literate Muslim tradition and a population that
was neither converted nor dispersed. Kazan Tatars are today the second-largest
nationality in the Russian Federation, and the republic that carries the name is
the direct territorial descendant of the khanate.

The khanate lasted a hundred and fourteen years. The thing it was made out of —
a settled Muslim society on the middle Volga — was four hundred years older than
the khanate and has outlasted it by five hundred.
