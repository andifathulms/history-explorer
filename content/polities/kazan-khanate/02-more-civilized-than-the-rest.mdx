The best outside description of the khanate of Kazan while it existed was written
by a Habsburg ambassador who never went there, from what the Muscovite court and
the Tatar princes living in Moscow told him in 1517 and 1526.

Herberstein's paragraph is short, and every clause in it is doing something.

## The country

> The kingdom of Kazan, with the city and fortress of the same name, is situated
> on the further bank of the river Volga, nearly seventy miles below Lower
> Novogorod. The king of this province can raise an army of thirty thousand men,
> principally foot soldiers, amongst whom the Czeremissi and Czubaschi are the
> most skilful archers. They say that the Czubaschi excel in the art of
> navigation.

Thirty thousand, *principally foot soldiers* — which no steppe khanate fielded.
The archers and the boatmen are the forest peoples of the khanate's own hinterland,
the Mari and the Chuvash, and Herberstein names them separately from the Tatars
because his informants did.

Then the sentence that explains the whole strategic problem:

> These Tartars are more civilized than the rest, in as much as they cultivate
> their lands, live in houses, and carry on various branches of merchandize.

He means it as a compliment and it is a description of a vulnerability. People who
cultivate can be starved; people who live in houses can be besieged; people who
trade have a relationship that can be cut.

## How Moscow managed it

Herberstein then sets out the dynastic mechanics, and his account is the clearest
statement anywhere of what a Muscovite protectorate over a Chinggisid throne
actually involved.

Alega succeeded his father as eldest son *but as he was not entirely obedient to
the commands of the Prince of Moscow* —

> he was on a certain occasion made drunk at a festival by some of the
> councillors of the Prince of Moscow, whom he had sent thither to watch the
> disposition of the king, and who in that state placed him in a carriage, as if
> with the intention of conveying him home; but on that same night he was driven
> towards Moscow.

He was confined, sent to Vologda and died there. His mother and two half-brothers
had already been moved to Beloozero. One brother was released from Beloozero and
put on the throne in his place; when he too was removed *for a similar reason*,
the other brother was brought out of confinement and installed.

That is a khanate run as a prison system. Moscow held the eligible candidates,
kept them alive, and issued them one at a time.

## Nur-Sultan

The figure Herberstein cannot stop returning to is the queen.

Nur-Sultan was married to one khan of Kazan; when he died childless she was taken
by the man who thereby *gained possession of the kingdom*, and bore him two sons.
After the fall of her stepson she married Mengli Giray, the khan of the Crimea.
Having no children by him, she travelled — in 1504, Herberstein has the date — to
Moscow to see one son in confinement and then on to Kazan to see the other on the
throne.

One woman, three khans, two khanates and a journey across the whole Jochid world
to visit her children, one of whom was a prisoner of the power that had installed
the other.

## What he could not see

Herberstein wrote all this down as background. Four years after his second embassy
Kazan threw the Muscovite party out and took a Crimean Giray instead, and the
management system he had described stopped working.

He records that too, at the end of the section, in the present tense: *The people
of Kazan have now rebelled against the Prince of Moscow; and as this rebellion has
given rise to many wars … and as the war remains unterminated up to the present
day, I have thought it right to describe its reason below.*
