Kazan's founder did not live to rule the city he founded. What
consolidated the khanate was his son's murder of him, and four decades
of wars that neither side could actually win.

## A son who killed his father

Ulugh Muhammad, driven out of the Golden Horde and settled on the middle
Volga, never returned to reclaim the throne he had lost further west.
In the autumn of 1445 his own son Mahmudek assassinated him at Kurmysh
and killed a younger brother, Yusuf, in the same stroke - the act that
actually founded the khanate as a going concern, whatever year its own
first camp on the Kazanka is dated to. Mahmudek then marched on Kazan
itself, killed the man who had usurped authority there in his father's
name, and took the city.

His own reign was short and combative in the pattern every khan after
him would follow: a raid on Ustyug in the very year of his accession,
a Russian counter-expedition in 1448, then years of wary peace with a
Grand Prince who preferred a quiet frontier to a costly war. He died at
an unrecorded date, leaving two sons, Khalil and Ibrahim, both of whom
in turn married the same Nogai princess, Nur-Sultan.

## Ibrahim's wars

Khalil's reign was brief and unremarkable. Ibrahim's, which followed,
was the khanate's longest and most consequential test of what
independence from Moscow actually cost.

Ivan III's uncle-by-marriage Kasim, exiled in Russia with his own claim
on Kazan's throne, talked Ivan into backing an invasion in 1467; a hard
winter broke it before it reached the city. Two years later the Grand
Prince tried again in earnest, sending a flotilla down the Volga under
Ivan Runo. Runo's men caught Kazan's outskirts by surprise at night,
burned houses with Tatar families still barricaded inside them, freed
Russian prisoners held in the city for as long as forty years, and then
sat idle on an island downriver long enough that his own side accused
him of taking a bribe from the khan to stop. Ibrahim answered with a
cavalry and river-boat force that badly mauled the following Russian
column; Ivan answered that with a second, far larger army under his own
brothers, which finally forced Ibrahim to terms and the release of
those decades of accumulated captives.

The peace did not hold either side for long. In 1478, on a false rumour
that Ivan had been defeated and wounded fighting Novgorod, Ibrahim
raided Viatka and carried off slaves; the following spring Ivan's own
revenge campaign burned villages up the Kama and the Volga and besieged
Kazan itself before a storm drove the Russians off. Ibrahim asked for
peace again, got it, and died shortly afterward, leaving children by
several different wives and no single clear heir.

## What forty years had actually decided

Nothing, in the sense either side would have wanted stated plainly.
Kazan under Mahmudek and Ibrahim burned Russian towns and lost Russian
sieges in roughly equal measure, released the same prisoners it had
spent decades gathering, and never once lost its own capital to a
Russian army in the field. What Ibrahim's death actually opened was not
a conquest but a vacancy - and it was the fight over that vacancy,
between his son Ali and Nur-Sultan's son Muhammad Amin, that gave
Moscow its first real opening to stop merely raiding the khanate and
start choosing who sat on its throne.
