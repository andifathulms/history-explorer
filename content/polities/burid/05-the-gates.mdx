title: The gates opened from inside

Mujir al-Din Abaq, the last Burid, tried to hold the position after Unur and had
nothing to hold it with. Nur al-Din had Aleppo, the northern Syrian army, the
reputation of the jihad and the argument — true, and repeated in Damascus by
preachers he was paying — that the Burids were the tributaries of the Franks.
Abaq responded by paying tribute to the Franks again, which was the only card he
had and confirmed the charge.

In April 1154 Nur al-Din brought his army to the walls, and the city opened. The
sources describe a blockade that had cut the grain, a population that had been
argued into it for six years, and a garrison that did not fight. Abaq was given
Homs and later exchanged it for an estate near Baghdad, and lived. Damascus and
Aleppo were under one ruler for the first time since the tenth century.

That unification is the thing on the next page. It is what made Nur al-Din the
counter-crusade rather than a claimant, it is what Saladin took from his heirs
twenty years later, and it is why the Ayyubid page can open with a man inheriting
Egypt and Syria together. Everything this region does between 1154 and 1250 runs
through a gate opening in Damascus.

This site types the ending `gradual absorption` rather than conquest, and the
distinction is worth keeping. The Burids were not defeated. They were argued out
of a city over six years by a neighbour with a better claim to be doing the
right thing, and their own subjects agreed with him.
