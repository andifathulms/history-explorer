title: The gates opened from inside

Mujir al-Din Abaq, the last Burid, tried to hold the position after Unur and
had nothing to hold it with. Nur al-Din had Aleppo, the northern Syrian army,
the reputation of the jihad and the argument — true, and repeated in Damascus
by preachers he was paying — that the Burids were the tributaries of the
Franks. Abaq responded by paying tribute to the Franks again, which was the
only card he had and confirmed the charge.

In April 1154 Nur al-Din brought his army to the walls, and the city opened.
The sources describe a blockade that had cut the grain, a population that had
been argued into it for six years, and a garrison that did not fight. Abaq was
given Homs and later exchanged it for an estate near Baghdad, and lived.
Damascus and Aleppo were under one ruler for the first time since the tenth
century.

That unification is what everything afterwards runs on. It is what made Nur
al-Din the counter-crusade rather than one more claimant; it is what Saladin
took from his heirs twenty years later; and it is why the man who eventually
recovered Jerusalem inherited Egypt and Syria together rather than having to win
them separately. What happens in Syria between 1154 and 1250 runs through a gate
opening in Damascus.

It is not a conquest, and the distinction is worth keeping. The Burids were not
defeated. They were argued out of a city over six years by a neighbour with a
better claim to be doing the right thing, and their own subjects agreed with
him.
