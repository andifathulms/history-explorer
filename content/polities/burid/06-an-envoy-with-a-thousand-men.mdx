The last Burid could not fight Nur al-Din and could not work with him, and Ibn
al-Qalanisi — who lived in Damascus through all of it and had been the city's
chief magistrate — records both failures at close range.

## The campaign that dissolved

In the spring of 1153 Mujir al-Din was obliged to take his entire *askar* north
and join Nur al-Din so that the two of them might support one another in the holy
war. He set out in April and joined forces; an agreement was reached between them
and all the commanders, the provincial amirs and the Turkmens, who had come with
vast forces.

Nur al-Din had just stormed the castle of Afis. The Franks and Armenians in it
were killed, the troops took a great quantity of money and captives, and the
combined army moved on the frontier castle of Banyas, encamping before it in May
at a moment when — the chronicler notes precisely — it was empty of its defenders
and the opportunity of seizing it was there.

Then this:

> They numbered approximately ten thousand horse and foot, and retired from
> Banyas in disorder before a single Frank had struck a blow at them or any
> Frankish troops had come up with them.

They regrouped at al-A'waj, resolved to renew the siege and take the place, and
then reverted from that decision *without cause or reason*, and parted company.
Mujir al-Din went back to Damascus with all his men safe. Nur al-Din went to
Homs.

Ibn al-Qalanisi does not explain it because he cannot. What he is describing is
an alliance in which neither party trusted the other enough to be second into a
breach — and which therefore could not take an undefended castle with ten
thousand men.

## The embassy

Ten months later the pretence stopped.

In the spring of 1154 Shirkuh arrived at the outskirts of Damascus as Nur
al-Din's envoy, and camped in the Marj with about a thousand troops.

An envoy with a thousand men is a siege wearing a diplomatic hat, and Damascus
read it exactly that way: the chronicle says the action was resented and caused a
feeling of hostility. Mujir al-Din would not go out to receive him and refused to
associate with him. Letters went back and forth as circumstances required and
produced neither concord nor any satisfactory arrangement.

Meanwhile the grain convoys were interrupted and the price of food rose.

That is the whole method, and it did not require a battle. A city that must eat
was cut off by a force too small to storm it and too large to ignore, sent under
a diplomatic description that made resisting it an act of aggression.

## The last argument

What Nur al-Din had that Mujir al-Din could not answer was not the army. It was
the accusation.

The Burids had bought survival for thirty years by paying tribute to the kingdom
of Jerusalem and occasionally fighting alongside it, which had been a rational
policy and had kept Damascus independent. By the 1150s it had become the single
thing that could be said against them from any pulpit in Syria, and Nur al-Din —
who had Aleppo, the northern army and the standing of the holy war — said it
continuously.

Mujir al-Din's answer, when the pressure came, was to pay the Franks again,
because it was the only card he had. It confirmed the charge.

In April 1154 Nur al-Din brought his own army up beside Shirkuh's, camped east of
the city and attacked day after day. Damascene troops and armed bands came out
and fought him, and went back in, and came out again.

Then the gates opened, and the chronicler — a Damascene official who had served
the Burids his whole career — records the entry of the man who ended them as a
relief: to the joy of all the people, because of their sufferings from famine,
the high cost of food, and the fear of being besieged by the Franks.
