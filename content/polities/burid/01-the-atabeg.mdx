---
title: What an atabeg was
drafted_from: el-azhari-1997
phase: formation
---

When the Great Seljuk empire divided Syria among princes of the house in the
1090s, each young prince was given an atabeg: a senior military officer,
generally a Turkish mamluk who had been bought as a boy and freed, appointed to
command his army and to run his principality until he was old enough to do it
himself. The word means something like father-lord. The institution was
designed to keep a fragmenting empire in the family.

It did the opposite everywhere it was tried. Duqaq, the Seljuk prince of
Damascus, died in 1104, and his atabeg Toghtekin — a freedman of Duqaq's father
Tutush — did not hand the city to the heir. He took it, married the prince's
mother, and founded a dynasty named for his son Buri.

Twelfth-century Syria produced that move four times over — Toghtekin at
Damascus in 1104, Zangi at Mosul in 1127, Nur al-Din's officers after him, and
finally Saladin taking Damascus from Nur al-Din's own heirs in 1174. The Burids
are the first of them, and they make the pattern legible: an institution built
so that a slave commander would hold a state in trust for a boy discovered,
immediately, that a slave commander holding a state has no reason to give it
back. It is the same mechanism the Bahriyya used in 1250 and the Ghaznavids
used in 962, running one level of the hierarchy down.

For the region's record the arrival matters for a second reason. Toghtekin's
army was a purchased Turkish askar. The Arab and Kurdish emirates that had held
Syria and the Jazira before him — Mirdasid, Uqaylid, Marwanid — raised their
horsemen through kinship, and none of them survived the century. With the
Burids the slave soldiers come back to the Syrian capitals, and every state
that governs Damascus or Cairo afterwards raises its army the same way — down
to Muhammad Ali's massacre of the Cairo beys in 1811.
