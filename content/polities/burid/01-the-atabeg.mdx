---
title: What an atabeg was
drafted_from: el-azhari-1997
phase: formation
---

When the Great Seljuk empire divided Syria among princes of the house in the
1090s, each young prince was given an atabeg: a senior military officer,
generally a Turkish mamluk who had been bought as a boy and freed, appointed to
command his army and to run his principality until he was old enough to do it
himself. The word means something like father-lord. The institution was designed
to keep a fragmenting empire in the family.

It did the opposite everywhere it was tried. Duqaq, the Seljuk prince of
Damascus, died in 1104, and his atabeg Toghtekin — a freedman of Duqaq's father
Tutush — did not hand the city to the heir. He took it, married the prince's
mother, and founded a dynasty named for his son Buri.

This site has now recorded that move four times: Toghtekin at Damascus in 1104,
Zangi at Mosul in 1127, Nur al-Din's officers, and finally Saladin taking
Damascus from Nur al-Din's own heirs in 1174. The Burids are the first of them
and the reason the pattern is legible: an institution built so that a slave
commander would hold a state in trust for a boy discovered, immediately, that a
slave commander holding a state has no reason to give it back. It is the same
mechanism the Bahriyya used in 1250 and the Ghaznavids used in 962, running one
level of the hierarchy down.

For the region's record the arrival matters for a second reason. Toghtekin's
army was a purchased Turkish askar. The three emirates on this site's preceding
pages — Mirdasid, Uqaylid, Marwanid — raised Arab and Kurdish horsemen through
kinship, and none of them survived the century. With the Burids the slave
soldiers come back to this region's capitals, and every state that governs
Damascus or Cairo afterwards raises its army the same way — down to Muhammad
Ali's massacre of the Cairo beys in 1811.
