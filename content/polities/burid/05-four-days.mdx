The Second Crusade came for Damascus, and the chronicler who wrote the best
account of it was inside the walls.

The armies had wintered badly, lost men to disease and hunger, and quarrelled
over where to go. They settled on Damascus, and Ibn al-Qalanisi says they were
so confident of taking it that they had already planned out how the city's
estates and districts would be divided.

Unur, who governed Damascus, prepared in the way a man does who knows what the
ground is worth. He fortified the approaches, put guards on the roads and
passes, cut off the supply routes to the enemy's likely camps, filled in the
wells and effaced the watering places.

## Saturday

The crusaders came on with something like fifty thousand horse and foot, and
enough baggage, camels and cattle to swell the column beyond counting. They
made for the usual camping ground south of the city, found no water there
because the water had been taken away, and moved to al-Mizza because it was
near a supply.

The Damascenes came out to meet them on Saturday 24 July 1148 — the regular
troops, the town militia, volunteers, and the *ghazis*, the irregulars who went
wherever there was fighting against the Franks.

They lost. The crusaders had the numbers and the equipment, took control of the
water, spread through the orchards and camped in them, and then pushed up to
the town and held a section of it that Qalanisi says no army ancient or modern
had ever succeeded in holding.

Two men are named for that day, and neither is a soldier. Yusuf al-Findalawi,
the Maliki jurist, was killed by the water near al-Rabwa because he stood in
front of the Franks and would not fall back, in obedience — the chronicler says
— to what God commands in his book. The ascetic Abd al-Rahman al-Halhuli died
the same way.

The Franks spent the night cutting down the orchards and building stockades out
of them, and the city spent it, in Qalanisi's phrase, discouraged and
straitened in spirit through fear of what it had seen.

## Sunday to Wednesday

They came out again on Sunday morning and this time the Damascenes had the
better of it. Unur fought all day and, according to a man who was in the city,
never wearied of it and never took a rest from it.

Meanwhile the letters had gone out. Turkmen cavalry and footmen kept arriving
from the outlying districts. On Monday large numbers of bowmen came in from the
Biqa'. By Tuesday the Damascenes were attacking the camp itself, shooting the
stockades to pieces and stoning them, and the crusaders would not come out at
all; not a man showed himself except a few skirmishers.

By then the townsmen and the villagers had worked out that there was money in
it. They lay in wait along the roads for Franks who thought they were safe,
killed the ones they caught, and brought the heads in to claim the reward. A
great many heads were brought in.

Reports reached the crusader camp that Muslim armies were converging on them.
They took counsel, decided there was no way out of the net they had walked
into, and retreated in disorder at dawn on Wednesday.

Four days.

## What it settled

The largest army Europe sent to Syria in the twelfth century besieged the
largest Muslim city in Syria for four days and went home, and its own
chroniclers spent the next century blaming each other for it.

For Damascus it was the peak and the beginning of the end at once. Unur had
held the city, and he had held it with militia, volunteers, villagers and
archers from the valley, against the allies with whom his government had spent
twenty years buying peace. The policy of paying Jerusalem to keep the Zangids
out did not survive the sight of Jerusalem's king in the orchards.
