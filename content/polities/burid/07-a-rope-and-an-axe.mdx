Nur al-Din took Damascus on Sunday 25 April 1154, and the man who wrote down
how it happened was a Damascene official who had twice been head of the city.

He does not describe a conquest. He describes a building nobody was defending.

## The approach

In January Shirkuh had arrived outside the city as Nur al-Din's envoy with a
thousand men and camped in the meadow. The Burid ruler would not go out to
receive him and refused to meet him; letters passed and settled nothing. The
grain convoys were interrupted and the price of food went up.

Nur al-Din himself came in April, camped east of the city and attacked day
after day. On the Sunday he mustered everything and came on early. The
Damascene troops went out as usual and were pushed back until they were up
against the wall by the Kaisan Gate.

And there was nobody on the wall. Through what Qalanisi calls the bad
management of the authorities, not one soul was up there, troops or townsmen,
apart from a negligible handful of Turks on one tower whose resistance could be
discounted.

## The rope

A foot soldier walked up to the wall. On the wall was a Jewish woman, who let
down a rope. He climbed it, got a footing unnoticed and was followed by others.
Nur al-Din's men planted a standard on the wall and shouted *ya Mansur*.

At that point, Qalanisi writes, the troops and the citizens stopped resisting —
because of the affection they had for Nur al-Din, and his justice and his good
reputation.

A woodcutter ran to the East Gate with his axe and broke the bolts. The gate
came open, Nur al-Din's army walked in and went through the main streets at
speed, and not one man opposed them. Then the Thomas Gate was opened too. Nur
al-Din came in with his household officers to the joy of all the people, troops
and soldiers alike — because they had been hungry, because food had been dear,
and because they were afraid of being besieged by the Franks.

The last Burid fled.

## What that is a picture of

A dynasty of Turkish atabegs had governed Damascus for fifty years by holding
the citadel, paying the kingdom of Jerusalem to stay away, and keeping the
militia on side. In the end the militia let the other man in.

The two people who actually opened the city are the ones to keep. Neither is a
soldier, neither is named, and one of them is a Jewish woman who let a rope
down to a besieger. A city changed hands because the people in it had decided
which government they preferred, and the wall of Damascus turned out to be
exactly as strong as their opinion of the men behind it.

Nothing was destroyed and nobody was punished. The Burids had been out-argued
rather than defeated, and their own subjects were the ones who settled it.
