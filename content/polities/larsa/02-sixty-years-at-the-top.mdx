Rim-Sin I inherited a kingdom already ascendant, and held it for sixty
years - one of the longest reigns of the whole ancient Near East. By
the time his rivalry with Isin reached its decisive turn, the older
city's own dynasty had already run its course under other, largely
unrecorded circumstances, and the way was open for Rim-Sin to absorb
Isin's territory and its old royal claim into a single southern
kingdom answering to Larsa alone. For a generation, Larsa was
unambiguously the dominant power of the whole Sumerian heartland,
its king outlasting rival after rival elsewhere in Mesopotamia
without serious challenge.

## An old man's kingdom, and a young rival's patience

What made Rim-Sin's long reign eventually a liability rather than an
asset was precisely how settled it had become. Babylon, under a king
named Hammurabi, spent the first twenty-nine years of his own reign as
one Amorite ruler among several, making alliances, fighting occasional
local wars, and building nothing that looked like the beginning of an
empire. Rim-Sin, secure in a dominance that had gone unchallenged for
decades, had no particular reason to treat the northern kingdom as a
serious rival - the imbalance of experience and attention between an
aging, unchallenged hegemon and a patient neighbor still finding his
own footing was, in hindsight, exactly the condition Hammurabi needed.

## A crown that outlasted its own relevance

Rim-Sin's sixty-year reign is itself part of what makes his kingdom's
sudden collapse so striking: nothing in the length or stability of his
rule gave any warning of how quickly it would end. A dominance built
patiently over six decades turned out to rest on an assumption -
that Babylon would remain what it had always been - that stopped
being true in a single campaign.
