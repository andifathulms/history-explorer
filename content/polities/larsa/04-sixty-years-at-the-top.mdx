Rim-Sin I held the throne for sixty years - one of the longest reigns of
the whole ancient Near East - and for a full generation after absorbing
Isin's own territory and title, Larsa was unambiguously the dominant power
of the whole Sumerian heartland, its king outlasting rival after rival
elsewhere in Mesopotamia without serious challenge.

## An old man's kingdom, and a young rival's patience

What made Rim-Sin's long reign eventually a liability rather than an
asset was precisely how settled it had become. Babylon, under a king
named Hammurabi, spent the first twenty-nine years of his own reign as
one Amorite ruler among several, making alliances, fighting occasional
local wars, and building nothing that looked like the beginning of an
empire. Rim-Sin, secure in a dominance that had gone unchallenged for
decades, had no particular reason to treat the northern kingdom as a
serious rival - the imbalance of experience and attention between an
aging, unchallenged hegemon and a patient neighbor still finding his
own footing was, in hindsight, exactly the condition Hammurabi needed.

## What the imbalance actually was

Rim-Sin's own advantage over Babylon, for most of his reign, was real: a
united south answering to one throne against a northern kingdom that was,
for three decades, simply one Amorite state among several. What changed
was not Larsa's own strength, which the record shows no sign of eroding,
but the use Hammurabi eventually made of the years he had spent being
underestimated - a subject the chapter that follows takes up directly.
