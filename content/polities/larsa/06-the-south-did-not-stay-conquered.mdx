Hammurabi's absorption of Larsa and the rest of the south looks, from the
vantage point of his own forty-three-year reign, like the permanent end of
independent rule in Sumer. It was not. Southern Mesopotamia broke away
again within a generation of the conquest that supposedly settled the
question, and stayed away for far longer than Larsa itself had ever
managed.

## What Babylonian rule actually looked like in the south

In the years immediately after Rim-Sin's defeat, Hammurabi governed the
former Larsa kingdom as conquered territory rather than as a partner: the
south's own administrative traditions, its temple establishments and its
scribal culture continued under Babylonian oversight, much as similar
institutions had continued at Ur and other southern cities under whichever
power happened to be collecting their taxes that generation. Larsa itself
does not disappear from the record; it becomes a provincial city rather
than a capital, governed for Babylon rather than governing anyone else.

## Samsu-iluna's south

That arrangement did not survive Hammurabi's own son. Under Samsu-iluna,
within a few decades of his father's conquest, the far south broke away
from Babylonian control under a native ruler named Ilum-ma-ili and became
what is known as the Sealand kingdom — named for the marshy country at the
head of the Persian Gulf where it was based. It remained independent of
Babylon for over two and a half centuries, considerably longer than
Larsa's own kingdom had lasted from Gungunum to Rim-Sin.

The Sealand kingdom was not a restoration of Larsa's own dynasty or of
Rim-Sin's line specifically; it was a new native assertion of southern
independence, rising in the same country and against the same northern
power that had ended Larsa's own run. What Hammurabi's conquest actually
achieved, from the perspective of the following two and a half centuries,
was not the permanent end of an independent south but one more chapter in
the same contest for the region that Gungunum had opened when he broke
from Isin: the north taking the south, the south eventually reasserting
itself, under whichever family and whatever name the moment produced.

## What that means for how the conquest should be read

Hammurabi's own reign is where the record of an independent Larsa ends,
and that ending is real: no source afterward names a king of Larsa as
Rim-Sin's line's own successor. But the larger claim, that southern
Mesopotamia's political independence ended with it, does not hold up
against what the south did next. The region's habit of producing its own
kings, broken by Hammurabi for a generation, resumed under a different
name almost as soon as Babylon's own attention moved elsewhere.
