title: An empire of magnates

This record codes `client-levy` for the army and null for the succession, and
the two together are the whole structure of the Parthian state.

There was no Parthian bureaucracy in the Achaemenid or Sasanian sense. What
there was instead was a king of kings over an assortment of things: great noble
houses — the Suren, the Karen, the Mihran — holding hereditary territories and
hereditary offices with their own armed followings; vassal kingdoms in Persis,
Elymais, Characene, Adiabene, Media Atropatene and Armenia, each with its own
dynasty and coinage; Greek cities keeping their assemblies and their civic
institutions; and nomadic groups on the eastern frontier under their own chiefs.

The army was the sum of what those parties supplied. The most famous Parthian
victory in history was won by a force raised and commanded by the head of one
noble house — Surena at Carrhae, with cataphracts and horse archers and a
baggage train of arrows — and the king had him executed shortly afterwards,
which tells you exactly what the relationship was.

This corpus has a name for the resulting condition and has used it about several
polities: an army that can make a ruler will eventually make one it prefers. The
Parthian version is chronic rather than acute. Arsacid civil wars are more or
less continuous from the first century BC, with rival kings coining
simultaneously, and the great houses backing candidates on their own account;
Rome repeatedly sponsored claimants, and the frontier wars are frequently
interventions in a succession rather than campaigns of conquest.

It is also why the empire lasted four hundred and seventy years. A state that is
a coalition cannot be destroyed by defeating its king, because the coalition
survives him — and Rome demonstrated that three times by taking Ctesiphon and
finding that nothing followed. Trajan in 116, Lucius Verus' generals in 165,
Septimius Severus in 198: three sacks of the capital, no conquest.

The end came from inside the structure, which is the only place it could come
from. Ardashir was a sub-king in Persis, one of the vassal dynasties, and in 224
he killed Artabanus IV in battle and took the empire. That is `dynastic
replacement` on this record and `formation` on the next.
