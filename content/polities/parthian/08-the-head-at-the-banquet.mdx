The Parthians were forgotten in Iran and remembered in Europe, and what Europe
remembered was one afternoon in 53 BC and its aftermath. Plutarch's account of
that aftermath has been retold for two thousand years as an atrocity story. Read
slowly, it is evidence of something quite different.

## The triumph at Seleucia

Surena, who had won the battle, staged a mock triumph in Seleucia. He dressed a
prisoner named Caius Paccianus, chosen because he looked most like Crassus, in a
woman's dress of the barbarian fashion, sat him on a horse, and had him answer
to the name of Crassus and to the title Imperator. Trumpeters and lictors went
in front on camels. Purses hung from the ends of the rods, and the heads of the
Roman dead were fixed on the axes. Behind came the singing women of Seleucia
with obscene songs about Crassus's cowardice.

Then Surena called the city's senate together and produced the pornography.
Certain wanton books by Aristides of Miletus had been found in the baggage of a
Roman officer, and Surena laid them before the assembly as a comment on the
sort of people who could not leave such things behind even on campaign.

Plutarch, who disapproves of everybody, notes that the Seleucians were entitled
to think of Aesop's fable of the wallet, since the general carrying a bag of
Milesian stories in front of him had a whole train of wagons of concubines and
lutes and castanets behind him — and adds that the Parthians had forgotten,
while mocking Milesian literature, that a good many of the royal Arsacid line
had been born of Milesian and Ionian mistresses.

That is a joke about the ancestry of the ruling house, and it only works if the
house was as Greek as Plutarch thought it was.

## The play

Meanwhile Orodes, the king of kings, had made peace with Artavasdes of Armenia
and married his son Pacorus to the Armenian king's sister, and the two courts
were feasting.

Plutarch stops to explain the company. Orodes knew Greek language and
literature. Artavasdes was expert enough in it to write tragedies, orations and
histories, some of which, Plutarch says, were still extant in his own day. The
entertainment was Greek, and the performer was a tragic actor from Tralles named
Jason, who was singing the scene from Euripides' *Bacchae* in which Agave comes
on carrying the head of her son.

The head of Crassus arrived at the door as the tables were being cleared.
Sillaces made his obeisance and threw it into the middle of the room. Jason
handed the costume of Pentheus to one of the chorus, picked up the head of the
Roman triumvir, and went on with the part —

> We've hunted down a mighty chase to-day,
> And from the mountain bring the noble prey

— to great applause. When the dialogue reached the line asking whose hand had
killed the beast, Pomaxathres, who had done it, stood up from the table and
claimed the answer for himself. The king was delighted and gave presents. Jason
got a talent.

## What the story is actually evidence of

The scene has been recycled for two millennia as proof of oriental barbarity.
Look at what it requires.

It requires a Parthian king who follows Euripides in the original. It requires
an Armenian king who writes Greek tragedy himself. It requires a professional
Greek actor on tour and employed at a court in Mesopotamia, a chorus with
costumes, an audience that knows the play well enough to recognise a
substitution in the middle of a scene and to enjoy it, and a Parthian nobleman
who can catch a cue in Greek verse and answer it in character.

Nobody stages that improvisation for people who need it explained.

The Sasanians would later write the Parthians out of the history of Iran as an
age of petty kings with no culture worth recording, and European scholarship
took roughly the same view from the other end — a feudal, derivative interlude
between two real empires. The single most famous Parthian scene in all of
Western literature is a refutation of both, and it has been read for two
thousand years as a confirmation, because the head in it is easier to remember
than the play.

## And afterwards

Plutarch finishes the account with the fate of the men in the room, and it is
worth having, because it is the same story the Arsacid dynasty told about itself
for the next two and a half centuries.

Surena was put to death by Orodes not long after, out of envy at his victory.
Orodes lost his son Pacorus in a battle with the Romans, fell ill with dropsy,
and was given aconite by his second son Phraates; the poison happened to cure
the dropsy, so Phraates strangled him instead.

An empire that Rome could not beat spent four hundred and seventy years being
run by a family that could not stop killing itself, and it still outlasted
everyone who wrote it down.
