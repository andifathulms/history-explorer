---
title: Taking Iran, and meeting two empires
drafted_from: overtoom-2020
phase: expansion
---

Mithridates I took Media in about 148 and Seleucia on the Tigris in 141, and the
second of those is the one that mattered. Babylonia is the richest agricultural
country between the Nile and the Indus, and taking it converted a dynasty
holding a plateau into the government of an empire.

The Seleucids tried twice to get it back. Demetrius II invaded, was defeated and
captured, and spent ten years as an honoured prisoner married to a Parthian
princess. Antiochus VII invaded in 130 with a large army, won two battles,
wintered his troops dispersed across Media, and was killed with most of them the
following spring. That is the end of Seleucid Iran.

Mithridates II, who ruled from about 124 to 88, is where the empire reaches its
height. He put down the nomadic incursions on the eastern frontier that had killed two of
his predecessors, took Mesopotamia as far as the Euphrates, imposed Parthian
suzerainty on Armenia, and took the title king of kings. That was the
Achaemenid style and it was claimed deliberately: a Parni chief's descendant
announcing himself the heir of Darius.

He also met both of the other great powers in the world within a few years of
each other, and both encounters can be dated.

In about 121 an embassy from Han China reached him. Zhang Qian's missions west
had opened the route, and the Chinese sources record the Parthians — whom they
call Anxi, after Arsaces — receiving the envoys on the frontier with twenty
thousand cavalry as a display, and sending an embassy back with ostrich eggs and
conjurors.

In about 96 a Parthian envoy met Sulla on the Euphrates, and Rome and Parthia
were formally in contact for the first time. Within forty-five years Crassus was
dead at Carrhae.

Sitting between those two facts is how the empire made its money. Parthia was
the land route between Rome and China for three centuries. It took a share of
everything that crossed, and — as the Chinese sources complain — took care that
the two ends never dealt with each other directly.
