---
title: Taking Iran, and meeting two empires
drafted_from: overtoom-2020
phase: expansion
---

Mithridates I took Media in about 148 and Seleucia on the Tigris in 141, and
this record's first turning point is the second of those. Babylonia is the
richest agricultural country between the Nile and the Indus, and taking it
converted a dynasty holding a plateau into the government of an empire.

The Seleucids tried twice to get it back. Demetrius II invaded, was defeated and
captured, and spent ten years as an honoured prisoner married to a Parthian
princess. Antiochus VII invaded in 130 with a large army, won two battles,
wintered his troops dispersed across Media, and was killed with most of them the
following spring. That is the end of Seleucid Iran.

Mithridates II, who ruled from about 124 to 88, is the peak on this record. He
put down the nomadic incursions on the eastern frontier that had killed two of
his predecessors, took Mesopotamia as far as the Euphrates, imposed Parthian
suzerainty on Armenia, and took the title king of kings — the Achaemenid style,
claimed deliberately, and the reason this site's Achaemenid record counts the
Parthians among its two successor claims.

He also met both of the other great powers of the world, within a few years of
each other, and the corpus can date it.

In about 121 an embassy from Han China reached him. Zhang Qian's missions west
had opened the route, and the Chinese sources record the Parthians — whom they
call Anxi, after Arsaces — receiving the envoys on the frontier with twenty
thousand cavalry as a display, and sending an embassy back with ostrich eggs and
conjurors. This site's Han record is the other end of that exchange.

In about 96 a Parthian envoy met Sulla on the Euphrates, and Rome and Parthia
were formally in contact for the first time. Within forty-five years Crassus was
dead at Carrhae.

Sitting between those two facts is the empire's economic basis and this record's
`trade-toll` coding. Parthia was the land route between Rome and China for three
centuries, took a share of everything that crossed, and — as the Chinese sources
complain — took care that the two ends never dealt with each other directly.
