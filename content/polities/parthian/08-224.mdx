The second century was, on the Roman side, a century of victories. Trajan took
Ctesiphon in 116; Lucius Verus' generals sacked it in 165 and brought a plague
back that killed a substantial part of the Roman empire's population; Septimius
Severus sacked it again in 198 and reportedly took a hundred thousand captives.
Rome annexed northern Mesopotamia and kept it.

None of it destroyed the Parthian state, and all of it damaged the Arsacids'
standing. A king of kings who cannot keep his capital is a king of kings the
great houses and the vassal dynasties have reason to reconsider, and the civil
wars of the early third century were unusually severe — Vologases VI and
Artabanus IV coining simultaneously and fighting each other while a Roman
emperor was campaigning in the country.

Into that came Ardashir, sub-king of Persis: one of the vassal dynasties on
which the whole structure rested, ruling the province the Achaemenids had come
from, with its own coinage, its own fire temples and a family claim going back
to a priest called Sasan. He took the neighbouring kingdoms, refused tribute,
and in 224 met Artabanus IV in battle at Hormozdgan and killed him.

<Figure id="ardashir-investiture" />

It was a replacement of one dynasty by another and nothing more than that. There
was no foreign conquest and no collapse. One of the empire's own
component kingdoms took the throne, kept Ctesiphon as the capital, kept the
title king of kings, kept the frontier on the Euphrates, and kept fighting Rome
for another four centuries.

What changed was everything about how it was held. The Sasanians centralised
what the Arsacids had distributed: a state church, a land survey, a fixed tax, a
paid army, a bureaucracy. It is the more impressive machine by a distance, and
it lasted two hundred years less.
