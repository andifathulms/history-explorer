title: The Parni
drafted_from: overtoom-2020

Around 247 BC a people called the Parni, from the steppe east of the Caspian,
moved into the Seleucid satrapy of Parthia and took it. Their leader was called
Arsaces, and every subsequent king of the dynasty took the name as a title,
which is why the Greek and Roman sources call them all Arsaces and why the
chronology is difficult.

Overtoom's book is the first full study of this period since 1938 and its
argument is a reframing worth carrying. The Parthians are conventionally
introduced as Rome's eastern enemy, which is where the surviving sources put
them and which begins two centuries after this chapter. What they actually were
for their first hundred and fifty years is a Hellenistic power among Hellenistic
powers — a state that emerged in the wreckage of Seleucid authority alongside
Bactria, Pergamon, Pontus and the rest, competed in the same system, used the
same diplomatic vocabulary, and won.

The circumstances made it possible. The Seleucid empire on the previous record
was fighting the Ptolemies over Coele-Syria and had a satrap in Bactria who had
already stopped acknowledging the king; the eastern provinces were four months
from Antioch, and the answer to a lapsed province was an expedition when one
could be spared. Antiochus III spared one, conducted the great anabasis between
212 and 205, obtained the Parthian king's submission, and left him where he was.

That is the pattern of the first century: the Parthians take a province, the
Seleucids come, the Parthians submit, the Seleucids leave, the submission
lapses. It is not a war of conquest and it is barely a war. It is a slow
transfer of authority conducted through repeated acknowledgements of a suzerain
who cannot stay.

Then in the 140s Mithridates I stopped submitting and took Media, and then
Babylonia, and the arrangement ended.
