---
title: Carrhae, and the shape of a stalemate
drafted_from: chi-3
phase: peak
---

In 53 BC Marcus Licinius Crassus took seven legions across the Euphrates and was
destroyed at Carrhae by a Parthian force perhaps a fifth the size. The Roman
infantry could not close with horse archers who withdrew as they shot, and could
not withstand the armoured lancers who charged when the formation tightened
against the arrows. Crassus was killed; the legionary standards were kept in
Parthian hands for thirty-three years, and their return by negotiation in 20 BC
was advertised in Rome as a triumph because it was the best available outcome.

The battle set a pattern that held for two and a half centuries and is the
reason both empires are on this site with frontiers that barely moved. Rome could
invade Mesopotamia, and did, repeatedly — Trajan reached the Persian Gulf in 116.
It could not hold what it took, because supply lines across northern Mesopotamia
were vulnerable to exactly the cavalry that had beaten Crassus. Parthia could
raid Syria and could not take fortified cities in numbers.

Two empires that could hurt each other and could not finish each other produced
something neither intended: a stable frontier, a hostage exchange and embassy
protocol, and a trade route across it that carried Chinese silk to Rome through
Parthian middlemen who had every incentive to keep both ends ignorant of each
other. The Han envoy on this site's Han page who turned back at the Persian Gulf
in 97 was talked out of continuing by people whose living depended on the
distance.
