---
title: Erased by their successors
drafted_from: chi-3
phase: aside
---

The Achaemenids were described almost entirely by their enemies. Alexander was
described by nobody who was there. The Parthians have both of those problems and
a third that is worse than either.

They left very little writing. There is no Parthian history, no chronicle, no
court literature that survives; what exists is coins in enormous quantity,
parchments and ostraca from Nisa and Dura-Europos, inscriptions, and
archaeology. Everything narrative comes from Greek and Roman writers describing
an enemy across a frontier, or from Chinese envoys' reports, or from Armenian and
Jewish sources with their own interests.

The third problem is that their successors removed them.

The Sasanians, who took the empire in 224, constructed a royal ideology that ran
from the mythical Kayanian kings of the Avestan tradition directly to themselves,
and the Parthians did not appear in it as an empire. The Iranian national
tradition that comes down through the *Shahnama* treats the four hundred and
seventy years between Alexander and Ardashir as the age of the *muluk
al-tawa'if* — the party kings, the petty kings — a period of division with no
central sovereign worth naming. Firdawsi disposes of it in a few hundred lines
and says explicitly that there is little to tell.

Four and a half centuries — longer than the Sasanians who replaced them, longer
than the Achaemenids they claimed descent from, longer than anybody else has
ever governed Iran — compressed into a gap in the national story.

The habit persisted in modern scholarship, which for a long time treated the
Parthians as a loose interlude between two real Iranian empires — feudal,
unadministered, culturally derivative. Overtoom's book belongs to a body of work
arguing against that, on the grounds that the judgement was not arrived at
independently: it was inherited from the people who overthrew them, and repeated
for seventeen hundred years by scholars reading sources the Sasanians had
curated.

That is worth pausing on, because it is a rare thing to be able to watch. Almost
every dynasty invents a claim to a predecessor; the Parthians did it themselves,
to the Achaemenids. What happened here is the opposite operation and it is much
harder to detect afterwards. A regime did not merely fail to claim its
predecessor — it removed him from the account of the country, replaced him with
a period of anarchy, and the substitution held so well that it was still the
scholarly consensus in the twentieth century.
