---
title: Erased by their successors
drafted_from: chi-3
phase: aside
---

The Achaemenid record on this site carries an aside about being described by its
enemies. The Macedonian record carries one about having no contemporary sources
at all. The Parthians have both problems and a third that is worse.

They left very little writing. There is no Parthian history, no chronicle, no
court literature that survives; what exists is coins in enormous quantity,
parchments and ostraca from Nisa and Dura-Europos, inscriptions, and
archaeology. Everything narrative comes from Greek and Roman writers describing
an enemy across a frontier, or from Chinese envoys' reports, or from Armenian and
Jewish sources with their own interests.

The third problem is that their successors removed them.

The Sasanians, who took the empire in 224, constructed a royal ideology that ran
from the mythical Kayanian kings of the Avestan tradition directly to themselves,
and the Parthians did not appear in it as an empire. The Iranian national
tradition that comes down through the *Shahnama* treats the four hundred and
seventy years between Alexander and Ardashir as the age of the *muluk
al-tawa'if* — the party kings, the petty kings — a period of division with no
central sovereign worth naming. Firdawsi disposes of it in a few hundred lines
and says explicitly that there is little to tell.

Four and a half centuries, the longest tenure of any polity in this site's
Iranian regions, longer than the Sasanians who replaced them and longer than the
Achaemenids they claimed descent from, compressed into a gap.

The habit persisted in modern scholarship, which for a long time treated the
Parthians as a loose interlude between two real Iranian empires — feudal,
unadministered, culturally derivative. Overtoom's book is part of a body of work
arguing against that, and this site follows it, because the alternative is to
record a Sasanian propaganda decision as a historical judgement.

The corpus should be able to say when it is doing that, and this page is the
clearest instance it has. The Sasanian record's `successor_claims` reads 1 and
this one's reads null; the Achaemenid record counts both of them as claimants on
it. Whose legitimacy was worth claiming is a measurable thing on this site. Whose
legitimacy was worth erasing is not, and it happened here.
