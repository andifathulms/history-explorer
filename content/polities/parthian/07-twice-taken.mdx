Ctesiphon was captured by Roman emperors in 116 and again in 198, and on both
occasions the Romans went home. Cassius Dio, who was a senator under the second
of them, is unusually direct about what that meant, and the answer is not what
either triumph claimed.

## Trajan, 116

Trajan crossed the Tigris and entered Ctesiphon, was saluted *imperator*,
established his right to the title Parthicus, and was voted by the senate,
among other honours, the privilege of celebrating as many triumphs as he might
desire.

Then he went down to the sea, because he wanted to. Mesene, the island kingdom in
the Tigris, came over without difficulty. He was nearly wrecked by a storm and
the backward flow from the ocean. The people of the palisade of Spasinus received
him kindly.

And at the shore of the Persian Gulf he watched a ship setting out for India, and
said — Dio gives it as direct speech —

> I should certainly have crossed over to the Indi, if I were still young.

He was sixty-three. He gave much thought to the Indians and was curious about
their affairs, and within two years the provinces he had just annexed had revolted
and he was dead, and his successor gave them all back.

## Severus, 198

Eighty years later Septimius Severus came down the Euphrates on newly built boats
— the timber was abundant along the river — and found Seleucia and Babylon
*abandoned*, which is its own comment on where the population of Mesopotamia had
gone.

Then Ctesiphon. He took it, turned his soldiers loose on the whole town, and Dio
records the result plainly: a great slaughter of men, and nearly a hundred
thousand taken alive.

And then the sentence that is the point of the chapter:

> However, he did not pursue Vologaesus nor yet occupy Ctesiphon, but as if the
> sole purpose of his campaign had been to plunder it, he thereupon departed.

Dio gives the reasons without softening them: ignorance of the country, and lack
of provisions. The return had to be made by a different route because the wood
and fodder on the first one were used up.

## Hatra

Immediately afterwards Severus attacked Hatra, a caravan city in the Mesopotamian
steppe, and accomplished nothing. His siege engines were burned, many soldiers
died, and vast numbers were wounded. He withdrew.

The emperor who had just sacked the Parthian capital could not take a fortified
Arab town a few days' march away.

## What it did to Parthia

The obvious reading is that Parthia was being beaten. The more useful one is that
both empires were discovering the same thing about this frontier.

Rome could reach Ctesiphon whenever it committed an emperor and a field army to
doing so. Rome could not hold anything past the Euphrates, because the logistics
were beyond it — Dio says so about Severus in as many words, and Trajan's
annexations were abandoned within two years of his death.

Parthia, for its part, survived both sacks and neither of them killed the
dynasty. But the capital had been taken twice in a century, a hundred thousand of
its people had been carried off in one afternoon, Seleucia and Babylon were empty,
and the vassal kingdoms had watched the king of kings fail to defend the centre.

What destroyed the Arsacids twenty-six years after Severus left was not a Roman
army. It was a vassal from Persis who had drawn the obvious conclusion.
