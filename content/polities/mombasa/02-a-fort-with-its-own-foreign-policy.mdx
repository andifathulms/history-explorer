Independence meant Mombasa now had rivals of its own choosing rather
than an Imam's enemies to inherit. A dispute over the division of
Pemba's tax revenue set the Mazaru'i against Pate: Pate seized
Kilindini and besieged the fort before the two sides came to terms
and Pate withdrew. Emboldened, the Mazaru'i then sent an expedition
against Zanzibar itself and had all but taken the island when the
governorship's own fragility showed through - intrigue broke out
among the family, and Ali bin Osman, the man who had founded the
independent governorship only a few years before, was murdered by his
own nephew. The Zanzibar expedition, leaderless, withdrew.

Pate's own internal troubles gave Mombasa a second opening. The
Mazaru'i sent an expedition to the island and forced it to accept a
Mazaru'i governor - who was afterward thrown out again - and decades
later, in 1807, did the same more durably: Mazaru'i troops helped
install Sultan Ahmed on Pate's throne and placed a governor of their
own, Ali bin Abdallah, alongside him. The faction that had favoured a
rival claimant, Fumoluti, fled to Lamu, and when the Mazaru'i followed
up with an attack on Lamu itself, they were repulsed.

## An admission, once, that the Imam still mattered

Not every encounter went Mombasa's way, and not every governor
pretended otherwise. When Ahmed, a son of the reigning Imam Sa'id,
visited Mombasa incognito and was recognised at the fort, he asked
outright to whom the town belonged. The governor answered, out of
courtesy more than conviction, "To the Imam of Maskat" - and Ahmed,
taking him at his word, asked for the statement in writing and got
it. It cost Mombasa nothing at the time: no tribute followed, no
garrison arrived, and the fort's governors went on ruling exactly as
they had before. But the paper existed, and Oman would remember it
existed for as long as it suited them to.

Lamu, meanwhile, drew its own conclusion from the Mazaru'i attack it
had survived. Fearing a second one, it appealed directly to Imam
Sa'id for protection. In 1811 Sa'id answered by sending a governor of
his own to Lamu and building a fort there - the first permanent Omani
foothold on the coast north of Mombasa since the Mazaru'i had made
themselves independent, and a signal that the family's freedom of
action was no longer something its neighbours would simply accept
without looking for help against it.
