Without the British flag, Sa'id came in force. In 1828 he sailed for
Mombasa in person aboard the Liverpool with twelve hundred men, and
through a mixture of intimidation, bribery and intrigue he obtained
the fort and installed his own garrison. He then turned to sail for
Zanzibar - but was called back to Oman by internal trouble before he
could consolidate the gain, and while his fleet was away the Mazaru'i,
with the town's own people behind them, besieged and retook the fort
before Sa'id's reinforcements could arrive.

He returned the following year, 1829, with a second fleet of fourteen
hundred men and fought for seven or eight days without breaking the
defence. Renewed trouble in Arabia forced him to make peace with
Mombasa and withdraw a second time. Twice in two years Sa'id had
brought the largest force Oman could spare against a single Swahili
fort, and twice he had been turned back.

## Betrayed at Kilindini and at Zanzibar

What neither siege could take, division inside the fort eventually
gave away. Sa'id spent the intervening years intriguing with the
people of Kilindini and cultivating dissension among the Mazaru'i
themselves, and in 1837 the strategy paid off: factions within the
family agreed to hand over the fort, and Sa'id installed a garrison
of five hundred Arabs and Baluchis in its place.

He then invited Rashid, the Mazaru'i governor, to visit him at
Zanzibar and pressed him to surrender Mafia Island or pay an annual
tribute. Rashid refused and sailed home - but he and about twenty of
the leading men of the family were seized by Sa'id's agents before
they could leave, carried to Zanzibar, and from there deported in
chains to Bandar Abbas on the Persian coast. The source credits the
deportation with removing "the last seeds of rebellion" from the East
African coast. Mombasa submitted without further resistance, and
Sa'id went on, over the years that followed, to extend his rule down
the coast as far as Cape Delgado - the empire the Mazaru'i had spent
a century keeping out of their own harbour.
