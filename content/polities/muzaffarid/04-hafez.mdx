Khwaja Shams al-Din Muhammad Hafez-i Shirazi lived in Shiraz from about 1315 to
1390, left the city rarely and perhaps never for long, wrote about five hundred
ghazals, and is the poet Persian speakers quote. His divan is used for
bibliomancy — opened at random for guidance — in houses that own no other book
of poetry, and his tomb in Shiraz is a place people go.

This corpus has been recording, page after page, that political weight and
cultural output come apart. This is the extreme case, and it runs in the
direction the site keeps finding. The Muzaffarid dynasty lasted about eighty
years, never controlled more than a corner of Iran, produced no institution, was
exterminated by Timur, and would be an entry in a genealogical manual and
nothing more. It also paid the household expenses of the man who wrote the
poems.

Hafez is on this page rather than on any other because he had no other patrons.
He wrote under the Injuids and praised Abu Ishaq; he lived through Mubariz
al-Din's prohibition and wrote about it; he was patronised by Shah Shuja and by
Shah Mansur and by the local notables and viziers around them. He was invited to
India and to the Deccan and did not go. Almost every political circumstance he
alludes to is a Shiraz circumstance, which is what makes the divan usable as
evidence for this page at all — and what makes it dangerous to use that way,
because a ghazal is not a report and the tradition of reading the poems as coded
political commentary is much younger than the poems.

What the site cannot do is put him in a field. There is no `poets` count, no
`literature` axis, and there should not be one: it would need a rule applicable
to sixty polities, and the corpus has no way to compare a Persian lyric
tradition with a Chinese historiographical one that would not be an opinion
wearing a number. The influence counts on this record are all null, correctly.
The largest thing the Muzaffarids did is in a chapter, which is where the
coding rules say it belongs when the vocabulary has no honest home for it.
