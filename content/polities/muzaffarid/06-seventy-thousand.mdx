Timur came into Fars in the autumn of 1387 for a stated reason: Zayn al-Abidin
had been summoned to appear before him and had refused.

Isfahan was on the road. He entered it and levied a heavy contribution, which
provoked a riot in which a good many of his tax-collectors and agents were
killed. What he did about that has a date attached to it — Monday, 18 November
1387 — and a figure. The massacre was general; the heads were counted, and the
count that comes down through the chronicles is seventy thousand; and they were
built into minarets. Browne, who is following Sharaf al-Din Yazdi's *Zafarnama*
here, gives all three details in one sentence and does not embellish them.

The figure should be held the way any such figure has to be held. It is a round
number produced by the winning side, in a book written for the conqueror's
grandson, and the counting of heads is itself part of the performance rather
than an audit. What is not in doubt is the method, because it recurs: Miranshah
put down a Khurasan rebellion two years later and had ten thousand heads built
into pyramids at Tus, and the Kartid ruler of Herat had done the same thing at
Badghis before Timur was old enough to campaign. The towers are the region's
existing grammar of terror, used by Timur at a scale nobody else reached.

Shiraz submitted the following month and was not touched.

It is on that occasion — December 1387 — that the meeting between Timur and
Hafez is supposed to have taken place, and it is worth stopping on it, because
it is the single most repeated story about this dynasty's city and it is almost
certainly untrue. In the version Dawlatshah gives, Timur sends for the poet and
upbraids him for the line offering to give away Samarkand and Bukhara for a
Shirazi Turk's mole: I have subdued most of the habitable globe with this sword
to embellish those two cities, and you would sell them both for a mole. And
Hafez bows and answers that it is precisely such extravagance that has reduced
him to his present condition, and Timur is so delighted that he pays him.
Browne points out that Dawlatshah dates the interview to 795/1392-3, by which
time Hafez had been dead four years; that no contemporary biographer of Timur,
Sharaf al-Din Yazdi included, mentions any such meeting; and that he has found
no trustworthy evidence for it whatever. He also records a second version he
heard in Persia and never met in a book, in which Hafez says he has been
misquoted and what he actually wrote was that he would give for the mole on her
cheek two maunds of sugar and three dates.

What actually happened in Fars in those weeks is less quotable and more useful.
Zayn al-Abidin fled to his cousin Shah Mansur at Shushtar, who put him in prison
— violating, as Browne notes, the claims of kinship and hospitality at the same
time. Most of the other princes of the house came to Shiraz and submitted.
Then a courier reached Timur from Samarkand, having covered the distance in
seventeen days, with news that Tuqtamish had risen behind him, and in February
1388 he went home — taking the scholar al-Sharif al-Jurjani with him as part of
the spoil, and leaving Shah Yahya at Shiraz, Sultan Muhammad at Isfahan, Sultan
Ahmad at Kerman and Abu Ishaq at Sirjan.

He had been in the province four months. He had killed seventy thousand people
at one end of it and appointed four Muzaffarid princes to govern the other, and
both acts were the same policy. For the next four and a half years he was
occupied with Tuqtamish and the north, and the princes were left with a reprieve
that none of them used to combine. Shah Mansur spent it blinding his relatives
and taking Shiraz, and by 1392 he held most of Fars, which meant that when
Timur came back there was exactly one Muzaffarid left who could put an army in
the field.
