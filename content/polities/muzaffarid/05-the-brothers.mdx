---
title: Five princes and a will
drafted_from: chi-6
phase: contraction

Shah Shuja's death in 1384 turned the appanage arrangement from a nuisance into
the whole of the dynasty's politics. His son Zayn al-Abidin held Shiraz; his
brother's sons held Isfahan and Kerman and Yazd; Shah Yahya and Shah Mansur and
Sultan Ahmad manoeuvred against each other, and the cities changed hands
repeatedly among cousins.

Timur came into that in 1387, and the sequence shows what he was for. He arrived
in Fars, received submissions from most of the princes, appointed and confirmed
them, and moved on — the method this site's Timurid page describes: take the
submission and the plunder, leave the local ruler in place, come back when it
lapses. Zayn al-Abidin, who had not submitted, was captured. Isfahan submitted
and then killed the collectors Timur left behind, and he returned and destroyed
the city; the accounts describe a death toll in the tens of thousands and the
towers of skulls that are the standard image of him.

The princes went on fighting each other afterwards, which is the part that is
hard to read charitably and easy to explain. None of them could stop
manoeuvring, because each of them held a city as his own property and the man
next door was the immediate threat; Timur was a periodic weather event and a
cousin was a permanent one. Shah Mansur, the most capable, blinded Zayn
al-Abidin and Shah Yahya's brother, took Shiraz in 1391, and had most of Fars
by 1392.

He was also the only one of them who fought.
