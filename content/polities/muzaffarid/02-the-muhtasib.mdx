title: The censor

Mubariz al-Din was a puritan, and by the standards of fourteenth-century
Iranian rulers an extreme one. He closed the taverns of Shiraz, banned wine,
broke the wine jars, enforced attendance at prayer, and is reported to have
executed men personally while reciting the Qur'an. His subjects called him the
*muhtasib* — the market inspector, the official responsible for public morals —
and they did not mean it kindly.

Hafez, who was living in Shiraz through all of it, wrote about him. Not by name;
the poems complain about the *muhtasib* and about a time when the wine has to be
drunk in secret, when the rose has no buyer, when the city's pleasure has gone
underground. The poems are read that way by the tradition and by the modern
scholarship, and they are among the reasons this dynasty is legible at all.

In 1358 his sons removed him. They seized him, blinded him — the standard
disqualification, since a blind man cannot rule — and imprisoned him until he
died in 1363. Shah Shuja took Shiraz.

The reversal was immediate and total. The taverns reopened, the poets were paid,
and Hafez wrote that the days of the censor were over and the wine could be
drunk in the open. It is the sharpest single change of cultural policy anywhere
in this corpus, it took place inside one family within one year, and the same
poet documented both halves of it from the same city.

It is worth resisting the obvious moral. The evidence is a body of lyric poetry
written by a man whose income depended on the second regime and not the first,
and Hafez is not a witness in the way a chronicle is. What can be said is that a
ruler's religious severity was politically survivable until his own sons decided
it was not, and that the constituency they were answering to was in Shiraz.
