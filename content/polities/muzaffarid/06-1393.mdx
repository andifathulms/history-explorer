title: The one who charged

Timur came back in 1393 to finish it. Shah Mansur met him outside Shiraz with a
force that the sources put at a small fraction of Timur's, and did the thing
nobody in the campaign had done: he identified the centre of the opposing army,
charged it directly with a few hundred men, and got close enough to Timur to
strike at him. The accounts say he cut through the guard and reached the
standard. He was killed there, in his early thirties, and his head was brought
to Timur.

Timur then summoned the remaining princes of the house — the cousins who had
submitted, been confirmed, and gone on fighting each other — and had them
executed. Not deposed, not blinded, not pensioned: killed, as a family, in one
decision.

That is why this record ends the way it does. The Jalayirids were defeated in
1410 and a remnant held Basra for twenty-two years; the Kara Koyunlu were
destroyed in a year and their tribes turned up inside somebody else's
confederation. The Muzaffarids have no remnant, no claimant and no successor
claim, because the house was extinguished on purpose.

What survives is Shiraz, which Timur did not destroy — he sacked and taxed it
and took its craftsmen, as he took Baghdad's and Damascus's, and the tomb of
Hafez, who had died three years earlier, was in it. The Muzaffarid century is
legible now almost entirely through the poetry it paid for, which is an odd
afterlife for a dynasty founded by a man who banned the wine the poems are
about.
