Timur exterminated the family in 1393 and there is nothing to follow. What
follows instead is what the dynasty had been holding, which was a city with a
poet in it, and the poet outlasted them by six hundred years and counting.

## Everybody wanted him and nobody could get him

The measure of Hafez's standing in his own lifetime is not what the Muzaffarids
paid him. It is who else was writing.

Sultan Ahmad ibn Uways the Jalayirid, ruling Baghdad — himself a poet, a
musician and by several accounts a painter — repeatedly tried to induce him to
come. Hafez declined, in a line that has been quoted ever since:

> The zephyr-breeze of Musalla and the stream of Ruknabad
> Do not permit me to travel or wander afield.

He sent verses in the sultan's praise anyway, including a compliment on his
descent and a couplet admitting that no rose-bud of delight had bloomed for him
from the earth of Fars, and sighing for the Tigris of Baghdad and the spiritual
wine. Which is to say he took the trouble to explain, in verse, that Baghdad
sounded lovely and he was not coming.

Then India. Mahmud Shah Bahmani of the Deccan invited him and he set out — got
as far as Hormuz, where a ship was waiting to carry him over. A storm blew up
just as he was embarking and alarmed him so thoroughly that he abandoned the
voyage, went back to Shiraz, and sent the sultan a poem instead.

Gertrude Bell's version of the relevant stanza is the one that has stuck:

> Full easy seemed the sorrow of the sea
> Lightened by hope of gain—hope flew too fast!
> A hundred pearls were poor indemnity,
> Not worth the blast.

A third invitation came from Bengal. He did not go there either.

## Which is the whole point

Set that beside what the Muzaffarids were.

They were a provincial house from Yazd that took Shiraz in the 1350s, ran a
corner of southern Iran for about eighty years, spent most of it fighting
relatives, produced no institution anybody kept, and were killed to the last man
on Timur's orders. On any political measure they are a footnote in the wreckage
of the Ilkhanate.

They also held, for the whole of his working life, the one city on earth that
the most sought-after poet in the Persian language refused to leave — for
Baghdad, for the Deccan, for Bengal, or for anywhere.

Three Muzaffarid rulers paid him. One of them, Mubariz al-Din, banned wine and
closed the taverns and got written about for it in terms that have outlived
every other fact about him. Hafez called him the *muhtasib*, the censor, and
when he was blinded and deposed by his own son the poet wrote that the days of
the censor were over.

## What survives where

The dynasty has no tomb anybody visits, no monument, no successor, and no
claimant.

Hafez's tomb is in Shiraz, in a garden, and people go there. His divan is opened
at random for guidance in houses that own no other book of poetry. He is the
poet Persian speakers quote when they want to say something they cannot say
plainly — which is a use the Muzaffarids themselves gave him plenty of occasion
to practise.
