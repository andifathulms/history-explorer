Ramesses III beat off the invasions that ended the Bronze Age, and then was
almost murdered by his own household. What the trial records show about the
state that survived is worse than the invasion was.

## The conspiracy

It was organised from inside the harem, on behalf of a prince called Pentaweret,
and the list of people involved is the point.

A chamberlain named Pebekkamen began by procuring wax figures of gods and men,
believing they would let him get messages past the harem guard. He and a royal
butler then recruited ten harem officials, four more royal butlers, an overseer
of the treasury, a general named Peyes, three royal scribes, and assorted
subordinates. Six wives of the officers of the harem gate came in, and proved
useful for passing messages out to relatives. One woman inside smuggled a letter
to her brother, who commanded the archers in Nubia, and brought him in too.

Almost every one of these people was in the pharaoh's personal service. The plan
was to kill him and raise a revolt outside the palace at the same moment.

It was discovered, and the old king — shaken, and possibly hurt in the attempt —
appointed a special court. His commission to it is a remarkable document from a
man who had just been the intended victim:

> As for the words which the people have spoken, I know them not. Go ye and
> examine them... Give heed and have a care lest ye execute punishment upon
> anyone unjustly.

He closes by placing himself among the just kings who are before Osiris, god of
the dead, which Breasted reads as a man who did not expect to see the verdicts.
He was right. He died before the trials ended, twenty days after the twenty-day
jubilee feast of his thirty-second year.

## Who was left to try them

The court was fourteen officials, seven of them royal butlers — and among those
were a Libyan, a Lycian, a Syrian named Maharbaal, and another foreigner probably
from Asia Minor. In his extremity the pharaoh was relying on the purchased loyalty
of foreign servants, because they were the only people in the palace not already
implicated.

It did not hold even so. Some of the accused women, led by the general Peyes,
worked on the bailiffs guarding them until the bailiffs took them to the houses
of two of the judges, who received them and drank with them. Those two judges,
the bailiffs, and a third judge who turned out to be innocent were put on trial
themselves. The innocent man was acquitted; the rest had their ears and noses cut
off, and one of them killed himself immediately afterwards.

Thirty-two people were convicted in the end, Pentaweret and Peyes among them, and
were permitted to take their own lives.

## The long slide

After Ramesses III came, in Breasted's phrase, a long line of nine weaklings all
bearing the great name Ramesses.

Ramesses IV's first act was to compile, for burial with his father, an enormous
inventory of everything Ramesses III had given the temples. His own recorded
bargain with Amun is a promise of more benefactions in four years than Ramesses
II had managed in sixty-seven. He went himself to the Wadi Hammamat quarries and
then sent an expedition of over nine thousand men, supported by pack trains and
ten carts each drawn by six yoke of oxen; nine hundred of them died of heat and
exposure, a tenth of the force. What the stone was for is not known. The only
substantial building he left is a continuation of work his father had started.
After him the Sinai mines stopped being worked, and the last royal name found
there is his.

The rest go past almost without content. A collateral branch supplanted the son
of Ramesses V. The seventh and eighth Ramesses cut tombs in the Valley of the
Kings and left nothing else.

## The priest at full height

While six of these kings succeeded each other, the high priesthood of Amun passed
once, from father to son.

Ramesnakht held it under Ramesses III and IV; his son Amenhotep held it under
Ramesses IX. In the king's tenth year Amenhotep was summoned to the great
forecourt at Karnak and presented, before his own political supporters, with gold
and silver vessels, decorations and precious ointments — and told that revenues
formerly paid to the pharaoh would now be collected directly by the scribes of
the temple. Breasted notes that the king's words of praise are pitched so that
one cannot tell whether they are spoken by a sovereign to a subject or the other
way round.

Amenhotep recorded the occasion twice on the temple wall, with a relief each
time. And in those reliefs he had himself carved at the same heroic stature as
the king.

That had never been done. From the beginning of Egypt, an official before the
pharaoh was drawn as a pygmy beside a towering figure; the convention was the
whole grammar of Egyptian art. A high priest quietly drew himself the same size,
and nobody stopped him — which is a more exact measure of where power had gone
than any document could give, because it was carved into the wall of the god's
own temple and left standing.
