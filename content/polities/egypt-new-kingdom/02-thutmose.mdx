Thutmose III came to sole rule in about 1457 BC, after twenty-two years in which
his stepmother Hatshepsut had reigned as king — with the full titulary, shown in
the reliefs in male dress and a false beard, and running one of the most
prosperous periods of the dynasty. She is one of very few women in this corpus
who held a sovereign office rather than exercising power beside one, and the
attempt to erase her from the monuments began decades after her death rather
than immediately, which argues against the old story of a resentful stepson.

His first campaign as sole king is the one this record carries as a turning
point. A coalition of Canaanite rulers had assembled at Megiddo; Thutmose
brought the army up through a narrow pass his officers had advised against, came
out unopposed in front of the enemy camp, and won the field — and then lost the
city for seven months because his troops stopped to plunder.

The Annals cut on the walls at Karnak afterwards are compiled from the army's
own day-books, and they are the first battle in history described in tactical
detail by participants: the route, the council of war, the dispositions, the
count of what was taken.

Sixteen more campaigns followed in twenty years, reaching the Euphrates. What
they produced was not annexation. The Levant became an Egyptian sphere held
through local rulers who kept their thrones, paid tribute, and sent their sons to
be raised at the Egyptian court — a system of hostage-education that produced
vassals who had grown up in Egypt. Nubia was different and was governed
directly, under a viceroy, as far as the fourth cataract, because Nubia had gold.

That is the empire at its greatest extent and it is the arrangement the next two
chapters are about: how it was administered, and what its own correspondence
shows it looked like from the inside.
