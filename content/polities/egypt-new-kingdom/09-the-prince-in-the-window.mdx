Around 1075, a temple official named Wenamun was sent from Thebes to Byblos to
buy cedar for the ceremonial barge of Amun. He wrote a report of the journey,
and it is the single most useful document about what had happened to Egypt,
because it is an account of an Egyptian official discovering it.

He was robbed at Dor on the way out. He had given away his letters of credit
before he left. He arrived at Byblos with a portable image of the god and no
money, and the prince of the city, Zakar-Baal, refused to see him for
twenty-nine days.

## The interview

When he was finally admitted, he found the prince sitting in an upper room with
his back to a window and the waves of the sea breaking on the shore behind him.

The questions came fast. How long since you left Egypt? Where is the letter of
Amun that should be in your hand? Where is the letter of the High Priest? You
gave them away? Then where is the ship you were given, and its crew — did he
not hand you over to that captain to have you killed and thrown into the sea?

Wenamun said there were Egyptian ships and Egyptian crews. The prince answered
that there were twenty ships in his own harbour doing business with the man in
question, and ten thousand at Sidon trading with another, and Wenamun records
his own reply exactly: *then I was silent in that great hour.*

## The account books

Asked what he had come for, Wenamun said timber for the barge of Amun — *thy
father did it, and thou wilt also do it.*

Zakar-Baal had the journals of his fathers brought in and read out. They
recorded a thousand *deben* of silver received. Then he made the point of the
whole episode:

> *If the ruler of Egypt were the owner of my property and I were also his
> servant, he would not send silver and gold, saying, "Do the commandment of
> Amon." It was not the payment of tribute which they exacted of my father. As
> for me, I am myself neither thy servant, nor am I the servant of him who sent
> thee.*

And then, with the forest behind the city: *if I cry out to the Lebanon, the
heavens open and the logs lie here upon the shore of the sea.*

## What Wenamun had left

He had no army behind him, no letters, no money and no cargo. What he had was
an argument, and he made it: the sea belongs to Amun and Lebanon belongs to
Amun, and the god is the lord of life and health, which the former kings'
silver was never able to buy.

Zakar-Baal grants him something — he is a courteous man in his way, and he
concedes that craftsmanship and teaching had reached Byblos from Egypt. But he
makes the concession as a compliment to a foreign country, not as an
acknowledgement of a superior, and he wants payment in advance.

## Two hundred years

Four centuries earlier, the princes of Byblos wrote to the Egyptian court in
terms of abject dependence, begging for troops and complaining that they would
be lost without them. Rib-Addi of Byblos sent dozens of such letters to Amarna,
and they survive.

The man in the window is his successor in the same city. He keeps ledgers of
what Egypt used to pay, produces them to settle an argument, and tells an
Egyptian envoy to his face that he is nobody's servant.

That is the end of the New Kingdom, described from outside by the person it
happened to, and it did not need a battle.

## What happened to the account itself

The report survives in exactly one copy, damaged, found at al-Hibeh in the
1890s and bought for a museum in Moscow, where it still is. Whether Wenamun
wrote what actually happened to him or a later scribe composed a story
around a real man's disgrace is argued and probably cannot be settled; the
language is Late Egyptian, of the kind used for narrative rather than
administration, and it reads more like a story told about a man than a
memorandum he filed.

Whichever it is, only the one papyrus was ever found, and even that one
breaks off before the end. Wenamun's own account of how he got home, if he
did, is lost along with the last lines of the page it was written on — an
appropriately unfinished ending for a report about an empire that had
stopped being able to finish its own business.
