title: A machine for moving grain

Described in the ordinary categories — a standing army, a land tax, hereditary
kingship, divine sanction — Egypt sounds like everywhere else, and the
ordinariness is misleading, because the way those things were done here has few
parallels anywhere.

The state was not primarily a tax collector in the sense a later empire is. It
was a redistributive apparatus: grain and goods came in to central and temple
granaries and went out again as rations, and for most of Egyptian history there
was no coinage at all. People were paid in bread and beer, measured in
standardised loaves and jars. Kemp's book is largely about that machinery and
about the mentality that ran it, which is a subject a political narrative
cannot reach.

Two consequences are worth stating.

The first is that the temples were not separate from the state. A New Kingdom
temple was a landholding corporation with estates, herds, ships, workshops,
granaries and a staff, and the largest of them — Amun at Karnak — held a share
of Egypt's cultivated land that grew steadily across the period. The kings
endowed them out of conquest, which converted a military success into a
permanent transfer, and by the end of the New Kingdom the priesthood of Amun
was a political power the crown could not remove. The state lived on a land
tax, and a great deal of the land was not the king's.

The second is Deir el-Medina. The workmen who cut and decorated the royal tombs
lived in a purpose-built village in the desert, were paid by the state in
grain, and were unusually literate — and they left thousands of ostraca: work
rosters, absence records with reasons, letters, laundry lists, lawsuits, love
poems, a dream book. It is the best-documented working community in the ancient
world by a wide margin.

Among the records is a strike. In the twenty-ninth year of Ramesses III the
rations were late, and the workmen walked off the job, sat down behind the
mortuary temple of Thutmose III, and refused to work until they were paid. It
is the first recorded labour strike in history, and it is the fiscal history of
the late New Kingdom told from the bottom: the machine described above had
stopped delivering.
