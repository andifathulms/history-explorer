In about 1274 BC Ramesses II marched north to take Kadesh on the Orontes from
the Hittites, was fed false intelligence by two captured Bedouin, and led his
leading division into a position where the Hittite chariotry could hit his
second division on the march. It very nearly destroyed the Egyptian army.

<Figure id="kadesh-relief" />

He got out of it, and then had the episode carved on temple walls across Egypt
in a version where he personally rallied the army and won — the Poem and the
Bulletin, illustrated with reliefs, repeated at Abydos, Karnak, Luxor, Abu
Simbel and the Ramesseum. It is the most extensively publicised battle of the
ancient world and it was, at best, a draw; the Hittites kept Kadesh.

The battle is the famous half of it. The treaty is the half that changed
anything.

Fifteen years later Ramesses II and Hattusili III signed a mutual peace,
non-aggression and extradition agreement. Both texts survive: the Egyptian
version cut on temple walls at Karnak, the Hittite version on a cuneiform
tablet excavated at Hattusa.

<Figure id="hittite-treaty" /> They are not identical — each side's copy says the
other side sued for peace — and they are recognisably the same document, sworn
before the gods of both countries, with clauses on mutual defence, the return
of fugitives, and the succession.

It is the oldest international peace treaty whose text is known from both
parties, and a copy hangs at the United Nations headquarters in New York. It
held for the rest of both kingdoms' existence, was reinforced by a marriage,
and ended six centuries in which the Levant was the thing the great powers
fought over.

Put that beside the Amarna correspondence and the two make one argument. The
Late Bronze Age Near East ran a genuine international system — a small set of
powers who called each other brother, married each other's daughters,
corresponded in a language native to almost none of them, and could sign and
keep a treaty. The next comparable arrangement anywhere is Rome and Persia, and
that is fifteen hundred years later.
