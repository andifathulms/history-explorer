---
title: Kadesh, and the treaty
drafted_from: shaw-2000
phase: peak

In about 1274 BC Ramesses II marched north to take Kadesh on the Orontes from
the Hittites, was fed false intelligence by two captured Bedouin, and led his
leading division into a position where the Hittite chariotry could hit his
second division on the march. It very nearly destroyed the Egyptian army.

He got out of it, and then had the episode carved on temple walls across Egypt
in a version where he personally rallied the army and won — the Poem and the
Bulletin, illustrated with reliefs, repeated at Abydos, Karnak, Luxor, Abu
Simbel and the Ramesseum. It is the most extensively publicised battle of the
ancient world and it was, at best, a draw; the Hittites kept Kadesh.

This site records the battle in a chapter and not in a field, and records the
treaty in the field instead, because the treaty is what changed.

Fifteen years later Ramesses II and Hattusili III signed a mutual peace,
non-aggression and extradition agreement. Both texts survive: the Egyptian
version cut on temple walls at Karnak, the Hittite version on a cuneiform tablet
excavated at Hattusa. They are not identical — each side's copy says the other
side sued for peace — and they are recognisably the same document, sworn before
the gods of both countries, with clauses on mutual defence, the return of
fugitives, and the succession.

It is the oldest international peace treaty whose text is known from both
parties, and a copy hangs at the United Nations headquarters in New York. It
held for the rest of both kingdoms' existence, was reinforced by a marriage, and
ended six centuries in which the Levant was the thing the great powers fought
over.

Put beside this record's Amarna chapter, the two make one argument. The Late
Bronze Age Near East ran a genuine international system — a small set of powers
who called each other brother, married each other's daughters, corresponded in
a language native to almost none of them, and could sign and keep a treaty. This
corpus's next comparable arrangement is the Roman-Sasanian one on its Iranian
pages, and that is fifteen hundred years later.
