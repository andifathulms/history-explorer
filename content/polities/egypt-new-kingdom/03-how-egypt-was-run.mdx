Egypt's institutions in this corpus's fields look ordinary — a standing army, a
land tax, hereditary kingship, divine sanction — and the ordinariness is
misleading, because the way those things were done in Egypt has few parallels
anywhere.

The state was not primarily a tax collector in the sense a later empire is. It
was a redistributive apparatus: grain and goods came in to central and temple
granaries and went out again as rations, and for most of Egyptian history there
was no coinage at all. People were paid in bread and beer, measured in
standardised loaves and jars. Kemp's book is largely about that machinery and
about the mentality that ran it, and this record's institutions are coded from
him because a political narrative cannot supply them.

Two consequences are worth stating.

The first is that the temples were not separate from the state. A New Kingdom
temple was a landholding corporation with estates, herds, ships, workshops,
granaries and a staff, and the largest of them — Amun at Karnak — held a share of
Egypt's cultivated land that grew steadily across the period. The kings endowed
them out of conquest, which converted a military success into a permanent
transfer, and by the end of the New Kingdom the priesthood of Amun was a
political power the crown could not remove. This site's `revenue_basis` field
says `land-tax`, and a great deal of that land was not the king's.

The second is Deir el-Medina. The workmen who cut and decorated the royal tombs
lived in a purpose-built village in the desert, were paid by the state in grain,
and were unusually literate — and they left thousands of ostraca: work rosters,
absence records with reasons, letters, laundry lists, lawsuits, love poems, a
dream book. It is the best-documented working community in the ancient world by
a wide margin.

Among the records is a strike. In the twenty-ninth year of Ramesses III the
rations were late, and the workmen walked off the job, sat down behind the
mortuary temple of Thutmose III, and refused to work until they were paid. It is
the first recorded labour strike in history, and it is on this page because it
is the fiscal history of the late New Kingdom told from the bottom: the machine
described above had stopped delivering.
