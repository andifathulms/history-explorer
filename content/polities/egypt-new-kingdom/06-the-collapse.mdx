---
title: What was left when the system stopped
drafted_from: shaw-2000
phase: end
---

Around 1200 BC the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean came apart. Hatti
ended; Ugarit was burned and not reoccupied; Mycenaean Greece collapsed; the
Levantine cities were destroyed or diminished; the international system this
record's Amarna and Kadesh chapters describe stopped existing.

Egypt survived it, and was the only great power that did. Ramesses III beat a
combined land and sea invasion in the delta in about 1177 and recorded it at
Medinet Habu in reliefs showing a naval battle in the Nile mouths — the first
depiction of a sea fight in Egyptian art.

Surviving cost more than the record admits. Within a generation the state could
not pay the tomb workers on time, which produced the strike on this record's
third chapter. The royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings were being robbed
systematically, and the investigations into it survive as court papyri naming
officials. Grain prices in the surviving accounts rise steeply. Nubia slipped
away; the Levantine possessions were gone.

The document that closes the period is the Report of Wenamun, an Egyptian
official sent to Byblos around 1075 to buy cedar for the barque of Amun. He is
robbed on the way, kept waiting for weeks, and finally told by the prince of
Byblos that his ancestors sent timber to Egypt because Egypt paid, that Egypt is
no longer owed anything, and that the god's business does not entitle an envoy
to a free cargo. Whether the text is a report or a literary composition is
argued; either way it was written by an Egyptian and circulated in Egypt, and it
is a Levantine ruler telling an Egyptian official that the empire is over.

This record ends in 1077 with `fragmentation` and no agent, because there was
none: the last Ramesside king was one of two powers in Egypt, with the high
priest of Amun holding the south, and the country divided without a battle.

Egypt continued for another thousand years as a place worth conquering. This
corpus's Assyrian record has it taken in 671; its Achaemenid record has it
annexed, lost for sixty years, and reconquered seven years before Alexander
landed; its Ptolemaic record has three centuries of Macedonian kings and ends
with Rome. It never again appears as one of the powers doing the conquering.
