---
title: What being conquered taught them
drafted_from: shaw-2000
phase: formation
---

For about a century before 1550, the northern half of Egypt was ruled by
foreigners.

The Hyksos — the Egyptian phrase means rulers of foreign lands — were a
Levantine dynasty established in the delta at Avaris, and while they held it
the Egyptian line at Thebes was one power among several, with a Nubian kingdom
at Kerma to its south and the Hyksos to its north. There is a surviving letter
from a Hyksos king to the ruler of Kush proposing a joint attack on Thebes,
intercepted in the desert. Egypt in the Second Intermediate Period was a middle
power being squeezed.

Ahmose expelled them around 1550 and took Avaris. What matters is what the
experience produced, because the New Kingdom is Egypt behaving differently from
the Egypt of the previous fifteen hundred years.

It kept a permanent army. It campaigned outside Egypt continuously rather than
occasionally, and it did not stop at the traditional frontiers: Nubia was
annexed and administered directly under a viceroy — the King's Son of Kush — as
far as the fourth cataract, and the Levant was held through vassal rulers whose
sons were taken to Egypt and educated there.

And the technology came from the people who had held the delta. The horse and
the light chariot, the composite bow, improved bronze-working and body armour
are all Hyksos-period introductions, and the army that conquered the Levant
used all of them.

That is the shape of the whole period, and it is a shape that recurs: a state
is conquered, absorbs what conquered it, and turns outward with the conqueror's
equipment. This is the earliest clear instance of it anywhere.
