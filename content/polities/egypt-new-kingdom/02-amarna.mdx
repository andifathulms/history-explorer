The Amarna archive is about three hundred and fifty clay tablets found at
Akhetaten, the short-lived capital Akhenaten built and abandoned. They are
diplomatic correspondence of the fourteenth century BC, written in Akkadian —
the international language of the period, native to none of the main
correspondents — between the Egyptian court and the courts of Babylon, Assyria,
Mitanni, Hatti and Cyprus, plus the Egyptian vassal rulers of the Levant.

The great kings address each other as brothers and behave accordingly. They
negotiate dynastic marriages, exchange gifts on a scale that functions as trade,
complain that the gold sent was of poor quality or the messenger detained too
long, and enquire after each other's health with formulaic warmth. There is a
recognised protocol, a shared language of equality among a small set of powers,
and a clear distinction between those powers and the vassals, whose letters beg
rather than negotiate.

It is a functioning international system, documented from inside, a thousand
years before the Achaemenids and two thousand before anything on this site's
Islamic pages. The closest comparison in this corpus is the Chanyuan treaty
between the Liao and the Song on this site's Liao page, which also required two
states to describe each other as equals and also produced a stable frontier.

The system ended with the Bronze Age collapse around 1200 BC, and Egypt's own
imperial phase ended with it.
