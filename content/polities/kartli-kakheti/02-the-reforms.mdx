---
title: What Erekle tried to build
drafted_from: suny-1994
phase: expansion
---

Erekle II is the last Georgian ruler before the twentieth century who attempted
to build a state, and the list of what he tried is short, coherent and mostly
unsuccessful.

He reopened the mines at Akhtala and Alaverdi and brought in Greek miners from
the Ottoman empire to work the silver and copper. He set up a printing press. He
tried to attract European advisers and craftsmen, corresponded with Catherine
II's court, and sent students abroad. He established a standing bodyguard, a
regular tax on the tavadi estates, and courts under royal rather than noble
jurisdiction.

And in 1773 he instituted the *morigeoba*: a rotational military obligation
under which households across the kingdom served in arms for one month a year in
turn, in rotation, so that the crown would always have a force in the field that
did not depend on the great houses agreeing to supply one.

It is worth setting that beside what David IV had done six centuries earlier,
because the problem was identical — a nobility monopolising force — and the
resources were not. David brought forty thousand Kipchak families over the
mountains and settled them on land against service. Erekle had nothing to offer
anybody and had to raise the equivalent out of the population he already
governed, by administrative obligation, which is a thing almost no state before
the nineteenth century managed to do.

It lapsed within about a decade. The tavadi houses resisted it, the peasantry
resented it, and a kingdom of perhaps half a million people could not
simultaneously farm, defend a frontier that was raided annually from Daghestan,
and staff a rotating national militia.

The mines, the press and the advisers went the same way, for the same reason. It
takes revenue to build a state and it takes a state to raise revenue, and the
kingdom had neither and was being raided while it tried.
