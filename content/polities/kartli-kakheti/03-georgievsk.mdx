In July 1783, at the fortress of Georgievsk in the north Caucasus, Erekle's
envoys and Catherine II's signed a treaty of protection.

The terms were explicit. Russia guaranteed the territorial integrity of
Kartli-Kakheti and the succession of the Bagrationi; the kingdom kept its
internal government, its church and its law. In exchange Georgia renounced all
dependence on Iran or any other power, undertook to conduct no foreign relations
without Russian consent, and accepted Russian troops.

From Tbilisi the calculation was straightforward and, on the information
available, sound. Two Muslim empires had partitioned the country for two
centuries; a Christian empire to the north was expanding, had just taken the
Crimea, and wanted a position in the Caucasus. A guarantee from it against the
Ottomans and against whatever emerged in Iran — where the Zands were failing and
nobody yet knew what would follow — was worth the loss of a foreign policy the
kingdom could not exercise anyway.

From St Petersburg it was cheap. Two battalions were sent and withdrawn again
in 1787 when the Russo-Turkish war began.

The judgement to make about it is not that Erekle was naive. He was a
considerably more experienced ruler than most of the people who have written
about the treaty, and the alternative he was choosing against was the one the
kingdom had been living under. The judgement is that a guarantee is worth what
the guarantor's interests make it worth on the day it is called, and that this
is a general fact about small states with large protectors which this corpus has
now recorded from several directions — on the Ak Koyunlu page, where the
Venetian guns never came; on the Trebizond page, where the marriage alliances
did not; and here.

It was called in 1795.
