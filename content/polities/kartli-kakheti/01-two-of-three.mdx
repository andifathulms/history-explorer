A royal council divided the kingdom of Georgia in 1490 among three branches of
the Bagrationi: Kartli in the centre, Kakheti in the east, Imereti in the
west.

What followed was two and a half centuries of the three kingdoms between the
Ottomans and the Safavids, who partitioned the country between them by treaty
in 1555 and again in 1639 — Imereti to Istanbul, Kartli and Kakheti to Isfahan.
Georgian kings converted to Islam to hold their thrones and reconverted when
the opportunity came; Georgians were bought and sold as soldiers across the
Islamic world, in the Safavid ghulam corps that Abbas I built to break the
Qizilbash and on the frontier Cairo bought its Mamluks from. Shah Abbas
deported tens of thousands of Kakhetians to Iran in the 1610s and settled
Turkic tribes in their place.

In 1762 Teimuraz II of Kartli died and his son Erekle II, who was already king
of Kakheti, inherited it. Two of the three were one kingdom again for the first
time in two hundred and seventy-two years.

What Erekle inherited was a small kingdom in a ruined country, with a nobility
that had spent two centuries becoming more powerful than the crown, a
population reduced by deportation and raiding, and two Muslim empires on either
side of it that had each spent the period treating Georgia as a province they
had mislaid.

He was fifty-two, had commanded troops for Nadir Shah in India, and had
thirty-six years left.
