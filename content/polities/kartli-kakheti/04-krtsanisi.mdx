---
title: Krtsanisi
drafted_from: suny-1994

Agha Muhammad Khan Qajar had spent fifteen years reassembling Iran and regarded
Georgia as a Safavid dependency that had been mislaid. In 1795 he sent Erekle a
demand: repudiate the Russian treaty and return to Iranian suzerainty.

Erekle refused, and appealed to Russia under the treaty of 1783.

The Qajar army came north through Azerbaijan in the late summer. On 11 September
1795, at Krtsanisi outside Tbilisi, Erekle met it with a force the sources put at
a small fraction of its size — the Imeretian and Kartlian nobles had not all
come — and was destroyed. He was seventy-five and had to be carried off the
field by his grandsons.

Tbilisi was taken and burned. The accounts of what followed are consistent
across Georgian, Persian and Russian sources: a large part of the population
killed, and something in the order of fifteen thousand carried off as slaves.
The city Vakhtang Gorgasali had founded, that David IV had taken back from a
Muslim emirate in 1122 and governed by conciliating its inhabitants, was
destroyed by a Persian army in a single day.

No Russian troops came. This corpus's Qajar record describes the campaign from
the other side and treats it as the act that established Agha Muhammad Khan as
shah; the Russian response was a punitive expedition in 1796 that was recalled
when Catherine died, and then nothing.

Erekle never returned to Tbilisi and died in 1798. His son George XII inherited a
burned capital, a divided nobility, a treasury with nothing in it and a treaty
that had been demonstrated to be worthless in the one circumstance it existed
for.

He asked Russia for a closer union.
