Eighty-seven years after the annexation, a young Englishman travelled through
Georgia and wrote down what he saw. Oliver Wardrop's book is affectionate,
opinionated and full of the assumptions of 1888, and it is also a first-hand
description of what had actually happened to the kingdom.

## Tsinondali

He went to Tsinondali in Kakheti, in a beautiful valley beyond Akuri. It had
been the home of Prince David Chavchavadze. It was now, he notes, the property
of Alexander Alexandrovich, Autocrat of All the Russias, who was having a vast
palace built on it at the time of the visit.

That is the annexation in one sentence of description. The estate of a Georgian
prince, held by the family that had held it, is a summer residence of the
emperor.

Wardrop also records what Tsinondali was famous for locally, and it is worth
putting beside the palace: it was the place from which the Princesses
Chavchavadze and Orbeliani were carried off by Shamil's men in July 1854. One of
the two had been widowed weeks before, her husband killed fighting the Turks. So
the same valley supplies the two facts about nineteenth-century Georgia — a
country raided from the mountains, and a country whose gentry's houses were
becoming imperial property.

## What the villages thought Russia was

Wardrop is precise about this and it is not what a reader might expect. In the
rural districts, he says, the people knew Russia only as a foreign power that
sent them tax collectors, justices of the peace and other civil servants, who
performed obnoxious functions in a manner not calculated to conciliate the
ratepayers. He adds that the *chinovnik* had an unpleasant reputation even among
Russians, and that those who accepted a posting in Transcaucasia were not
precisely the flower of the service.

Then he reports a conversation that does more work than the rest of the chapter.
A country squire, talking about a small market town near his house, told him:
they have posted a gendarme there; until he came nobody ever bothered about
politics; now there is nothing else talked of.

## The thing the empire made

Wardrop's judgement follows directly, and eighty-seven years of hindsight have
not improved on it: the measures produced the very thing they were intended to
prevent.

Young Georgian nobles serving in the Russian army had gone through a phase of
revolutionary socialism in the 1870s, and some of them had suffered for it. By
the 1880s, he says, the national feeling had become so strong as to leave no
room for those ideas. While he was in Tiflis a rumour ran that a hundred or two
young noblemen were to be exiled before the Tsar's visit to his new palace at
Tsinondali. Nobody knew whether it was true. Wardrop's point is that everyone
concerned believed it, which tells you what kind of administration they thought
they were living under.

## What was left to take

The kingdom's institutions had been dissolved in 1801 and the church's
autocephaly in 1811. What the empire had not been able to dissolve was the
language, the poem everybody could recite, and the sense — sharpened by the
gendarme in the market town — of being a nation that had lost a state rather
than a province that had always been one.

That is what was standing in 1918 when the Russian empire went and the Georgians
declared a republic.
