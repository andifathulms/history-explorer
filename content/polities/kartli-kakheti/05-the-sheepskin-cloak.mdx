Agha Muhammad Khan burned Tbilisi in September 1795. What Georgia remembered
about it afterwards was not the battle but what happened to the king, and the
memory had already been shaped into literature by the time an English traveller
wrote it down.

Oliver Wardrop went up the military road from Tbilisi in the 1880s and stopped at
Ananuri, which he calls the darkest page in the mournful latter-day history of
Georgia. Erekle II had escaped from the ruins of his capital with a few servants
and taken refuge in the fortress there. He was seventy-seven. Wardrop then quotes
a Georgian account of what a passer-by would have seen: in the corner of the
monks' orchard, in an old half-ruined cell, a man in a rough sheepskin cloak,
sitting with his face turned to the wall, and beside him one elderly Armenian
servant.

The passage that follows is the servant answering people who ask who that is, and
it is worth taking as what it is — a piece of nineteenth-century Georgian writing
about a national humiliation, not a transcript — because the choices it makes are
the argument.

He whom thou seest was once a man of might, and his name was honoured throughout
Asia. His people never had a better ruler; he strove for their welfare like a
father, and for forty years kept his empire together. Then the three explanations,
in order. Old age had weakened him. To prevent quarrels after his death he had
divided the kingdom among his children while he still lived, and his hopes in
them were deceived; when the invasion came they refused to help him or their
native land, because there were so many of them that each calculated he would be
fighting for a brother's advantage rather than his own. And the King of Georgia
had to ask help from the King of Imereti, and anyone who had been in Tbilisi
would have seen how shamefully the Imeretians behaved. Erekle fought with a
handful of men against a hundred thousand and lost his throne only because his
children pitilessly forsook him.

Then the sentence the whole speech is built to arrive at. He who was chief eunuch
of Nader Shah when Erekle was a leader in the Persian army now marched against
him in his feeble old age — and Erekle was left to be defeated by a wretched
gelding.

Two men out of the same wreckage of Nader's empire, one of them a Georgian prince
commanding in it and the other a castrated hostage in it, ending fifty years
later with the second burning the first's capital. That is accurate, and it is
also the part a Georgian writer would reach for, because it turns a strategic
catastrophe into an insult and an insult can be borne.

The servant's closing line does the same work in the other direction. His
courtiers, all those who have eaten his bread and been pressed to his bosom, have
left him — not one has followed his master, excepting only me, a poor, despised
Armenian.

Erekle never returned to Tbilisi. He died at Telavi in January 1798, having spent
his last years trying to hold together a kingdom he had already divided, and the
succession he had arranged produced exactly the quarrel he had divided it to
prevent. Six years after the fire, the arrangement he had signed at Georgievsk to
prevent all of this was used by the other party to abolish the kingdom.
