Ashurbanipal assembled at Nineveh a collection of some thousands of cuneiform
tablets, gathered by systematic requisition from temples and private collections
across Babylonia: omen texts, medical and astronomical compendia, lexical lists,
rituals, and literature including the fullest surviving version of the epic of
Gilgamesh.

It burned when Nineveh fell in 612, and the fire is why it survives. Clay
tablets are baked hard by burning; papyrus and parchment libraries of the same
period are gone entirely. The single largest body of Mesopotamian written culture
we possess exists because the empire that collected it was destroyed violently
enough to fire the archive.

The collection also tells you what the state thought knowledge was for. It is
heavily weighted toward divination — reading the future in livers, in the sky, in
irregular births — because an empire that campaigned annually needed to know
whether to campaign, and the king's decisions were validated by specialists
reading signs. This is administrative literature, not antiquarianism.

Nothing on this site's four axes registers any of it. The Assyrian page shows a
reach figure, a span, an empty population bar and three null influence counts,
and the library is not among the things the coding rules can see. The prose is
where that has to go, which is the arrangement the site was built on: numbers
where numbers are cited, and everything else in the chapters.
