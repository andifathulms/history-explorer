Ashurbanipal assembled at Nineveh a collection of some thousands of cuneiform
tablets, gathered by systematic requisition from temples and private
collections across Babylonia: omen texts, medical and astronomical compendia,
lexical lists, rituals, and literature including the fullest surviving version
of the epic of Gilgamesh.

<Figure id="flood-tablet" />

It burned when Nineveh fell in 612, and the fire is why it survives. Clay
tablets are baked hard by burning; papyrus and parchment libraries of the same
period are gone entirely. The single largest body of Mesopotamian written
culture we possess exists because the empire that collected it was destroyed
violently enough to fire the archive.

The collection also tells you what the state thought knowledge was for. It is
heavily weighted toward divination — reading the future in livers, in the sky,
in irregular births — because an empire that campaigned annually needed to know
whether to campaign, and the king's decisions were validated by specialists
reading signs. This is administrative literature, not antiquarianism.

No measure of an empire registers any of it. Assyria can be given a size, a
duration and a body count, and the library appears in none of those, although
it is the single reason most of Mesopotamian literature exists to be read.
