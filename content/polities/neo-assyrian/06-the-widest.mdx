Sennacherib moved the capital to Nineveh in 705 and built there on a scale that
had no precedent: a palace he called the Palace Without Rival, with reliefs
running for kilometres of wall, and an engineered water supply bringing water
from the hills through canals and an aqueduct at Jerwan built of two million
dressed stones. The gardens he describes at Nineveh — irrigated by that system,
planted with trees from everywhere the army had been — are the strongest
current candidate for the Hanging Gardens the Greek tradition attributed to
Babylon.

He is also the king who destroyed Babylon. After years of failing to hold
Babylon through client kings and after the Babylonians handed his own son to
the Elamites, he took the city in 689, demolished its temples, and diverted a
canal over the ruins. It was an act against the most prestigious city in
Mesopotamia and against the gods Assyria itself worshipped, his own son
reversed it and rebuilt, and it poisoned the relationship that eventually
destroyed the empire.

Esarhaddon, that son, took Egypt in 671 — the first time any Mesopotamian power
had done it — and Ashurbanipal held it, sacked Thebes in 663, and then
destroyed Elam in a war of extermination in the 640s that the reliefs record in
detail including the famous scene of the king dining in a garden with an
Elamite king's head hanging in a tree.

That is the widest extent: the Nile to western Iran, everything in between, the
largest state the world had yet seen.

Two things about that peak are worth stating. The first is that Egypt was not
held: Assyria installed clients, the clients revolted, and by about 655 it was
gone — the ordinary fate of a rich province at the far end of a long line.

The second is the royal women, whose absence from older narratives Frahm's book
sets out to correct. Sammu-ramat, regent for her son in the ninth century,
appears on monuments in ways no earlier Assyrian queen does and is the origin
of the Greek legend of Semiramis. Naqia, Sennacherib's wife and Esarhaddon's
mother, was a substantial political actor in her own right and had her own
inscriptions and her own succession oath imposed on the empire's subjects.

Any account that asks only who held the throne will miss both of them, which is
how they came to be missed for a century of modern scholarship.
