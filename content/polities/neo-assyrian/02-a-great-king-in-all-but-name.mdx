Adad-nirari III died in 783, and the four decades that followed his reign are
the closest the reconquest that began in 911 came to reversing itself before
it had finished.

## Kings who left almost nothing

Three kings held the throne in succession — Shalmaneser IV, Ashur-dan III,
Ashur-nirari V — and between them they left the empire's annals almost blank.
No royal inscription of any length survives from any of the three describing a
campaign in the king's own voice, which is not how an Assyrian reign is
supposed to look. The eponym lists that record each year by the name of the
official who held that year's chief magistracy are the main source for the
period, and what they record is trouble: plague twice, in 765 and again in
759; a revolt in the Assyrian heartland itself in 763 and 762; further revolts
at Arrapha and at Guzana in the years just after.

In the middle of it, on the fifteenth of June 763, the sky went dark at
midday. The eponym list for that year records the eclipse directly, and
modern astronomical calculation confirms the date precisely enough that this
single line fixes the absolute chronology of the entire Assyrian eponym
system against it. Nobody recorded what anyone thought it meant. They did not
need to: a state already recording plague and revolt in the same years had an
omen to match the mood without anyone having to invent one.

## The man who did not need the throne

What filled the gap the kings left was a single official, Shamshi-ilu, who
held the post of turtanu — commander-in-chief — for something close to forty
years, under three kings in a row.

He commanded armies, negotiated a boundary settlement with a rival king in
his own name rather than the king's, and had inscriptions cut at his own
residence at Til Barsip in the same style a king used for his own conquests —
crediting himself with victories, in his own titulary, with no royal name
attached at all. No source accuses him of taking the throne outright. None
needed to. A subordinate who commissions monuments in the register of
kingship has already answered the question of where the empire's real
authority was sitting, whatever the man on the throne was called.

## What ended it

The crisis did not end with a reform proposed by any of the three kings it
happened under. It ended with a coup.

In 746 a revolt broke out at Kalhu, the capital, and by the next year the
throne had passed to a man named Pulu, who took the throne-name
Tiglath-Pileser. Whether he was Ashur-nirari V's brother with a legitimate
claim or a provincial governor who took the palace by force is argued about;
the Assyrian King List calls him Ashur-nirari's son, his own inscriptions
claim descent from Adad-nirari III instead, and he founds his own right to
rule on the gods' choice rather than on the bloodline either version would
require. What is not argued about is that the family occupying the throne
changed, that it changed by violence at the capital rather than by
succession, and that the man who came out of it had spent his career, on the
likelier reading, as exactly the kind of provincial officeholder the
preceding forty years had shown could outgrow the throne that nominally
commanded him.

A man who may have owed his own crown to exactly the kind of independent
regional power the old system had allowed spent the rest of his reign making
sure nobody after him could repeat what he had just done.
