title: The machinery of empire

Assyria is where a great deal of the apparatus later empires used was first
assembled: a standing professional army rather than a seasonal levy, provinces
under appointed governors rather than tributary kings, a road and messenger
system, systematic record-keeping, and the mass deportation of conquered
populations as a deliberate instrument for breaking regional identities.

That last is what it is best remembered for, largely through the Hebrew Bible,
which was written in part by people on the receiving end. The reputation for
calculated terror is not a distortion — Assyrian royal inscriptions describe
flaying and impalement with evident satisfaction, as policy communicated on
purpose.

Most of that apparatus dates from one reign. Tiglath-Pileser III converted
conquered kingdoms into provinces under appointed officials instead of
leaving them as tributaries, broke up the great provincial commands so that
no governor held too much, enlarged and professionalised the standing army,
and made deportation a system rather than an occasional punishment. The
empire doubled inside forty years.

It fought with a standing professional core and conscripts behind it, and it
lived, in a strict and unmetaphorical sense, on plunder. Assyria campaigned
every year, the campaign brought back movable wealth and people, and the
accounting of what it brought is a standard section of the royal inscriptions.
The annual campaign was a fiscal institution before it was a strategic one.
