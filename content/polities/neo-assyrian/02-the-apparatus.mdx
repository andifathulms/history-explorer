Assyria is where a great deal of the apparatus later empires used was first
assembled: a standing professional army rather than a seasonal levy, provinces
under appointed governors rather than tributary kings, a road and messenger
system, systematic record-keeping, and the mass deportation of conquered
populations as a deliberate instrument for breaking regional identities.

That last is what it is best remembered for, largely through the Hebrew Bible,
which was written in part by people on the receiving end. The reputation for
calculated terror is not a distortion — Assyrian royal inscriptions describe
flaying and impalement with evident satisfaction, as policy communicated on
purpose.

Most of that apparatus dates from one reign. Tiglath-Pileser III, who took the
throne in 745 after a period in which the empire had stagnated and provincial
governors had become powerful enough to matter, converted conquered kingdoms
into provinces under appointed officials instead of leaving them as tributaries,
broke up the great provincial commands so that no governor held too much,
enlarged and professionalised the standing army, and made deportation a system
rather than an occasional punishment. The empire doubled inside forty years.

This site's `military_basis` field reads `standing-professional` and `conscript`
on that account, and its `revenue_basis` includes `plunder` in the strict sense
coding rule 6 requires: Assyria campaigned every year, the campaign brought back
movable wealth and people, and the accounting of what it brought is a standard
section of the royal inscriptions. The annual campaign was a fiscal institution
before it was a strategic one.
