Two hundred years after Nineveh fell, Xenophon marched ten thousand Greeks up
the Tigris past the ruins of the Assyrian capitals, and did not know what they
were.

He describes a deserted city with a great pyramid beside it, and gives its name
as Larissa, which is a Greek word and not a local one. He was told it had been
a Median city, abandoned when the Persians took the empire. Nothing in his
account mentions Assyria at all.

The place was Kalhu — Nimrud — which had been the capital of the largest empire
the world had yet seen, and whose kings had left palaces there covered in
inscriptions describing what they had done to everybody between the Zagros and
the Nile.

## What Layard found

When Austen Henry Layard rode through the region in 1840 he stood on a mound at
night and looked across the plain at exactly what Xenophon had seen: a line of
lofty mounds, one of pyramidal form rising above the rest. He recognised it
immediately from the Greek text — *this was the pyramid which Xenophon had
described, and near which the ten thousand had encamped: the ruins around it
were those which the Greek general saw twenty-two centuries before, and which
were even then the remains of an ancient city.*

Local tradition attributed the mounds to Nimrod, which is where the modern name
comes from and which is a biblical figure rather than an Assyrian one.

Fifty miles down the Tigris he explored another vast ruin, Qalat Sherqat. It
was, he wrote, a shapeless mass covered with grass, showing scarcely any trace
of human work except where the winter rains had cut ravines down its almost
perpendicular sides and laid its contents open. He and his companion searched
it for the best part of a day, on the strength of an Arab report of strange
figures carved in black stone, and found a few potsherds and inscribed bricks.
Then they rode on. Their escort, alarmed at the emptiness of the country,
turned back alone and was killed by horsemen on the way.

That mound was Ashur: the city that gave the empire, the god and the country
their single name.

## How complete the erasure was

Between 612 BC and 1845 the following was the sum of what anybody knew about
Assyria: some hostile chapters in the Hebrew prophets, a few pages of Greek
legend about Sardanapalus and Semiramis, and a set of grass-covered mounds
attributed to Nimrod or to the Medes.

The palaces were forty feet down with their reliefs standing. Ashurbanipal's
library, buried in the same rubble that had preserved it, had been sitting
unread for two and a half thousand years.

Layard began digging in 1845. Within six years he had shipped enough sculpture
to London to fill a wing of the British Museum, and within thirty the tablets
were being read.

An empire that deported entire populations to make itself remembered, and left
inscriptions on every wall it could reach, was forgotten so thoroughly that its
own capital was pointed out to a Greek general as somebody else's.
