title: The policy, examined

Assyria deported conquered populations on a scale nobody had attempted, and the
practice is the single thing the empire is remembered for. It deserves a chapter
that says what it actually was, because the popular version and the documentary
version differ.

The scale is real. The royal inscriptions record numbers, and modern estimates
of the total across three centuries run to something over four million people.
The purpose is stated plainly in the sources and is administrative rather than
punitive: a population moved away from its land, its shrines and its leaders
cannot organise a revolt, and a province settled with people from three other
provinces has no shared identity to revolt around. Deportees were resettled as
farmers, as labour on royal building projects, and as soldiers.

They were also, generally, moved intact. Families travelled together, with
their possessions and often with their livestock; the reliefs show ox-carts,
women and children, and guards escorting rather than driving. Skilled workers
were assigned to work matching their skills. This was not extermination and
Assyria had no interest in reducing its own tax base; it was the compulsory
redistribution of an empire's population for the convenience of governing it,
carried out with the administrative care that implies and the human consequences
that follow anyway.

The reputation for calculated terror is separately earned and is not a
distortion. Assyrian royal inscriptions describe flaying, impalement and the
piling of heads with evident satisfaction, and they were meant to be read that
way: the point of describing what happened to a city that resisted was that the
next city should not. This corpus's later pages have polities that did the same
things and did not write them down.

Two consequences ran the other way from what was intended.

The Hebrew Bible is the first. Israel was deported in 722, Judah was besieged in
701 and deported by Babylon in 587, and a substantial part of the Hebrew
scriptures is literature written by people processing conquest and exile — which
is why an empire that left tens of thousands of its own cuneiform documents is
known to most of the world through the account of one small deported kingdom.

The second is Aramaic. Moving populations around the Near East for three
centuries, with Aramaean deportees among the largest groups, produced a common
spoken language across the whole empire, written in an alphabet on parchment
rather than in cuneiform on clay. The Assyrian state adopted it as a second
administrative language, and it went on to be the language of the Achaemenid
chancery on this site's Iranian pages, of the Talmud, and of the Church of the
East's missions to China. An empire trying to eliminate local identities created
the first Near Eastern lingua franca, and it outlasted the empire by two
thousand years.
