title: The widest it ever was
drafted_from: frahm-2023

Sennacherib moved the capital to Nineveh in 705 and built there on a scale that
had no precedent: a palace he called the Palace Without Rival, with reliefs
running for kilometres of wall, and an engineered water supply bringing water
from the hills through canals and an aqueduct at Jerwan built of two million
dressed stones. The gardens he describes at Nineveh — irrigated by that system,
planted with trees from everywhere the army had been — are the strongest current
candidate for the Hanging Gardens the Greek tradition attributed to Babylon.

He is also the king of this record's third turning point. After years of failing
to hold Babylon through client kings and after the Babylonians handed his own
son to the Elamites, he took the city in 689, demolished its temples, and
diverted a canal over the ruins. It was an act against the most prestigious city
in Mesopotamia and against the gods Assyria itself worshipped, his own son
reversed it and rebuilt, and it poisoned the relationship that eventually
destroyed the empire.

Esarhaddon, that son, took Egypt in 671 — the first time any Mesopotamian power
had done it — and Ashurbanipal held it, sacked Thebes in 663, and then destroyed
Elam in a war of extermination in the 640s that the reliefs record in detail
including the famous scene of the king dining in a garden with an Elamite king's
head hanging in a tree.

That is the widest extent: the Nile to western Iran, everything in between, the
largest state the world had yet seen.

Two things about that peak belong on the record. The first is that Egypt was not
held — Assyria installed clients, the clients revolted, and by about 655 it was
gone, which is a familiar shape in this corpus for an empire holding a rich
province at the far end of a long line.

The second is the royal women, whose absence from older narratives Frahm's book
sets out to correct. Sammu-ramat, regent for her son in the ninth century,
appears on monuments in ways no earlier Assyrian queen does and is the origin of
the Greek legend of Semiramis. Naqia, Sennacherib's wife and Esarhaddon's
mother, was a substantial political actor in her own right and had her own
inscriptions and her own succession oath imposed on the empire's subjects. This
site's `rulers` fields on this page name three men, and the corpus has said on
its Sulayhid record that a schema asking who held the sovereign office will miss
women who exercised power without holding it. Assyria is another instance.
