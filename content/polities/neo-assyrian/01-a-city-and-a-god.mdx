Ashur is a city on the Tigris, a god, and a country, and in Akkadian they are
the same word. That identity is not a coincidence of naming and it is the
starting point for everything else: the god is the city, the king is the god's
appointed steward, and the state's business is extending the territory in which
the god's order applies.

The city is old — a trading centre in the third millennium, a kingdom in the
second, and the head of a substantial empire in the thirteenth century BC that
finished off Mitanni.

Then three and a half centuries in which it was none of those things, which is
why the empire everybody means by "Assyria" starts as late as it does. After
about 1050 BC Assyria contracted to little more than its own heartland under
pressure from Aramaean movements, and the Bronze Age system around it had
already collapsed. What happened from 911 was a reconquest of territory Assyria
considered its own, conducted by a succession of kings — Adad-nirari II,
Ashurnasirpal II, Shalmaneser III — with a persistence that is the
distinguishing Assyrian characteristic and is visible in the annals, which
record the same campaigns against the same places year after year until they
stopped needing to.

By the ninth century the reconquest had become an expansion, and by the seventh
the empire ran from western Iran to Egypt.

The Assyrian version of divine sanction is worth distinguishing from its
neighbours'. Naram-Sin of Akkad became a god. Egyptian kings were gods. The
Assyrian king was emphatically not a god: he was the vice-regent of one, and
the royal inscriptions are written as reports to Ashur explaining what has been
done with his instructions. That is a more durable arrangement, and it is the
version that reaches the later Near East.
