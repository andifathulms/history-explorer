---
title: A city and a god with the same name
drafted_from: frahm-2023
phase: formation

Ashur is a city on the Tigris, a god, and a country, and in Akkadian they are
the same word. That identity is not a coincidence of naming and it is the
starting point for everything on this page: the god is the city, the king is the
god's appointed steward, and the state's business is extending the territory in
which the god's order applies.

The city is old — a trading centre in the third millennium, a kingdom in the
second, and the head of a substantial Middle Assyrian empire in the thirteenth
century BC that took Mitanni, which is this site's neighbouring record and whose
`ended.by` field is empty because the corpus's Assyrian entry begins three and a
half centuries after that conquest.

Those three and a half centuries are the reason this record starts where it
does. After about 1050 BC Assyria contracted to little more than its own
heartland under pressure from Aramaean movements, and the Bronze Age system
around it had already collapsed. What happened from 911 was a reconquest of
territory Assyria considered its own, conducted by a succession of kings —
Adad-nirari II, Ashurnasirpal II, Shalmaneser III — with a persistence that is
the distinguishing Assyrian characteristic and is visible in the annals, which
record the same campaigns against the same places year after year until they
stopped needing to.

By the ninth century the reconquest had become an expansion, and by the seventh
the empire ran from western Iran to Egypt.

This site's `legitimation` field reads `divine-sanction` alone, and the
Assyrian version is worth distinguishing from the two records either side of it
in the corpus. Naram-Sin of Akkad became a god. Egyptian kings were gods. The
Assyrian king was emphatically not a god: he was the vice-regent of one, and the
royal inscriptions are written as reports to Ashur explaining what has been done
with his instructions. That is a more durable arrangement, and it is the version
that reaches the later Near East.
