The most consequential figure the Ziyadid state produced was not a Ziyadid.

Husayn ibn Salama was an Abyssinian slave who rose to be vizier at the end of
the tenth century and governed in the name of a dynasty that had stopped
governing. What he is remembered for is not politics. He built: congregational
mosques, cisterns, wells and marked stages the length of the road from Hadramawt
to Mecca, and the tradition that reaches Umara is a tradition about
infrastructure rather than about campaigns.

This is worth pausing on, because it is the point at which the state was at its
most effective and the point at which the family that owned it had become
irrelevant to it. Both things are true at once and they are the same fact seen
from two ends. The men who could raise revenue, move troops and build across
five hundred kilometres of road were the household slaves. The men whose name
was in the khutba were minors in a palace at Zabid.

This site records the coding `slave-soldier` for the Ziyadid military basis on
that basis, and it is worth noticing where else the value appears in this
corpus and how consistently the sequel follows. Samarra. Ghazna. Cairo under
four successive dynasties. Aleppo and Damascus. The Mamluk page argues that the
recurring failure of the arrangement is that an army which can make a ruler will
eventually make one it prefers, and the Ziyadid record is that argument in its
purest form, because there was not even a coup: the household simply kept
running the state until there was no longer anyone else in it.

The frontier is the only unfamiliar part. These men were bought across the
strait from the Horn of Africa rather than on the Kipchak steppe, which is what
a Red Sea polity does, and which is why the dynasty that finally took the name
in 1022 was Abyssinian.
