The most consequential figure the Ziyadid state produced was not a Ziyadid.

Husayn ibn Salama was a Nubian slave — the property of an Abyssinian eunuch who
was himself the property of the ruling family — who rose to be vizier in 981 and
governed for thirty years in the name of a dynasty that had stopped
governing. What he is remembered for is not politics. He built: congregational
mosques, cisterns, wells and marked stages the length of the road from Hadramawt
to Mecca, and the tradition that reaches Umara is a tradition about
infrastructure rather than about campaigns.

<Figure id="cistern" />

This is worth pausing on, because it is the point at which the state was at its
most effective and the point at which the family that owned it had become
irrelevant to it. Both things are true at once and they are the same fact seen
from two ends. The men who could raise revenue, move troops and build across
five hundred kilometres of road were the household slaves. The men whose name
was in the khutba were minors in a palace at Zabid.

Governments run by purchased men were the normal arrangement across the Islamic
world in this century — at Samarra, at Ghazna, at Cairo, at Aleppo — and they
failed in a consistent way. An army that can make a ruler will eventually make
one it prefers. What is unusual at Zabid is that the failure took no violent
form for two generations. There was no coup, because there was nothing to
overthrow: the household simply went on running the state until there was
nobody else in it.

The frontier is the other unfamiliar part. These men were bought across the
strait from the Horn of Africa rather than on the Kipchak steppe, which is what
a Red Sea state does, and it is why the dynasty that eventually took the name
was Abyssinian.
