---
title: A governor who stayed
drafted_from: bosworth-1996
phase: formation
---

In 819 the caliph al-Ma'mun sent an officer named Muhammad ibn Ziyad down to the
Yemeni coast to put down a rising among the Alid partisans of the Tihama. This
was an ordinary piece of Abbasid administration and there is no reason to think
anybody in Baghdad expected it to produce a dynasty. Governors were sent to
Yemen regularly. They were recalled, or they died, or they failed.

Ibn Ziyad did the job and then did the thing that turns an appointment into a
state: he built somewhere to govern from. In 820 he laid out Zabid on the
coastal plain, forty kilometres inland from the sea and set among the seasonal
watercourses that come down off the highland escarpment. It was a working
administrative seat rather than a captured city, and his family held it for two
hundred years.

The Samanid page on this site makes an argument about Isma'il ibn Ahmad's move
to Bukhara: that the point at which a provincial family becomes a state is
often the point at which it acquires a centre of gravity that is its own rather
than somebody else's. The Ziyadid case is the same move made two years into the
family's existence rather than seventy, and by a man who never stopped calling
himself the caliph's servant.

That is the whole tension of this record. The Ziyadids were an Abbasid
governorship in their own account of themselves from beginning to end. They
named the caliph in the Friday prayer for two centuries, including the long
stretch after Baghdad had lost the power to appoint or remove anyone in Yemen.
No secession was ever declared, and this page carries no `seceded from` edge.
What happened instead was that the empire receded and the governorship stayed
where it was, which is a different thing and is much harder to date.
