---
title: A governor who stayed
drafted_from: bosworth-1996
phase: formation

In 819 the caliph al-Ma'mun sent an officer named Muhammad ibn Ziyad down to the
Yemeni coast to put down a rising among the Alid partisans of the Tihama. This
was an ordinary piece of Abbasid administration and there is no reason to think
anybody in Baghdad expected it to produce a dynasty. Governors were sent to
Yemen regularly. They were recalled, or they died, or they failed.

Ibn Ziyad did the job and then did the thing that turns an appointment into a
state: he built somewhere to govern from. In 820 he laid out Zabid on the
coastal plain, forty kilometres inland from the sea and set among the seasonal
watercourses that come down off the highland escarpment. It was a working
administrative seat rather than a captured city, and his family held it for two
hundred years.

A provincial family usually becomes a state at the moment it acquires a centre
of gravity that belongs to it rather than to whoever appointed it. Most of them
take a generation or two to reach that point. Ibn Ziyad reached it in his second
year, and did it while calling himself the caliph's servant, which he went on
doing for the rest of his life.

That is the tension the dynasty never resolved and never seems to have wanted
to. The Ziyadids were an Abbasid governorship in their own account of
themselves from beginning to end. They named the caliph in the Friday prayer for
two centuries, including the long stretch after Baghdad had lost the power to
appoint or remove anyone in Yemen. No secession was ever declared. What happened
instead was that the empire receded and the governorship stayed where it was,
which is a different thing and much harder to date.
