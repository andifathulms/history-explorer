Someone told Marjan that the prince's aunt was writing to Najah. Nafis, who was
already the favourite and already feared, complained of it to their master, and
Marjan laid hands on the princess and on her nephew — the boy whose name nobody
recorded — and handed them both over to Nafis.

Nafis had them immured. They were walled into a chamber alive, and they stood
praying for mercy and adjuring him in the name of God for as long as an aperture
remained, until the wall was closed.

That is the end of the Ziyadid dynasty of Zabid. It had lasted two hundred and
six years from the founding of the city.

## What happened next

Nafis took the royal umbrella, struck the coinage in his own name, and made
himself the ruler of the Tihama.

Najah, in the northern provinces, heard what had been done to his masters and
called in his neighbours, Arab and non-Arab, and marched on Zabid. Four battles
are named. At Rima' and at Fashal he was beaten. At al-'Ukda he won. At al-'Irq,
outside the gate of Zabid, Nafis was killed, with five thousand dead between the
two sides. Najah took the city in the month of Dhu'l-Qa'da 412, which is
February or March of 1022.

He then asked Marjan what Nafis had done with their masters. They are in that
wall, Marjan said. Najah had the bodies taken out, prayed over them, and built a
mausoleum over the place where he buried them. Then he had Marjan walled up
alive, in the same wall, with the corpse of Nafis.

Afterwards he took the umbrella and the coinage himself, wrote to Baghdad
offering his submission, and received a title, the right to appoint the judges,
and the administration of Yemen. He ruled the Tihama and much of the highlands
and was addressed as king.

## The four years

The gap between 1018 and 1022 is not an interregnum in which nothing happened,
and it is not, as the tidier version of this story has it, a house that quietly
outlived itself. Two Abyssinian slaves of the same master fought a civil war
across the coastal plain for the possession of a state whose owners were bricked
into a wall in its capital.

What is true in the tidier version is that nobody outside the household was
involved. No highland army came down, no Egyptian fleet arrived, no caliph
intervened. The entire succession — murder, usurpation, four battles, five
thousand dead and a new dynasty recognised from Baghdad — was transacted between
men who had all been bought, by masters who had themselves been bought, inside
one government at Zabid.

The wall is the right image for it. A ruling family that had been ornamental for
two generations was put out of the way by being enclosed in the fabric of its
own palace, and the man who did it was buried in the same masonry four years
later by the man who replaced him.
