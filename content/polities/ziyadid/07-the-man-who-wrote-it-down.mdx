---
title: The man who wrote it down
drafted_from: kay-1892
phase: aside
---

Almost everything known about the Ziyadids comes from one book, and the man who
wrote it was hanged in Cairo for trying to bring back the Fatimids.

Umara ibn Ali al-Hakami was born on the northern Yemeni coast, in the country of
the Banu Hakam, around 1121. He went to the college at Zabid — the Ziyadid
capital — as a student in 1136, and became a jurist and, more profitably, a poet.
He was in Cairo repeatedly on Yemeni business, and by the late 1150s he had
settled there under the protection of the Fatimid vizier Tala'i ibn Ruzzik. The
vizier was a committed Isma'ili and spent some effort trying to bring Umara into
the sect; Umara declined, and Tala'i went on protecting and paying him anyway for
the rest of his life, which is a fair description of how a court poet's career is
supposed to work.

Then the Fatimid state came apart around him. Tala'i was killed; his son was
assassinated; the dissension gave Nur al-Din a pretext and he sent Shirkuh, and
for five years Egypt was fought over by Syrian troops and Crusaders. When the
Fatimid caliphate was finally abolished in 1171, the change reached most Egyptians
only as an alteration in the wording of the Friday sermon.

It did not reach Umara that way. A conspiracy was formed in Cairo to restore the
Fatimids with the help of the king of Jerusalem; it was found out and suppressed;
Umara was among the accused, was convicted, and was executed on 2 Ramadan 569 —
April 1174. His body was gibbeted and left on public view for three days.

The book survived him by a thin margin. Kay worked from a manuscript of
eighty-four folios in the British Museum with neither the author's name nor the
title on it, and the History of Yemen exists as a text at all because later
Yemeni historians quoted it so heavily that its absence would have been obvious.
Its deficiencies are real: Umara is writing three centuries after the Ziyadid
foundation, he is a poet by trade and interested in the things a poet is
interested in, and where he touches events in his own lifetime he is a
participant rather than an observer.

Which is worth stating plainly, because the alternative is worse. The list of
Ziyadid rulers, the million dinars, the wells and the mosques of Husayn ibn
Salama, the name nobody could establish — all of it rests on one manuscript by
one interested man who was killed for a conspiracy. A polity that lasted two
hundred years is known to us because a poet from its province got a job in Cairo
and wrote about home before the rope.
