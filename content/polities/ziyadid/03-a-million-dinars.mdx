Abu'l-Jaysh Ishaq ibn Ibrahim held the Tihama for something like eighty years,
which is long enough that a historian writing two centuries later could take his
reign as the shape of the dynasty rather than an episode in it. Umara, who had
the court records of Zabid to work from, does something better than praise him.
He copies out an account.

He had seen, he says, a statement of Ibn Ziyad's revenues for the year 366 —
976 or 977 — and notwithstanding the reductions they had undergone by then, they
came to a million 'Aththari dinars.

Then he lists what the million did not include. Duties levied on ships from
India. Contributions of musk, camphor, ambergris, sandalwood and Chinese goods.
Taxes on ambergris gathered along the shores of Bab al-Mandab, at Aden, at
Abyan, at Shihr and elsewhere. Imposts on the pearl fisheries. And the tribute
of the ruler of Dahlak, the island group off the African coast, which was paid
partly in people: a thousand head of slaves a year, of whom five hundred were
Abyssinian and Nubian women.

The kings of the Abyssinians, on the far side of the sea, sent him presents and
sought his alliance.

## What that describes

It describes a customs state.

The million in coin is the land revenue, the ordinary tax on the irrigated grain
and cotton of the coastal plain, and Umara notes it as the diminished figure. On
top of it sat everything the Red Sea produced: a percentage of the Indian
trade at the point where it entered the Egyptian route, the specialised
commodities of the ocean, the ambergris washed up along four hundred kilometres
of coast, the pearls, and an island tribute levied in human beings.

The slave figure is the part to sit with. Five hundred women a year, from one
tributary, as a standing item in a revenue account, alongside camphor and
pearls. The dynasty's own army and administration were built out of the same
traffic in the same direction, and the men who eventually took the state came up
that way. It is one commerce, and the Ziyadids were both its customer and its
tax collector.

## Whose name was on the coin

The revenue statement is dated to the eighth decade of the reign, and Umara is
explicit that by then some of the provinces had separated themselves. What that
separation consisted of is the interesting part, and it is easy to misread.

Abu'l-Jaysh did not govern most of Yemen. The king at San'a, a Himyarite named
As'ad ibn Ya'fur, held San'a and Bayhan and Najran and Jurash. Sulayman ibn
Tarf held 'Aththar on the northern coast, a territory seven days' journey long,
with revenues of half a million dinars of his own. Al-Harami held Hali. In the
northern mountains the Zaydi sharif at Sa'da had raised a revolt that no lowland
army was going to reach.

And every one of them recited Abu'l-Jaysh's name in the Friday prayer and struck
his name on their coinage.

Ibn Tarf paid tribute and sent presents but would not come to court in person.
As'ad ibn Ya'fur paid nothing at all — no tribute, no contributions of stores,
not even the customary gifts — and put the coastal king's name on his money
anyway.

That is what supremacy in Yemen looked like at its height, and it is worth
taking seriously rather than reading as a fiction. Nobody in the country was
going to be conquered by anybody: the highlands were fortresses, the plain was
hot, and the distances were long. What could be had was precedence — the
acknowledgement, in the two places where a medieval Muslim ruler's standing was
published, that somebody else was the government. An old man at Zabid had that
from every prince in Yemen while taxing a fraction of it, and a million dinars
besides.
