Two things the Ziyadid state made outlasted it by a long way, and one of them is
the reason anybody knows about the other.

## The road

Husayn ibn Salama's chain of mosques and wells ran from Hadramawt to Mecca, and
a hundred and fifty years after his death a young man from the Tihama walked
large parts of it and wrote down what he found.

Umara ibn Ali al-Hakami was a jurist of Zabid, trained in the Shafi'i law there,
and his account of the road is an eyewitness one. Some of these works I have
seen, he writes, either in good order or in ruins. He had visited al-Makhnak on
the coastal road, one night's journey from Aden, and looked at its well of
eighty fathoms and its ruined mosque. He had seen the great mosque of al-Hawali
on the highland road, still in good order as Husayn built it. He had seen the
mosque at al-Janad, which was the size of Ibn Tulun's at Cairo, and where the
country people had come to believe that a visit in the first week of Rajab was
worth the pilgrimage to Mecca itself — so that a man in debt would ask his
creditor to leave him alone until after the pilgrimage, and mean the trip to
al-Janad.

He lists the stages the whole way north, on the mountain road and the coast road
and the royal highway between them, naming the wells and their depths. Every one
of these, he says, he has seen himself, either in repair or fallen down.

It is one of the more concrete pictures anyone has of what a medieval Islamic
public work amounted to on the ground, and it exists because a lawyer from the
right town happened to travel it and to be interested. A day's stage, a mosque,
a minaret, a well: repeated for sixty days' journey, and by his time about half
gone.

## The book

Umara left Yemen and made his career in Cairo, where he arrived as an envoy and
stayed as a poet at the Fatimid court. The qadi al-Fadil, chief secretary of the
Fatimid government, asked him to write the history of his own country, and he
did — the dynasties of Yemen from the Abbasid governor who founded Zabid down to
his own day, with the court records of Zabid behind him and, for the earliest
part, a book on the history of the city written by one of the kings who had
followed the Ziyadids. That earlier book is otherwise lost. What Umara quoted of
it is what survives.

Then Saladin deposed the Fatimids, and Umara would not let it go. He wrote a
lament for the fallen dynasty — you have blighted, O Fortune, the noblest of
hands — and defended them loudly enough and long enough to become dangerous
company. When a conspiracy to restore them was discovered he was named in it,
tried and condemned. Sentence was carried out on 2 Ramadan 569, in the spring of
1174, and the body was gibbeted for three days.

The qadi who had commissioned the history was by then an adherent of Saladin's
and no longer a friend. He was seen speaking privately to the sultan as the
sentence was pronounced, and Umara called out not to listen to what he was
saying. He was interceding for your life, Saladin answered. Umara put his head
down and said nothing.

## What is left

Zabid outlived all of it. The town Muhammad ibn Ziyad laid out in 820 to govern
a coastal plain from went on being the capital of the Tihama under the
Abyssinian kings who replaced him, and under everyone who replaced them, and its
mosques and its schools of law went on producing the jurists whose lives fill
the Yemeni biographical dictionaries — arranged, as those books are, town by
town, with Zabid always among the fullest entries.

So the dynasty's afterlife is a town that kept working, a road that was half in
ruins within two centuries, and a book written in exile by one of the town's
lawyers, who was hanged for loyalty to a different dynasty in a different
country and whose history is the only reason the four hundred and twelfth year
of the Hijra at Zabid is remembered at all.
