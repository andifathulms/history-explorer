Husayn ibn Salama died in 1012 after thirty years in charge, and the state
passed to a child of the Ziyadid family. Umara, who is the best-informed writer
on this dynasty and had spent his youth in Zabid, cannot tell us the boy's name.

He says so plainly: sovereignty passed to a child of the family whose name I do
not know, but I believe it to have been 'Abd Allah. He offers Ibrahim as an
alternative elsewhere. He is not being careless — he names hundreds of people in
this book, down to the jurists of small towns and the men who told him
anecdotes about a vizier's piety. He simply could not establish what the last
kings of Zabid were called, a century and a half after they lived, in the city
where they lived, from the records of the state they nominally headed.

That is the most exact measurement of the Ziyadid position available. The
dynasty had become an item of protocol.

## Slaves of slaves of slaves

What had happened to the government by then is best set out as a chain.

The boy was placed under the guardianship of a paternal aunt and of a eunuch
named Marjan, who held the office of vizier. Marjan had been a slave of Husayn
ibn Salama. Husayn had been a Nubian slave of an Abyssinian eunuch named Rushd,
who had been a slave of Abu'l-Jaysh and had held the guardianship of the
previous child-ruler alongside *his* aunt. And Marjan in turn had two Abyssinian
slaves whom he had brought up from childhood and, when they came of age,
appointed to run the administration: one named Nafis, who took the capital, and
one named Najah, who took the four best provinces north of Zabid.

Four generations of servitude, each one owning the next, each one exercising
more of the state than the family that owned all of them.

There is no coup in this and no moment where anybody seizes anything. Each step
is a household appointment, made for the ordinary reason that the man was
competent and the master trusted him, and the cumulative effect is a state in
which every person who can raise a soldier or collect a tax is the property of
somebody who is himself property.

## The arrangement holding

It is worth saying that the arrangement worked, for a while, better than the
family had.

Husayn had inherited a kingdom whose outlying provinces had fallen into decay
and whose highland governors had helped themselves to the districts they were
posted to. He made war on the mountain chiefs and brought them back to
submission; Ibn Tarf and Ibn al-Harami re-entered obedience; he recovered, in
Umara's phrase, the original limits of the kingdom, and founded two new towns
while doing it.

None of that was done in the name of a policy or a dynasty. It was done by a
slave on behalf of a child, and when the slave died the reconquest held only as
long as the next slave could hold it.

The weakness in it is obvious in retrospect and was probably obvious then. Power
that rests on a household rather than on a family or a law has no rule for what
happens when two members of the household want the same thing. Marjan preferred
Nafis. Najah had the northern provinces and, Umara says, the affection of the
people, where Nafis was feared. The aunt of the little prince was said to favour
Najah and to be in correspondence with him.

That is the whole of the situation in 1018, and everything that followed came
out of it.
