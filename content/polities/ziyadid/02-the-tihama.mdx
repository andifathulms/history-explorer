---
title: The Tihama
drafted_from: kay-1892

The Tihama is a strip of hot coastal plain between the Red Sea and the wall of
the Yemeni highlands, and it is not obvious country to build a state on. It has
almost no rain. What it has is the water that comes off the escarpment in the
monsoon and runs down a set of great seasonal wadis, and a system of diversion
works that spreads that flood across fields of grain, cotton and dates before it
reaches the sea. Zabid sat on that system.

Ziyadid authority ran up and down the plain and, in the accounts, at times a
good deal further — over the ports, out to the islands, and inland towards
Hadramawt. How far it reached at any given date nobody can now say in figures.
Umara, writing three centuries later from within the tradition, is describing a
remembered greatness, and the boundaries in his account move without warning
between what was taxed, what was garrisoned and what merely read the ruler's
name in the Friday prayer.

What can be said is what the plain was for. The Red Sea run to Egypt passed the
Tihama ports, and the pilgrim traffic came through them, and a state sitting on
the diversion works and the customs houses had two sources of money where the
highland powers above it had one. The grain and the cotton paid for the
government; the sea paid for what the government could not otherwise have
afforded.

The highlands were another matter. Above the escarpment were the Zaydi imams at
Sa'da from 897, and tribes that had been governing themselves for a very long
time, and the Ziyadids never held that country in the way they held the plain.
The division outlasted them by nine hundred years. Every dynasty that afterwards
ruled the coast ran into it, and so did the Ottomans and the Egyptians when
their turn came.
