Everything told about Pelayo and Covadonga in the previous chapter
comes from chronicles written well over a century after the events
they describe - above all the Chronicle of Alfonso III, itself
preserved in two versions that disagree with each other on real
points of detail. Only one narrative source from anywhere close to
the actual events survives: the Chronicle of 754, compiled by a
writer living under Umayyad rule in the south, finished within a
generation or two of Pelayo's own lifetime.

## What the nearest witness does not say

That chronicle says nothing at all about an Asturian uprising. The
silence would be easier to dismiss as an accident of a distant,
poorly informed author if the same chronicle did not go out of its
way to describe events considerably farther from its own vantage
point than Asturias was - the battle of Poitiers in Frankish Gaul, a
major Umayyad defeat in North Africa. A writer capable of reporting
distant catastrophes across the Mediterranean world offers no obvious
reason to have missed, or chosen to ignore, a successful rebellion
much closer to home, if it was already being talked about in the
terms later chroniclers would use.

## What the gap between sources actually means

None of this proves Pelayo's kingdom is invention - later documentary
and archaeological evidence for Asturias as a real, functioning state
is solid enough by the later eighth century. What it shows is that the
vivid, detailed story of Pelayo's own election, his relations with a
Muslim governor named Munnuza, and the battle's own scale, all rest on
a chronicle tradition assembled generations later for a royal court
that had every reason to want a heroic founding story - not on
anything a contemporary actually wrote down. The distinction between
what happened at Covadonga and what was later said to have happened
there is not a minor scholarly quibble; it is close to the whole
problem of writing this kingdom's own earliest history at all.
