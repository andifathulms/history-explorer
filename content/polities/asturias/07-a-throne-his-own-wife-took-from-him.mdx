Alfonso III's final years turned, with a speed the length of his
earlier reign gives no warning of, into a family crisis rather than a
foreign one. His own sons rose against him, and in a detail chroniclers
found worth preserving precisely because of how unusual it was, his
wife joined the rebellion as well rather than stand by her husband
against their own children. Whatever combination of ambition,
resentment at a long wait for the throne, or simple family discord lay
behind it, the coalition against the old king proved too broad for him
to resist.

## Division rather than deposition

Alfonso III was forced to abdicate in 909 or 910, but what followed
was not a straightforward transfer of power to a single successor.
The kingdom he had spent his reign expanding was carved into three
pieces and divided among his own sons: one took León, another Galicia
together with the Portuguese territory to its south, and the third
kept Asturias itself, the original core of the kingdom Pelayo had
founded two centuries before. The old king himself was left nothing
but the single town of Zamora to his name.

## A kingdom that outlived its own unity by exactly as long as its king

Unified Asturias, in any meaningful sense, ended in the same year its
greatest king's reign did - not through conquest by an outside enemy,
who had in fact been unusually quiet during these years, but through
a division imposed by the people who had the most to gain from
Alfonso III no longer being in a position to stop them. What the three
resulting kingdoms did with their separate inheritances - León above
all, which would go on to absorb the others and carry the Asturian
royal line's own claim to primacy among the Christian kingdoms of the
peninsula - belonged to a history no longer written under one crown.
