Alfonso III, who took the Asturian throne in 866, ruled during a
period of real weakness in the Umayyad state to the south - internal
strife that gave a Christian king room to expand in a way none of his
predecessors had enjoyed. He used the opening as fully as any Asturian
king ever would, extending the kingdom's own territory further than
it had reached at any point since Pelayo's own day and earning, in the
memory of later chroniclers, the epithet "the Great."

<Figure id="cruz-victoria" />

## A reign built on an enemy's weakness, not just his own strength

The gains of Alfonso III's reign were real, but they rested on
circumstance as much as on anything the Asturian kingdom itself had
built up structurally - a caliphate too distracted by its own internal
troubles to defend its northern frontier with the same energy it had
shown a century before. This mattered for what came after: territory
won while an enemy was looking elsewhere is not the same as territory
an expanding state could reliably hold once that enemy's attention
returned, and nothing in Alfonso III's own conquests changed the
kingdom's underlying fragility - a nobility still prone to faction,
a crown still dependent on counts and lords who answered to it only
when it suited them.

## The reign that ended in the family that owed it everything

That fragility would not wait for an external enemy to expose it.
Alfonso III's own success, and the length of his reign, gave his
grown sons decades to imagine what they might do with his throne
before he was inclined to give it up - and by the reign's final years,
the greatest king Asturias had produced found the loyalty he could no
longer count on closer to home than any Umayyad frontier.
