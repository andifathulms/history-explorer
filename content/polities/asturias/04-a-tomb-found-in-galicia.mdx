Alfonso II, who reigned from 791 to 842 and is remembered as "the
Chaste," moved the kingdom's seat from Pelayo's old base at Cangas de
Onís to Oviedo, giving Asturias a fixed capital it would keep for the
rest of its existence. He continued the campaigning tradition of
Alfonso I, but two developments of his reign mattered more than any
battle he fought.

## An alliance that provoked a legend

Seeking to secure his position, Alfonso II opened relations with
Charlemagne and, later, with Charlemagne's son Louis the Pious. The
alliance itself is genuine, but it produced something else besides
diplomacy: the legend of Bernardo del Carpio, a supposed Asturian
hero said to have forced the king to abandon treaties with foreign
rulers that lowered Spanish dignity. Bernardo himself is a later
literary invention with no basis in the actual record, and the story
he anchors reflects less an early flowering of Spanish nationalism -
which did not yet exist in any real sense - than the ordinary anxiety
of a mountain nobility watching their king reach for outside allies
who might make him too strong to control at home.

## The discovery that outlasted every battle of the reign

Far more consequential was a religious discovery made during Alfonso
II's own reign: what was believed to be the tomb of the apostle James
was identified in northwestern Galicia. A bishopric was founded on
the site, and the settlement that grew up around it took the name
Santiago de Compostela. Within a generation it was drawing pilgrims
not only from across Spain but from the whole of Christian Europe,
carrying outside influence into the Asturian kingdom along with them.
The discovery's longer-term military use - as a rallying point to
fire Christian fighters against the Muslim south, particularly once
the war against them took on a more overtly religious character in
later centuries - lay still ahead, but the site itself, and the
pilgrimage traffic it created, was already reshaping the Christian
northwest well within Alfonso II's own lifetime.
