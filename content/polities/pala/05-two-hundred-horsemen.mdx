By the end of the twelfth century the dynasty that had once ruled
from Bengal to the borders of the Punjab held little more than
Magadha, under a king remembered by tradition as Indradyumna - though
no inscription proves his own family connection to the Pala line
outright. What ended his rule did not come from a rival Indian
kingdom at all.

## A fort seized before anyone could resist

In 1193 the Turkic general Muhammad, son of Bakhtiyar, already
notorious for repeated plundering raids into Bihar, seized the fort
and city of Bihar in a single daring stroke. A near-contemporary
Muslim historian later met a survivor of the raid, in 1243, and
recorded what he learned: the fort had fallen to a party of only two
hundred horsemen, who rushed the postern gate and took the place
before any organized defense could form. The slaughter that followed
fell hardest on the Buddhist monasteries the Pala kings had built and
endowed for centuries - the killing of the "shaven-headed Brahmans,"
as the Muslim chronicler's own account calls the monks, was so
complete that when Bakhtiyar's men later wanted someone to explain the
contents of the monastery libraries, not a single literate survivor
could be found.

## Eighteen horsemen at the gates of Nadia

The following year Bakhtiyar turned south against the Sena kingdom
that had absorbed most of Bengal from the Palas over the preceding
century. Riding ahead of his main army with a bodyguard of only
eighteen horsemen, he reached the Sena capital at Nadia and rode
straight through the gates - the city's own people mistook him for a
horse-trader rather than an invader. Only at the door of the royal
palace did he draw his sword and attack the unsuspecting household.
The reigning king, caught at his own dinner, fled barefoot out the
back of his palace, abandoning his treasury, his wives, his servants
and his war-elephants to a raiding party a fraction the size of any
garrison that might have stopped it. He escaped to the shrine of
Jagannath in Orissa and died there in exile; Bakhtiyar destroyed Nadia
outright and built his own new capital at Lakhnauti in its place,
endowing mosques and colleges across the conquered kingdom with the
proceeds of the sack.

## What outlasted the conquest

Magadha's own fall came without the same drama recorded for it - no
survivor's account, no named battle, simply the disappearance of the
Pala line from the historical record at the moment Bakhtiyar's
campaigns reached it. A dynasty that had begun four and a half
centuries earlier with a regional election out of chaos ended the same
way most of its neighbors would over the following decades: overrun
by a conquest its own aging and divided successor states had no real
capacity to resist. What survived it was not political - Buddhist
monks fleeing the destruction of Bihar's monasteries carried
manuscripts and teachings north into Nepal and Tibet, work that
preserved, in exile, learning the invasion had tried to erase
outright at home.
