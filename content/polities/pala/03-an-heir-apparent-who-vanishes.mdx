Devapala's own copper-plate grants name a crown prince, Rajyapala,
son and designated heir. After Devapala's death around 850, Rajyapala
disappears from the record entirely - no grant, no inscription, no
later mention explains what became of him, and historians reading the
silence have generally concluded he predeceased his own father rather
than ever taking the throne he had been named to inherit.

## A family that had no strong claimant left

What followed, reconstructed from two rival sets of royal charters
rather than from any continuous narrative, looks like a divided
succession. One line of grants, associated with a king called
Surapala, was favored by the same ministerial family that had served
Dharmapala and Devapala - suggesting Surapala belonged to the direct
line, possibly a younger son of Devapala or even a son of the vanished
Rajyapala himself. A separate line traces power instead to
Vigrahapala, son of Jayapala and grandson of Vakpala, a collateral
branch of the family descended from an earlier, more distant Pala
ancestor. The two sets of records never mention each other's king by
name, and the pattern - a ministerial family's declared loyalty on one
side, a rival branch's own claim on the other - is consistent with the
empire genuinely being split between two men claiming to rule it at
the same time, though the sources are too thin to prove it beyond
reconstruction from silence and omission.

## A collateral line that inherited by default rather than victory

Whatever the precise mechanics of the dispute, the throne settled with
Vigrahapala's branch: by the reign of his son Narayanapala, control of
the empire rested with that family alone, and the ministerial house
that had once served Surapala's line was reconciled to the new
dynasty within Narayanapala's own seventeenth regnal year. The
historians who have studied this transition since call the whole
stretch that followed - five kings, from Vigrahapala I through a later
Vigrahapala II - "a period of stagnation": the energy that had built
the empire under Dharmapala and Devapala simply was not there in their
successors, who held what they had rather than expanding it, and who
proved unable to stop encroachment on every side. Pratihara forces
took parts of Bihar and northern Bengal during this stretch, and
Kamboja chieftains in the northwest edged toward something close to
independence - the slow erosion that would eventually produce the
outright Kamboja seizure of the throne itself.
