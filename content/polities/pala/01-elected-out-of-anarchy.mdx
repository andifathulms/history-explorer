title: Elected out of anarchy

Bengal in the early eighth century had no settled ruler, only what
Smith calls anarchy - and out of that disorder, around 730, the
region's own leading men elected a chieftain named Gopala as king.
It was an unusual beginning for a dynasty: not conquest, not
inheritance, but a choice made by people tired of having neither.
Gopala reigned some forty-five years by tradition, extending his own
authority westward into Magadha, South Bihar, toward the end of his
life. He was a devout Buddhist, and founded a great monastery at
Uddandapura - the modern town of Bihar - that would serve later Pala
kings as an occasional capital. Because "pala" formed part of his own
name and those of his successors, the line became known simply as the
Pala kings of Bengal.

## A client on the throne of Kanauj

Gopala's son Dharmapala, credited with a reign of sixty-four years and
attested for at least thirty-two, extended the family's reach further
than his father had dared. The Tibetan historian Taranath describes
his rule stretching from the Bay of Bengal to Delhi and Jalandhar in
the north, and Dharmapala's own actions bear this out: soon after 800
he dethroned Indrayudha, king of Panchala at Kanauj, and installed a
client named Chakrayudha in his place, doing so, an inscription notes,
"with the assent of the neighbouring northern powers." It was a rare
moment of Pala paramountcy over the very city that would dominate the
dynasty's own foreign relations for the next two centuries - a
paramountcy that lasted only a few years before the Pratihara king
Nagabhata deposed Dharmapala's own client and moved his capital there
himself.

Dharmapala held his own court for a time at the ancient site of
Pataliputra, recovering somewhat from the ruin Hiuen Tsang had found
there a century and a half earlier, and founded the monastery of
Vikramasila - said to have held 107 temples and six colleges - on a
hill above the Ganges. Between his own long reign and his son's, the
Pala kingdom would spend more than a century as one of the genuine
great powers of early medieval India.
