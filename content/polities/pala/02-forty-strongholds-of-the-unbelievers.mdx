Devapala, Dharmapala's own son and the third Pala king, is remembered
by the oldest Bengali chroniclers as the most powerful ruler his
family ever produced. Smith credits him with a reign of forty-eight
years, running from around 810, and records that his general Lausena
carried Pala arms as far as Assam and Kalinga. Like every king of his
line Devapala was a committed Buddhist, and tradition holds that he
waged war on unbelievers vigorously enough to destroy forty of their
strongholds - a claim impossible to verify in detail but consistent
with a reign contemporaries treated as the dynasty's own high-water
mark. His court moved between Pataliputra and a new base at Monghyr,
from which a grant dated to his thirty-third regnal year survives.

## An empire measured in centuries, not conquests

What set the Pala dynasty apart from most Indian ruling houses, Smith
argues, was not any single Devapala-era conquest but sheer endurance:
among major Indian kingdoms only the Andhra dynasty matched its four
and a half centuries on the throne. The century-plus spanning
Dharmapala's and Devapala's reigns together, from roughly 780 to 892,
was also one of real intellectual and artistic flowering - the
painters Dhiman and his son Bitpalo built reputations across the
subcontinent, and the monasteries the two kings founded or endowed,
Vikramasila chief among them, became centers of Buddhist learning that
outlasted the political power that had built them. No later Pala king
would command the same reach Devapala held, but the institutions his
reign left behind gave the dynasty a cultural weight its own later
military decline never fully erased.
