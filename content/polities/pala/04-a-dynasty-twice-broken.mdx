The Pala line's four and a half centuries were not uninterrupted.
During the later tenth century hillmen called Kambojas seized the
throne outright and set up one of their own chiefs as king, an
episode commemorated by an inscribed pillar at Dinajpur dated to
around 966. The usurpation held for over a decade before Mahipala I,
the ninth Pala sovereign, expelled the Kambojas and restored his own
family's rule around 978 - a reign long enough, at fifty-two years by
tradition and at least forty-eight by inscription, that songs in his
honour were still sung in remote corners of Orissa and Kuch Bihar
centuries later. His own reign also saw a genuine cultural export:
Buddhist monks from Magadha traveled to Tibet in 1013 and again in
1038 to help restore a religion that had been weakened there by an
earlier persecution, work that succeeded where Pala military power at
home was already beginning to falter.

## A peasant rebellion and a prince's long exile

The second rupture was worse. Mahipala II imprisoned his own brothers
on taking the throne and governed badly enough to provoke open
revolt, led by a Kaivarta chief named Divyoka whose tribe held real
power in Northern Bengal at the time. The rebels killed Mahipala II
and took the province of Varendra for themselves, passing it after
Divyoka's death to his nephew Bhima. The surviving prince, Ramapala,
spent years traveling India to gather support before finally
assembling an army that included Rashtrakuta troops supplied by his
own in-laws; Bhima was defeated and killed, and Ramapala recovered his
family's throne. His own reign, extending Pala authority into Mithila
and keeping Buddhism flourishing in Magadha's monasteries even as it
declined elsewhere in India, struck some later Bengali chroniclers as
the dynasty's real closing chapter - though inscriptions prove five
more Pala kings followed him.

## Ground lost that was never recovered

Those final kings ruled a shrinking inheritance. Over the dynasty's
last century the Palas lost nearly the whole of Bengal to a rival
Hindu line, the Senas, while holding onto Magadha and northern Bihar
with comparatively little interruption - a slow territorial erosion
rather than any single decisive defeat, and the ground the last Pala
kings had left to lose by the time a foreign conquest finally arrived.
