The Ligurian Republic lasted eight years and was annexed to France in 1805.
Then Napoleon fell, and for a few months in 1814 it looked as though Genoa would
simply be put back.

Lord William Bentinck entered the city to loud cheering, talked a great deal
about restoring the old regime, and went as far as appointing a provisional
government on the republican model while he waited for instructions from London.
The instructions, when they came, were that Genoa had already been disposed of.

## Vienna

The annexation of Genoa to the house of Savoy was a British plan. Pitt had
wanted a strengthened Piedmont as a counterweight to French influence in Italy;
Castlereagh carried the policy forward; Metternich agreed with it. It was
settled before the Genoese were consulted, and the consultation never really
happened.

They sent Pareto to Paris and Brignole to Vienna to argue for their independence
— the name is the same as that of the republic's last doge — and got nowhere.
Metternich told a Genoese lady in Paris that in his estimate scarcely one
Genoese in ten sincerely wanted the old republic back. The emperor Francis put it
to Brignole more directly: *the republic is no longer of our days*.

They were not simply being brushed off. The people of the two Rivieras, from
Savona to Spezia, disliked Genoa and had centuries of reason to; the countryside
had been governed as a subject territory and behaved like one. There was talk of
making the city a sort of Hanseatic town with no territory outside its walls, and
talk of a principality on the same footing, and none of it survived contact with
what the allies actually wanted.

On 27 December 1814 the British commander publicly notified the Genoese of the
orders received from Vienna. The republic that had held colonies from the
Pillars of Hercules to the Black Sea was enrolled among the dominions of the
house of Savoy as a duchy. The doge Bentinck had temporarily set up resigned his
post and knelt before Victor Emmanuel I.

## Bent's verdict, and the objection to it

Bent, writing in 1881, thinks this was for the best and says so with a Victorian
flourish: better this than dragging on as a factious, querulous republic when
that form of government was superannuated in Italy. He goes further and says that
all hope of Genoa's past glory had faded when Columbus set foot in America, and
that it would have been better for her if the last three centuries could be
struck from her history and she had died while her honours lay thick around her.

The first half of that is a judgement and the second half is nonsense. The three
centuries Bent wants deleted are the centuries in which Genoese houses financed
the Spanish empire and the city was, per head, probably the richest place in
Europe. What offends him is that it was rich without being important, which is a
complaint about glory rather than about Genoa.

He is also wrong about what the city had left in it, and he says so himself two
paragraphs later.

## What was still there

Giuseppe Mazzini was born in Genoa in 1805, in the year the Ligurian Republic
was absorbed into France, and is buried in the city's cemetery. Genoa under the
Sardinian crown became the kingdom's principal port and its commerce recovered.
And in 1860 Garibaldi's expedition to Sicily — the one that made Italy — sailed
from Genoa.

That is a strange and rather exact afterlife for this particular republic. Genoa
spent seven hundred years as the place where somebody else's projects were
financed, crewed and shipped: the Byzantine reconquest of Constantinople, the
Spanish silver system, the Atlantic voyages of a Genoese sailor working for
Castile. It ended as a duchy, and then supplied the harbour, the ships and the
propagandist for the unification of the country it had spent those seven
centuries refusing to be part of.

The city did not become important again. It went on being the place things left
from.
