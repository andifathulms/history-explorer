Andrea Doria was an admiral with a fleet under contract, first to the pope, then
to France. In 1528, unpaid and unhappy, he took the fleet and the city out of
French service, put both under the protection of Charles V, and rewrote the
constitution.

The new arrangement had a doge elected for two years and ineligible immediately
afterwards, a Great and a Lesser Council, and — the decisive part — the old
clans dissolved and their members redistributed into twenty-eight artificial
*alberghi*, each an association of families required to take a common surname.
The factions were abolished by administrative fiat and reassembled in shapes that
cut across the old alignments.

It held for two hundred and sixty-nine years, which is longer than anything else
Genoa tried.

## What it cost

Independence, more or less, and it is worth saying so plainly rather than
treating 1528 as a restoration.

Genoa after Doria was in the Spanish system: the fleet served the Habsburgs, the
republic followed Spanish foreign policy, and Genoese banking houses became the
principal financiers of the Spanish crown. The silver from Potosí and Zacatecas
was landed at Seville and disbursed across Europe through Genoese bills of
exchange, and Spanish armies in Flanders were paid with money Genoese houses
advanced against fleets that had not yet arrived.

It made a period historians call the Genoese century, from roughly 1557 to 1627,
in which the city was probably the richest place in Europe per head and had
almost no political weight of its own. Then Spain defaulted, repeatedly, and the
houses that had lent to it did not recover.

## The man who would not be doge

Doria never held the office his constitution created. He was offered the
dogeship and refused it, and directed the republic for the next thirty years as
a private citizen with a fleet under contract to a foreign king.

That is not modesty. A doge was elected for two years, bound by the constitution
he had just written, and replaceable. An admiral whose ships the emperor needed
was neither. Doria arranged Genoa so that nobody could dominate it through its
offices, and then dominated it from outside them, and the arrangement held for
as long as he lived and for two and a half centuries after he died.

It is the most Genoese arrangement in four hundred years of them. The
republic's decisive figure held no post in it, and the constitution that saved
the city was written by a man under contract to the Habsburgs.
