The most consequential thing Genoa produced was not a fleet or a bank. It was
sailors, and most of them worked for somebody else.

In 1291 two brothers named Vadini fitted out two galleys; Tedisio D'Oria and
Ugolino Vivaldi joined them; and together they passed the Pillars of Hercules and
sailed out into the Atlantic. The Genoese registers for that year list two ships
belonging to D'Oria, the *S. Antonio* and the *Allegrancia*. They did not come
back.

The Genoese have claimed the Canaries on the strength of it ever since, and the
argument is a pleasing piece of onomastics: one of the islands is called
Allegranza, and another is Lanzarote, which the French assign to a countryman of
theirs — while a Genoese cartographer in 1455 drew a Genoese flag at the Canaries
and wrote under it *Maroxello Lanzerotto*, and a Marocello family with a member
named Lanzerotto was living in Genoa when D'Oria sailed. Bent's own verdict on
the claim is that it is probably one of those points that will never be
satisfactorily proved.

## The man on the Gambia

A hundred and sixty-three years later a Genoese named Antonio Uso di Mare turned
up on the west coast of Africa. He was of a consular family and, Bent notes with
satisfaction, a spendthrift travelling to escape his creditors, which is why his
letters home survive alongside his journal.

In 1454 he met a Venetian, Cadamosto, at the Cape Verde Islands, and the two
agreed to push down the African coast together looking for a route to India — the
year after Constantinople fell to the Ottomans, with the Genoese trembling for
their Black Sea colonies and needing another way to the east. Two men from the
rival republics, on the far side of Africa, jointly looking for the road that
would eventually put both of them out of business.

They were shot at with poisoned arrows on the Gambia and moved on to the
territory of a local ruler, who gave Uso di Mare thirty-one slaves, elephant
tusks, parrots and some civets, and lent him a secretary to smooth things over
upriver. And there Uso di Mare reported meeting five men from the country of
Prester John, one of whom said he was of Genoese descent — the last of the line of
a Vivaldi wrecked on the coast of Guinea a hundred and sixty years earlier.

Bent prints it and then says the sensible thing: marvellous indeed, if true. It
is exactly the sort of story a man writes home to creditors. It is also exactly
the sort of thing the Genoese did.

## 1477

The pattern is a republic that produced navigators and then had no use for them.
Genoese seamen and merchants went in crowds to the Portuguese court and sailed
under the Pessagno family's direction on the voyages down Africa; Antonio di Noli,
a Genoese, is credited with the Cape Verde Islands. And then the case that makes
the pattern impossible to miss.

Christopher Columbus was the son of Domenico Colombo, a Genoese woolstapler — not
a poor man, Bent is careful to say, but a trader with a house outside the Porta S.
Andrea, a business along the Riviera, and full eligibility for the honours of the
republic. When Columbus had matured his plans, in 1477, he laid them before the
Genoese government before going anywhere else.

Genoa turned him down. Bent's explanation is a description of the city's ordinary
condition: the treasury was empty, the Adorni and the Fregosi were tearing the
government apart, one of them backed by Milan and the other stirring revolt in
Corsica, and the people of the two Rivieras were in complaint over taxes. The
republic that had sent galleys past the Pillars of Hercules in 1291 could not
find the money in 1477, because its factions had spent it fighting each other.

He went to Castile. Bent adds the part that is usually left out: being Genoese,
which had given Columbus his training in the best school of navigation in Europe,
told against him for the rest of his life. The Aragonese had fought the Genoese
over Corsica and Sardinia for generations, and had had their king taken prisoner
by a Genoese fleet at Ponza in 1435. He owed everything to Isabella of Castile,
including the ships, and was resented by her husband's people from first to last.

## What that adds up to

Genoa's overseas empire was run by syndicates, its public debt was run by a
shareholders' bank, its wars were fought by admirals under contract to foreign
kings, and its greatest navigator sailed for Spain. There is a temptation to read
all of that as failure, and Bent gives in to it.

It is better read as the same thing described four ways. This was a republic
that never succeeded in being a state, and never needed to. What it had was
capital, ships and men, all of them for hire, and it exported all three to
whoever could pay — including, in the end, the monarchy that used them to take a
hemisphere Genoa had been offered first and could not afford.
