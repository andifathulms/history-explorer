The Genoese century Doria's constitution had built collapsed with
Spain's own repeated defaults, and what followed was a hundred and
seventy years in which the Republic's independence survived mostly
because nobody found it worth the trouble of finishing off - until
Louis XIV decided otherwise.

## Thirteen thousand bombs in two days

Louis had said for years that whoever held Genoa held the keys to
Italy, and by the 1680s he had assembled reasons to act on it: Genoese
loyalty to Spain, a refused French demand over ship salutes, exiled
Fieschi conspirators clamoring for restored property at his own court.
In 1684 he sent an ambassador with an ultimatum - abandon Spain, salute
the French flag on demand, open a salt depot at Savona, restore the
Fieschi estates - backed by a fleet of a hundred and sixty ships and
eight thousand soldiers. The Genoese senate refused. On 17 and 18 May
the French admiral Seignelay bombarded the city until his supply of
bombs was exhausted - some thirteen thousand three hundred shells,
by contemporary count, reducing whole streets of palaces to rubble
while the government relocated to the Albergo dei Poveri for safety.
Rumors spread of an infernal machine set to finish the city in one
blow; residents wept in the streets and had to be restrained from
massacring French prisoners once the fleet, out of ammunition,
withdrew without warning.

## A doge who found himself at Versailles

With no help forthcoming from an exhausted Spain, a distracted Germany,
or an indifferent England, the senate accepted Louis's terms through
papal mediation: the Spanish alliance abandoned, indemnities paid, and
the doge himself sent to Versailles in person to apologize - a
condition no doge had ever been required to meet, since Genoese law
forbade the office-holder from leaving the Republic's territory at
all. Francesco Maria Lercaro made the journey with a hundred
attendants, was received by Louis with an embrace and a state banquet,
and was asked by the king, touring him through the palace gardens,
what had surprised him most of everything he had seen there. "To see
myself here," the doge replied. He returned to Genoa by night, and no
one spoke publicly of the humiliation.

## A rebellion Genoa could not put down, and an island sold instead

The Republic's Corsican possession, restless for generations, rose in
open rebellion under Pasquale Paoli in the 1750s and 60s, and Paoli's
militia and administrative reforms - a university at Corte, a
representative assembly, a law code that outlawed the vendetta - built
something close to a functioning independent state faster than
Genoese arms could dislodge it. When Genoese envoys offered Corsica
near-total autonomy in exchange for keeping only the two loyal
garrison towns of Bonifacio and Calvi, Paoli refused any settlement
short of complete Genoese withdrawal, gambling that France - already
courting him - would back full independence instead. He gambled wrong.
With her treasury exhausted and Austria eyeing the island as well,
Genoa concluded in 1768 that the wiser course was to sell her claim to
Corsica outright to the French crown, the terms drawn up at Versailles
between the Duc de Choiseul and the Genoese envoy Domenico Sorba.
France's own conquest of Paoli's Corsica followed within a year - and
Genoa entered its final three decades as a republic that had bought a
little more time by giving away the one overseas possession that had
outlasted its medieval trading empire.
