In 1407 the republic's creditors were consolidated. The various funded debts,
each secured on a particular tax, were merged into a single chartered
institution — the Casa di San Giorgio — whose shareholders were the state's
creditors and to whom the taxes servicing the debt were assigned directly.

The republic then went on failing to pay, and the Casa went on taking. By the
later fifteenth century it was administering most of Genoa's indirect taxes, and
because a colony that could not pay its garrison was a defaulting asset, it took
the colonies too: Corsica for long stretches, Caffa and the Black Sea stations,
and various Ligurian towns. A public bank owned by bondholders governed
territory, appointed its officials, kept its accounts and fought its wars, on
behalf of its shareholders, for most of two centuries.

Machiavelli, who noticed, wrote that Genoa was a state within a state and that if
the Casa ever acquired the whole of it the republic would be more remarkable than
Venice.

## What the Casa actually was

It is easy to say bank and let the word do work it cannot do.

The Casa di San Giorgio did not lend to the republic; it *was* the republic's
debt, reorganised into shares that traded. Its shareholders were people who had
lent to the state and who now, collectively, employed the tax collectors, kept
the books, appointed the governors of overseas possessions, garrisoned them, and
went to law and occasionally to war on their own account. Its accounts were
better kept than the government's. Its credit was better than the government's.
When Corsica revolted, it was the Casa's problem.

A joint-stock company administering territory on behalf of shareholders, with its
own revenue and its own troops, is the Dutch and English East India companies
three hundred years early. The difference is that those companies were chartered
to go out and acquire things, and this one acquired an empire by foreclosing on
one.

## The families, still

None of this stopped the politics.

Genoa in the fifteenth century changed government roughly every two years,
alternated between French and Milanese lordship, and was sacked by its own
factions more than once. The doges elected for life after 1339 averaged
something closer to three years in office than a life, and several were
overthrown by the family that had put them in.

What is worth holding onto is that the commerce did not stop while this happened.
The Genoese abroad — in Seville, in Lisbon, in Chios, in Caffa, in Bruges — went
on operating, because the network was a network of families and contracts rather
than a state, and it did not need the city to be governed. Epstein's point is that Genoa was strong
roughly in inverse proportion to how much its government mattered.

That is the opposite of what the neighbouring republic demonstrates, from an
almost identical starting position, and it is the reason the pair is worth
thinking about together. Venice's strength was its state. Genoa's strength was
everything the state had subcontracted, delegated or lost hold of — and for
about four hundred years it worked.
