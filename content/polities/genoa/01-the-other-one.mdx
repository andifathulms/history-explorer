Genoa and Venice were founded on the same problem and answered it the same way.
Both are ports with almost no agricultural hinterland — Liguria is a strip of
coast under a mountain wall, which is a different kind of nothing from a lagoon
and is equally unfarmable. Both lived by carrying other people's goods. Both
ended in 1797 at the hands of the same French general.

Everything between the beginning and the end is different.

## The Compagna

Venice's constitution is a machine and Genoa's is a series of attempts.

The Genoese commune begins in 1099 as the *Compagna Communis*, a sworn
association of the city's men formed for a term of years and renewed — the oath
of a group of merchants to act together, with elected consuls, which is an
arrangement for a voyage applied to a city. It was renewed, altered, allowed to
lapse and reconstituted repeatedly over the following century.

There is no founder to name. There was a meeting, and an oath, and a renewal
date.

## Four families

What the Compagna did not do was resolve who ran it, and Epstein's book is
unusually willing to say that the Genoese constitution did not work.

The Doria, Spinola, Fieschi and Grimaldi — with a longer list behind them — were
clans holding towers, quarters of the city, client networks and their own armed
followings, and Genoese politics from the twelfth century to the sixteenth is
those families in combination against each other. The constitutional record is a
sequence of devices for containing them: elected consuls, then a *podestà*
imported from another city on a one-year contract precisely because he was not
Genoese, then *capitani del popolo*, then in 1339 a doge elected for life, then
in 1528 a doge elected for two years.

None of them held. Genoa spent long stretches of the fourteenth and fifteenth
centuries under the lordship of Milan or of the king of France, in every case
because one faction had invited them in against another.

## The same paperwork, opposite results

Both republics elected their heads of state by documented constitutional
procedure, in writing, with the qualifications and the method set out. One of
them was never taken over from inside in five hundred years. The other spent a
good part of two centuries governed by foreigners because its leading families
kept fetching them.

The difference is not in the constitution. Genoa's arrangements were often
ingenious — importing a chief magistrate from another city specifically so that
he would have no relatives in this one is a serious idea, seriously implemented.
What Genoa did not have was a body of people who preferred the constitution to
their own faction, and no amount of drafting supplies that.

It is worth holding onto, because the reverse assumption is very natural: that
the republic which lasted must have had the better machinery. Venice's machinery
was better. It was not better enough to explain the difference, and Genoa is the
control case that shows it.
