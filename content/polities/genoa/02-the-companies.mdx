The Genoese overseas empire was not, for the most part, acquired by the Genoese
state. It was acquired by companies of investors who financed an expedition and
were repaid with the revenue of whatever it took.

The instrument was the *maona*. A group of creditors advanced the money for a
fleet; the republic granted them the conquest to exploit until the debt was
discharged; the debt was rarely discharged. The Maona of Chios held that island
from 1346 until the Ottomans took it in 1566 — two hundred and twenty years of a
shareholders' company governing a territory, its members adopting a common
surname, Giustiniani, to hold the shares in.

The same shape covers Phocaea on the Anatolian coast, whose alum works were the
principal European source of the mordant without which cloth cannot be dyed, and
Caffa in the Crimea, the terminus of the routes out of central Asia and one of
the great slave markets of the medieval world.

## Who was actually doing it

The ordinary questions to ask about a state are what its army rested on and where
its income came from. Both of them assume a state that raises armies and collects
income, and for long stretches Genoa did neither.

The wars were licensed to syndicates. The colonies were administered by the
people who had financed them. The republic supplied a flag, a charter and a
legal personality, and took a share. It is a genuinely unusual way to run an
overseas empire and it is the reason Genoa could hold territory from the Aegean
to the Crimea while its own government was collapsing every couple of years at
home.

## Nymphaion

The single most profitable thing Genoa ever did was pick the right side in 1261.

The Nicaean government in exile needed a fleet to take Constantinople back from
the Latins and the Venetians. Genoa
provided one, in exchange for exemption from Byzantine customs, quarters in every
significant port, and the trade of the Black Sea. Constantinople fell to a
Nicaean scouting party weeks later, before the Genoese fleet had done much of
anything, and the privileges were honoured anyway.

What Genoa got out of it was Pera, later Galata, the walled suburb across the
Golden Horn from Constantinople. By the 1340s the customs revenue of that colony
was reportedly several times the emperor's own, taken from ships using the same
waterway, and Byzantium fought a naval war to recover the difference and lost.

It is the mirror image of the customs exemption Venice had extracted from a
Byzantine emperor in 1082, and between them the two documents are most of the
explanation for why the eastern empire had no money in its last two centuries. A
Greek emperor bought a fleet with his own customs revenue, twice, from two
different republics, and neither purchase was reversible.
