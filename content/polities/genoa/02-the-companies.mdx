The Genoese overseas empire was not, for the most part, acquired by the Genoese
state. It was acquired by companies of investors who financed an expedition and
were repaid with the revenue of whatever it took.

The instrument was the *maona*. A group of creditors advanced the money for a
fleet; the republic granted them the conquest to exploit until the debt was
discharged; the debt was rarely discharged. The Maona of Chios held that island
from 1346 until the Ottomans took it in 1566 — two hundred and twenty years of a
shareholders' company governing a territory, its members adopting a common
surname, Giustiniani, to hold the shares in.

The same shape covers Phocaea on the Anatolian coast, whose alum works were the
principal European source of the mordant without which cloth cannot be dyed, and
Caffa in the Crimea, the terminus of the routes out of central Asia and one of
the great slave markets of the medieval world.

## What the coded fields make of it

`military_basis` on this record reads `mercenary` alone and `revenue_basis` reads
`trade-toll, monopoly`, and neither says the thing this chapter is about, which
is that the actor was frequently not the state.

That is not a gap in the evidence. It is a gap in what a vocabulary of
institutions can express. This site's fields ask what the polity's army rested on
and where its income came from, and both questions presuppose a polity that
raises armies and collects income. A republic that licenses its wars to
syndicates and lets its creditors administer its colonies is answering a
different question, and the fourth turning point on this record is what happens
when the arrangement is taken to its conclusion.

## Nymphaion

The single most profitable thing Genoa ever did was pick the right side in 1261.

The Nicaean government in exile — this site carries it as a record — needed a
fleet to take Constantinople back from the Latins and the Venetians. Genoa
provided one, in exchange for exemption from Byzantine customs, quarters in every
significant port, and the trade of the Black Sea. Constantinople fell to a
Nicaean scouting party weeks later, before the Genoese fleet had done much of
anything, and the privileges were honoured anyway.

What Genoa got out of it was Pera, later Galata, the walled suburb across the
Golden Horn from Constantinople. This corpus's Byzantine record states the
consequence in its chapter on 1204: by the 1340s the customs revenue of the
Genoese colony across the water was reportedly a multiple of the emperor's own,
from the same waterway, and Byzantium fought a naval war over it and lost.

It is the mirror image of the Venetian golden bull of 1082, and between them the
two documents are most of the explanation for why the Byzantine record's last two
centuries have no money in them.
