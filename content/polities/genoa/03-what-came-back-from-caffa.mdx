Genoa's empire was a chain of trading stations at the far end of other people's
routes, and the furthest and most profitable of them was Caffa in the Crimea,
where the roads out of central Asia met the sea. In 1346 a Tartar army besieged
it. What sailed home from that siege is the reason the fourteenth century is
remembered for something other than trade.

The account everybody quotes was written by Gabriele de' Mussi, a notary of
Piacenza. It is the source of the single most famous image in the history of
epidemic disease, and the man was not there.

## The siege

De' Mussi's narrative begins in 1346, when an immense number of Tartars and
Saracens in the East fell victim to a mysterious and sudden death, and whole
provinces and cities were depopulated in a brief space. Tana, on the sea of Azov,
where Italian merchants much resorted, was besieged and taken; the Christian
merchants expelled from it were received for the protection of their persons and
property inside the walls of Caffa, which the Genoese had built in that country.

The Tartars followed them and besieged Caffa in turn. The inhabitants, completely
encircled, could hardly obtain the necessaries of life, and their only hope lay
in the fleet that brought them provisions. Then the death broke out in the Tartar
host, and thousands were daily carried off, *as if arrows from heaven were
striking at them and beating down their pride*.

> At first the Tartars were paralysed with fear at the ravages of the disease,
> and at the prospect that sooner or later all must fall victims to it. Then they
> turned their vengeance on the besieged, and in the hope of communicating the
> infection to their Christian enemies, by the aid of the engines of war, they
> projected the bodies of the dead over the walls into the city.

The defenders held their ground and committed as many of the bodies as they could
to the sea. The air became tainted and the wells poisoned, and the disease spread
so fast in the city that few of the inhabitants had strength enough to fly from
it.

## The ships

Some of the ships that got out of Caffa were bound for Genoa, some for Venice,
some for other parts of the Christian world, and a few of the sailors were
already infected. What de' Mussi wrote about the arrival is not a chronicle
entry. It is an address:

> Tell, O Sicily, and ye, the many islands of the sea, the judgments of God.
> Confess, O Genoa, what thou hast done, since we of Genoa and Venice are
> compelled to make a chastisement manifest. Alas! our ships enter the port, but
> of a thousand sailors hardly ten are spared. We reach our homes; our kindred
> and our neighbours come from all parts to visit us. Woe to us for we cast at
> them the darts of death! Whilst we spoke to them, whilst they embraced us and
> kissed us, we scattered the poison from our lips.

Three plague ships put into Genoa in January 1348, and by another account a
fourth carried the contagion to Venice at the same time. From those two harbours
it went over the whole of Italy and then over Europe.

He adds the detail people believed and acted on: that the clothes and effects of
the dead carried it. Four soldiers near Genoa took a woollen bed-covering from a
house at Rivarolo on the coast, where the sickness had killed everybody; they
slept under it that night and in the morning all four were dead.

## The witness who was not there

Gasquet, translating all this in 1893, introduces de' Mussi as an eyewitness who
came home on one of the ships. Then, at the bottom of the same page, he prints
the footnote that demolishes his own sentence: a scholar named Tononi had shown
that de' Mussi acted as a notary at Piacenza throughout those years, and that his
statement that the siege lasted three years is a mistake of the kind a man who
had been present would not make.

So the founding scene of the Black Death in Europe — the corpses lobbed over the
walls of a Genoese colony by a besieging army — comes from a lawyer several
thousand miles away, writing up what returning sailors told him, in the rhetorical
mode of a prophet denouncing a city. It may be substantially true. Caffa was
besieged, the plague was in the Tartar army, and the ships did come home. Whether
anybody loaded a trebuchet with a body is a claim resting on one man's second-hand
report.

## What it says about the empire

The story survived because it is a perfect description of what this republic
actually was.

Genoa had built a walled town at the end of the caravan routes in order to stand
where Asia met the sea, and had made itself the shortest line between the inland
of one continent and the ports of another. That was the whole business model:
speed, distance, and a chain of harbours nobody else could reach so quickly. The
plague used the same line, at the same speed, in the same hulls. A trading
republic organised to move goods from the furthest place to the nearest one had
no mechanism at all for not moving this.
