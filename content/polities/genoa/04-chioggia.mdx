The two republics fought four wars. The fourth, from 1378, is the one that
settled it, and Genoa came closer to destroying Venice than any power ever did.

A Genoese fleet under Pietro Doria took Chioggia, at the southern entrance to the
Venetian lagoon, in August 1379. Venice was blockaded from the sea and attacked
on land by Padua and Hungary at the same time. It offered terms; Doria's reply,
as the Venetian chroniclers have it, was that he would come and bridle the horses
of Saint Mark himself.

Venice armed everything, raised money by promising membership of the Great
Council to thirty families who paid — the only breach of the Serrata in five
hundred years — and instead of fighting the Genoese fleet, sank hulks in the
channels behind it and blockaded the blockaders. The Genoese in Chioggia spent
the winter cut off, and in June 1380 the survivors surrendered.

## Which one won

Both fleets were destroyed and both republics were bankrupt. The peace, mediated
at Turin in 1381, went formally against Venice, which gave up Dalmatian claims
and the island of Tenedos.

What happened afterwards is the answer. Venice recovered, took the Italian
mainland within twenty-five years, and held the Levant trade for another two
centuries. Genoa did not seriously contest the eastern Mediterranean again, and
spent the following decades under French and then Milanese lordship.

The difference is not naval. It is that Venice had a government capable of
requisitioning the wealth of its citizens in an emergency and Genoa had four
families who would not.

## After 1380

Genoa's centre of gravity moved from shipping to money, and it moved completely.

The Genoese did not stop trading, but the thing they became indispensable at was
finance: bills of exchange, the fairs at Besançon and later Piacenza where much
of Europe's debt was cleared, and eventually the business of lending to the crown
of Spain against silver that had not yet arrived from America.

A merchant republic that loses a trade war and turns into a banking centre is a
recognisable modern story, and this is a very early full version of it. What
makes it worth watching is that Genoa got richer doing it. The century after
Genoa stopped being a naval power was the century in which Genoese houses were
the most important lenders in Europe — which is a warning against reading a lost
war as the beginning of decline, and against reading wealth as strength.
