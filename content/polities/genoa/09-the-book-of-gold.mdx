Genoa and Venice both tried to stay neutral in the wars of the French Revolution,
and neutrality turned out to be the one position neither side would allow.

The English consul in Genoa, Drake, demanded that the republic expel the French
minister, ally itself with Britain, and open its harbour to George III's ships,
and said that if it refused it would be treated as an enemy. Admiral Hood was in
the Mediterranean with a dozen vessels to make the point. Two English ships
seized a French vessel inside Genoese neutral waters, and the French then
demanded compensation for the loss — so the Genoese government paid the French
claim out of its own members' pockets, to preserve a neutrality that was already
being violated by both parties.

In the spring Drake went to the council hall, demanded the expulsion of every
French agent in the city, was quietly and firmly refused by the doge, declared
Genoa at war with England, and left for Leghorn.

## Nelson

The incident that finished it belongs to a more famous officer.

On 11 September 1796 the French were unloading a ship full of arms just outside
Genoa. Nelson was in the harbour at the time, under the neutral flag. He sailed
out as though putting to sea, went a mile or so, turned sharply round, seized the
French ship and made off with it.

That was the end of the neutrality. The French minister in the city made the most
of it, and the Genoese government, seeing no way left to stand aside, sent an
agent to Paris to get what terms he could. The terms were: close the ports to
the English, give no help to France's enemies, and hand over two million francs
from the bank as indemnity, with another two million to be advanced as a loan by
the republic's wealthy citizens. In exchange France would protect Genoa if
Britain attacked it, and would respect Ligurian territory.

A republic that had spent five hundred years being difficult to govern was
manoeuvred into an alliance nobody in it wanted, by two navies, over a period of
about eighteen months.

## The rising

What the alliance brought was French revolutionary agitators, working openly
under the protection of the French legation, and by the following spring the city
was ready to go off.

On the night before, according to Bent, every square and alley of Genoa was full
of stump orators and drunken audiences under torchlight. At dawn on 22 May the
insurgents came out with tricolour tassels in their caps, opened the prisons and
the dockyard, armed the criminals and the galley slaves, and set about
overthrowing the government. The senate sat in the council hall and did nothing
at all, when — as events proved within the day — it still had the strength to
stop them.

What stopped them instead was the other half of the city. Artisans, porters and
coal-heavers, who regarded the whole business as an attack on their religion,
tied small images of the Madonna to their caps, seized the arsenal, and came out
better armed and with most of the regular soldiers behind them. Napoleon called
them Genoese priests. They won: the rising was beaten in a day of street
fighting, its leader was killed, and his corpse was kicked through the town.

Bent tells one story about that day and hedges it himself. A Turkish galley
slave had been let out of the prison hulks on condition that he go about shouting
*Viva il popolo*. He did, until he met a band of the other faction, who beat him
and released him on condition that he shout *Viva Maria* instead. He did that
too, until he met some revolutionaries, who beat him until he could not stand.
Lying on the pavement, he is supposed to have said: the Christians must be mad.

## The bonfire

Winning the street fight did not help.

Napoleon complained that Frenchmen had been killed in the disturbance, passing
over the detail that they had been killed taking part in a sedition, and that the
government had used the Madonna men to assassinate them. He demanded the release
of every prisoner, which meant turning loose everyone who had tried to overthrow
the state, and the disarming of the men who had defended it. The senate complied.
Then it sent a deputation to his headquarters at Montebello to accept whatever
constitution he cared to impose.

The terms came back on 14 June. The privileges of the nobility abolished; a doge
and council representing the whole body of citizens; a further large indemnity;
and, in exchange, French recognition of Genoese independence. Bent's printed
dates for these events read 1796, which the surrounding narrative contradicts;
the year is 1797.

The city celebrated. The crowd went to the palace, brought out the *Libro d'Oro*
— the book of gold, the register in which the noble families of Genoa had been
entered since Andrea Doria's constitution of 1528 — carried it to the Piazza
d'Acquaverde, and burned it. Ballot boxes went on the fire, and ducal chairs, and
coats of arms, while a crowd danced round the embers turning the leaves of the
book over with their bayonets. Doria's statues were pulled down and their heads
kicked through the town.

Napoleon, at Montebello, called the Genoese foolish idiots.

Six hundred and ninety-eight years after a group of merchants swore an oath to
act together, the thing that ended the Republic of Genoa was a bonfire of its own
membership list, lit by its own citizens, on terms dictated by a general who
despised them.
