Dalem Waturenggong's reign is the one every later Balinese account treats as
the golden age — the point the courts of the nine kingdoms measured themselves
against for the next four hundred years.

Gelgel under him held Bali, the eastern tip of Java, Lombok and parts of
Sumbawa, which is the widest this polity ever reached. But the reason the reign
is remembered is not the territory. It is that this is when the Javanese
arrived.

## Nirartha

Danghyang Nirartha was a Javanese brahmin priest who came to Bali in
Waturenggong's reign as Majapahit was ending, and the Balinese tradition
credits him with a great deal: the reorganisation of the priesthood, a body of
religious literature, the *padmasana* shrine that now stands in the north-east
corner of every Balinese temple, and the founding of temples along the south
and west coasts — Uluwatu, Tanah Lot, Rambut Siwi — which are the ones a
visitor photographs.

Whether one man did all of that is doubtful in the way such traditions usually
are. What the tradition is describing, whoever did it, is a reorganisation of
Balinese religion on Javanese lines inside a single reign, carried out by
people who had just arrived from a court that had stopped existing.

## The caste order

The other thing dated to this period is the *wangsa* system: the division of
Balinese society into the three high groups — brahmana, satria, wesia — and the
*jaba* majority outside them, with the ruling houses of the later nine kingdoms
placing themselves in it by descent from named Javanese ancestors who came with
Waturenggong's court.

That is what the Majapahit connection actually consists of, and it is worth
being clear that it is not a sentimental genealogy. It was the organising
principle of who could marry whom, who could hold which office, and what level
of Balinese a person was addressed in — and it was still doing that work in the
twentieth century.

Descent from a Javanese court that had ceased to exist was not a memory on
Bali. It was the constitution.
