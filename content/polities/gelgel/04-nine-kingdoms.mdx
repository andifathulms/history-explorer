Gelgel's paramountcy collapsed around 1686 and the court moved to Klungkung.
Its ruler kept the title Dewa Agung and the ritual precedence, and lost the
power.

Bali spent the next two hundred years as nine kingdoms — Klungkung, Badung,
Mengwi, Gianyar, Bangli, Karangasem, Buleleng, Tabanan and Jembrana —
quarrelling with each other, and from Karangasem holding Lombok across the
strait.

Only one of the nine claimed to be the continuation: the house of Klungkung,
which kept the Dewa Agung title. The other eight argued their standing by
descent from the same line without claiming its precedence — which is a more
stable arrangement than it sounds, because it gave every ruling house a share
in the same ancestry and only one of them a claim on the top of it.

Java went the other way in the same century. When Mataram was divided at
Giyanti in 1755 it produced two courts, both of which claimed to be the
continuation, and both of which still exist.

## Hindu rulers over Muslim subjects

The Balinese kingdoms projected across the strait to Lombok from the
seventeenth century, and from Karangasem they installed Balinese Hindu rule
over a Sasak population that was Muslim.

That is worth sitting with, because it runs against the whole current of the
period. Islam had been moving outward through these islands since a throne on
the Sumatran coast took it in the thirteenth century — to Melaka, to Java's
north coast, to Borneo, Sulawesi, Maluku and the southern Philippines, port by
port, for three hundred years.

Bali is where the movement met something going the other way. A Hindu kingdom
that had been made by refugees from an Islamised Java crossed a strait and put
Hindu rulers over Muslim subjects, and held that arrangement into the
nineteenth century. It is the one place in the archipelago where the tide ran
backwards.
