Gelgel collapsed into nine kingdoms in 1686, and what happened to those
kingdoms two centuries later belongs here, because the line that ended in 1908
was the one this kingdom had carried.

The Dutch took northern Bali in the 1840s and the rest of the island between
1906 and 1908. What they met at Badung in 1906, at Tabanan the same year, and
at Klungkung in 1908 was *puputan*: the court, the household and the retainers
walking out of the palace in white, armed, into rifle fire, until there was
nobody left.
At Klungkung the Dewa Agung — holder of the paramount title in
unbroken descent from the Gelgel line — died that way with his court.

<Figure id="puputan-square" />

Dutch officers wrote accounts of it. The scale is disputed and the fact is not.

## The line that ended there

The Dewa Agung's title had been continuous since Majapahit installed a
governing line on Bali in 1343. Five hundred and sixty-five years, from a
Javanese appointment on a conquered island to a man in white walking into rifle
fire in front of his own palace.

## And what did not end

Almost everything else.

The subak still allocate water, by the same associations, on the same
principles. The *padmasana* still stands in the corner of every temple. The
wangsa distinctions are still socially live. The priesthood, the caste order
and the irrigation associations that the Javanese arrivals reorganised in the
sixteenth century outlasted the dynasty that reorganised them, and then
outlasted the empire that destroyed the dynasty.

Bali is the one place in the archipelago where the Hindu-Buddhist world of
Medang, Kediri, Singhasari and Majapahit did not end but changed hands. When
Majapahit fell, its literature and its religion moved east across the strait.
This is the far end of that sentence: what moved outlived every polity on both
sides of the water, including the one it moved to, twice.
