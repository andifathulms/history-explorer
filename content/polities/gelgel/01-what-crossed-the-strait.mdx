When Majapahit ended on Java, its world did not end. It moved two miles east
across a strait.

What crossed was not a court in flight so much as a tradition with people
attached: priests, aristocrats, the Old Javanese literature, the ritual, the
caste order, and the genealogies that ran the Balinese ruling houses back to
Majapahit's own. Bali became — and has remained — the place holding the
essentials of an Indo-Javanese religion and literature that Islam caused to
disappear rapidly on Java itself.

## Two heirs, wanting different things

Majapahit had two claimants and they wanted opposite inheritances.

Demak, the Muslim sultanate on the north coast, claimed it by descent — Raden
Patah's line traced to Majapahit's own royal house — and used the claim to
justify ruling Java after absorbing what was left of that court. A political
claim, made by the polity that ended it.

Gelgel claimed everything except the politics. The Balinese kingdoms never held
an acre of Java and never said they should; what they asserted was continuity
of the priesthood, the texts, the ritual and the ranks. One heir took the
throne and changed the religion. The other took the religion and never wanted
the throne.

## The same year, from three directions

1527 is the same event on three different islands and it looks like three
different things.

From Java's inland court it is the moment the state stopped. From the north
coast it is a conversion completed. From Bali it is the year the priests
arrived — and the beginning of this kingdom's golden age under Dalem
Waturenggong, which is the next chapter.

Nothing was destroyed on Bali in 1527. Something was received.
