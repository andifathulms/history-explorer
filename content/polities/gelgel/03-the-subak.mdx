Gelgel lived on a tax on rice land, like most agrarian kingdoms. What made Bali
different is what sat between the state and the land.

Balinese wet rice is watered by *subak*: associations of the farmers who share
a single irrigation channel, which allocate water in rotation, maintain the
works, settle disputes among their own members, and are organised around a
water temple whose ritual calendar is the scheduling mechanism. A subak is not
a village and does not map onto one — membership follows the water, not the
settlement.

They are older than this kingdom. Inscriptions referring to irrigation
associations on Bali run back to the ninth century, three and a half centuries
before Majapahit installed the line this kingdom descends from. They outlasted
it too: subak still allocate water on Bali, and the landscape they produced is
on the UNESCO list.

## A third party

A land tax assessed on Balinese rice land is assessed on land whose
productivity is set by an institution the state did not create, does not run,
and cannot easily override — because the water schedule is a ritual calendar
and the priest who keeps it is not a royal official.

That is a genuinely unusual arrangement. Most agrarian states of this period
sit directly on the cultivator: they assess, they collect, and where they want
more they push. A Balinese king pushing on the water would have been arguing
with a temple about a festival date, against farmers who had been settling that
question among themselves since the ninth century.

It is worth comparing with what the same phrase covers elsewhere. On Java, a
kingdom's land revenue ran through *sima* grants — land signed away permanently
to a religious foundation, recorded on a stone at the site. In Burma the
equivalent endowments ran so far that Aung-Thwin argues they eventually
bankrupted Pagan: land kept leaving the tax base and never came back.

Three kingdoms living on rice land, and underneath the same three words are an
endowment that alienates land forever, a monastic economy that swallowed a
state, and a farmers' association older than the throne.
