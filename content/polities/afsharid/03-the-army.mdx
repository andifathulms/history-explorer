title: The thing nobody could afford
drafted_from: axworthy-2006

This corpus has now told the same story four times. At Otlukbeli in 1473 the Ak
Koyunlu lost to Ottoman artillery and drilled infantry with firearms. At
Chaldiran in 1514 the Safavids lost to the same combination. At Marj Dabiq and
Ridaniya in 1516 and 1517 the Mamluks did. Each losing side understood precisely
what had beaten it, and each of the pages says the same thing about why it could
not answer: what the Ottomans had was not guns, which anyone could buy, but a
standing paid infantry that could be drilled to use them, and none of these
polities had a revenue system that could support one.

Nadir built one anyway.

His army was recruited across every people he ruled and several he did not —
Persians, Afghans, Turkmen, Uzbeks, Georgians, Armenians, Bakhtiari — paid in
cash, kept permanently under arms, drilled, and equipped with muskets and light
artillery he could move fast. He used it the way the Ottomans used theirs:
firepower in a prepared position, cavalry to exploit rather than to decide. At
Karnal in 1739 it destroyed a Mughal army several times its size in about three
hours. It beat the Ottomans repeatedly in country they had held for a century.
At its height it was probably the most effective army in the world outside
Europe, and possibly not excluding Europe.

This site codes it `standing-professional`, and the coding is the point: it is
the only polity in the Iranian and Turkic half of this corpus that carries that
value. The Ottomans have it. Nobody between the Bosphorus and India otherwise
does.

And the reason nobody else does is the reason it did not last. Iran in the 1730s
had a small tax base, a population that had been through a decade of invasion
and famine, and no fiscal apparatus of the kind that fed the janissaries. Nadir
paid for the army with the Indian treasure, and when that was gone he paid for
it by taxing a ruined country at rates that produced revolts he then had to use
the army to suppress. The instrument consumed the state that held it inside a
decade.

The conclusion the four battles pointed at was correct after all. The polities
that lost at Otlukbeli and Chaldiran could not afford a standing professional
army — and the one that built it anyway demonstrated why in the most direct way
available.
