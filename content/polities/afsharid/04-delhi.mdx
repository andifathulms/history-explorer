Nadir took Kandahar in 1738 after a year's siege, and then kept going. The
pretext was that the Mughals had failed to close their frontier to Afghan
fugitives; the reason was that the Mughal empire was visibly weak and
extraordinarily rich, and his army needed paying.

At Karnal, north of Delhi, on 24 February 1739, he met a Mughal army that
outnumbered his by a large margin — the figures given are unreliable in both
directions — and destroyed it in a few hours, using entrenched infantry with
firearms and light artillery against massed cavalry and elephants. Muhammad Shah
surrendered. Nadir entered Delhi with him.

Then a rumour spread that Nadir was dead, and Delhi rose and killed several
hundred of his soldiers. He ordered a general massacre; it lasted a day, and the
reported death toll runs to tens of thousands. He then negotiated, took what he
had come for, restored Muhammad Shah to a throne he no longer effectively held,
and went home after fifty-seven days.

What he took is the largest single transfer of movable wealth in the period. The
imperial treasury, the jewels, the Peacock Throne, the stone that became the
Koh-i-Noor, and enough besides that he remitted three years of taxation across
Iran on the strength of it. This site's Mughal page dates the empire's decline
from a longer process, and Karnal is not the whole of it; but the Mughals were
never again a power in northern India, and the provinces that went their own way
afterwards did so in the space Nadir made.

He came back and campaigned for another eight years — Bukhara and Khiva in 1740,
Daghestan, the Ottomans again, a fleet built in the Persian Gulf to take Oman —
and did not conquer anything else that stayed conquered. The Indian expedition
was the peak in the exact sense: after it, everything he did was paid for out of
it, and then out of Iran.
