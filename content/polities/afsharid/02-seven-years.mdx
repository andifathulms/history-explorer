title: Seven years to make a country

Between 1729 and 1736 Nader retook Iran from three foreign armies and one
domestic dynasty, and his own court historian wrote the campaigns down while
they were happening. Mirza Mahdi Khan is a court historian and reads like one:
the victories are providential and the shah is never at fault. But he was there,
and what he is good at is the mechanics — how a beaten army is put back on its
feet, what a review is for, which general would not come out of his trenches.

The Afghans went first. Nader pursued Ashraf across Iran, and in one of his
sweeps found an Afghan force holding a narrow pass between two mountains with
their artillery sited on it, the kind of ground on which, Mirza Mahdi observes,
a small number of resolute men could have stopped a whole army in the days
before firearms. The Persians went in hard enough that the Afghans left the
position and ran. Ashraf, who had grown cautious since his first defeat, pulled
back to Isfahan and massacred most of the inhabitants in cold blood — among
them, the historian is careful to say, a number of learned men who had withdrawn
from the world and hoped to end their days quietly.

He came out again in November on the plain of Murchakhur. Nader arrived in the
late evening and would not wait: he sent a body of Kurds against the Afghan
advance guard that night, and they killed some four hundred and came back with
prisoners, who reported that an Ottoman general, the governor of Hamadan, had
joined Ashraf with an army. That was unwelcome news, and it defines the next six
years. Getting the Afghans out of Iran was the easy half. The hard half was that
the Ottomans held the western provinces and the Russians held the Caspian ones,
and both of them had taken their shares while Iran was prostrate and had no
intention of giving them back.

The Ottoman war produced the one clear defeat of Nader's career, and it is the
most instructive thing in the book. In 1733 he was besieging Baghdad when Topal
Osman came down to relieve it. Nader broke off, and when his line began to give
way he sounded the retreat and sent orders to the twelve thousand men left in
front of Baghdad to raise the siege and follow. The troops on the east bank of
the Tigris got away; those on the west found that the defenders had demolished
the bridge and burnt the boats, and had to go round through forests and over
mountains, guided by wandering Arabs who were, says Mirza Mahdi, always glad to
show their detestation of the Turkish nation. Two thousand Persians were killed
and more than twice as many Turks, and Osman entered Baghdad in triumph and was
received by the inhabitants as a guardian angel.

What Nader did about it is the reason he won. He summoned the governors of every
province to bring their best troops to a plain near Hamadan, arrived there on
2 August, reviewed the army, and — Mirza Mahdi's phrasing is exact — as if his
late defeat had been a signal victory, distributed a large sum among his
soldiers, giving each man double the value of the arms or the horse he had lost
at Baghdad. Seven weeks later he was back in the field with the provincial
contingents added, marched on Kermanshah so fast that the Ottoman commanders
waiting to block him fled without striking a blow and left their ammunition, and
halted at a place called Elmidaran, where he reviewed the army again and
exhibited a mock battle with all the evolutions and manoeuvres of a real
engagement.

Topal Osman, meanwhile, had come out of Baghdad and entrenched himself near
Kirkuk, and was sufficiently elevated by his victory that he would not meet
Nader in person and sent a small detachment under a subordinate instead. It was
beaten and ran for Erzurum, and the reports of Nader's strength kept coming in,
and the Ottoman general began to be careful.

That is the whole method in one season. He lost a battle, paid his men more than
they had lost, drilled the army in front of itself until it believed the defeat
had been an accident, and was back on the same ground inside two months against
an opponent who had started to think about his own position. By 1735 the
Ottomans had been beaten out of the western provinces and the Russians had
handed back the Caspian ones by treaty rather than fight him for them.

He had restored the frontiers of Iran, and he was still, formally, the general of
a Safavid king.
