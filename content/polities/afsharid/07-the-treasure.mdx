Nadir was murdered by his own officers in June 1747, and what he left behind was
a quantity of loot, an army that immediately dispersed, and a reputation that
Europe found more interesting than Iran did.

## The plunder, and where it went

The Delhi treasure was the largest single transfer of movable wealth in the
eighteenth century, and almost none of it stayed where he put it.

The Afghan contingent rode east under Ahmad Khan Abdali with as much of it as
they could carry, and founded the Durrani state with it — including the
Koh-i-Noor, which went to Kandahar, and then in due course to Lahore, and then
to Punjab's British conquerors, and is in London. The Peacock Throne was broken
up. The rest was fought over by Nadir's own commanders within weeks.

He had remitted three years of taxation across Iran on the strength of what he
took at Delhi. By the time he died he was extracting revenue with a brutality
that had raised the provinces against him, and the treasure was in a fortress at
Kalat that his successors could not hold either.

## The witness

Jonas Hanway was an English merchant of the Russia Company who travelled from
London through Russia into Persia in the 1740s trying to open a Caspian trade,
lost his goods to a rebellion, and came home and wrote it up. His *Revolutions
of Persia* was published in 1753, six years after the murder, and it is the
fullest contemporary account in English.

He is not a historian and does not pretend to be. He is a merchant explaining to
other merchants why the Persian trade had failed, and the answer he gives is
that the country had been governed by a man whose whole system was conquest,
and that when the conquest stopped there was nothing underneath it.

That judgement is hard to improve on. Hanway had gone to Iran to sell British
woollens and found a state that had no revenue system worth the name, an army
that had to be paid every month in cash, and a ruler extracting the difference
by torture. He got out with his life and not much else.

## What Europe made of him

Nadir arrived in the European imagination as the last of the world-conquerors,
and the timing was everything: he sacked Delhi in 1739, when England and France
were beginning to think about India seriously, and the news that the Mughal
capital could be taken by twenty thousand men from Khurasan was read in London
and Paris as information rather than as a story.

Voltaire wrote about him. Hanway's book went through editions. He is the reason
educated Europeans of the mid-eighteenth century knew there was a Persia and
thought it was formidable — at exactly the moment it had ceased to be.

## What Iran made of him

Much less, and this is the asymmetry worth ending on.

Nadir left no dynasty that lasted, no capital that survived him as a capital, no
administration, no building anybody visits, and no religious settlement — his
attempt to have Twelver Shi'ism recognised as a fifth Sunni school died with
him and was not mourned. His grandson held Mashhad and Khurasan as a rump for
another half-century, blinded, ruling one province of a country his grandfather
had ruled the whole of.

He restored Iran's borders, expelled two invading powers, took Delhi, and is
remembered in Iran chiefly as the man who did those things and then wrecked the
country paying for them. The Timurids left a court style three empires worked
from. Nadir left a hole where a government had been, and the Koh-i-Noor in
somebody else's crown.
