In January 1736 Nadir summoned an assembly to the Mughan plain in Azerbaijan:
tribal leaders, provincial officials, and the senior Shi'i clergy. He had them
elect him shah. It is one of the very few entries in this corpus coded
`election` in the legitimation field, and it is the only one where the candidate
assembled the electorate himself, arranged the outcome, and had a dissenting
cleric strangled during the proceedings.

The conditions he attached are the substantial part. Nadir made his acceptance
conditional on Iran abandoning the public cursing of the first three caliphs —
the practice the Safavids had introduced in 1501 and made the mark of the state
— and on Twelver Shi'ism being reconstituted as a fifth legal school, the
Ja'fari madhhab, taking its place beside the four Sunni schools with a pillar of
its own at the Ka'ba. He pressed the proposal on the Ottomans for eleven years,
in successive treaties and embassies, and never got it.

This site's Safavid record calls the decree of 1501 the one entry in the corpus
where a state set out to change the confession of its population. What Nadir
attempted at Mughan is the only attempt anywhere in the corpus to reverse a
conversion by negotiation.

His motives are argued about and Axworthy's reading is the practical one. The
confessional frontier was the reason for two centuries of war with the Ottomans;
a ruler who intended to fight in Anatolia, Iraq, India and Central Asia at once
needed the western war to be endable, and the sectarian claim was the obstacle.
He also had no personal stake in Safavid legitimacy — it was the thing he had
just displaced — and considerable interest in detaching the clergy from the
endowments that made them a power. He confiscated a great deal of religious
property at the same time.

It failed in every direction. The Ottomans would not concede the fifth school;
the Iranian clergy resented the confiscations and the doctrine; and the country
had been Shi'i for two hundred and thirty-five years and did not want to
negotiate about it. The settlement of 1501 outlasted the man who tried to undo
it by rather a lot.
