The three-year tax remission ran out, and the army did not. Nadir went on
campaigning — in Daghestan for years, inconclusively, in country that suited
mountaineers and not artillery — and the money had to come from somewhere. It
came from Iran, collected by force, at rates the country could not meet after
twenty years of war and invasion.

Axworthy's account of the last five years is one of the grimmest passages in
this corpus's sources, and its argument is structural before it is
psychological. A ruler with a salaried army and no revenue system to support it
has only one lever, and pulling it produces revolt, and revolt requires the army,
which requires more money. Provinces rose — Fars, Astarabad, Shirvan — and were
put down with towers of skulls, a deliberate citation of Timur that Nadir's
propagandists were happy to make.

The personal deterioration is documented and is not separable from the position.
In 1741 he was shot at in a forest in Mazandaran and became convinced his eldest
son Reza Quli had arranged it. In 1742 he had him blinded, and then, hearing what
he had done, had the witnesses who had accused him executed. He is reported to
have said afterwards that he had blinded himself.

By 1747 he was extracting money from his own officers and had begun to suspect
the Persian troops in the army as against the Afghan and Uzbek ones. In June, at
Fathabad in Khurasan, a group of his own commanders — who had good reason to
believe they were on a list — came into his tent at night and killed him.

He was about forty-nine, and he had ruled eleven years as shah. The empire
outlived him by roughly a fortnight.
