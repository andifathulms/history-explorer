Nadir Quli was born about 1698 into the Afshar, a Turkmen tribe in northern
Khurasan, in circumstances the sources agree were poor and the later court
histories worked hard to improve. He was carried off in a Uzbek raid as a boy
and escaped. He was a local commander of irregulars, then a bandit, then a
commander again, and in 1726 he attached himself and his followers to Tahmasp
II, the Safavid claimant with a title and no army.

Within three years he had beaten the Afghans at Damghan and Murche-Khort and put
Tahmasp into Isfahan. Within six he had taken most of the western provinces back
from the Ottomans and the Caspian coast back from the Russians. In 1732 he
deposed Tahmasp for making a peace he disapproved of, installed Tahmasp's infant
son as Abbas III, and ruled as regent. In 1736, with the infant conveniently
dead, he summoned an assembly and took the crown.

Axworthy's biography is a corrective to two traditions at once — the nationalist
one that made Nadir the restorer of Iran, and the older European one that made
him an oriental despot in the Tamburlaine mould — and its method is to take the
military history seriously. The interesting question about Nadir is not his
character, which was formidable and became terrible. It is how a man with no
lineage, no province and no legitimacy assembled in ten years the best army in
Asia, and what having one did to the country that paid for it.

The corpus has met the type before. Timur could not be khan and held his
following by victory; Nadir could not be Safavid and did the same thing four
centuries later in the same country. The difference is that Timur's army was a
tribal following and Nadir's was salaried.
