The royal burials at Meroë stop. The inscriptions stop. The town continues to be
occupied on a reduced scale and then is not. Nobody wrote down that the kingdom
had ended, and 350 is a convention covering a fourth century in which the
evidence simply runs out.

## The tidy story, and why it is not here

The standard account for a long time was that Aksum destroyed Meroë.

Ezana of Aksum — the king whose conversion to Christianity is dated from a change
of symbol on his coinage — left an inscription describing a campaign down the Nile against a people called the Noba,
in which he took towns, killed and captured, and set up a throne. It mentions
Kasu, which is Kush. It was read for most of the twentieth century as the
destruction of Meroë by an Ethiopian army.

It is now generally thought that Meroë had already collapsed and that Ezana came
down a river where somebody else had moved into the ruins. The Noba he describes
fighting are the people who had displaced the Kushites, not the Kushites; the
archaeological sequence at Meroë does not show a destruction of the right date;
and the campaign reads as a punitive expedition against a nuisance rather than
the felling of an empire.

So nobody destroyed Meroë, and the state that was credited with it arrived
afterwards.

## The shape of the mistake

It is worth naming the shape, because it is a common one and it has been made
about African kingdoms more than most.

Ghana is supposed to have been destroyed by an Almoravid conquest in 1076. That
rests on one late source, is unsupported by the contemporary material, and shows
no destruction layer in the ground. Majapahit is supposed to have fallen to Demak
and to Islam in 1478; in fact the capital was taken by a rival Hindu prince and
the dynasty went on for fifty more years.

In each case the ending of a kingdom nobody was writing about has been attributed
to whoever turned up next with an army, because a conquest is a story and a slow
loss of revenue is not. The difference here is that Ezana's inscription is
genuine and the campaign happened; what is wrong is only the identity of the
people he was fighting, who were the ones that had displaced the Kushites
already.

## What actually happened, as far as anybody can say

The Nile trade routes shifted. Aksum and the Red Sea took the traffic that had
been going up and down the river, and a kingdom whose income depended on being
the road between Egypt and inner Africa stopped being the road.

The Butana's environment may have degraded under long cultivation and
charcoal-burning for the iron. Groups from the west moved into the river valley.

The state stopped being able to pay for itself, and stopped.

## Three hundred years of nothing

Then, in the sixth century, there are Christian kingdoms on the same stretch of
river — Nobatia, Makuria and Alodia — with a Byzantine liturgy, an alphabet
adapted from Coptic, and three letters borrowed out of Meroitic.

What happened in between cannot be said. Three centuries on the same ground, with
wealthy burials and imported goods and no name, no king-list, no date and no
text. It is the largest single gap on the Nile and there is no prospect of
filling it.

The three borrowed letters are the only thing that crosses it.
