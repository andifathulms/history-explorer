Nobody can say for certain how a Kushite king was chosen, and the two candidate
answers are both strange.

## An oracle chose the king

The Kushite royal stelae describe the succession as a decision by Amun of Napata.
The candidates — the king's brothers, in the accounts that survive — are brought
before the god. The god's image, carried by priests, moves towards the one he
wants. The chosen man is acclaimed.

Whatever was actually happening in that room, that is what the state said was
happening, and it is not a version of any of the familiar mechanisms. It is not
a council conferring an office, and it is not a predecessor naming an heir. The
acclamation that follows is real and is described, but it comes after the
decision and does not make it.

The obvious suspicion — that the priests moved the god towards whoever had
already won the argument — is unprovable and probably beside the point. A
succession system is a way of making a transfer of power unarguable, and a god
who cannot be cross-examined does that job well.

## And the royal women

The throne passed between brothers far more often than from father to son. The
king's mother held a formal position of the first importance and appears in royal
inscriptions and reliefs beside her son. From about the second century BC there
are queens ruling in their own right — the *kandakes*, a Meroitic title for the
queen mother which Greek and Latin writers mistook for a personal name and passed
on to English as Candace.

They were noticed abroad. Strabo's account of the war with Rome has a one-eyed
queen in command. The Acts of the Apostles has an official of "Candace queen of
the Ethiopians".

What all this adds up to is argued. One reading is a matrilineal rule, with
eligibility running through the royal women — an arrangement well attested
elsewhere in Africa, from Ghana, where al-Bakri states the rule and gives the
reason, to Mali, where Ibn Battuta records it as a general principle of
inheritance and disapproves, to Makuria downriver, to Asante much later, where
the matriclan defined who could rule.

The other reading is a succession running among a royal kin-group of both sexes,
with the queen mother as its senior member. That is a different constitutional
arrangement, and it would produce exactly the same evidence.

The distinction matters more than it looks. Queen mothers with formal office,
ruling queens, and the matrilineal inheritance of property are all perfectly
compatible with a throne that descends in the male line. Prominence is not a
rule, and Kush is the standing example of how easily the first gets read as the
second.
