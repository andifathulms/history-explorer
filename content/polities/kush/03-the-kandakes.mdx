This record's `succession_rule` is null, and the data comment gives two reasons.
Either would empty the field on its own. Together they make this page the sixth
in the corpus to be null there for a reason that is about the vocabulary rather
than about the evidence.

## An oracle chose the king

The Kushite royal stelae describe the succession as a decision by Amun of
Napata. The candidates — the king's brothers, in the accounts we have — are
brought before the god; the god's image, carried by priests, moves towards the
one he wants; the chosen man is acclaimed.

Whatever was actually happening in that room, it is what the state said happened,
and this site's seven succession values cannot say it. `election` means a body
conferring the office. `nomination` means a predecessor designating a successor.
`acclamation` is the shouting afterwards, which the stelae also describe and
which is not the decision.

A god picking a king out of a line is none of the three, and coding rule 7 takes
the rule in force rather than the nearest familiar label.

## And the eligibility may be matrilineal

The second reason is the one that connects this page to four others.

The throne passed between brothers far more often than from father to son. The
king's mother held a formal position of the first importance and appears in royal
inscriptions and reliefs beside her son. From about the second century BC there
are ruling queens in their own right — the *kandakes*, a Meroitic title for the
queen mother that Greek and Latin writers took for a personal name and passed on
to English as Candace. Strabo's account of the war with Rome has a one-eyed
queen commanding; the Acts of the Apostles has an official of "Candace queen of
the Ethiopians".

If the line of eligibility ran through the royal women — and a good many
Meroitic scholars read it that way — then this is a fifth instance of the
arrangement this corpus's Makurian record calls a category it can describe and
cannot code.

## The count, and the honest hedge

The Makuria record's third chapter, written one pass before this one, set the
tally at four and said a fifth would make the argument on the merits that the
rulebook requires for a vocabulary change.

Ghana, where al-Bakri states the rule and gives the reason. Mali, where Ibn
Battuta records it as a general rule of inheritance and disapproves. Elam, on the
Ur III record's last chapter, passing from brother to brother and then to a
sister's son. Makuria, where it is attested in the Arabic accounts and in the
royal titulature.

This record does not make it five, and saying so is the point of this chapter.

Kushite succession is genuinely argued about. The brother-to-brother pattern is
clear. The prominence of the royal women is not in doubt. What is disputed is
whether that adds up to a rule of matrilineal descent or to a system in which
succession ran among a royal kin-group of both sexes with the queen mother as its
senior member — which is a different constitutional arrangement that would also
produce the evidence we have.

So this record counts as a possible fifth rather than a certain one, and the tally
stands at four confirmed with a fifth in doubt. That is a worse-looking number
than five and it is the one the evidence supports.

## What is at stake in it

Nothing, immediately. This site changed a closed vocabulary once, after nine
records had each recorded the same gap, and no change is being proposed on four
or on four-and-a-doubtful-fifth.

What the accumulation is doing instead is what the Median record's four kinds of
null were doing before it: making a shape visible. Six records in this corpus now
have `succession_rule: null` for reasons that are about the list rather than
about the sources — Ghana, Mali, Makuria, Elam by way of another page, this one
twice over, and the Serbian record whose two-hundred-article law code legislates
everything except the throne.

A vocabulary of seven values built out of European, Islamic and steppe practice
turns out to have nothing for an oracle, nothing for a sister's son, and nothing
for a code that does not say. That is worth knowing about the vocabulary.
