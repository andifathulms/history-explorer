Around 300 BC the royal burials move from the Napata region three hundred
kilometres upstream to Meroë, in the Butana between the Nile and the Atbara.

The reasons that are usually given are practical and probably right: better
rainfall, so agriculture that does not depend entirely on the flood; grazing;
iron ore and the wood to smelt it; and distance from Egypt, which by then was
Ptolemaic and had a standing army. This corpus carries the Ptolemies, and their
southern frontier is this record's northern one.

## The pyramids nobody counts

There are more pyramids in Sudan than in Egypt. Something over two hundred of
them, at Meroë and the earlier royal cemeteries, in three main groups on the
desert ridge above the town.

They are not the Egyptian kind. They are much smaller — twenty to thirty metres
at most against Khufu's hundred and forty-six — and much steeper, close to
seventy degrees where the Giza pyramids are fifty-two, with a chapel attached at
the east face and a shaft tomb beneath. This corpus's Egypt Old Kingdom record's
chapter on Giza is about a technology that peaked in a century and was abandoned.
This is that technology adopted a thousand five hundred years later by a
different people who made it their own shape and kept using it for six hundred
years after the Egyptians had stopped.

Forty of them were blown open in the 1830s by an Italian doctor named Giuseppe
Ferlini, looking for treasure. He found some, in one, and destroyed the tops of
the rest.

## Iron

Meroë has slag heaps. Large ones, still there, and an early excavator called the
site the Birmingham of Africa, which is the kind of phrase that gets repeated for
a century and then gets qualified.

The qualification is that nobody has demonstrated industrial-scale production for
export, and the quantity is consistent with a substantial local industry
supplying a kingdom rather than an economy built on iron. What is not in question
is that Meroë was working iron on a scale unusual for the period and the region,
and this record's `revenue_basis` carries `mining` for it and for the gold both.

## What the kingdom looked like from outside

Herodotus knew of it and got it mostly wrong. The Ptolemies traded with it and
hunted elephants on its frontier. Rome fought it once, in 23 BC, and this
record's fourth turning point is what came of that: a raid, a punitive
expedition, a sack of Napata, and then a negotiated peace on terms Strabo is
visibly uncomfortable reporting.

The Kushites had taken a bronze head from a statue of Augustus at the frontier,
and they buried it under the threshold steps of a temple at Meroë so that anybody
entering trod on the emperor's face. It stayed there until 1910. It is in the
British Museum now, in a case, with an explanation.

That is as close as this corpus gets to a joke made by one of its polities at
another's expense, and both of them are on the site.
