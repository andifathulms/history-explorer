The Kushite conquest of Egypt is known mainly from one object: a granite slab
about six feet high, found in the temple at Gebel Barkal in 1862, carrying the
longest historical inscription any Egyptian-language monument bears. Piye set it
up at Napata to record what he had done in the north, and Breasted, who
translated it, called it the most instructive surviving document on the internal
condition of Egypt in a period without a central power.

It is also, unusually for a royal inscription, a text about piety, protocol and
horses.

## The orders to the army

Piye sent his troops north and gave them instructions, which the stela records
before any fighting.

The military part is what a commander says: if the enemy's infantry and chariotry
are in another city, wait for his army and fight as he says; if his allies are
elsewhere, hurry to them and force battle on them first. *Yoke the war horses,
the best of thy stable; draw up the line of battle. Thou knowest that Amon is the
god who has sent us.*

Then the part nobody else writes down:

> When ye arrive at Thebes, before Karnak, ye shall enter into the water, ye
> shall bathe in the river, ye shall dress in fine linen, unstring the bow,
> loosen the arrow. Let not the chief boast as a mighty man; there is no strength
> to the mighty without him. He maketh the weak-armed into the strong-armed, so
> that multitudes flee from the feeble, and one alone taketh a thousand men.
> Sprinkle yourselves with the water of his altars, sniff the ground before him.

An army on campaign is ordered to stop at the great temple of the god, disarm,
wash, change into clean linen and lie down on the floor in front of him before
going on to fight. That is the Kushite claim in a paragraph: not that a foreign
power was taking Egypt, but that the proper servants of Amun were coming north to
put right a country that had stopped serving him correctly.

## The stables at Hermopolis

The most quoted passage in the inscription comes after the surrender of Nimlot,
prince of Hermopolis, who had broken his allegiance and stood a siege.

Piye took the city. He refused to look at the women sent to intercede with him.
Then:

> His majesty proceeded to the stable of the horses and the quarters of the
> foals. When he saw that they had suffered hunger, he said: "I swear, as Re
> loves me, and as my nostrils are rejuvenated with life, it is more grievous in
> my heart that my horses have suffered hunger, than any evil deed that thou hast
> done, in the prosecution of thy desire."

A king who has just reduced a rebel city announces on the record that starving
the horses during the siege was worse than the rebellion. Nothing else in Egyptian
royal literature sounds like it, and it is the reason Piye is the one Kushite
pharaoh with a personality attached.

## The four kings and the fish

The stela ends with an image of submission that is also a statement about who
these people were.

Everything had fallen; there was not a nome closed against him; the west, the
east and the islands in the midst were on their bellies in fear of him. In the
morning four rulers came to do homage, wearing their uraei — and the princes of
the north, coming to behold the beauty of his majesty, had legs *as the legs of
women*.

Then the last line of the reception:

> They entered not into the king's-house, because they were unclean and eaters of
> fish; which is an abomination for the palace. Lo, King Nimlot, he entered into
> the king's-house, because he was pure, and he ate not fish. There stood three
> upon their feet, but only one entered the king's-house.

Three kings of Egypt were left standing in the courtyard by a Nubian because
their diet made them ritually unfit to come in.

## What the document is doing

Piye then loaded his ships with silver, gold, copper, clothing and every product
of the north, sailed upstream, and went home. He did not stay in Egypt, did not
rule from it, and did not put a governor over it; the Delta princes kept their
titles.

That is what the inscription is for. It was cut at Napata, in Kush, for a Kushite
audience, and what it says to them is that their king had gone north, found the
country in the wrong hands and the temples neglected, corrected it with the
correct rituals and returned. The taking of Egypt is presented as an act of
observance, and the only thing in the whole document the king loses his temper
about is a stable full of hungry horses.
