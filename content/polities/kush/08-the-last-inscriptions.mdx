Meroë's decline has no event in it. What it has is a set of curves, all of them
going the same way from about the second century AD, and none of them
individually decisive.

The royal pyramids get smaller and cruder. The grave goods thin out. The
inscriptions get shorter and then stop. Building at the temple sites falls off.
The imported Mediterranean pottery that had been coming up the river through
Roman Egypt becomes scarcer and then rare. Settlement contracts.

Nobody in the kingdom explains any of it, and nobody outside it noticed enough to
write it down.

## What was probably happening

Three things, all of them slow, and the sources permit only informed guesses
about how they weighed against each other.

**The road moved.** Meroë's wealth came from being the passage between Egypt and
inner Africa, and in these centuries that traffic increasingly went by the Red
Sea instead — up the coast to the Egyptian ports, through Adulis and the Aksumite
highlands, on ships. A kingdom that is a road stops being wealthy when the
traffic finds a different road, and there is nothing it can do about it, because
the advantage was never anything the kingdom made.

**The ground got worse.** The Butana, the grassland between the Nile and the
Atbara that fed Meroë, had been cultivated and grazed for centuries and stripped
of wood to smelt iron. Environmental degradation of that kind is hard to
demonstrate and easy to assert, and the case here is circumstantial: a large
population on marginal rainfall, an industry that consumed timber, and a
settlement pattern that shrinks.

**People moved in.** Groups from the western deserts came into the river valley
in these centuries. Whether they arrived as raiders, as settlers or as both is
not recoverable. The Noba who appear in the fourth-century sources are on ground
the Kushites had held.

## The Roman episode, and what it showed

The one dramatic thing in the late period had happened earlier, and it is worth
reading as a measure of what the kingdom could still do.

In 23 BC a Kushite force raided into Roman Egypt. Rome sent a punitive
expedition, which went a long way up the river and sacked Napata. Then there was
a negotiated peace, and the terms — Strabo reports them with visible discomfort —
were favourable to Kush: the frontier was fixed, and the tribute Rome had
demanded was remitted.

An empire that had recently annexed Egypt fought a kingdom on its southern
frontier and settled on terms rather than continue. Three hundred years later,
nobody had to fight that kingdom at all.

## What contraction looks like without a chronicle

This is what most states' declines actually consist of, and it is almost never
what gets written about them.

There is no battle to date it to, no bad king to blame, no invasion, no
catastrophe. There is a stretch of river that made money for a thousand years
because of where it was, and then trade moved a few hundred miles east, and the
kingdom got poorer every generation until it could not pay for the things that
had made it a kingdom.

Its own writing continues throughout, in a script that can be pronounced and not
understood, and it may well say precisely what was happening.
