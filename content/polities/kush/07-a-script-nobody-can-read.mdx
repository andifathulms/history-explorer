Around 200 BC the Kushites stopped writing in Egyptian and started writing in
their own language, in a script they made for it.

Meroitic has twenty-three signs. There are two forms — a hieroglyphic one for
monuments, using Egyptian sign-shapes, and a cursive one derived from demotic for
everything else — and they map one to one. It is alphabetic with syllabic
features: consonant signs carry an inherent vowel, four vowel signs modify, and a
word-divider of two or three dots separates units.

Francis Llewellyn Griffith worked out the sound values in 1909, from a bilingual
inscription and from Egyptian transcriptions of Meroitic names. So the signs can
be read aloud.

Nobody knows what the words mean.

## A hundred and sixteen years

Meroitic is not a mystery like Linear A, where the script itself is unread. The
phonetics have been settled for over a century. The problem is the language: it
has no established relatives.

Egyptian is Afroasiatic and has cousins. Sumerian is an isolate too, and is read
easily, because the Akkadians who took over Mesopotamia went on learning it in
school for two thousand years and left dictionaries. Elamite is an isolate and is
readable because the Achaemenids published the same text in three languages on a
cliff at Behistun.

Meroitic has no long bilingual and no successor state that kept it as a learned
language. What survives is a few thousand short texts, mostly funerary, mostly
formulaic. Perhaps a couple of hundred words are understood — enough to tell that
a stele records somebody's mother and father and the offerings they should
receive, and not enough to read a sentence with confidence.

Proposals connecting it to Nilo-Saharan languages have been made and are not
settled.

## What that costs

Almost everything anybody would want to know.

What can be said about how Meroë chose its kings — that an oracle was involved,
that eligibility may have run through the mother — comes from Egyptian-language
stelae of the earlier Napatan period and from Greek and Latin outsiders looking
in. For the six hundred years after the script changes, the kingdom is writing
continuously about itself, and none of it can be used.

That is a rarer situation than it sounds. Most lost civilisations are lost
because the writing did not survive, or was never made, or has not been found.
This one is on museum walls in Khartoum, Berlin and London: legible, transcribed,
pronounceable, and meaningless.

## And what it left

Three letters.

When the Christian kingdom downstream needed to write Old Nubian, six hundred
years later, it took the Coptic alphabet and added three signs out of Meroitic
for sounds Coptic had no letters for. Those three characters are the only part of
this kingdom's writing system whose value anybody can currently be sure of,
because they are used in a language that can be read.

Egyptian hieroglyphs to Meroitic to Old Nubian, on one river, across two and a
half thousand years — and the middle one is the only one nobody can read.
