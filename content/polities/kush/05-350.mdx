The royal burials at Meroë stop. The inscriptions stop. The town continues to be
occupied on a reduced scale and then is not. Nobody wrote down that the kingdom
had ended, and the date on this record's span is a convention covering a fourth
century in which the evidence runs out.

## The tidy story, and why it is not here

The standard account for a long time was that Aksum destroyed Meroë.

Ezana of Aksum — whose reign is this corpus's Aksum record's third chapter, and
whose conversion is dated from the change of symbol on his coinage — left an
inscription describing a campaign down the Nile against a people called the Noba,
in which he took towns, killed and captured, and set up a throne. It mentions
Kasu, which is Kush. It was read for most of the twentieth century as the
destruction of Meroë by an Ethiopian army.

It is now generally thought that Meroë had already collapsed and that Ezana came
down a river where somebody else had moved into the ruins. The Noba he describes
fighting are the people who had displaced the Kushites, not the Kushites; the
archaeological sequence at Meroë does not show a destruction of the right date;
and the campaign reads as a punitive expedition against a nuisance rather than
the felling of an empire.

So this record's `ended.by` is empty and there is no edge from this page to the
Aksum one, and the two records sit in the same region with nothing drawn between
them.

## The second time this corpus has said that

The Ghana record's fourth chapter is the same shape and is worth reading beside
this one.

There, the standard account was that the Almoravids conquered Ghana in 1076 and
broke it, and it rests on one late source, is not supported by the contemporary
material, and shows no destruction layer in the ground. That record carries the
non-event as a turning point marked contested, on the reasoning that a story
which shaped the field for a century leaves a reader worse off if it is silently
omitted.

This record does not carry Ezana as a turning point, because the difference
matters. The Almoravid conquest was an assertion about Ghana that the field made
and has withdrawn. The Aksumite destruction of Meroë is an assertion about a
document that says something else — Ezana's inscription is real, the campaign
happened, and what is wrong is the reading of who he fought.

Both are tidy causal stories about the end of an African kingdom, told by
attributing it to whoever arrived next with an army. That both have been
dismantled, on two different rivers, in the same corpus, is worth noticing.

## What actually happened, as far as anybody can say

The Nile trade routes shifted. Aksum and the Red Sea took the traffic that had
gone up the river — this corpus's Aksum record's first chapter is about exactly
that route and this record's `revenue_basis` carries `trade-toll` for the one it
displaced. The Butana's environment may have degraded under long cultivation and
charcoal-burning for the iron. Groups from the west moved into the river valley.

The state stopped being able to pay for itself and stopped.

Three hundred years later there are Christian kingdoms on the same stretch of
river, and this corpus carries the middle one of the three. The Makuria record's
first chapter names this kingdom as one of the absences on its own river, and it
is not an absence now.

What that record cannot do, and this one cannot either, is say what happened in
between. The gap between this record's ending and Makuria's beginning is three
centuries on the same ground, and both pages describe it as a silence rather than
filling it.
