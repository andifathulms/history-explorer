Egypt's name for the country upstream of the first cataract was Ta-Seti, the land
of the bow. Egypt's word for gold was *nub*, and Nubia is named from it.

Those two words are most of the relationship. For three thousand years before
this record begins, Egypt went south for gold and came back with archers, and
periodically tried to hold the country directly — this corpus's Egypt New Kingdom
record has Nubia governed under a viceroy as far as the fourth cataract, because
Nubia had gold, and its collapse chapter has Nubia slipping away.

When the New Kingdom's grip failed, what grew in the space was a Nubian kingdom
that in about 727 BC came down the river and took Egypt.

## Piye

The campaign is on a granite stele from Gebel Barkal, and it is one of the
longest and most detailed royal narratives from anywhere in the ancient Nile
valley — this corpus's Egyptian records have nothing of the kind at that length.

Piye came north, reduced the delta princes one at a time, and received their
submission. The text is unusually interested in two things. It is pious to the
point of piety being the argument: Piye stops to make offerings, insists on
ritual correctness, and presents the campaign as restoring proper worship that
the delta had let lapse. And it is furious about horses. At Hermopolis, having
taken the city, he goes to inspect the stables, finds the animals have been left
hungry during the siege, and tells the defeated prince that starving his horses
is worse than anything else he has done.

A Nubian dynasty then ruled Egypt for about sixty years, as its twenty-fifth.

## The only time the river ran the other way

That is the fact worth stating plainly, because the general histories mostly
mention it and move on.

Conquest on the Nile ran north to south for three thousand years, and once — for
sixty of them — it ran south to north. Taharqa, this record's peak ruler, was
building at Karnak and campaigning in the Levant, and he appears in the Hebrew
Bible as Tirhakah king of Cush, coming out to fight the Assyrians.

The dynasty's programme in Egypt was archaism. They restored temples, revived
Middle Kingdom artistic conventions and Old Kingdom texts deliberately, and took
the full five-name pharaonic titulature. This record's `legitimation` carries
`titulature` for it, and this corpus's Egypt New Kingdom record now counts a
successor claim it did not carry before — the only one on that page.

Rule 1 asks for a later polity asserting an earlier one's legitimacy in its own
public self-description. A Nubian king restoring Egyptian temples and calling
himself the Horus of Gold is doing exactly that.

## And the Assyrians

Esarhaddon took Memphis in 671 and Ashurbanipal sacked Thebes in 663, and the
twenty-fifth dynasty withdrew to Nubia and did not come back.

This corpus's Neo-Assyrian record carries Esarhaddon taking Egypt in 671 as the
first time any Mesopotamian power had done it. It does not say who was holding
Egypt at the time. This record is the answer, and the two pages between them
describe one war from opposite ends of it.

What happened next is the more interesting half. A dynasty evicted from the
richest country in the ancient world went home, kept the titles, moved its
capital further from the frontier, stopped writing in Egyptian, and lasted
another thousand years.
