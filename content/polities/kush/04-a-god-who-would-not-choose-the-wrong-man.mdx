After the Assyrians pushed Kush's kings out of Egypt for good, the
dynasty ruled on at Napata for centuries with no Egyptian scribe left
to record it. What survives from this stretch comes from the kings' own
stelae, cut at Gebel Barkal in their own voice rather than a
conqueror's - and one of them describes, in more procedural detail than
almost any other document from ancient Kush, exactly how a king here
was chosen.

## An election the god had to be talked into

Aspelta's own coronation stela, raised at Gebel Barkal early in his
reign, sets out a ceremony that reads less like a hereditary succession
than a formal selection process. Twenty-four senior officials of the
kingdom - army captains, seal-bearing priests, palace chancellors,
learned scribes, six of each - gathered at the temple of Amen-Ra after
the previous king's death, uncertain who among the "royal brethren"
eligible for the throne the god would actually choose. They processed
the candidates before the god's statue once, and the god rejected them
all. Only when Aspelta himself was brought forward a second time did
the god indicate him as king, at which the assembled officials
prostrated themselves and acknowledged him as ruler. The stela traces
his own legitimacy not through his father's line but through his
mother, Queen Enenselsa, and names her female ancestors back five
further generations to a woman who had herself been a queen of Kash -
a genealogy carried through royal women across six generations, cut in
stone for anyone who doubted the new king's claim.

## A king who did not skip the questions the throne asked of him

What the stela records after the election matters as much as the
ceremony itself. Aspelta is shown going before the god a second time to
ask directly for the strength and guidance to rule, and only after
Amen-Ra had promised him "life, stability, power, health, gladness, and
long life" did the new king put on the crown and take up the royal
sceptre in front of his assembled subjects. He marked the occasion with
festivals founded to be kept every year afterward, and gifts to the
god's temple substantial enough that the text specifies the quantity of
beer alone at a hundred and forty barrels. None of this reads as
theater performed for an already-settled outcome. It reads as a
kingship that still needed, in its own official record, to explain why
the god had chosen this candidate and not another.

## A story about the kingdom nobody could conquer

The same centuries that give almost no continuous royal narrative
produced one story too good for classical writers to leave alone.
Herodotus records that around 525 BC the Persian king Cambyses, having
just taken Egypt, sent envoys south with gifts to feel out the Nubian
king before invading. The king they met, in Herodotus's telling, saw
through the gesture immediately, called the envoys spies, and handed
them an unstrung bow with the message that the Persians might attack
once they could draw it. Cambyses marched south anyway, without
adequate supply, and lost his army to hunger and desert sand before it
came anywhere near the Nubian capital - by Herodotus's own account, an
expedition so disastrous that soldiers began eating each other before
what remained of it staggered back to Thebes. Whatever the story's own
factual core, it captures something the stelae confirm independently:
a kingdom the era's greatest empire could not even successfully invade,
let alone conquer, in the centuries between Kush's expulsion from Egypt
and its own move to Meroë.
