In 1814 John Lewis Burckhardt, travelling up the Nile as a poor Syrian trader,
was collecting the names of the villages in the country of the Sheygya from
people who had been there. He wrote them down in a list — Gos, Hannek el Zebeyr,
Koreyr, Koray, Abramnar, Wosta, Tongazy, Koro, Kadjeba — and then, against one of
them, added a note:

> Merawe, a singular coincidence in sound with the ancient Meroe.

He was right that there was a coincidence and wrong about which place it pointed
to; the Merowe he was told about sits by Gebel Barkal, at the other royal centre.
But the observation is the thing. In 1814 a well-read European on that river knew
the name Meroë from Herodotus and Strabo, heard something like it in the mouths of
people living there, noted the resemblance in half a line, and went on. He had no
means of pursuing it and no expectation that anything would come of it.

## What he found instead

The Sheygya, the people holding that stretch of the river, were in Burckhardt's
account the most powerful state north of Sennar and a genuinely formidable one.

They fought entirely on horseback, in coats of mail bought from the merchants of
Suakin and Sennar, on Dongola stallions, carrying four or five lances in the left
hand when charging and throwing them a great distance with much dexterity. He
compares their horsemanship to the Mamluks'. They trained their horses to make
violent springs with the hind legs at the gallop, and rode, like the Abyssinians,
with only the great toe in the stirrup.

They also, he notes, made books. He had seen manuscripts copied at Merawe in as
fine a hand as any.

Two thousand five hundred years after the kings of Kush went north out of that
country with an army of archers, the people living on the same ground were the
best cavalry between Egypt and Ethiopia, fighting in mail with lances, and
copying books.

## What was done to the pyramids

Twenty years after Burckhardt, an Italian doctor named Giuseppe Ferlini went to
Meroë looking for treasure and blew the tops off about forty of the pyramids.

He found gold in one — a hoard of a queen's jewellery, which he took to Europe —
and demolished the rest for nothing.

That is a considerable part of why the site looks as it does. More than two
hundred pyramids stand on the ridges above the river, in three groups, and a
great many of them are missing their upper courses because of one man in the
1830s, working with no record kept and nothing to compare against afterwards.

## Sound without sense

The script was cracked in 1909. Griffith worked out the values of the signs, and
Meroitic became a language that anybody with the alphabet can read aloud.

Nobody can understand it. A hundred and some years later, a couple of hundred
words are known — enough to see that a stele names somebody's mother and father
and lists the offerings owed — and the language has no demonstrated relatives, no
long bilingual, and no successor who kept it as a learned tongue.

So the kingdom that ruled Egypt, fought Assyria and Rome, buried two hundred
kings under pyramids and wrote continuously about itself for six hundred years is
recoverable in outline from its neighbours' documents, and its own voice is a
sound with no meaning attached.
