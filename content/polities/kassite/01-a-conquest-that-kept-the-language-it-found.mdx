The Hittite raid that ended Hammurabi's dynasty in 1595 did not
install a new government; Mursili I took Babylon's wealth and went
home. What actually filled the vacuum was a foreign people the source
identifies, on linguistic grounds, as probably Aryan by race and
closely related to the ruling caste that would later impose itself
on Mitanni. But the takeover was neither sudden nor complete. Kassite
chieftains had already been settling in the western districts of Elam
and drifting into Babylonia as harvest labor for generations before
any of them held a throne, and even after Gandash, the dynasty's
founder, took Babylon, holdout kings of the Sealand in the far south
kept up independent resistance for decades more.

## A family that ruled itself as much as it ruled Babylon

The early Kassite kings spent as much energy fighting each other as
consolidating the country. Gandash's son Agum reigned twenty-two
years before a rival Kassite, Kashtiliash, took the throne; Kashtiliash's
own sons then split the spoils of conquest between them, with one
brother ruling Babylon while another, Ulam-Buriash, conquered the
Sealand outright and reigned there as its own king. The pattern
repeated a generation later, when Kashtiliash's grandson Agum-kakrime
recovered the cult statues of Marduk and his consort from exile in a
foreign land and restored them to Babylon - a king remembered for
returning something the dynasty's own conquest had displaced, rather
than for conquering anything new himself.

## Horses, and a country renamed

What the Kassites actually changed about Babylonia was narrower than
their own dynasty's longevity might suggest. They introduced the
horse as a beast of burden throughout the region, replacing a
draught economy built on asses and cattle, and simplified the
country's unwieldy system of dating documents by an elaborate yearly
title in favor of counting years by a king's own reign. Otherwise
they governed in Akkadian, staffed their administration with
Babylonian scribes, and renamed the unified country Karduniash while
leaving the older names Sumer and Akkad to survive as geography and
royal titulature. It was a conquest that left the conquered culture
standing and simply took the throne at the top of it - which is
likely why no native chronicle bothered to record how it happened at
all.
