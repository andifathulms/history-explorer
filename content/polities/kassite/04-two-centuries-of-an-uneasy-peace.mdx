Babylon's other major relationship of the Amarna period, with the
rising power of Assyria to the north, tells a quieter story than the
Egyptian correspondence, through its own formalities rather than
through complaint. Kara-indash, a contemporary of Egypt's own Amenhotep
III, concluded a friendly agreement fixing the common boundary with the
Assyrian king Ashur-rim-nishishu; a generation later Burna-Buriash
himself found it necessary to negotiate a similar treaty with a later
Assyrian counterpart.

<Figure id="kara-indash-facade" /> That such agreements needed drawing up twice in
two generations is itself evidence of the friction beneath them.

## A peace that needed renewing because it kept slipping

Neither side was yet strong enough to dominate the other outright, but
the boundary the treaties fixed did not hold on its own. Intermittent
wars punctuated the two centuries that followed, and over time they
generally cost Babylon ground rather than gained her any - a slow
erosion along the northern frontier that no single defeat explains,
but that the accumulated record of the period shows clearly enough.
Assyria was not yet the power that would one day march a Kassite king
north in chains, but it was already the neighbor whose patience Babylon
could least afford to test twice, and by the century's end it would not
need to be tested at all.
