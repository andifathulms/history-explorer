The death stroke, when it finally came, came from Elam rather than
from the Assyrian frontier the dynasty had spent two centuries
watching. Shutruk-Nakhkhunte, the Elamite king, invaded Babylonia,
met the reigning Kassite ruler Zamama-shum-iddin in battle, and killed
him. It was the kind of aggression that had upended Babylonian
politics before, in earlier centuries, and would again - but this
time the kingdom that had ruled Babylon longer than any dynasty before
or since could not absorb it.

## A son who finished what his father started

Shutruk-Nakhkhunte did not campaign alone. His own son, Kutir-Nakhkhunte,
fought alongside him in the invasion and, together with his father,
sacked the temple city of Sippar and carried its treasures - among
them, cult objects with a long history of being fought over between
the two countries - home to Susa.

<Figure id="usurped-stele" /> The partnership mattered for what
came after: when the Kassite throne outlasted Zamama-shum-iddin's own
death by a few more years under an obscure successor, it was
Kutir-Nakhkhunte, ruling in his own right by then, who delivered the
second blow that actually ended the dynasty.

## What Sippar's sack actually settled

Sippar was not simply a wealthy target. It was one of Babylonia's
oldest religious centers, and its treasury held property that a
dynasty already famous for having once recovered Marduk's own statue
from foreign captivity could not afford to lose again without visible
consequence. The Kassite kings never got the chance to try: the family
that had governed Babylon since Gandash's own generation, secure
enough in its permanence that no source had bothered to record how it
began, spent its final years absorbing a defeat it would not survive.
