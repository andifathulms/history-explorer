Assyrian domination of Babylon did not end with Tukulti-Ninurta's own
death - it ended with a revolt of Kassite nobles against whichever
arrangement his successors had left in place, and the man that revolt
put on the throne, Adad-shum-usur, is the one king of the whole four
centuries who can be credited with a clear victory over Assyria rather
than a treaty, a stalemate, or a loss.

## The one clear win the rivalry produced

Adad-shum-usur met the Assyrian king in battle, defeated and killed
him, and pushed his own advantage as far as the enemy capital itself -
a reversal of everything the two centuries of boundary treaties and
slow border losses that preceded Kashtiliash's own captivity had
established as the normal shape of the relationship. It did not undo
what Tukulti-Ninurta had done; Kashtiliash IV was not restored, because
there was no restoring him. But it answered it, in the currency the
rivalry had always been conducted in.

## A recovery with limits

His descendants held the advantage for a time, and the boundary-stone
records of their reigns give a rare, detailed picture of how the
kingdom's internal affairs were run in its final generations - land
grants, tax exemptions, and the kind of routine administrative
business that only survives when a state is stable enough to bother
recording it. But the recovery did not last indefinitely. Assyria
reasserted itself under a later king, recovering the frontier territory
it had lost, and the old pattern of the two centuries before
Kashtiliash's own captivity reasserted itself along with it: an uneasy
border, drifting slowly in Assyria's favor, that no single Babylonian
victory would settle for good.
