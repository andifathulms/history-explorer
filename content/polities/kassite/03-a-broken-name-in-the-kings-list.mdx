The dynasty's later kings still found occasion to celebrate real
victories. Adad-shum-usur, who owed his own throne to a revolt of
Kassite nobles against a period of Assyrian domination, defeated and
killed the Assyrian king in battle and pushed his advantage as far as
the enemy capital itself - the one clear Babylonian victory over
Assyria the whole long rivalry produced. His descendants held that
advantage for a time, and the boundary-stone records of their reigns
give a rare, detailed picture of how the kingdom's internal affairs
were run in its final generations. But the recovery did not last.
Assyria reasserted itself under a later king, recovering the frontier
territory it had lost, and the Kassite dynasty did not long survive
the defeat.

## A blow from a direction nobody was watching

The death stroke, when it came, came from Elam rather than Assyria.
Shutruk-Nakhkhunte, the Elamite king, invaded Babylonia, defeated and
killed the reigning Kassite ruler, Zamama-shum-iddin, in battle, and,
with his own son Kutir-Nakhkhunte, sacked the temple city of Sippar
and carried its treasures home to Susa. It was the kind of aggression
that had upended Babylonian politics before, in earlier centuries and
would again, and this time the kingdom that had ruled Babylon longer
than any dynasty before or since could not absorb it.

## A dynasty too obscure at its own end to be named with confidence

The Kings' List itself preserves the name of the very last Kassite
ruler only as a broken word, and he reigned for no more than three
years before a further Elamite blow, a few years after
Zamama-shum-iddin's death, brought the dynasty to its actual close.
Whether that last name should be restored as one reading or another
is a dispute the source discusses without resolving. What is certain
is only the shape of the ending itself: a dynasty that had spent four
centuries as a fixture of the Babylonian throne, secure enough in its
own permanence that no one had thought to write down how it began,
went out just as unrecorded - reduced, in the one document meant to
preserve every king's name in order, to a name too damaged to read.
