The Toba were one of three wandering Xianbei tribes that had settled, by
historical times, in the country between Beijing and Datong and further
west again — a minor branch of a people whose more famous relatives, the
Murong and the Khitan among them, would go on to found dynasties of their
own in the centuries that followed. The Toba's own rise began quietly, as a
reward for loyalty rather than a conquest.

<Figure id="nw-belt" />

## A patent, then an empire

In 315 the Jin court, still nominally ruling all of China though already
losing its grip on the north, made Ilu — chief of the westernmost of the
three Toba tribes — Prince of Tai, the region around modern Datong, in
recognition of his services against the Xiongnu and a rival Xianbei people.
The grant came with a proper staff of officials, the ordinary apparatus of
a Chinese client state rather than a purely steppe arrangement. Three
generations later, Ilu's grandnephew Zhaobugen became the first of the
family to introduce a reign-period of his own — the formal mark of
sovereign rather than merely delegated authority — commanding as many as
five hundred thousand Tartars by that point. It was Zhaobugen's grandson,
Tuoba Gui, who took the final step: proclaimed in 386 as the first actual
emperor of what would be known as the Northern Wei.

## What the family already had

By the time Tuoba Gui took the imperial title, his family had spent seven
decades building exactly the kind of administration a Chinese patent
implied rather than the looser structure of an ordinary steppe confederacy
— officials, a fixed seat of government, a chain of inherited authority
running cleanly from Ilu through three more generations without the
succession disputes that broke so many of the era's other short-lived
northern states before they properly began. What the new dynasty inherited,
in other words, was not a conquest to defend but an institution already two
generations tested, ready to be pointed at the rest of a fractured north.
