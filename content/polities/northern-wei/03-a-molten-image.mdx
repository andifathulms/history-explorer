Wei's court kept a custom through much of the dynasty's history that Parker
calls singular and, so far as the record shows, invariable: the mother of
an heir-apparent was put to death as soon as her son was formally named
successor. It reads, on its face, as simple cruelty, and Parker offers a
more specific explanation without pretending it explains away the horror of
the practice itself — a survival, he suggests, of the older Xianbei custom
of tracing descent through the mother's line rather than the father's. Once
a prince's claim to the throne ran cleanly through his father's house
instead, a surviving empress dowager became a rival source of legitimacy
the new arrangement had no place for, and the dynasty removed the rival
rather than risk it.

## Casting the succession in bronze

A second custom governed who might be proposed as empress or heir-apparent
in the first place: a molten image of the candidate was cast, and the
success or failure of the casting read as an omen of whether the choice was
sound. Parker records the practice without elaborating on how often a poor
casting actually changed a succession already decided by more ordinary
politics — omens of this kind, in most courts that kept them, had a way of
confirming what powerful people had already resolved to do.

## What the customs said about the family that kept them

Neither practice survived the dynasty's later sinicisation intact, and
neither needed to: by the time Wei's rulers had traded their own language,
dress and clan name for Chinese equivalents, the older Xianbei logic that
had once made a mother's execution seem a political necessity had already
stopped applying to a court that no longer reckoned descent the way its
own founders once had.
