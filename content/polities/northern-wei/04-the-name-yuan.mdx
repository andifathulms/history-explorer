By the middle of the fifth century, Parker observes, the Toba were "rapidly
becoming Chinese in sentiment" — a shift that had been building for
generations in small ways and that the dynasty's later rulers began, from
the 470s onward, to enact deliberately and in law rather than leave to
custom.

## Marriage, measurement, and a new calendar

In 483 the court followed Chinese practice and formally forbade marriage
between people sharing the same clan name, a rule with no particular basis
in older Xianbei custom but a familiar one to any Chinese household. A new
calendar had already been introduced a couple of decades earlier with the
help of scholars from the old Liang principality Wei had absorbed - proof
that the conquered territory's learning was by then being drawn on rather
than merely governed. In 476 the ruling house set aside its own clan name,
Tuoba, for the Chinese surname Yuan - the same word, by no coincidence
Parker is happy to point out, that Kublai Khan would choose for his own
dynasty eight centuries later.

## The ban that finished it

The clearest single act of the whole sinicisation programme came in 495: an
imperial order prohibiting Tartar costume, the Tartar language, and the
weights, measures and standards the dynasty's founders had brought with them
from the steppe. The emperor by this point drafted his own decrees in
Chinese and ordered a search of the realm for ancient books. Clan names were
formally regularised for the whole Toba people - what English usage would
call a surname - and six of them were singled out as especially
aristocratic, in conscious imitation of the old Xiongnu clan hierarchies the
dynasty's own ancestors had once lived under.

## A court that patronised, and then needed, the faith it had once burned

Even religion, which Taiwu had persecuted root and branch a half-century
earlier, found its way back into official favour by degrees rather than by
any single reversal - some later Wei emperors patronising one faith, some
another, until in 518 an embassy was sent all the way to Gandhara
specifically to obtain Buddhist texts. A dynasty that had once burned sacred
books to prove its Confucian seriousness was, within two generations,
sending ambassadors to Central Asia to replace them.
