Taiwu came to the throne in the way a dynasty's ablest rulers rarely do: at
the end of a costly failure rather than a triumph. His father had invaded
the southern Song territories the moment its own founder died, sending his
best generals and finally coming in person to press the siege of a small
fortress called Houlao, held by an officer named Mao Deci with a resolution
that outlasted seven months of everything Wei's army could throw at it. When
Houlao finally surrendered, the victors found nothing but a pile of ruins to
show for the cost of taking it, and Taiwu's father died soon afterward, worn
down by the hardship of the campaign as much as by its disappointment — his
one lasting credit the magnanimity he showed Mao Deci once the ruined
fortress finally changed hands.

## Finishing what the north had left unfinished

Taiwu inherited a throne and an unfinished war, and turned his own reign
toward completing what three generations of steppe rivals had left
undecided. He absorbed the Liang principality in the west and the Murong
state in the east, closing out the last of the rival claimants that had
divided the north since the Jin collapse, and pushed Wei's own frontier
walls further south than any of his predecessors had managed.

## A ruler in two registers

He governed, in the meantime, like a man determined to be taken seriously
in both the traditions he had inherited. He encouraged the study of
Confucian texts among his officials and set a legal limit of six years on
how long any magistrate could hold the same office, a measure meant to keep
local government answerable rather than entrenched. At the same time he
turned against the Buddhist monasteries spreading through his territory with
a severity his Confucian sympathies did nothing to soften: their temples
pulled down, their sacred books burned, and those monks who could not escape
killed outright. The faith's excesses, his own edict declared, would not be
tolerated in Wei — the same errors, in his reading of history, that had
helped bring the Han dynasty itself to ruin.

## The reckoning he did not finish either

In 450, encouraged by recent successes against the nomad tribes on his own
northern border, Taiwu crossed into Song territory at the head of a hundred
thousand men, confident the moment had come to settle the account his
father had opened and left unpaid. He fared better than his father had, but
not decisively: detained for months before a place of no great importance
while the Song court gathered its own forces, he eventually withdrew,
baffled in his larger object but without having suffered any serious defeat
either. Two more engagements followed near the town of Chenching, both
costly to each side and neither conclusive. The war that had killed his
father settled nothing for his own generation either, and would still be
unfinished when the next one inherited it in turn.
