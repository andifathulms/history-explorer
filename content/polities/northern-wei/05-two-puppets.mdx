A century and a half of unbroken succession — fifteen emperors, Parker
counts, from Tuoba Gui's proclamation in 386 down through Taiwu's conquests
and the later court's wholesale adoption of Chinese law, language and dress
— ended not at the hands of a steppe rival or a southern rival dynasty, but
inside the family itself. What no external enemy had managed in a hundred
and fifty years, two of Wei's own generals accomplished within a single
year.

## A dynasty split rather than deposed

In 534 and 535 the Wei court divided into two rival camps, each backing a
different claimant and each controlled, in practice, by the general who
had raised him rather than by the prince himself. One faction held Chang'an
in the west, in the modern province of Chihli's neighbourhood, under a
Wei prince whose actual authority belonged to one of the Jumen commanders
serving him. The other held Luoyang and Yecheng in the east, in Henan, under
a different Wei prince controlled just as completely by the general
Gao Huan. Neither side deposed the Wei house outright. Both sides simply
kept a member of it on hand to rule through.

## What outlasted the family that founded it

The fiction did not last long in either half. Within a generation the sons
of each controlling general set themselves up as emperors in their own
right rather than continue governing through a Wei figurehead — one line
founding the Northern Qi in the east, the other the Northern Zhou in the
west — and the Wei name, having governed the north of China for a century
and a half and rebuilt itself twice over in the process, from steppe
confederacy to sinicised empire, disappeared from the throne it had held
since 386 without a single dramatic battle marking the moment it happened.
The dynasty that had persecuted Buddhism and then courted it, that had
banned its own founders' language in law, ended the same way it had spent
its last sixty years reshaping itself: by legal fiction rather than by
conquest, until the fiction itself stopped being worth maintaining.
