In February 1570 the Portuguese captain at Ternate invited Sultan Hairun into
the fort to talk, and had him stabbed as he was leaving.

Fifty-eight years of alliance ended in that room. Whatever the arrangement of
1512 had been — and it had been an alliance, entered into by Ternate, on
Ternate's initiative — nothing about it could be argued for afterwards.

Hairun's son Baabullah besieged the fort. It took five years. In July 1575 the
garrison surrendered and left the island, and Ternate under Baabullah went on
to extend its authority further than it ever had before or would again: across
Halmahera, the Sula islands, into eastern Sulawesi, out along the Papuan coast.

## How rare that is

Throwing a European power out by force and keeping your own capital afterwards
is not something that happened often anywhere.

The Ya'ariba took Muscat from the Portuguese in 1650 and spent the next forty
years raiding Portuguese Asia from it. Ethiopia destroyed an Italian army at
Adwa in 1896 and kept its independence. Ternate's siege is seventy-five years
earlier than the first of those, and by an enormous margin the smallest party
to any of them: an island of forty-odd square kilometres, against the empire
that had taken Melaka and Hormuz and Goa.

It is worth stating plainly what happened next, because the victory settled
less than it looks. Ternate won in 1575 and was a Dutch company's vassal by
1683. The two facts are not in tension. What arrived in the seventeenth century
was a different kind of thing from what Baabullah had expelled, and the last
chapter is about what the difference was.
