The Dutch East India Company wanted what the Portuguese had wanted and went
about it differently. Where the Portuguese had tried to hold a fort at the source
and buy, the company set out to control the supply itself — and the supply,
uniquely among the commodities of early modern trade, was small enough to
control absolutely.

The method was *extirpatie*. Clove trees growing anywhere other than the islands
the company chose were to be cut down and burnt, and the annual expeditions that
did the cutting were Ternatan: the sultan's own kora-kora, crewed by his own
dependencies, destroying the crop on islands that owed him tribute, under the
company's direction and on the company's schedule.

The treaty of 1683 is the date this record ends on, and `ended.type` is `gradual
absorption` rather than `conquest` because nothing was conquered. The sultan
signed, kept his title, kept his palace, and kept his throne for another two
hundred and thirty years as an official of somebody else's empire. What he
stopped having was a polity.

## `by` is empty

For the fourth time on these four pages. The Dutch East India Company is not in
this corpus and there is no obvious way for it to be one: it is not a polity, it
had no succession, and half of what this site measures does not apply to it. The
Melakan record has the same empty field for Portugal, the Acehnese for the
Netherlands.

The Acehnese page calls this a Dutch-shaped absence, after the Kilwa page's
Portuguese-shaped one. Ternate is where the shape is least like a state at all:
a polity whose entire revenue was one crop was made to destroy that crop
wherever it could not be watched, by a joint-stock company acting under a
charter, and the site's `ended` block can say that it happened in 1683 and that
nobody in this corpus did it.

The company burned the trees for a century and a half. The clove was out of
Maluku by the 1770s, carried to Mauritius by a French administrator with some
seedlings and from there to Zanzibar — where this corpus's Al Bu Sa'id record
picks it up, in a chapter about an Omani sultanate that moved its capital to
East Africa and built plantations on it.

So the monopoly Ternate held for centuries without needing to enforce, and that
the company enforced at the cost of a region, ends as a cross-reference between
two pages on this site that have nothing else in common. The value in this
record's `revenue_basis` was never really about a fiscal system. It was about a
tree that grew in one place, until it did not.
