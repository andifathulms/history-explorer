The Dutch East India Company wanted what the Portuguese had wanted and went
about it differently. Where the Portuguese had tried to hold a fort at the
source and buy, the company set out to control the supply itself — and the
supply, uniquely among the commodities of early modern trade, was small enough
to control absolutely.

The method was *extirpatie*. Clove trees growing anywhere other than the
islands the company chose were to be cut down and burnt, and the annual
expeditions that did the cutting were Ternatan: the sultan's own kora-kora,
crewed by his own dependencies, destroying the crop on islands that owed him
tribute, under the company's direction and on the company's schedule.

The treaty of 1683 is where the sultanate ends, and nothing was conquered. The
sultan signed, kept his title, kept his palace, and kept his throne for another
two hundred and thirty years as an official of somebody else's empire. What he
stopped having was a polity.

## What became of the clove

The company burned the trees for a century and a half, and then lost them
anyway.

The clove was out of Maluku by the 1770s, carried to Mauritius by a French
administrator with some seedlings, and from there to Zanzibar — where an Omani
sultanate that had moved its capital to East Africa built plantations on it and
grew rich in exactly the way Ternate once had.

A monopoly that Ternate had held for centuries without needing to enforce, and
that the company enforced at the cost of a whole region, was undone in the end
by a man with cuttings in a pot. It was never really a fiscal system. It was a
tree that grew in one place, until it did not.
