The company's vassalage did not end when the company did. The VOC itself
dissolved in 1799, bankrupt, and its rights in the clove islands passed
directly to the Dutch state, which kept running the same arrangement under
a different flag: a sultan who still held his palace and his title, and
whose own fleets were still the instrument used to destroy any clove tree
growing where the government had not licensed one.

## What 1910 actually took

The deeper claim on Ternate's own land came later, and it was more total
than tribute or tree-burning had ever been. A "short declaration" imposed on
the sultans of Ternate, Tidore and Bacan in 1910 stripped them of their
personal land rights outright - the aha kolano, the sultans' own customary
holdings across Halmahera and the surrounding islands, converted at a
stroke into land the colonial government administered directly. Communities
that had lived under a sultan's authority in the interior began moving to
the coast, into a system now run from Batavia rather than from Ternate's
own palace.

## A resistance that outlasted the sultan's own authority to grant it

Ternate's own submission did not mean Halmahera's did. Communities across
the island rose against the tightening monopoly repeatedly through the
turn of the century - at Jailolo in 1876, at Kao in 1904, at Loloda from
1906 to 1909, at Galela in 1906-07, and at Jailolo again in 1914 - fighting
a Dutch administration that had, by then, made the sultan himself as much a
functionary of the system as any of his own subjects.

## What came back after independence

The claim did not die with the colonial period either. After Suharto's
resignation in 1998 loosened decades of enforced quiet, demonstrations in
the town of Ternate demanded the return of every piece of land the
argument held had been unlawfully taken from the sultanate under Dutch
rule - the same land the 1910 declaration had converted from a sultan's
customary right into a colonial government's asset, now claimed back from
an Indonesian one. In 2006 the argument found a very literal piece of
ground to attach itself to: a dispute over four villages surrounding
Sultan Baabullah Airport, named for the sixteenth-century sultan whose
five-year siege had once driven the Portuguese off the island for good.
The runway sits on land the sultanate's own history says was never
properly given away in the first place.
