---
title: Ternate and Tidore
drafted_from: andaya-1993
phase: expansion
---

Tidore is visible from Ternate. The channel between them is about two
kilometres wide. They grew the same crop, spoke closely related languages, had
courts of the same form, and spent five hundred years in opposition to one
another.

Andaya's account of Maluku is built around that opposition, and argues that it
was not a rivalry in the ordinary sense of two states that happened to be
adjacent — it was the organising structure of the region. Maluku was understood
by the people in it as a set of paired and complementary halves with two centres,
and every island, every district, every dependency out along the Papuan coast
attached itself to one side or the other. A conflict between the two courts was
not a breakdown of the system. It was the system running.

This site has no field for that. There is no way in the schema to say that a
polity's identity was constituted by a permanent counterpart, and the honest
consequence is that Tidore is absent from every field on this page. It is not in
`ended.by` — the Dutch company ended Ternate, not Tidore. It is not an edge:
succession requires one thing to come after another, and these two are
contemporaneous for their entire existence. It is not in the influence counts,
which measure what a polity sent outward.

What the schema can carry is in the turning points, and it is the first of them.
The Portuguese did not force their way into Maluku in 1512. They were invited by
Ternate, as leverage against Tidore, into a quarrel that was already several
centuries old — and when Spain arrived by the other ocean a decade later, Tidore
took the Spanish for the same reason. Two European empires spent a century in
the north Moluccas as the hired halves of a local dualism, which is close to the
inverse of the story usually told about the spice islands.
