---
title: Ternate and Tidore
drafted_from: andaya-1993
phase: expansion
---

Tidore is visible from Ternate. The channel between them is about two
kilometres wide. They grew the same crop, spoke closely related languages, had
courts of the same form, and spent five hundred years in opposition to one
another.

Andaya's account of Maluku is built around that opposition, and argues that it
was not a rivalry in the ordinary sense of two states that happened to be
adjacent — it was the organising structure of the region. Maluku was understood
by the people in it as a set of paired and complementary halves with two
centres, and every island, every district, every dependency out along the
Papuan coast attached itself to one side or the other. A conflict between the
two courts was not a breakdown of the system. It was the system running.

That is a genuinely awkward thing to describe, because the ordinary vocabulary
of history has little use for it. Tidore did not precede Ternate and did not
follow it. It did not conquer Ternate and was not conquered by it. It was not a
vassal, an overlord, a colony or an heir. It was the other half, continuously,
for five hundred years — and a Ternate without Tidore is not a smaller Ternate
but a different kind of object altogether.

What the arrangement produced is dateable, and it starts in 1512. The
Portuguese did not force their way into Maluku. They were invited by Ternate,
as leverage against Tidore, into a quarrel that was already several centuries
old — and when Spain arrived by the other ocean a decade later, Tidore took the
Spanish for the same reason. Two European empires spent a century in the north
Moluccas as the hired halves of a local dualism, which is close to the inverse
of the story usually told about the spice islands. Neither was let in because
it was strong. Each was let in because the other side had not got it.
