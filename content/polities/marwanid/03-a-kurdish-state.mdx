title: A Kurdish state
drafted_from: ripper-2000
phase: aside

Kurdish states are rare in the record of this period. The Ayyubids were Kurdish
by origin, but Saladin's house was Arabized within a generation, ruled in
Arabic, and is remembered as an Egyptian and Syrian dynasty rather than a
Kurdish one. The Marwanids are the other entry, and they are the one that
stayed what it was.

Ripper's monograph — the only book-length study of the dynasty, and in German,
which is worth stating rather than hiding — makes a claim about the emirate
that goes beyond its hundred years. The Marwanid century is when the westward
movement of Kurdish population into Diyar Bakr intensified under a Kurdish
ruler, and it is a substantial part of why the upper Tigris is Kurdish country
now rather than Armenian or Arab or Syriac. A polity that lasted from 983 to
1085 is still visible in a map of who lives where.

That is the kind of consequence nothing measures. The Marwanids carried no
religion outward, produced no script, and nobody claimed to succeed them; by
every ordinary index of influence they left nothing at all.

What they left was people, in places where those people are still living. It is
the largest thing they did and the hardest to state as a fact, because
migration in this period is attested unevenly, is contested wherever it is
attested, and resists being counted by anybody honest about the evidence.
