Fakhr al-Dawla ibn Jahir had been the Marwanids' vizier. He left, went to
Baghdad, rose in the Seljuk administration, and in 1085 persuaded the sultan
Malikshah that Diyar Bakr was worth taking and that he was the man to take it.
He led the campaign in person. Mayyafariqin fell, the emirate ended, and the
treasury a hundred years of careful neutrality had accumulated in the citadel
was carried east.

The sources on that treasury are the kind of thing chroniclers enjoy — the
libraries, the jewels, the manuscripts, the itemised catalogue of a small
dynasty's century — and the enjoyment is the point of including it here. A
polity like this one is legible in the record mainly at the moment it is
looted. For fifty years under Nasr al-Dawla the chronicles say very little
about Mayyafariqin because nothing was happening there that a chronicler in
Baghdad needed to explain, which is the condition every ruler in the period
claimed to want and almost none of them achieves.

Diyar Bakr became a Seljuk province and never governed itself again. Within
twenty years the Artuqids were at Hisn Kayfa and then Mardin — Turkmen, holding
the same rocks by the same logic — and what governed the country next begins in
1102. Nobody afterward claimed to be continuing the Marwanids, and the only
thing joining them to anything else is the plain fact of who ended them: the
Seljuks, from outside the region entirely.

What the emirate left was not a claim or an institution. It was a population.
