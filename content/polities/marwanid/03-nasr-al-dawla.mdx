Nasr al-Dawla Ahmad ibn Marwan ruled Diyar Bakr from 1011 to 1061. Fifty years
on one throne is exceptional anywhere in the medieval Near East, and he spent
them in a corner of the world that four larger powers were fighting across.

The method was not military. Nasr al-Dawla was on terms with the Byzantines
under Basil II and after him, with the Buyids and then the Abbasids at Baghdad,
with the Fatimids in Egypt, and eventually with the Seljuks — simultaneously,
and by acknowledging whichever of them was closest that decade while conceding
nothing that mattered. He held two strong cities, Mayyafariqin and Amid, on
ground that is difficult to march an army into and expensive to besiege, and he
made the price of taking them consistently higher than the value of having
them.

<Figure id="mw-nasr" />

The result was a half-century of prosperity that the chroniclers, who are not
usually sentimental about small dynasties, describe with something like
astonishment: full markets, working irrigation, walls and bridges and mosques
built and kept, and a court that paid poets and scholars and physicians well
enough that they came from Baghdad. Mayyafariqin in the middle of the eleventh
century was a place people went to.

Political weight and the quality of life underneath it come apart, and this is
one of the clearest cases. The Marwanids were never a great power and
contributed nothing to anybody's military history — and the fifty years they
bought were, for the people living in Diyar Bakr, better than anything the
region got from the empires on either side of them.
