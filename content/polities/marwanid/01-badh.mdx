Badh ibn Dustak was a Kurd of the Humaydi tribe on the upper Tigris, and the
Arabic sources are unanimous and slightly derisive about where he started: he
kept cattle, then took up arms. In the 980s the Buyid grip on northern Iraq was
loosening and the Hamdanids of Mosul were finishing, and Badh took Mayyafariqin
and then Amid and then most of Diyar Bakr, in the manner of a man who has
noticed that nobody is coming.

He overreached in the usual direction — south, towards Mosul, which was the
prize everyone in the Jazira wanted — and it killed him. In 990 the Uqayl, whom
the Buyids had settled in the region precisely as a counterweight to Kurds like
him, beat and killed him. That fight is on this site twice: it is the beginning
of the Uqaylid emirate on the next page, and the end of the founder on this one.

What happened afterwards is the interesting part, and it is why this is a polity
and not an episode. Badh's conquest did not dissolve. His sister's sons — the
sons of a man called Marwan, who never ruled anything — took it up, held it, and
gave it a hundred years and a name. Abu Ali al-Hasan first, then his brother
Mumahhid al-Dawla, then the third brother Ahmad, and the third brother turned
out to be the reason anyone remembers the family.

Kennedy's framing of the tenth and eleventh centuries in the Jazira applies
exactly: the caliphate's collapse outward did not produce anarchy, it produced
locally-raised powers that could hold a city and negotiate with an empire. What
the Marwanids added to the pattern is that they did it for three generations,
which none of their Arab neighbours managed.
