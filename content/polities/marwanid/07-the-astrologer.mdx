---
title: The astrologer
drafted_from: ibn-khallikan
phase: afterlife
---

An astrologer once came to Nasr al-Dawla, the great Marwanid, and told him
among other things that a man he had been the benefactor of would attack his
kingdom and take it from his children.

The prince thought for a moment, then raised his head and looked at Fakhr
al-Dawla ibn Jahir, who was sitting there. *If these words are true*, he said,
*this shaykh is the man.*

Then he turned to him and commended his children to his care.

## What happened

Ibn Jahir left Marwanid service, rose to be vizier at Baghdad, and came back in
1085 at the head of a force of Turkomans, Kurds and emirs supplied by the
Seljuks. Amid fell to his son after a hard siege. Three months later he took
Mayyafariqin from Nasr al-Dawla's son and seized the treasures of the Marwanid
dynasty.

Ibn Khallikan tells the prophecy and the outcome in the same paragraph, without
any suggestion that it was a coincidence, and then declines to give the details
of the conquest because they would take too long. The anecdote is the part he
wanted preserved.

## Why the story was told

It is not really a story about astrology. It is a story about what everyone in
eleventh-century Iraq and the Jazira knew: that the man who ended a dynasty was
usually somebody it had raised.

That had happened to the Abbasids, whose Turkish guards took over the
caliphate; to the Samanids, whose slave general founded Ghazna; to the Buyids
and to the Seljuks after them. The Marwanids had themselves come out of an
adventurer's household. Nasr al-Dawla did not need an astrologer to tell him
where the danger was, and the story preserves the moment as a joke he made at
his own expense in front of the man concerned.

The bleak part is the last line. Having identified his successor's murderer, so
to speak, he commended his children to that man's protection — because there
was nobody else to commend them to. The only people strong enough to defend a
dynasty are the people strong enough to take it.

## What was taken

The Marwanid treasury went to Baghdad. Ibn Jahir did not keep Diyar Bakr
either; the Seljuks who had lent him the army took the country a few years
afterwards, and it was governed from outside for the next four hundred years.

What was left in place was the population. The Kurdish settlement of the upper
Tigris that the Marwanid fifty years had consolidated outlasted the treasury,
the dynasty, the vizier who took it, the sultans who took it from him, and
every government that has ruled the region since.
