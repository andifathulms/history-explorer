Itzcoatl died in 1436 after thirteen years as king and more than thirty
as the Mexica's own general, having taken a tributary city and made it,
within a single reign, master of the people who had once ruled it. The
four electors who chose his successors did not deliberate long: with no
surviving brother to inherit, the choice fell to a grandson, Moctezuma
Ilhuicamina, whose own record of service made him the obvious candidate.
Both allied kings of the Triple Alliance confirmed the election and sent
gifts befitting the honour.

## A custom that made war a coronation requirement

Before he would allow himself to be formally crowned, Moctezuma
followed what Clavigero records as either established law or his own
strong preference: a new king went to war first, to take prisoners for
sacrifice at his own coronation. Moctezuma chose the Chalca as his
target, in revenge for an old insult - he had once been seized and
imprisoned by them while travelling as an ambassador - and defeated them
in the field, taking captives without yet reducing the whole Chalca
state to tribute, so as not to delay the ceremony any further. On the
day appointed for his coronation, tribute and gifts from every
already-conquered territory arrived in ordered procession: gold, silver,
fine featherwork, and the prisoners whose sacrifice would mark his reign
as beginning the way every reign before his own house's rise had not
been able to.

## A name remembered two ways

Some historians, Clavigero notes, identify this same Moctezuma with
another figure much celebrated in Mexica tradition under the name
Tlacaelel - a warrior of such courage in youth that he earned that
title, meaning roughly "man of great heart," alongside Ilhuicamina,
"archer of heaven." Other early chroniclers treat Tlacaelel as a
separate man entirely, a minister serving under Moctezuma rather than
the king himself. Clavigero argues at length against that reading,
pointing out contradictions it creates elsewhere in the record - a
dispute unresolved in his own day and, in the absence of a single
surviving Mexica archive to settle it, unlikely to be resolved by
argument alone.
