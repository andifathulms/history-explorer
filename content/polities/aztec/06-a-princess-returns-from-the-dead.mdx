Moctezuma II inherited an empire at the height of its reach, but
tradition remembers his reign as shadowed from early on by a string of
portents no one at the time could explain away. In 1509 the turrets of
the Great Temple caught fire and burned on a calm, windless night with
no apparent cause; the following year the waters of Lake Texcoco surged
so violently that houses in the city were destroyed, though there was
no wind, no earthquake, nothing that could ordinarily account for it.
Figures of armed men were reported fighting each other in the sky.

## A sister who woke from her own funeral

The most striking of these stories concerns a woman said to be
Moctezuma's own sister, a princess who fell gravely ill and, believed
dead, was laid in a stone sepulchre. She revived there, climbed out, and
made her way to the palace garden, where she was found by her own
household. She told Moctezuma she had been led along a riverbank by a
radiant young man marked with a cross on his forehead, who showed her
human bones and skulls along the way and told her they were the souls
of her ancestors, tormented for their sins. On the river itself, he
showed her ships crowded with bearded, pale-skinned men in armour,
telling her God's will was that she live to witness the revolutions
these men would bring to the kingdom - and that when the fighting was
over and a new faith proclaimed, she should be the first of her own
people to accept it.

## A king who stopped visiting his own sister

Moctezuma was struck with such dread on hearing this that he withdrew
without a word to a palace reserved for occasions of grief, and never
again visited the sister whose vision had unsettled him so completely,
preferring not to hear the prophecy repeated. She, for her part, lived
on for years afterward in quiet retirement, and in 1524, three years
after the conquest her vision had foretold, became one of the first of
her people baptized, taking the name Doña Maria Papantzin. Whatever
these stories owed to hindsight once the conquest had actually happened,
Clavigero records that they were set down in detail in both Mexica and
Acolhuan historical accounts, and that the omens, real or reinterpreted,
did nothing to slow Moctezuma's own military campaigns in the years that
followed.
