By the custom Chimalpopoca had begun, no Mexica noble could be crowned
king without having first served as commander of the armies - proof, it
was reasoned, that a man fit to lead a warlike nation in war should also
know how to govern it in peace. Ahuitzotl came to the throne already
tested in that role, and one of his first acts as king was to finish a
project his predecessor had begun: the rebuilding of Tenochtitlan's
chief temple on a grander scale than before.

## Four years of war fought to fill a temple

The work went forward with an enormous body of workmen and was
completed within four years. Through that whole period Ahuitzotl kept
campaigning - against the Mazahuas to the west, the Zapotecs three
hundred miles to the southeast, and several other nations - and every
prisoner taken in those campaigns was deliberately held back rather than
sacrificed at the time of capture, reserved instead for the temple's own
consecration.

## A festival historians could not agree how to count

When the building was finished, Ahuitzotl invited both allied kings and
the nobility of all three cities of the league to its dedication, and
the crowds that gathered, Clavigero writes, were the largest ever seen in
Mexico, drawn from the most distant reaches of the alliance's own tribute
network. The festival ran four days, during which every prisoner
collected over the preceding four years was sacrificed at the temple's
upper sanctuary. Historians disagreed even at the time on how many that
was: Torquemada put the total at seventy-two thousand three hundred and
forty-four; other accounts gave sixty-four thousand and sixty. To
manage the killing with the intended spectacle, the victims were formed
into two files, each a mile and a half long, running from the roads of
Tacuba and Iztapalapan to the temple itself, arriving in a continuous
procession as the sacrifices proceeded. The year was 1486.
