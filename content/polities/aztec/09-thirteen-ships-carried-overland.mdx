Cortés spent the months after the retreat from Tenochtitlan rebuilding
his own shattered force and preparing for a second attempt he intended
to win outright. The centrepiece of his plan was a fleet of thirteen
brigantines, small armed vessels built from timber cut and shaped inland
at Tlaxcala, carried in pieces overland to the shore of Lake Texcoco, and
assembled there - a way of putting Spanish firepower onto the same water
that had trapped and drowned so many of his men the year before.

## A city cut off from its own drinking water

Before closing on the capital itself, Cortés's forces marched to
Chapultepec and destroyed the aqueduct that carried fresh water into
Tenochtitlan, fighting off a Mexica force sent to stop them and losing
the city's own water supply for the whole length of the siege that
followed. With the brigantines controlling the lake and the causeways
blockaded by land, the three-way league that had once dominated the
valley through its own tribute network found itself surrounded by
former tributaries only too willing to help finish it.

## A city destroyed almost as thoroughly as it was conquered

The siege that followed lasted some ninety-three days, fought house by
house and canal by canal as Spanish and allied troops advanced, filled
in waterways to bring their own artillery forward, and levelled
buildings as they went rather than leave any position the defenders
might use against them. Smallpox, carried unknowingly by the invaders'
own earlier landing, spread through a population with no prior immunity
and killed alongside the fighting itself. By the time the defenders had
been pushed into the last quarter of the city, Tenochtitlan's own
survivors were starving, its causeways broken, and its king,
Cuauhtemoc, was already making plans to escape across the lake rather
than surrender the city that could no longer be defended.
