By the early fifteenth century Tenochtitlan had grown from a refuge into
a functioning city, but it remained a tributary of Azcapotzalco, the
capital of the Tepanec people on the western shore of the lake - a
relationship of subordination, not the equal alliance it would later
enter into. Azcapotzalco's own power reached across much of the Valley
of Mexico, extending to the rival city of Texcoco, whose ruler was
killed when the Tepanecs invaded in 1418. His fifteen-year-old heir,
Nezahualcoyotl, watched his father die from a hiding place in the
branches of a tree overhanging the battlefield.

## Years of flight, betrayal and disguise

Nezahualcoyotl's own life over the following decade reads like a string
of narrow escapes. Captured once and imprisoned, he was freed only when
the fortress's governor took his place and paid for the substitution
with his own life. Pardoned for a time and allowed to study under an old
tutor, he was nearly murdered at a banquet, then hunted through his own
capital by soldiers sent to kill him outright - escaping through a
secret passage concealed by a censer's smoke while the assassins waited
in an adjoining room. A price was set on his head, and he spent long
stretches hiding in caves, thickets and, on one occasion, under a pile
of maguey fibre a peasant woman covered him with as pursuers searched
her cottage. Despite the reward offered, no one who sheltered him ever
gave him up.

## A coalition formed in exile

What kept Nezahualcoyotl alive was less his own concealment than the
loyalty he inspired and the resentment Azcapotzalco's own harsh rule was
generating among its neighbours, Tenochtitlan included. Word reached him
that a coalition was forming against the Tepanec state, and when the day
came he found himself at the head of an army strong enough to face his
enemies directly - the beginning of a war that would end not only his
own exile but Azcapotzalco's dominance over the whole valley.
