Tenochtitlan's own king Chimalpopoca died amid the same Tepanec
aggression that had killed Nezahualcoyotl's father, and the Mexica
nobility, finding themselves without a ruler and pressed hard by
Azcapotzalco's new tyrant Maxtla, moved quickly to elect a successor
capable of leading them through the crisis. Their choice fell on
Itzcoatl, a son of the earlier king Acamapichtli by a slave woman - a
birth that might have counted against him elsewhere, but who had spent
more than thirty years as commander of the Mexica armies and was judged
the most prudent, just and courageous man the nation had. He was seated
on the royal mat and acclaimed king by the assembled nobles.

## An embassy that became an alliance

Itzcoatl's first act as king was to send word to Nezahualcoyotl of his
own election and his determination to join forces against Maxtla. The
exiled prince accepted gladly, and the combined armies moved against
Azcapotzalco directly, defeating Maxtla's forces, driving him to the
baths of his own capital where he was dragged out and put to death by
the customary rites the Mexica reserved for enemies, and razing the
city itself, whose wasted territory became, from then on, a slave
market for the whole valley.

## A league that outlasted a century

With the war won, Itzcoatl formalized what had been a wartime coalition
into a standing arrangement. Nezahualcoyotl was restored, with Mexica
help, to the throne of Texcoco. Totoquihuatzin, a Tepanec noble who had
taken no part in the war against his own people, was made king of
Tlacopan, a lesser city that would receive a fifth of whatever the
alliance's campaigns took in plunder, the remaining share divided
between Texcoco and Tenochtitlan in Tenochtitlan's own favour. Each king
was made an elector with a voice in confirming the others' successions,
and each was bound to send aid to the other two when called upon.
Itzcoatl then travelled to Texcoco to crown Nezahualcoyotl with his own
hands. The three-city league that resulted, later historians would
record, held firm and unbroken for a hundred years, and it was this
alliance, not any one city alone, that made possible the rapid conquests
that followed.
