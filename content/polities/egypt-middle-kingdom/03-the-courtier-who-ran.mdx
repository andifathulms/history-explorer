The best evidence for how frightening Amenemhat's murder was to the people
closest to it is not an official record at all. It is a story, told in the
first person by a fictional courtier named Sinuhe, and copied out by scribes
in training for the next six hundred years — more manuscripts of it survive
than of almost any other Middle Kingdom text.

## What Sinuhe does

Sinuhe is an attendant of Senusret's household, on campaign with the prince in
Libya when a messenger arrives with news of the king's death. He overhears it
by accident, is not meant to have heard it, and immediately deserts — not
toward the court, where the new king is, but away from Egypt entirely,
crossing the eastern frontier alone into Syria-Palestine:

> My heart was distraught, my arms spread out, trembling fell on all my limbs.
> I removed myself by leaps and bounds, to seek myself a hiding place; I put
> myself between two bushes, in order to await the passersby.

Nothing in the text says why. Sinuhe is not accused of anything, is not a
plotter, and is never shown doing wrong. He runs on the strength of nothing
but the political weather at the exact hour a king was killed — which is
itself the most honest evidence the period offers for what that hour actually
felt like to the people living through it, dramatised rather than reported,
but written by people close enough to it to know what needed no explaining to
their own audience.

## What Sinuhe gets

He spends decades in exile among the Semitic chieftains of the Levant,
prospers, marries, raises a family, becomes a local ruler's champion in single
combat — and then, old, writes to the reigning king of Egypt asking to come
home and be buried there instead of abroad. Senusret I's reply, quoted in the
text, is a royal pardon addressed personally to a man who fled his father's
household at the worst possible moment and was never charged with anything:

> Do not think of night, cross over by day, come home! Have you thought of the
> day when you will be buried? A night will be made for you with oils and
> wrappings... A funeral procession will be made for you on the day of joining
> the earth.

Sinuhe returns, is received at court without punishment, and is given
everything a loyal official would have been given: a house, an income, a tomb
built and equipped by the state. The story's entire tension — a man's terror
at a moment of crisis, set against a pardon so complete it reads as an
argument for royal mercy as state policy — is fiction, but it was copied for
six centuries by scribes who used it to teach handwriting, correct grammar and
model courtly language to the next generation of Egyptian officials. Whatever
it says about the crisis of 1962, later Egyptians kept finding it worth
repeating.
