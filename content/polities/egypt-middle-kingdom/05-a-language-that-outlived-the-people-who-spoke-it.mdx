Most of what makes the Middle Kingdom legible in more than administrative
outline is a body of writing scribal schools kept copying for centuries after
the kingdom that produced it had ended, and the copying itself is worth
pausing on, separately from anything any king did.

## What got copied, and why

*The Instructions of Amenemhat* and *The Tale of Sinuhe* were both composed
within a generation of the events they describe or claim to describe, and
both survive today only in copies made hundreds of years later by students
learning to write. So does *The Eloquent Peasant*, in which a robbed traveller
petitions a royal official in nine escalating speeches of formal rhetoric and
is kept talking on purpose so the court can go on enjoying the performance —
and *The Admonitions of Ipuwer*, a lament describing a kingdom turned upside
down, the poor grown rich and the rich reduced to labour, addressed to a king
in a style closer to complaint literature than to any surviving chronicle of
an actual collapse.

None of these were written for the audience that ended up preserving them. A
schoolboy in the New Kingdom, copying *Sinuhe* onto an ostracon to practise
his hand, was not reading current events. He was being trained in a language
his own spoken Egyptian had already moved well past.

## The standard that stuck

Egyptian, like most living languages, changed with use — the language spoken
under the New Kingdom pharaohs was audibly different from the language spoken
under the Twelfth Dynasty. What did not change at the same pace was the
written standard scribal schools taught: Middle Egyptian, fixed in its
administrative and literary use during this period, went on being the
language of formal monumental inscription for roughly two thousand years
afterward, read and carved on temple walls under kings who spoke something
else entirely, down to the Ptolemaic and Roman periods.

That is not a fact about any king's conquest or any army's reach. It is a
fact about what a scribal establishment decided was worth preserving exactly
as it stood, generation after generation, long after the political world that
produced it was gone — the one part of the Middle Kingdom nobody had to win a
war to keep.
