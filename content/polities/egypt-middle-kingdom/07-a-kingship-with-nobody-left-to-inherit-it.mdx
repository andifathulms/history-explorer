The Thirteenth Dynasty that followed Sobekneferu is, on paper, a single
continuous line of over fifty kings. In practice it is a record of a kingship
that had stopped being able to reproduce the stability the Twelfth Dynasty had
spent two centuries building.

Reigns that had run for decades under Senusret III and Amenemhat III now run
for one, two, sometimes a handful of years apiece, with little sign of
dynastic continuity between most of them — the throne passing, as far as the
fragmentary evidence allows a pattern to be seen at all, between officials and
military men whose route to it is nowhere explained. Merneferre Ay, who held
the throne for roughly twenty-three years and is attested by monuments in both
the north and the south, is the exception that shows how far the norm had
drifted: the last king this period's own record can call plausibly national,
holding office for a length of time that would not have been remarkable two
reigns after Mentuhotep II and had become extraordinary by his own.

## What did not wait for a strong king to arrive

Egypt's Delta had a long-standing population of Levantine origin, settled
there over generations through trade and migration rather than conquest, and
as central authority thinned through the Thirteenth Dynasty this population's
own rulers stop needing Memphis or Itjtawy's permission for anything. A
separate line, ruling from the eastern Delta town of Avaris, is attested
governing its own territory independently well before any source describes an
invasion — the fragmentation did not wait for an external army; it simply
stopped being resisted from the inside.

By around 1650, conventionally taken as the line between this period and the
next, the pattern is set: a Delta kingship at Avaris ruling in its own right,
a diminished Egyptian kingship still claiming the old titulary somewhere
further south, and — behind Egypt's own southern border, in a kingdom around
Kerma that two centuries of Nubian fortresses had never actually conquered — a
third power watching the whole thing happen. None of the sources describe a
single battle that ended the Thirteenth Dynasty's claim to rule the whole
country. It did not need to lose one. It had already stopped being able to
enforce the claim years before anybody thought to contest it by force.
