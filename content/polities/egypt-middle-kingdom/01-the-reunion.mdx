For over a century after the Old Kingdom broke apart, Egypt had two kings at
once. A northern line ruled from Herakleopolis, near the entrance to the
Faiyum, served by the nomarchs of Asyut, whose tomb inscriptions are almost
the only surviving account of the period from either side. A southern line
ruled from Thebes, a town of no particular importance before this, and spent
several generations fighting the north for the rest of the country.

The Theban line's fourth king, Mentuhotep II, is the one who finished it. In
his thirty-ninth regnal year — the fourteenth of active campaigning, by the
count of his own inscriptions — Herakleopolis fell, and for the first time in
well over a century one man ruled the whole river valley again.

## What the win actually settled

It settled less than it looks like it settled. Mentuhotep's own titulary
records the change in stages rather than as a single triumphant moment: early
in his reign he is titled simply king of Upper Egypt, matching the reality of
a Theban prince ruling a rump state; only later does he add the epithet
"Uniter of the Two Lands," and only in his final years does his Horus name
change again, to one proclaiming him lord of both halves outright. Unification
was a process he narrated in his own monuments as it happened, not an event he
announced.

The Turin Canon — the king list drawn up centuries later as an administrative
document rather than a monument, and for that reason one of the more reliable
sources for the period's own sequence of rulers — resumes its count of kings
at Mentuhotep's dynasty without a break, folding the Herakleopolitan line out
of the official reckoning entirely. Whoever actually held more territory for
longer, the record that survived is the winner's.

## Why the win held

A country splitting in two once is not unusual; the First Intermediate Period
was itself not the first fragmentation and the Second Intermediate Period,
four centuries later, would not be the last. What made this reunification
different from a temporary reconquest is what followed it: sixty more years of
uninterrupted Theban kingship, then a change of dynasty managed without another
civil war, then two centuries in which the reunited kingdom's institutions were
rebuilt from the ground rather than simply reasserted.

Mentuhotep himself did the rebuilding only partway. His mortuary temple at
Deir el-Bahari — cut into the cliffs on the west bank at Thebes, combining a
pyramid, colonnades and a rock-cut tomb in a design no earlier king had
attempted — is the clearest evidence of a court with resources and ambition
again. The administrative machinery to match it, and the new capital to run it
from, belonged to the dynasty that replaced his.
