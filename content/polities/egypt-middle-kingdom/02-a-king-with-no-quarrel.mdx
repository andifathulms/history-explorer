Mentuhotep II's own line lasted one more generation after him and then ended,
apparently without violence, in the reign of a fourth Mentuhotep whose own
vizier — a commoner named Amenemhat, who had led an expedition of ten thousand
men to the Wadi Hammamat quarries in his master's name — took the throne
himself soon after. Nothing in the surviving record explains the transition,
and the absence of an explanation is itself the point: a man with no blood
claim on the Theban kingship became king of Egypt, and the sources that would
normally supply a justification for that are simply silent.

## Building somewhere new

Amenemhat I did not rule from Thebes. He built an entirely new capital near
the mouth of the Faiyum, at a site called Itjtawy — "Seizer of the Two Lands"
— close enough to both the Delta and the valley to govern the whole country
from the middle of it rather than ruling the north from a base two hundred
miles south of Memphis. Nothing at the site has been excavated above the
modern water table; its location is known only from later texts naming
towns near it, not from the ruins themselves.

A king with no dynastic claim to inherit was, in effect, choosing not to
inherit anything, including a capital his predecessors had made their own.
Everything about the Twelfth Dynasty's administration — a reorganised court,
new provincial titles, a building programme that reasserted royal authority in
places Thebes' own kings had never needed to reassert it — starts from that
decision to begin again somewhere with no prior owner.

## The night it nearly ended again

It did not stay bloodless. In his thirtieth and final year on the throne, a
decade into a co-regency that had already made his son Senusret joint king,
Amenemhat was murdered in his own bedchamber by men of his own household
guard, with no foreign enemy and no rival claimant recorded anywhere near the
event — Senusret himself was away leading an army in Libya when it happened.

The fullest account is not a chronicle but a literary composition, *The
Instructions of Amenemhat*, written as the dead king's own voice addressing
his son and warning him never to trust a subordinate, "for whom he had
promoted from nothing":

> It was after supper, when night had fallen, and I had spent an hour of
> happiness. I was asleep upon my bed, having become weary, and my heart had
> begun to follow sleep. When weapons of my counsel were wielded, I had become
> like a snake of the necropolis. As I came to, I awoke to fighting, and found
> it was an attack of the bodyguard.

Whether the text was composed for Amenemhat's own reign, to justify a
co-regency he had already set up, or written after his death at his son's
order to secure a shaken succession, cannot be settled from the evidence — but
either reading points at the same institutional response, which is the
subject of what follows.
