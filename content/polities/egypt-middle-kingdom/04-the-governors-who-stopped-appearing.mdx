Senusret III is the Middle Kingdom's best-documented soldier-king, and the
part of his reign easiest to narrate is also the least important part of what
it changed. Between his sixth and nineteenth years he led at least four
campaigns into Nubia, recorded on boundary stelae he had cut at the Second
Cataract forbidding any Nubian from passing it by water except to trade —
under royal licence, at one designated post, and nowhere else.

Behind that frontier he built or rebuilt a chain of fortresses running the
length of the Second Cataract's rapids — Buhen, Semna, Kumma, Mirgissa, and
others — close enough together to signal along, each garrisoned, provisioned,
and walled on a scale nothing earlier in Egyptian history had needed a
frontier post to be. Nubia under Senusret III was not raided. It was
occupied, administered, and closed to everyone the administration had not
licensed.

## The change nobody campaigned for

The fortress line is what the inscriptions boast about. The change that
mattered more is one the inscriptions do not mention at all, because it
happened at home and nobody had reason to commemorate it: sometime in this
same reign, the great provincial governorships — hereditary in some Upper
Egyptian families since the chaos of the First Intermediate Period, when
central authority had been too weak to prevent local lords from passing
office to their sons — stop appearing in the record.

The nomarchs' rock-cut tombs, a major genre of Middle Kingdom art for two
centuries, stop being built at the same moment. Provincial administration
does not disappear; it reappears reorganised into new titles reporting
upward through a reformed vizier's office rather than downward through a
local dynasty's own household. No source describes a purge, a rebellion
suppressed, or a single dramatic act of centralisation. The nomarchs are
simply there in the reign before Senusret III and not there in the reigns
after him.

## Two reforms, one reign

Read separately, a frontier closed to unlicensed traffic and a class of
hereditary local rulers quietly absorbed into central administration look
like two different stories. Read together they are the same story: a king
willing to redraw, by decree and by walls, exactly where authority was
allowed to exist independently of the crown — on Egypt's own border and
inside its own provinces alike. Nothing else in the Middle Kingdom's two
centuries changed the shape of the state this completely, and almost none of
it required a single battle fought inside Egypt itself.
