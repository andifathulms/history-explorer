Fourteen centuries after the Twelfth Dynasty ended, a Greek historian visiting
Egypt was told about a king who had conquered the known world. Herodotus
calls him Sesostris, and the story he records bears only a strained
resemblance to anything a Middle Kingdom king actually did — which is exactly
what makes the shape of the exaggeration worth tracing back to its source.

## What the priests told Herodotus

Sesostris, in the account Herodotus was given at Egyptian temples, took a
great army and marched the length of the known continent, subduing every
nation in his path. Where a people resisted, he raised a pillar recording, in
his own words, that he had conquered them by force. Where a people submitted
without a fight, the pillar said so too, with an addition Herodotus reports
without softening: an image marking the people as cowards for not resisting.
He crossed from Asia into Europe and beat the Scythians and the Thracians,
further north and west than any Egyptian army before or after him is
otherwise recorded reaching. Herodotus himself claims to have seen some of
the pillars still standing in Syria-Palestine in his own day, and describes
two rock-cut figures in Ionia, spear in one hand and bow in the other, that
local tradition also credited to this king.

Later writers kept building. Manetho gives him a forty-eight-year reign and
has him subdue the whole of Asia and Europe as far as Thrace in nine of those
years. Other Greek and Roman authors added a detail Herodotus does not
report: a chariot drawn not by horses but by the kings of the nations
Sesostris had conquered, harnessed to it as a public humiliation. Each
retelling made the reach a little wider and the humiliation a little more
specific.

## What a Twelfth Dynasty king could not have done

None of it fits the twelfth-century BC world the Middle Kingdom actually
occupied. Egypt had no horses and no chariots in Senusret III's own reign —
both arrive in the Nile valley only centuries later, with the Hyksos. A king
credited with conquering Europe from a chariot he did not yet own, drawn by
horses his kingdom did not yet have, is not a garbled memory of a specific
campaign. He is a composite, built by later Egyptians and the Greeks they
told it to, out of whichever earlier king's reputation was flattering enough
to attach to at the time the telling was needed.

The candidate scholars keep returning to is Senusret III. His actual
campaigns went south into Nubia and, on a smaller scale, into the Levant —
not west into Europe — and the boundary stelae he raised at the Second
Cataract forbidding Nubians to pass without licence are written in exactly
the tone Herodotus attributes to Sesostris's own pillars: a declaration of
conquest, in the king's own voice, warning off whoever reads it next. Two
kings' names, Senusret and Sesostris, are close enough in sound that the
derivation is barely disputed. What travelled into Greek was not the
campaign. It was the register the campaign had been announced in.

## What actually survived, next to what was invented

Set beside each other, the two records do not compete so much as show what
each kind of source is for. The stelae at Semna are administrative
instruments — a closed frontier, a licensed trading post, a named penalty for
crossing without permission, addressed to Senusret III's own successors as
much as to Nubia. Herodotus's Sesostris is a story about what Egyptians,
fourteen centuries later, needed an ancestor to have been: not merely secure
on a closed southern border, but master of everywhere anyone telling the
story had heard of.

The Middle Kingdom's own frontier policy was cautious enough to be enforced.
Its legend was not, and it is the legend — marching through Asia, humbling
kings, harnessing them to a chariot Egypt did not yet possess — that a Greek
audience kept retelling for a thousand years after the stelae themselves had
stopped being read by anyone who could still do anything about a Nubian
crossing the border.
