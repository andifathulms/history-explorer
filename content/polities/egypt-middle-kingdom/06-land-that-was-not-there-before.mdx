The Faiyum is a natural depression west of the Nile valley, fed by a channel
off the river, holding a lake that expands and contracts with the flood.
Twelfth Dynasty kings had been draining and diking its margins for
generations before Amenemhat III, but it is his reign that completed the
project at a scale the region had not seen: dikes, sluices and a network of
canals that pushed the lake's shoreline back and turned what had been marsh
and open water into cultivable land, on a scale later Greek writers — arriving
over a thousand years afterward, when the works were still partly functioning
— described with evident astonishment.

## What that bought

New agricultural land, in an economy where the amount of arable ground was
close to the whole basis of state revenue, meant new towns, new temple
endowments, and new administrative posts to run them — the Faiyum becomes, for
the first time, a populated province in its own right rather than a
lake-edge backwater. Amenemhat III's own reign, by the length of it alone —
around forty-five years — and by the sheer number of his surviving monuments,
is remembered as the Twelfth Dynasty's most materially prosperous, the peak of
what two centuries of reunification, co-regency and provincial reorganisation
had been built toward.

Mining expeditions multiply in the same reign: to the turquoise galleries of
Sinai, to the gold-bearing wadis of the Eastern Desert, recorded in numbers
and detail no earlier reign matches. A state confident enough of its own
stability to keep sending large organised expeditions to remote frontier
workings, year after year, is a state that has stopped worrying about its own
survival — which makes what happened immediately afterward harder to explain,
not easier.

## The reign after his

Amenemhat III was followed by a fourth Amenemhat of uncertain relation to him,
and then, briefly, by a woman: Sobekneferu, the first ruler of Egypt securely
attested as taking the full royal titulary in her own name rather than as a
regent for a son. She reigned under four years. After her the Twelfth
Dynasty's line ends outright, and the kingship passes — by what process no
surviving source explains — to the string of short-reigning Thirteenth
Dynasty kings whose accelerating turnover the Faiyum's own prosperity gives no
warning of at all.
