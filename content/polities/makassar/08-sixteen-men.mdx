Gowa was not abolished in 1669. There was still a sultan, still a court, still
a kingdom on the map. What it no longer had was anybody who had to come when it
called.

Some years after the war, Sultan Abdul Jalil sent out the *bila-bila* — the
summons that told the warriors of Gowa when and where to assemble — because the
Company had asked for troops against the Makassar exiles fighting in Java.

Sixteen people appeared. Seven of them were nobles.

The rest had gone over to a rival claimant living under Dutch protection at
Fort Rotterdam, a few kilometres up the same shore, or had left South Sulawesi
entirely.

## The arrangement that replaced it

What governed South Sulawesi after 1669 was a partnership, and it worked
unusually well for the people who ran it.

Arung Palakka took the internal affairs and the Company took the external ones.
He became ruler of Bone and, over the following quarter-century, the effective
overlord of every state on the peninsula; the Dutch held the fort that had been
Gowa's own — Ujung Pandang, renamed Fort Rotterdam after the birthplace of the
admiral who had taken it — and controlled what left the coast by sea.

It was efficient enough to close off the ordinary ways a South Sulawesi society
had of registering dissent. There was no longer a rival power to defect to, no
neighbouring court to appeal against your own, no war anybody could win. So
people left instead.

## The outflow

They left in numbers that alarmed everybody, including the winners.

By the middle of 1679 the Dutch counted some six hundred Makassar nobles living
in the shadow of Fort Rotterdam alone, three hundred of whom had fought against
them, and reckoned about three thousand Makassar people in the settlement all
told — men, women, children and slaves. The Dutch believed most of them would
leave for Java as soon as they could find boats.

Those who had already gone were the ones who mattered. Makasar and Bugis
fighting men turned up as mercenaries, traders and freebooters from Siam to
Sumatra to the Java Sea, and they were formidable enough to threaten not only
weak kingdoms elsewhere in the archipelago but the settlement at home. Their
defeat in Java in 1679 ended the last serious challenge to the joint
overlordship Arung Palakka and the Company had built.

## The rest of Arung Palakka's life

He spent it making sure the arrangement outlived him, and he was not gentle
about it.

He had one of his staunchest supporters, Arung Bakké Todani, assassinated on
suspicion of standing too well with the Dutch. He used persuasion, intimidation
and, when they did not answer, force, until no ruler in South Sulawesi would
contest him. Less than a year before he died the assembled armies of the
peninsula turned out to demonstrate their unity and their respect for him,
which is the sort of display a man arranges when he wants his heir to see it.

He died in 1696. His successor La Patau ruled unchallenged.

Gowa's sultans went on reigning through all of it. They just no longer had a
kingdom that answered the summons.
