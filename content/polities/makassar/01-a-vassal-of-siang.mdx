Gowa began as somebody else's dependency, and the somebody has been more or
less forgotten.

A sixteenth-century Portuguese account has Gowa subjugated by Tallo, and Tallo
in turn acknowledging a third kingdom, Siang, further up the west coast, as
larger and more powerful than itself. Siang dominated that coast between
roughly the fourteenth and the sixteenth centuries. Manoel Pinto put its
population at about forty thousand in 1545, and its ruler was confident enough
of his resources to offer to supply the entire food requirements of Melaka.

Antonio de Paiva, a Portuguese trader who called in 1542 and again in 1544,
found Gowa a large city — one which, he noted, "had once been a vassal of
Siang, but not any longer."

Something had changed in between, and the Gowa chronicle says what.

## Tumapa'risi' Kallonna

The ninth Karaeng of Gowa came to the throne early in the sixteenth century,
and a Portuguese visitor at about that moment observed that "the region which
particularly bears the name Makassar is very small."

He reorganised it. The chronicle credits him with turning a loose confederation
of communities into a single state: a formal union with Tallo, sealed by an
oath that anyone who set the two against each other would be punished by the
god; a promulgated code of law and of rules for warfare; and a system of taxes
and customs dues collected under a *syahbandar*.

That last item is the port. Everything Makassar later became — the open
harbour, the toll, the argument with the Dutch about the freedom of the sea —
begins with a harbourmaster and a published tariff, instituted by a king whose
reign the chronicle praises in the conventional formula for a good one: the
harvests were good and the fish-catch was plentiful.

He then went to war with his neighbours and won, and Gowa's rulers spent the
next century and a half doing the same. His successor Tunipalangga carried it
much further, and the chronicle lists his conquests in a paragraph that runs to
twenty place-names.

## What became of Siang

An alliance of Gowa and Tallo — the two kingdoms Siang had once outranked —
broke it, some time late in the sixteenth century.

The word Andaya uses is oblivion. Siang had been the commercial and probably
the political centre of the west coast for two hundred years, and it left
almost nothing: a mention on a Portuguese map of about 1540, a few travellers'
remarks, some archaeology, and a local tradition around Pangkajene that still
remembers having been the senior party.

The Gowa chronicle records the victory over it in a sentence, in the same flat
tone it uses for everything else.
