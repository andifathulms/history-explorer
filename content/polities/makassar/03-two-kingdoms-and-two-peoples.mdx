This record's id is `makassar` and its `name.latin` is the Sultanate of Gowa,
which is already a compression.

The polity was two kingdoms. Gowa and Tallo occupied adjoining stretches of the
same coast, and from the early sixteenth century they operated as one — Gowa
supplying the ruler and Tallo supplying, for most of the period that matters, the
chancellor. The formula in the sources is that they were two kings but one
people. The conversion of 1605 came through Tallo's ruler first; Karaeng
Pattingalloang, the chancellor of this record's second chapter, was Tallo's.

This corpus's Ternate and Tidore records are two pages for two courts on two
islands that defined each other in opposition. Gowa and Tallo are one page for
two courts on one coast that defined each other in partnership, and the site has
no more apparatus for the second arrangement than for the first — it just happens
that a partnership can be compressed into one record and an opposition cannot.

## And the other distinction

South Sulawesi is Makasar country and Bugis country, two related but distinct
peoples with distinct languages, and Gowa-Tallo is the Makasar power.

The Bugis kingdoms — Bone, Soppeng, Wajo — are the ones Makassar fought into
accepting Islam between 1608 and 1611, in the campaigns this record's
`religions_carried` count is for, and Bone is the one that came back with a Dutch
fleet in 1667.

That is the single most important fact about this record's ending and it is
invisible in every field. `ended.by` is empty because neither the Dutch company
nor Bone has a page here. What the empty field hides is not just a missing
polity: it is that the war of 1666-69 was a Bugis war against Makasar
overlordship, sixty years in the making, which a European company joined.

Andaya's book is written around Arung Palakka, the Bugis prince who led it, and
its argument is exactly that. This record's last chapter says so. No column on
this site can.
