"Makassar" is a convenience, and so is "the Sultanate of Gowa". The polity was
two kingdoms.

Gowa and Tallo occupied adjoining stretches of the same coast, and from the
early sixteenth century they operated as one: Gowa supplying the ruler, and
Tallo supplying, for most of the period that matters, the chancellor. The
formula in the sources is that they were two kings but one people. The
conversion of 1605 came through Tallo's ruler first. Karaeng Pattingalloang —
the chancellor who kept the library, ordered the telescope and read Latin,
Spanish and Portuguese — was Tallo's.

It is a partnership rather than a merger, and it held for a century and a half
without either half absorbing the other. Ternate and Tidore, a few hundred
miles east, show what the alternative looked like: two courts on two islands
that defined themselves against each other, and never stopped.

## And the other distinction

South Sulawesi is Makasar country and Bugis country — two related but distinct
peoples with distinct languages — and Gowa-Tallo is the Makasar power.

The Bugis kingdoms are Bone, Soppeng and Wajo. They are the ones Makassar
fought into accepting Islam between 1608 and 1611, and Bone is the one that
came back with a Dutch fleet in 1667.

That is the most important thing about how Makassar ended, and it is the part
most often left out. The war of 1666–69 is usually filed as a Dutch conquest,
and the Dutch company was certainly in it. But Andaya's book is written around
Arung Palakka, the Bugis prince who led it, and its argument is that the war
was a Bugis war against Makasar overlordship, sixty years in the making, which
a European company joined.

A trading company with a fleet did not arrive and take South Sulawesi. It was
recruited into somebody else's quarrel, and the quarrel had been going since
the conversions were imposed at spear-point.
