Sombaopu, the fortress at Makassar, fell on 12 June 1669. Its walls were four
metres thick and had to be mined rather than breached.

The treaty of Bongaya had been signed twenty months earlier and had not ended
the war. Its terms removed every foreign trader except the Dutch company,
stripped Makassar of its dependencies, required the demolition of its forts,
and installed Arung Palakka's Bone as the paramount power in South Sulawesi.
Hasanuddin signed it, and then went on fighting.

## Whose war it was

The usual shorthand — the Dutch destroyed Makassar — is not so much wrong as
badly incomplete.

Andaya's account is written around Arung Palakka, a Bugis prince from Bone
whose people Makassar had conquered and set to forced labour, who escaped,
spent years in Batavia, and came back with a company fleet to destroy the
polity that had held his country down. On that reading 1669 is a South Sulawesi
war which the company joined, not a colonial conquest that Sulawesi suffered.

Both descriptions are true and they weigh differently. The company supplied
ships, siege engineering and money. Bone supplied the war, and the reason for
it, and most of the men. What was settled in 1669 was not who would govern
South Sulawesi from Amsterdam but which of two South Sulawesi powers would be
on top — and the answer held for the next century.

## The diaspora

What left Makassar after 1669 mattered more than what stayed.

The Makasar and Bugis who would not accept the settlement went to sea — as
mercenaries, traders, and freebooters, from Siam to Sumatra to the Java Sea —
and they turn up all over the archipelago for the next hundred years.

The Bugis who took power at Riau after 1699, and made themselves the real
authority behind Johor's throne, came out of this diaspora. So did the Makasar
allies of Trunajaya, whose rebellion of 1677 dragged the Dutch company into
Javanese succession politics for the first time and kept it there for two
hundred years.

Makassar's most consequential export turned out to be its own defeated.
