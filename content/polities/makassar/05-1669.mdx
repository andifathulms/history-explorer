Sombaopu, the fortress at Makassar, fell on 12 June 1669. Its walls were four
metres thick and had to be mined rather than breached.

The treaty of Bongaya had been signed twenty months earlier and had not ended the
war. Its terms removed every foreign trader except the Dutch company, stripped
Makassar of its dependencies, required the demolition of its forts, and installed
Arung Palakka's Bone as the paramount power in South Sulawesi. Hasanuddin signed
it, and then went on fighting.

## Whose war it was

`ended.by` is empty because neither the Dutch company nor Bone has a page on this
site, and the emptiness conceals a real argument rather than only a missing
record.

Andaya's book — the source this record is coded from — is written around Arung
Palakka, a Bugis prince from Bone whose people Makassar had conquered and
enserfed, who escaped, spent years in Batavia, and came back with a company fleet
to destroy the polity that had held his country down. His account treats 1669 as
a South Sulawesi war that the company joined, not a colonial conquest that
Sulawesi suffered.

Neither version fits in a list of ids, and this site would need a Bone record
before it could hold even one of them.

## The diaspora

What left Makassar after 1669 mattered more than what stayed.

The Makasar and Bugis who would not accept the settlement went to sea — as
mercenaries, traders, and freebooters, from Siam to Sumatra to the Java Sea — and
they are visible on this site's other pages for the next century.

The Bugis who took power at Riau after 1699, in this corpus's Johor record's last
chapter, came out of this diaspora. So did the Makasar allies of Trunajaya, whose
rebellion in 1677 is the hinge of this corpus's Mataram record and the point at
which the Dutch company became a participant in Javanese succession.

Two records on this site have their turning points set in motion by people who
left this one. It is the clearest case in the archipelago of a polity's most
consequential export being its own defeated.
