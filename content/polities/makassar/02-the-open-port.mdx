The Dutch East India Company's whole strategy in the eastern archipelago was to
be the only buyer. At the production end that meant clove trees cut down and
burned everywhere except the islands the company chose, by expeditions the
company made the sultan of Ternate supply.

Makassar was the hole in it.

The port on the south-west arm of Sulawesi took a toll and asked no questions.
Portuguese expelled from Melaka went there and formed a community of several
thousand. The English had a factory. So did the Danes. Chinese, Gujarati,
Malay, Javanese and Bugis shipping called, and what they bought was largely
cloves and nutmeg from Maluku that had reached Makassar in small boats through
a coastline of islands nobody could patrol.

The company complained about this for fifty years, blockaded it, and eventually
destroyed it.

## Why it could be there

Makassar lived on tolls and on rice, and the rice is why the tolls could be
levied on those terms.

The south-west arm of Sulawesi grows a surplus. A free port has to feed the
people who come to it and the people who run it, and a polity that imports its
food cannot afford to annoy whoever controls the sea. Melaka had been in
exactly that position, buying its rice from Java. Makassar had a hinterland
behind it, so it could take a position and hold it.

## The sea belongs to nobody

The position is on the record because a Dutch envoy wrote it down. Told that
the company had a right to exclude others from the Moluccas trade, the Makassar
court answered that God had made the earth and the sea, had divided the earth
among mankind and given the sea in common, and that it had never been heard
that anyone should be forbidden to sail it.

That is 1615 or thereabouts. Grotius had published *Mare Liberum* in Latin in
Leiden six years earlier, arguing the freedom of the seas against the
Portuguese, on behalf of the same Dutch company — which by the time it reached
Sulawesi was arguing the opposite.

A toll meant something different here from what it meant at Melaka or
Srivijaya. There it was the price of protection on a route the polity
controlled. At Makassar it was the price of admission to a market that was open
on principle — and the principle had been put in writing, to the people who had
come to close it.
