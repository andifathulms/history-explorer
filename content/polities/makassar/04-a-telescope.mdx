Karaeng Pattingalloang was chancellor of Makassar from the 1630s until his
death in 1654, and he read Latin, Spanish and Portuguese.

He had Blaeu's globe. He ordered a telescope from the Netherlands and books to
go with it, corresponded about mathematics and astronomy, kept a library that
visitors described at length, and questioned every European who arrived about
the geography, the politics and the technology of wherever they had come from.
A Jesuit who met him wrote that he could have discussed the fine points of
European science with anyone in Europe.

## What a chancellor was doing with a telescope

It is tempting to file this as a curiosity, and it is not one. Pattingalloang
was the chief minister of a state whose entire position rested on knowing more
than the people it dealt with.

Makassar's argument with the Dutch company was about law, geography and the
limits of a monopoly. Its port handled Portuguese, English, Danish, Chinese,
Gujarati, Malay and Bugis shipping simultaneously, in several languages, under
competing legal claims. Its defences were being redesigned around European
artillery. A chancellor who read three European languages, collected treatises
on fortification, and interrogated every arriving captain about the politics of
wherever he had come from was not indulging a hobby. He was doing the job.

The library is also the clearest available evidence against a habit of mind
that still shapes how this region gets written about — in which European
knowledge arrives and local courts receive it. At Makassar the traffic went the
other way round: the court sent to Europe for the instruments it wanted, chose
the books, and put the questions.
