Chola power never again matched the reach of Rajendra I's own reign,
though it took over a century to visibly fail. Ceylon, always the
hardest conquest to hold from across open water, slipped away for good
around 1070: a Sinhalese prince who had spent years rebuilding his own
strength in the island's interior entered the old capital and ended
Chola rule there for the last time, and the latest Chola inscription
found in Ceylon dates to that same year. The Pandya country, never
reconciled to Chola overlordship in the first place, seized every
period of weak central rule to reassert its own independence, forcing
successive Chola kings into repeated reconquests that held only as
long as the king who won them stayed strong.

## A throne with no one strong enough to hold it

By the reigns of Rajaraja III and Rajendra III in the thirteenth
century, the pattern had become the norm rather than the exception.
Local chieftains contracted mutual defense alliances without reference
to the throne at all — one such compact among three Tanjore-district
lords was sealed in a regnal year of Rajaraja III himself, as though
the king's own dating conventions were the only thing his authority
still reliably supplied. Rajaraja III was captured at one point by a
rebellious feudatory and had to be rescued by the neighboring Hoysalas;
Rajendra III, recognized as heir apparent only after years spent
rebuilding what central authority he could, held together an alliance
with the Hoysalas and the Telugu-Codas that briefly restored a
semblance of Chola standing between roughly 1235 and 1250 — the source
credits him with genuinely reversing the kingdom's decline for a time,
a "restorer of the race of Manu" in his own court's own words.

## A revived rival too strong to outlast

The recovery did not survive contact with a stronger enemy. Jatavarman
Sundara Pandya I, who came to the Pandya throne in 1251, built what the
source calls the second Pandya empire's greatest period, defeating both
the Hoysalas and the Cholas in turn, killing the Hoysala king Someshvara
in battle, and reducing Rajendra III to tribute well before the end of
that decade. Rajendra III and his own ally Vira Ramanatha were finally
defeated outright around 1279 by Sundara Pandya's successor, Maravarman
Kulasekhara. No Chola inscription is known from any later year. A
dynasty that had once sent an army to the Ganges purely to prove it
could, and a fleet to Sumatra to punish a trading rival an ocean away,
ended not in a recorded siege of its own capital but simply in silence
— the record stops, and nothing takes its place under the Chola name.
