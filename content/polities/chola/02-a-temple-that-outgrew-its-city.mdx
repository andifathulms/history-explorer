Rajaraja I came to the throne in 985 after three decades in which
Chola power had drifted without a strong hand at the center, and set
about rebuilding it with a thoroughness his predecessors had not
matched. He restructured land-revenue assessment across the kingdom,
built a centralized administrative staff answering directly to the
throne, established a system of audit over village assemblies without
stripping them of their own autonomy, and raised a standing army and a
navy that would prove decisive within a generation. Taking advantage of
a military revolt that had thrown the Sinhalese court into confusion,
he also opened a Chola invasion of Ceylon that his son would finish.

## A temple built to outlast its builder

Rajaraja's reign is remembered above all for the Rajarajesvara temple
he raised at Tanjore — a Shiva shrine whose central tower rises nearly
two hundred feet on a square base a hundred feet across, still standing
as, in the source's own judgment, the finest specimen of Tamil
architecture at its best. Villages across the empire were assigned
fixed annual contributions of labor and material for its upkeep, and
endowments from the court and its officials funded a permanent
institutional life around it. On the two hundred and seventy-fifth day
of his twenty-fifth regnal year, Rajaraja himself dedicated the
copper finial meant to crown the tower — a king who had, in the words
of a contemporary verse, made Vishnu's own incarnation an insufficient
comparison for his reign.

## A patron whose tolerance crossed his own conquests

Devout in his own worship of Shiva, Rajaraja extended royal favor
without regard to creed. His inscriptions record grants to Vishnu
temples alongside his own great Shaiva foundation, and in his
twenty-first regnal year — around 1006 — he permitted the Sailendra
king of Srivijaya, Sri Mara-vijayottungavarman, to build a Buddhist
monastery, the Chudamani Vihara, at the port of Negapatam, granting it
a village's revenue for its upkeep. The gesture reflected two decades
of profitable, peaceable relations between the Chola court and the
kingdom controlling the Melaka strait — a relationship neither ruler
could have known was approaching its own end.
