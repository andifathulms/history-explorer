Around the middle of the ninth century, a Pallava king facing a Pandya
invasion called on his subordinates for help, among them a minor Kaveri
delta chieftain named Vijayalaya. The Pandya army had already pushed
deep into Chola territory when the Pallava ruler Aparajita rallied his
allies and won a decisive battle nearby. In the campaign's aftermath
Vijayalaya took Tanjore, held until then by a local dynasty called the
Muttaraiyar, and kept it — nominally still fighting for his Pallava
overlord, but building, without either man quite realizing it, the seat
of a kingdom that would outlast the Pallavas entirely.

## A tiger-cub trained to a taste for blood

Little is recorded of Vijayalaya's own reign beyond the bare act of
conquest and a temple he founded to the goddess Nisumbhasudini at his
new capital. His son Aditya I did the rest: within twenty-five years of
Sri Purambiyam he had turned a small principality around Tanjore and
Uraiyur into a kingdom stretching from Madras to Kalahasti, stripped
the Pallavas of most of their remaining territory, and brought the
Ganga power into subordinate alliance. What had begun as a vassal's
opportunism had become, within a single generation, a Pallava
overlord's undoing.

## A forty-eight-year reign that ends in catastrophe

Aditya's son Parantaka I carried the expansion further still,
defeating the Pandya king outright and driving his influence to
Kanyakumari at the peninsula's southern tip, subjugating the Simhalas
and earning the title "Madurai-konda" — captor of Madurai — within
three years of his own accession. But the momentum did not hold. Late
in a reign that had lasted forty-eight years, the Rashtrakuta king
Krishna III invaded from the northwest in overwhelming force; Parantaka's
own eldest son and heir, Rajaditya, was killed in the fighting, and
Parantaka himself did not long survive the disaster. Three decades of
weak and contested rule followed, a gap in Chola history that would not
close until the accession of Rajaraja I in 985.
