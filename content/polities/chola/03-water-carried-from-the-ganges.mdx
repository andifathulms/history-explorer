Rajendra I inherited a war already in progress. By his fifth regnal
year, 1017, his forces had completed the conquest of Ceylon his father
had begun, cornering the Sinhalese king Mahinda V, who had already fled
into the island's interior during an earlier military revolt. Chola
troops seized his crown, his queen, his treasury, and — pointedly — the
Pandya royal regalia that earlier Ceylonese kings had held in trust
after Parantaka I failed to recover it by force himself. Mahinda was
carried to the mainland and lived out his remaining years in Chola
territory while his own kingdom became, for a generation, a Chola
province governed from Polonnaruwa.

## An army sent to fetch a river

Rajendra's most celebrated campaign carried Chola arms further than
any expedition before or after it. Ostensibly launched to punish rulers
in Kalinga and Orissa for backing a rival claimant to the throne of
Vengi, the campaign Rajendra's general led north in 1022 and 1023
developed into a rapid strike through Bengal and beyond, defeating a
string of local kings — Indraratha, Dharmapala, Ranasura, Mahipala among
them — and reaching the Ganges itself. The general met his king back on
the banks of the Godavari carrying water of the sacred river, which
Rajendra used to consecrate a great tank, the Cholaganga, at a new
capital he built to receive it: Gangaikonda Cholapuram, "the city of
the Chola who took the Ganges." The campaign lasted well under two
years and left no territory permanently annexed — its purpose was
demonstration, not conquest, a show of reach meant to be remembered
rather than administered.

## A fleet where no Chola fleet had gone before

Two years after the water of the Ganges reached his new capital,
Rajendra sent a very different kind of expedition in the opposite
direction. In 1025 a Chola fleet crossed the Bay of Bengal and struck
the ports controlling the Melaka strait, Srivijaya's own capital among
them, ending the friendly relations that had let a Sailendra king build
a monastery on Chola soil less than two decades before. Unlike the
Ganges campaign, this was not a demonstration alone: the raid broke
something in Srivijaya's own standing as guarantor of safe passage
through the strait that never fully recovered, though the fleet itself,
like the army that had marched to the Ganges, returned home without
holding what it had struck.
