After 901 the Saffarids stopped being a claimant to Khurasan and went back to
being what they had started as: the power in Sistan. They stayed that, on and
off, for another century, under Samanid and then Ghaznavid overlordship, as the
maliks of Nimruz.

## Two endings, both real

That is why the dynasty has two ending dates instead of one, and why they are a
hundred and two years apart.

The imperial Saffarid state — the thing that took Nishapur, frightened Baghdad
and marched on Iraq — was finished at Balkh in 900 and buried when the caliph
handed Khurasan to the Samanids. Nothing after that has any resemblance to it.
The family, on the other hand, went on governing the Helmand basin from Zaranj
until Mahmud of Ghazna deposed Khalaf ibn Ahmad in 1003.

Both statements are true about different objects, and it is not obvious that
either has a better claim to be the ending. The first is the end of a state; the
second is the end of a family holding a province.

## What that says about how a dynasty stops

The Saffarids are unusual in having the distinction so cleanly separated, but
they are not unusual in having it.

Very few dynasties stop the way a battle stops. Most of them stop being one
thing and go on being a smaller thing, sometimes for a very long time — a
sovereign becomes a governor, a governor becomes a landholder, a landholder
becomes a family that used to matter. What makes the case here legible is the
gap: an empire that lasted forty years, followed by a hundred years of the same
family in the same province, doing something that would never have been worth
recording if the forty years had not happened.

Ya'qub's whole career was an argument that a man with an army needs nobody's
permission. His descendants held Sistan for a century on somebody else's
permission, which is the answer his brother had given him, and which was
correct.
