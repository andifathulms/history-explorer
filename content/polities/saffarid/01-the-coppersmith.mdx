Ya'qub ibn al-Layth was a *saffar*, a coppersmith, in Sistan. The nickname
stuck to the dynasty, which is unusual — most dynasties are named for an
ancestor or a place, not a trade — and it stuck because it was accurate and
because his enemies found it useful.

Sistan in the 850s was held by the *ayyars*, irregular volunteer bands who had
grown out of frontier warfare against the Kharijite rebels and the pagan
territories to the east. Ya'qub rose through these. By 861 he had control of
Zarang, and from there he did something that is easy to state and was
genuinely new in the region: he built a standing army out of the frontier
irregulars, paid it himself, and went looking for provinces.

He did not seek an appointment first. There is a well-known exchange, recorded
in the Persian *Tarikh-i Sistan*, in which a caliphal envoy arrives with a
document of investiture and Ya'qub shows him a sword and a plate of onions and
coarse bread, and says roughly: this is my food, and if I lose I go back to it,
and this is how I got what I have. Whether or not it happened, it is the right
story for what he did.

Every other polity in this thread acquired legitimacy first and power
afterwards, or acquired both at once. The Saffarids are the case that ran the
sequence backwards, and it is worth watching what that cost them.
