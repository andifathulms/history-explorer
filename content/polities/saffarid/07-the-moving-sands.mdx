The capital outlived the dynasty by nearly four hundred years, and the
geographers who describe it are describing a place unlike anywhere else they
went.

## A city built against the wind

Zaranj sat at the edge of the Helmand delta with salt marshes and date palms on
one side and desert on the other, and the wind there never stopped. It blew
continually and violently, moving the sand about in a way the Arabic writers
found genuinely dangerous — whole villages overwhelmed, cultivated districts
buried. Ibn Hawkal gives a long account of how in the 970s the Great Mosque of
Zaranj was choked with sand.

What the inhabitants did about the wind is the detail worth keeping. They used
it. Sistan had windmills, which Le Strange notes were a feature peculiar to that
country: the same ceaseless wind that was burying their fields turned their
grain. Later a great dyke was thrown up to hold the sand off the city, which
Mustawfi says was originally built by an ancient king and restored by another,
though the names he gives belong to legend rather than to a reign.

Muqaddasi, in the tenth century, adds the riches and the learning of the
inhabitants, the strongly fortified castle, and the two famous minarets of the
Great Mosque.

One of the two had been built by Ya'qub the Coppersmith.

## Which is a strange thing to be left

Consider what that means about the sources. Ya'qub is remembered in the Iraqi
chronicles as a brigand who terrified the caliphate, and in his own province's
history as a hero; and the item of his that a Palestinian geographer thought
worth recording a century after his death is a minaret on a mosque.

He put it up in a city he had made the capital of an empire, and it stood there
through the Samanid overlordship and the Ghaznavid one and the Seljuks, and it
was still standing when Chinggis Khan sent his armies into Sistan in 1222 and,
unusually, the capital came through more or less intact — under a Mongol
governor afterwards, and described by Mustawfi in the fourteenth century as
still very flourishing, with excellent fruit in gardens watered from a canal off
the Helmand.

## Timur

In 1383 Timur came to Sistan and took the fortress of Zarah. Then he came to
the city, which by then was simply called Shahr-i Sistan.

It closed its gates and refused to surrender. After a short siege it was taken
by storm, every inhabitant who could be found was killed, the walls were razed
and the houses destroyed.

Le Strange's sentence is the last word on it: since that time Zaranj has come to
be a nameless ruin.

So the sequence runs: a coppersmith from a frontier province takes an empire and
loses it, his family holds the province for another century, the city he built
in outlasts them by four hundred years and survives the Mongols, and then a
different conqueror arrives and there is nothing on the map at all. The wind is
presumably still blowing.
