Sistan is the basin of the Helmand, on the edge of the Iranian world, and in
the ninth century it was the kind of province a caliphate holds on paper.

<Figure id="chah-nimeh-reservoir" /> It
had a governor and it had a frontier, and between the two it had something the
settled provinces did not: a permanent condition of low-level war, and a social
form that had grown out of it.

The *ayyars* are hard to translate and worth not translating. They were bands
of armed young men — volunteers, irregulars, in the hostile sources brigands
and in the friendly ones a brotherhood with a code of loyalty, hospitality and
protection of the weak. They existed across the Iranian world in this period,
in the towns as well as the frontier districts, and what made Sistan's
different was that there was constant fighting available. The Kharijites had
been established in the region for two centuries, and beyond it lay territory
that was still not Muslim; a man with a following and no prospects could find
employment.

Ya'qub came up through that. So did everyone he commanded.

What he did with that material is the reason anybody remembers him. He took
a
form of organisation that was episodic, local and unpaid — a band follows a
leader while the leader is winning — and made it permanent: a standing force
that he paid himself, out of what it took, on a schedule. When he beat the
Kharijites in Badghis he did not disperse the survivors; he enrolled them as a
formed contingent in his own army, the *jaysh al-shurat*, the army of the
Kharijites, which then fought for him.

The result was not a mercenary army, and the distinction matters. Mercenaries
can leave. What Ya'qub built was an army with no
other employer, no tribal home to return to, and no land, whose entire
existence was the campaign — and it is the reason a coppersmith from the far
edge of the empire could take Nishapur inside twelve years and then turn west
towards Baghdad.
