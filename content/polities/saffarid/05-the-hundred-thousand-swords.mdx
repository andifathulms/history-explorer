---
title: A hundred thousand swords
drafted_from: ibn-khallikan
phase: contraction
---

Amr asked for Transoxiana. It was already held, competently and quietly, by
Isma'il ibn Ahmad the Samanid, and the caliph's grant of it was therefore a
licence to go and take it rather than a province delivered. Amr understood this
perfectly well at the moment he received the document.

Ibn Khallikan preserves the scene, from a historian of Khurasan writing not long
after. The envoy came to Nishapur with the diploma and a set of presents Amr had
already refused once, among them seven robes of honour. He put them on Amr one
at a time, and at each robe Amr prostrated himself and prayed two prostrations.
Then the diploma was handed over and Amr asked what it was. This, said the
envoy, is the thing you asked for. Amr answered: what use is it to me? Isma'il
ibn Ahmad will not give up that province unless I enforce the demand with a
hundred thousand swords. You asked for it yourself, said the envoy; now get
ready to go and govern in his place. Amr took the diploma, kissed it, set it
down, sent the embassy seven hundred thousand dirhams, and began raising an
army.

Isma'il wrote to him before it came to anything. You have been given the
government of a vast country and I hold Transoxiana; be content with what you
have and leave me this frontier province. Amr refused. Someone raised the Oxus
and how hard it would be to cross, and he said that if he chose to dam it with
bags of money and walk over on them he could.

The first army he sent did not reach the river. Isma'il crossed it instead and
destroyed the force Amr had given to Muhammad ibn Bishr, in October 899, while
Amr himself sat at Nishapur. There is a story attached that is really a comment:
on the eve of the battle a Sistani officer found Ibn Bishr having his head
shaved and asked whether he had got Isma'il's permission to do it, meaning that
by fighting Isma'il at all he had already made the man master of his head. Ibn
Bishr cursed him out of the tent. The next morning he was routed, taken, and
beheaded, and Isma'il — who had to ask the prisoners to identify which head was
the commander's — heard the remark repeated and was unsettled by it.

Amr went north himself the following spring and posted himself at Balkh. Isma'il
called in every landholder under his authority, crossed to the western bank, and
occupied the country round about instead of offering battle; Amr, finding
himself half blockaded, is said to have regretted the whole enterprise and asked
for a truce, which was refused. Almost no serious fighting took place. On the
morning of the battle Amr learned that his own secretary had gone over to
Isma'il in the night, taking a general and a large body of troops with him, and
that most of his partisans had followed. He lost his nerve and rode. Told there
was a short cut through a jungle, he sent the main body by the highway and went
into the wood with a small escort; his horse got into marshy ground and fell,
his companions rode on without noticing, and Isma'il's men came up and found him
sitting on a horse that could not move.

The authorities do not agree on the day — one gives 20 March 900, another 21
April — and Ibn Khallikan reports both rather than choose. On the rest they
agree. He was put in irons and taken to Samarkand, and Isma'il let him choose
between staying there as his prisoner and being sent to the Commander of the
Faithful. He chose Baghdad, and reached it on 23 April 901, ahead of the robes,
the standard and the diadem that were travelling the other way to Isma'il.
