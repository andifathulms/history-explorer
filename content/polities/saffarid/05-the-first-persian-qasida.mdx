---
title: Why do you recite what I cannot understand
drafted_from: bosworth-1994
phase: aside

Ya'qub took Herat in 867, and his poets did what poets did: composed an ode in
Arabic celebrating the conquest and recited it to him. He is reported to have
asked why he was being read something he could not understand, and his
secretary, Muhammad ibn Wasif of Sistan, went away and produced the verses in
Persian instead.

That poem is conventionally the first New Persian court *qasida* — the
beginning, in the tradition's own account of itself, of a literature that runs
through Rudaki, Firdawsi, Nizami, Hafiz and everything after.

Take the anecdote at the level it deserves. It is almost certainly tidied. What
it records accurately is a condition: a ruler at the head of a Persian-speaking
army from a frontier province, with no Arabic education and no interest in
acquiring one, at whose court Arabic was therefore useless. Persian court
poetry began because the patron could not follow the alternative.

Set the three holders of Khurasan side by side and the sequence is not a
progress. This site's Tahirid page has a family of Persian governors who
cultivated Arabic assiduously, because their position depended on being the
caliph's most reliable servants. The Saffarids had no such position to protect
and no such education, and got the vernacular by default. The Samanids, a
century later, turned it into a programme — commissioning, paying, building a
library. What looks in retrospect like a Persian revival unfolding in three
stages was one dynasty avoiding a language it needed, one dynasty unable to use
it, and one dynasty deciding it was worth money.

The Saffarids also have something almost nothing else in this corpus has: a
local chronicle. The *Tarikh-i Sistan* is an anonymous history of the province,
written in Persian, by someone from it, and it is where the sword and the
onions come from. Every other account of Ya'qub in circulation was written in
Iraq by men who regarded him as a bandit who had frightened the caliph. The
Sistani version is not neutral either — it is proud of him — but it is a second
voice, and this corpus has repeatedly had to say that a polity's record was
written entirely by its enemies. Here, unusually, it was not.
