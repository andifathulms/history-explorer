title: The hostage
drafted_from: perry-1979

When Muhammad Hasan Khan Qajar was killed in 1759, Karim Khan took his son to
Shiraz. This was normal practice: the heir of a defeated rival was kept at court,
treated well, given an allowance and a position, and thereby prevented from
becoming the focus of a revolt. Agha Muhammad Khan lived in Shiraz for sixteen
years on those terms.

He had been castrated as a small child, on the orders of Adil Shah Afshar, in
the round of mutilations that followed Nadir's murder. He was therefore
disqualified from ruling by every convention his world recognised, and the
disqualification is part of why Karim Khan felt safe keeping him.

What he did in Shiraz was watch. He was in the presence of a functioning
government for sixteen years, in the household of the man who had beaten his
father, learning how a tribal leader with no legitimacy held Iran together — the
alliances, the tax policy, the treatment of defeated enemies, the deliberate
refusal of the title.

He drew the opposite conclusion from all of it. When Karim Khan died in 1779 he
left Shiraz within days on a hunting excuse, reached Mazandaran, raised the
Qajars, and spent the next fifteen years taking the country with a cruelty that
is remarkable even by the standards of this region's eighteenth century. He
hunted the last Zand across Iran for four years. He blinded twenty thousand
people at Kerman, by the standard account, for having sheltered him. He took the
title of shah in 1796 in a coronation that deliberately invoked the Safavids and
Nadir at once, and he had himself crowned with Nadir's jewels.

The Zand state and the Qajar state are therefore one story told twice, and the
second one begins in the first one's palace, with a boy watching.

It is also as clean a demonstration as exists that the succession problem these
governments kept failing at was not a technical difficulty to be solved by a
better rule. Karim Khan governed better than any of his neighbours for thirty
years, and the arrangement did not survive him by a fortnight, because the thing
holding it together was him.
