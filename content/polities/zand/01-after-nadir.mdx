Nadir was killed in June 1747 and Iran spent the next twelve years being fought
over by his officers, his relatives, and every tribal leader with enough men to
try. The country had been at war more or less continuously since 1722, had been
taxed to the edge of destruction to pay for the army that had won the wars, and
had lost a substantial part of its population to invasion, famine and flight.

Three men mattered. Azad Khan, an Afghan commander of Nadir's, held Azerbaijan.
Muhammad Hasan Khan Qajar held the Caspian provinces with the Qajar tribe behind
him and was the most dangerous. And Karim Khan, leader of the Zand — a Lak tribe
of the central Zagros, obscure before this and never numerous — held the centre
and the south.

Karim Khan won, and Perry's account of how is not a story of battles. He was
beaten repeatedly, twice very badly, and kept coming back because he was better
than his rivals at the two things that actually decided it: keeping the
allegiance of tribal leaders who had no reason to be loyal, and not destroying
the country he was fighting over. He treated captured enemies well and
conspicuously. He did not sack cities that submitted. Where his rivals extracted,
he taxed lightly and made a policy of it, and the result was that after a decade
the people of the disputed provinces preferred him.

In 1759 Muhammad Hasan Khan was killed by his own men. By 1762 Azad Khan had
submitted and been pensioned. Karim Khan was the government of Iran, and had
been fighting for it for twelve years.

He then did something none of his predecessors in this region had done, which
was to stop.
