title: The wars he did fight

Edward Scott Waring, who spent a year in Shiraz in 1802 and wrote the first
connected English account of the Zands, begins by explaining what is wrong with
both of his sources, and the explanation is worth having before the narrative.

He worked from two Persian manuscripts. One was a history written by a Shirazi
covering the period from Karim Khan to the capture and death of Lutf Ali Khan;
the other was by a man who had been secretary to a grandson of Karim Khan's
brother and was an actor in a good deal of what he relates. Waring's warning is
even-handed and blunt. A historian living under a despotic monarchy, writing up
the successes of the family that currently holds the sceptre, will in many cases
be guilty of unpardonable flattery and wilful falsehood; and a historian
attached to the interests of Karim Khan's family may naturally be suspected of
attributing to fortune what was in fact the result of provident wisdom. His
method, he says, was to temper the animosity of the second with the pliancy of
the first.

What the two of them agree on is the order of the conquest. Karim Khan
established his government in Azerbaijan, Iraq-i Ajam, Rasht and Gilan — the
northern and western provinces, taken piecemeal over more than a decade of the
fighting — and then marched south to Shiraz accompanied by the principal
chieftains and men of consequence of the subdued provinces, which is the
arrangement itself: the leaders of every province he had taken came with him and
stayed where he could see them. He entered the city on 11 Safar 1189.

The southern and eastern campaigns he delegated. His troops under Sadiq Khan,
Nazar Ali Khan and Muhammad Khan Zand reduced Kerman and Sistan, which were
being held by the inhabitants and a body of Afghans, and brought in several other
places that had refused his authority. And then, in Waring's single sentence for
the one war outside Iran, the important city of Basra was added as an appendage
to the Persian government after a long and bloody siege.

That is the whole of it, and the brevity is not an accident of the source. Karim
Khan fought for twelve years to get the country and then spent the next fourteen
not expanding it. Waring's manuscripts have him at Shiraz devoting his time to
securing the tranquillity and independence of the government, founding colleges
and baths, and putting up buildings that would add to the comfort and ease of the
inhabitants; and the Vakil's bazaar, he adds, will hand down his memory to an
admiring and grateful posterity. The fort he built round the city took Agha
Muhammad Khan infinite labour and expense to destroy, which Waring thinks the
man enjoyed.

The judgement Waring reports is uniform and he says he will not dispute it: under
the Vakil anarchy was subdued, the country was cultivated, an excellent police
was established in every city, justice was administered to the meanest
individual, and the ear of the Vakil was open to the most trivial complaint. The
inhabitants of Shiraz, he says, enjoyed thirteen years of the greatest security
any of them had known.

The fourteenth year is where his manuscript changes register. The cup-bearer saw
that his days were completed, and filling the bowl of his existence with the
bitter wine of death, he was attacked with a violent and inveterate distemper;
and towards the morning of 13 Safar 1193 he delivered up his soul to the giver of
life.
