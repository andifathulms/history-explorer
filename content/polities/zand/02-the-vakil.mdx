title: Vakil

Karim Khan never called himself shah. For twenty-eight years he ruled as
*vakil* — deputy, agent, advocate — nominally on behalf of Isma'il III, an
infant Safavid he had installed and then kept in comfortable confinement for the
rest of his life. And in his own formulation the deputyship was not only of the
dynasty: *vakil al-ra'aya*, deputy of the subjects, a title borrowed from the
official who in Safavid practice represented a city's population to its
governor.

Whether that was modesty, calculation, or a piece of political theatre is
argued about, and Perry's answer is that it does not have to be one of them.
Safavid legitimacy was still the only legitimacy the country recognised — Nadir
had spent eleven years demonstrating that no amount of victory substituted for
it — and a Lak tribal leader with no descent and no claim was better off
governing in the name of a child than being one more usurper. That it also
happened to be true to how he governed made it more effective, not less.

This site's `legitimation` field reads `conquest`, which is accurate and
uninformative, and the comment on the record does the work. It is the fourth
polity in this corpus whose ruler's public ground was an authority he held on
somebody's behalf rather than claimed for himself: the Jafnid phylarchs holding
Roman rank, Timur ruling beside a Chinggisid puppet, Hasan Buzurg doing the
same with Ilkhanid ones, and now a vakil for a Safavid infant. The vocabulary
has one value for this arrangement, `caliphal-investiture`, and it applies only
when the sovereign being deputised for is a caliph.

The practical consequence of refusing the title was that he also refused the
programme. A shah is expected to reconquer the Caucasus, fight the Ottomans and
restore the empire's frontiers. A vakil is not. Karim Khan fought one foreign
war, against the Ottomans for Basra in 1775, and it was about trade routes.
