In 1786 an English officer named William Francklin travelled from Bengal to
Persia, spent eight months at Shiraz living in a Persian household, and wrote
down what he saw. He arrived seven years after Karim Khan's death, in the middle
of the wars that were dismantling everything the man had built, and his phrase
for the ruler of the city is the whole chapter.

He calls Ja'far Khan *the present possessor of Shiraz*.

## Ten years of cousins

Francklin sets out how it happened, and the sequence is depressingly simple.

After Karim Khan's death Sadiq Khan openly assumed the government. As soon as
the news reached Ali Murad Khan at Isfahan, that lord instantly rebelled —
deeming himself to have an equal right to the government, Francklin says, *as in
fact he had* — and declared himself a competitor for the empire. Persia was by
this means again involved in all the horrors of a civil war.

Ali Murad eventually brought about twelve thousand men to Shiraz and besieged
it. He had no artillery. The city had a most excellent fosse, a good wall and
plenty of provisions, and he found the siege much harder than he had expected.

That is the Zand state after 1779: two members of one family, each with a real
claim, taking turns to besiege the capital their uncle had fortified.

## What the fortifications were for

Francklin describes them with a soldier's eye, and everything he admires is
Karim Khan's work.

A wall right round the city, twenty-five feet high and ten thick, with round
towers every eighty paces. A dry ditch sixty feet deep and twenty broad, which
he thought would by itself let the place hold out a long time against any power
in Persia — where, he adds, artillery is little known and less used. Six gates,
one of them the Darvaza-yi Sa'di, named for leading to the poet's tomb. And the
citadel, the Ark, also Karim Khan's, where Ja'far Khan lived and which also
served occasionally as a state prison.

At the door of the Ark was a painting of Rustam fighting the White Demon out of
the *Shahnama*, full length and, Francklin thought, ill-proportioned. Opposite,
in a large handsome square, a gallery where the khan's trumpets and kettledrums
played at sunrise and sunset.

A man who had refused to be called shah built a wall his nephews could not
take, and his family spent a decade proving it.

## The one who got away

Francklin also records, in the flat manner of a man who does not know what it
will come to, where the other player was.

Agha Muhammad Khan, son of Husayn Khan Qajar, found means to escape from Shiraz
on the night following Karim Khan's death, fled north, collected troops, and
soon made himself master of Mazandaran and Gilan — being proclaimed at about the
same time as Ali Murad Khan set up in the south.

Francklin published in 1790. He did not know that the escaped hostage would take
Shiraz four years later, and that the wall, the ditch and the drums would go to
the man who had spent sixteen years inside them as a guest.
