He settled on Shiraz in 1765 and rebuilt it: a citadel, a walled bazaar that is
still the city's bazaar, a mosque, a bath, a garden, new walls and a moat. It is
a modest programme against Abbas I's Isfahan, and the comparison is the point —
this was a government spending on the town it governed from rather than on a
statement.

<Figure id="arg-karim-khan" />

The economic policy was of a piece. Taxes were set low and collected without the
exactions of the Nadir years; internal tolls were reduced; merchants were
protected and, in several documented cases, compensated by the government for
losses. He worked to bring the Gulf trade back through Iranian ports, gave the
English East India Company a factory at Bushehr in 1763, and in 1775 sent an army
to take Basra from the Ottomans, holding it for four years — a campaign about
customs revenue and the routing of the India trade rather than about territory,
and he gave the city back.

<Figure id="vakil-mosque" />

None of this made Iran a power again. The Zand state held the centre and the
south; Khurasan was the blind Afsharid's, Azerbaijan was intermittent, the
Caspian provinces were Qajar country he never fully subdued, and the Caucasus
was gone. Karim Khan is not remembered because he restored an empire.

He is here because of what the thirty years were like to live in, which the
sources are unusually consistent about, and which is rare enough anywhere
to be worth stating plainly. He is the ruler Iranians remember without
qualification: not the greatest, not the most successful, but the one under whom
the country was not being destroyed by its own government. Perry's book takes
that reputation seriously as evidence rather than as sentiment, and concludes
that it is substantially deserved.

He died in 1779, in his seventies, of natural causes, in bed, which almost
nobody else in this region's six centuries managed.
