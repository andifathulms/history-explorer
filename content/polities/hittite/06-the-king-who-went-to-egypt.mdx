Thirteen years after the treaty, the Hittite king travelled to Egypt in person to
give his eldest daughter to Ramesses II. He brought rich gifts and a military
escort, and the escort mixed with Egyptian troops who had fought them on the
Syrian plains.

It is the only occasion in the whole of the Bronze Age when the ruler of one
great power went to the capital of another. And the Egyptian record of it is a
lesson in how a peace between equals gets published.

## What the treaty had actually said

Breasted is careful about the terms, and the care matters.

In the treaty itself the Hittite king is recognised as *on an equality with the
Pharaoh* and receives the same conditions. Neither party is superior. The
boundary in Syria is one both sides recognised, and the cuneiform tablets from
Boghazköy show that the Hittites went on controlling Amurru on the upper Orontes
afterwards — so Ramesses had not permanently advanced his father's frontier
inland, whatever the temple walls say.

He nevertheless spent the rest of his reign styling himself the conqueror of the
Hittites.

The peace held. It cost Ramesses his ambitions in Asia and it suited both sides,
and it lasted through his very long reign and into his successor's.

## The visit, as Egypt wrote it

The princess was given an Egyptian name, Matnefrure — *Who Sees the Beauty of
Re* — and took a prominent position at court. The visit was carved on the front
of the temple at Abu Simbel with narrative inscriptions, and she was given a
statue beside her husband at Tanis.

Then the court poets went to work, and the reframing is total.

They had the Hittite king write to the king of Kode summoning him to come along
so that they might do honour to Pharaoh. They had the god Ptah explain to
Ramesses what had really happened:

> I have made the land of Kheta into subjects of thy palace; I have put it into
> their hearts to present themselves with fearful steps before thee bearing
> their impost, which their chiefs have captured, all their possessions as
> tribute to the fame of his majesty. His eldest daughter is in front thereof to
> satisfy the heart of the Lord of the Two Lands.

A sovereign arriving to conclude a dynastic marriage with an equal becomes a
vassal delivering tribute, with his daughter carried at the head of it as one
more item of the impost.

## Both readings survive

What makes this the most useful episode on the record is that we can check it,
which is almost never true of Egyptian royal claims.

The treaty exists in two versions — the Egyptian one on temple walls at Karnak
and the Ramesseum, and the Hittite one on tablets from the Hittite capital — and
they agree on the substance while differing in exactly the way you would expect:
each side's copy is drafted from its own side. The Hittites did not think they
had submitted. The tablets show them still holding the territory the Egyptian
account implies they surrendered.

So the Abu Simbel version is not a lie about a fact anyone could check at the
time; it is a claim addressed to Egyptians, in a language of kingship where a
foreign ruler approaching Pharaoh can only be approaching as a subject. There
was no grammar available for a peer.

## The story that came afterwards

The marriage also produced a folk-tale, and it did what folk-tales do.

Long afterwards — the surviving text is from Greek times — a story circulated
that the Hittite princess's father had asked for an image of Theban Khonsu to be
sent to his country, so that the god could drive the evil spirits out of her
afflicted sister. The land is called Bekhten. Breasted thinks something of the
kind may well have happened during the dealings between the two kings.

An Egyptian god travelling to Anatolia to perform an exorcism at a foreign
king's request is, in its own way, the accurate version. What actually crossed
between the two courts in these decades was not tribute. It was daughters,
doctors, gifts, and the confidence to ask.
