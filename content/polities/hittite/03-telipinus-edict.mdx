Around 1500 BC a king named Telipinu, who had come to the throne by deposing his
brother-in-law and who had had that brother-in-law's family killed, published a
document. It opens with a history: it names his predecessors in order and it
records, without varnish, who murdered whom. Hantili killed Mursili. Zidanta
killed Hantili's heir. Ammuna killed Zidanta. Huzziya would have killed Telipinu
and Telipinu did not kill him, and then somebody did, and Telipinu says he
punished them.

Then it legislates. A prince of the first rank shall be king. If there is no
prince of the first rank, one of the second rank. If there is no son at all, then
a first-rank daughter's husband shall be taken as king. Princes are not to be
killed; a king who kills his kin is to be tried by the panku, the assembly of the
great men, and the panku is instructed in so many words that if the king does
this it must speak.

## Why this record's succession field is coded from a law

The `succession_rule` field on this page reads `nomination`, and this document is
why. The rule is designation by the reigning king from a defined class of sons —
the king named a *tuhkanti*, a crown prince, and the edict fixed who was eligible
to be named.

That is the earliest place in this corpus where the field is filled from a
written law rather than inferred from a pattern of what actually happened. The
Assyrian record, eight hundred years later, has Esarhaddon's succession oath of
672, which this site calls the most elaborate succession instrument in the corpus
— an oath sworn by every subject and vassal, drafted at length, surviving in
copies from Nimrud and Tell Tayinat. The Hittite edict is earlier, shorter, and
doing a different job: Esarhaddon's oath binds everyone to a named heir, and
Telipinu's edict binds the king.

Both failed in the same way. Esarhaddon's oath did not prevent a civil war
between the two sons it was written to protect. Telipinu's edict did not prevent
usurpation either; the great Suppiluliuma I, on this record as its peak ruler,
took the throne by killing the designated heir, and Hattusili III, who signs the
treaty in the fifth chapter, took it from his nephew and spent his reign writing
a book defending the fact.

## The panku

The clause giving an assembly standing to try a king is unusual enough to be
worth resisting the temptation to over-read. It has attracted a good deal of
enthusiasm about constitutional monarchy on the Anatolian plateau, and Bryce is
sceptical: the panku is a body of royal kinsmen and great officers, not an
institution independent of the palace, and it does not appear later as a check on
anything. This site codes `legitimation` as `divine-sanction, descent` and does
not code an elective element, because the edict constrains who may be designated
and does not transfer the designating.

What the edict did do was work for a while. The killing largely stopped, the
succession became boring, and the kingdom spent the next century and a half
recovering the territory that the century of murders had lost.
