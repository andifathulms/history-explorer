Around 1500 BC a king named Telipinu, who had come to the throne by deposing
his brother-in-law and who had had that brother-in-law's family killed,
published a document. It opens with a history: it names his predecessors in
order and it records, without varnish, who murdered whom. Hantili killed
Mursili. Zidanta killed Hantili's heir. Ammuna killed Zidanta. Huzziya would
have killed Telipinu and Telipinu did not kill him, and then somebody did, and
Telipinu says he punished them.

Then it legislates. A prince of the first rank shall be king. If there is no
prince of the first rank, one of the second rank. If there is no son at all,
then a first-rank daughter's husband shall be taken as king. Princes are not to
be killed; a king who kills his kin is to be tried by the panku, the assembly
of the great men, and the panku is instructed in so many words that if the king
does this it must speak.

## A rule, written down

What the edict establishes is designation: the king names a *tuhkanti*, a crown
prince, from a defined class of sons, and the edict fixes who is eligible to be
named.

That makes it the earliest surviving case anywhere of a succession rule set out
as law rather than inferred from what happened to happen. The nearest
comparison is eight hundred years later — Esarhaddon's succession oath of 672,
sworn by every subject and vassal, drafted at enormous length, surviving in
copies from Nimrud and Tell Tayinat. The Hittite edict is earlier, shorter, and
doing a different job: Esarhaddon's oath binds everyone to a named heir, and
Telipinu's edict binds the king.

Both failed in the same way. Esarhaddon's oath did not prevent a civil war
between the two sons it was written to protect. Telipinu's edict did not
prevent usurpation either. Suppiluliuma I, the greatest of these kings, took
the throne by killing the designated heir; Hattusili III, who signed the treaty
with Egypt, took it from his nephew and spent his reign writing a book
defending the fact.

## The panku

The clause giving an assembly standing to try a king is unusual enough to be
worth resisting the temptation to over-read. It has attracted a good deal of
enthusiasm about constitutional monarchy on the Anatolian plateau, and Bryce is
sceptical: the panku is a body of royal kinsmen and great officers, not an
institution independent of the palace, and it does not appear later as a check
on anything. The edict constrains who may be designated; it does not move the
power of designating anywhere else.

What the edict did do was work for a while. The killing largely stopped, the
succession became boring, and the kingdom spent the next century and a half
recovering the territory that the century of murders had lost.
