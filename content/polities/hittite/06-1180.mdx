Hattusa was burned and abandoned around 1180 BC and the Hittite state ceased to
exist. Nobody says who did it.

That is the whole difficulty of this record's `ended` field, and it is worth
setting out what is actually absent. There is no victory inscription. No
chronicle entry anywhere in the Near East records the fall. No later power claims
to have done it. Assyria, which had beaten Hittite armies in the field and would
have had every reason to boast, does not. The archaeology shows the city
systematically emptied — the archives and cult statues removed before the fire,
which suggests an evacuation rather than a surprise — and then burned, and then
squatted in by a much smaller population.

The field reads `fragmentation`, `by: []`. It is not `conquest`, because no
conqueror is nameable. It is not `still contested`, because that value would
describe the state of the scholarship rather than the state of the polity, and
this site's vocabularies are meant to describe events. What happened is that the
central state came apart and its Syrian branches carried on, which is what
`fragmentation` means.

## The last letters

The final documents from the region are unusually vivid for something so poorly
understood. From Ugarit, a Hittite vassal on the Syrian coast, come letters found
in a kiln — baked by the fire that destroyed the city before they could be sent.
One is from the last Hittite king or his officials demanding grain and stating
that it is a matter of life or death. Another, from Ammurapi of Ugarit, reports
that enemy ships have appeared, that his own fleet is away in Lycia and his army
in Hatti, and that the country is undefended.

Grain being shipped from Egypt and Syria to Anatolia in quantity, in the last
years, is one of the few concrete things the record supplies. Whether the driver
was drought, or the loss of the Syrian revenues, or the Kaska, or the general
dislocation that took down Mycenae and Ugarit and the Egyptian Levant in the same
half-century, is exactly the argument this site is not equipped to adjudicate and
will not pretend to.

## What Egypt says

The Egyptian record on this site closes with the same collapse from the southern
end, and the Egyptian sources are the ones that name an agent: Ramesses III's
inscriptions at Medinet Habu describe a coalition of peoples moving by land and
sea, and say that no land stood before their arms, listing Hatti among the fallen.

That is a claim made by the surviving power about its neighbours' destruction,
twenty-five years after the fact, in a triumphal inscription about a battle it
won. It is evidence and it is not a citation this site will convert into an
`ended.by`. The Sea Peoples as a single organised agent of collapse is a
nineteenth-century synthesis built largely on that one text, and hard rule 2's
spirit — do not manufacture a figure by combining sources — applies to causes as
well as to numbers.

## What continued

Carchemish. Piyassili's line went on ruling there, and at Melid, Tabal, Karkemish
and half a dozen other places in northern Syria and south-eastern Anatolia, small
kingdoms kept the Hittite royal titles, the Luwian language, the Anatolian
hieroglyphs and the name. The Assyrians called that whole country Hatti for the
next four centuries; so does the Hebrew Bible. This site's Assyrian record's
campaigns west are, in Assyrian terms, campaigns against Hatti, three hundred
years after the state on this page ended.

The `successor_claims` count of 1 on this record is that. The kings of Carchemish
styled themselves Great King, publicly and in monumental inscriptions, and coding
rule 1 counts titulature asserted whether or not anybody accepted it. One claim,
counted once for one claiming polity, though several courts shared the style.
