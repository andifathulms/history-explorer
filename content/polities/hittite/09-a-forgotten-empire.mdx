An empire of four centuries, with an archive of thirty thousand tablets, a
capital of a hundred and eighty hectares and a peace treaty with Egypt carved
on a temple wall, vanished so completely that its existence had to be argued
for.

For most of the nineteenth century the Hittites were a handful of verses.
Abraham buys a burial cave from Ephron the Hittite. Esau marries two Hittite
women. A traitor flees into the land of the Hittites and builds a city there.
Solomon's merchants sell Egyptian horses to the kings of the Hittites and the
kings of Syria. And in Second Kings, the Syrians besieging Samaria panic in the
night because they think the king of Israel has hired the kings of the Hittites
and the kings of Egypt against them.

## The scholar who was sure

Sayce opens his 1888 book on that last passage, and he quotes a distinguished
critic of forty years earlier who had picked it out as an example of the
Bible's unreliability. Its *unhistorical tone*, the man had written, is too
manifest to allow of easy belief. No Hittite kings can have compared in power
with the king of Judah, the real and near ally, who is not named at all; nor is
there a single mark of acquaintance with the contemporaneous history.

Sayce's answer is one line long, and it has aged well: recent discoveries have
retorted the critic's objections upon himself. It is not the biblical writer
but the modern author who is now proved to have been unacquainted with the
history of the time.

## What he had to work with

It is worth being exact about what Sayce could and could not know when he wrote
that, because it is the measure of how completely a great power can disappear.

Boğazköy had not been excavated; the digging began in 1906. The archive had not
been found. The language had not been deciphered; Hrozný did that in 1915.
Nobody knew where the capital was, what the kings were called, how long the
empire had lasted or that it had ever taken Babylon.

What existed was the biblical verses, the Egyptian inscriptions of Ramesses II
about the war and the treaty, Assyrian references to a country called Hatti in
northern Syria, and a scatter of monuments and undeciphered inscriptions in
Anatolia and Syria that Sayce and a few others had argued belonged to one lost
people.

From that, he wrote a book called *The Story of a Forgotten Empire*, and he was
right about the main thing.

## Two ways to disappear

The distinction the book turns on is worth keeping.

The Hittites were forgotten as a state and remembered as a name. The Assyrians
went on calling all of northern Syria Hatti for four centuries after Hattusa
burned. The Hebrew scriptures preserved the word without any idea what had
stood behind it. The successor kingdoms at Carchemish and Malatya kept the
titles and the language and were eventually absorbed by Assyria, and after that
nobody anywhere had any reason to remember that a great power had governed the
Anatolian plateau from a cursed and waterless rock.

Everything now known about the Hittites has been recovered from the ground
since 1906, in a century and a bit. What preserved the name in the interval was
a handful of verses in another people's holy book, describing them as
neighbours, horse-buyers and a threat worth panicking about in the dark.
