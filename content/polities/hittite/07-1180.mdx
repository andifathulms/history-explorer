Hattusa was burned and abandoned around 1180 BC and the Hittite state ceased to
exist. Nobody says who did it.

That is the whole difficulty of the ending, and it is worth setting out what is
actually absent. There is no victory inscription. No chronicle entry anywhere
in the Near East records the fall. No later power claims to have done it.
Assyria, which had beaten Hittite armies in the field and would have had every
reason to boast, does not. The archaeology shows the city systematically
emptied — the archives and cult statues removed before the fire, which suggests
an evacuation rather than a surprise — and then burned, and then squatted in by
a much smaller population.

It was not a conquest, because no conqueror can be named. What happened is
that the central state came apart and its Syrian branches carried on.

## The last letters

The final documents from the region are unusually vivid for something so poorly
understood. From Ugarit, a Hittite vassal on the Syrian coast, come letters
found in a kiln — baked by the fire that destroyed the city before they could
be sent. One is from the last Hittite king or his officials demanding grain and
stating that it is a matter of life or death. Another, from Ammurapi of Ugarit,
reports that enemy ships have appeared, that his own fleet is away in Lycia and
his army in Hatti, and that the country is undefended.

Grain being shipped from Egypt and Syria to Anatolia in quantity, in the last
years, is one of the few concrete things the record supplies. Whether the
driver was drought, or the loss of the Syrian revenues, or the Kaska, or the
general dislocation that took down Mycenae and Ugarit and the Egyptian Levant
in the same half-century, is an argument that has been running for a century
and is not going to be settled here.

## What Egypt says

Egypt went through the same collapse from the southern end, and the Egyptian
sources are the only ones that name an agent: Ramesses III's inscriptions at
Medinet Habu describe a coalition of peoples moving by land and sea, and say
that no land stood before their arms, listing Hatti among the fallen.

That is a claim made by the surviving power about its neighbours' destruction,
twenty-five years after the fact, in a triumphal inscription about a battle it
won. It is evidence, and it is not the same thing as knowing who destroyed
Hattusa. The Sea Peoples as a single organised agent of collapse is largely a
nineteenth-century synthesis built on that one text, and the temptation to
manufacture a cause out of the only source that offers one is exactly the
temptation to resist.

## What continued

Carchemish. Piyassili's line went on ruling there, and at Melid, Tabal,
Karkemish and half a dozen other places in northern Syria and south-eastern
Anatolia, small kingdoms kept the Hittite royal titles, the Luwian language,
the Anatolian hieroglyphs and the name. The Assyrians called that whole country
Hatti for the next four centuries; so does the Hebrew Bible. Assyrian campaigns
westward are, in Assyrian terms, campaigns against Hatti, three hundred years
after Hattusa burned.

That is the one real continuation. The kings of Carchemish styled themselves
Great King, publicly and in monumental inscriptions, for centuries after there
was anybody left to object.
