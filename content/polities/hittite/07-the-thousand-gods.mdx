Hittite treaties end with a list of divine witnesses, and the lists are long. The
formula that recurs in the state cult is *the thousand gods of Hatti*, and it is
not rhetorical: the pantheon was genuinely enormous, because it was cumulative.
When the Hittites took a town they took its gods, physically — the cult statues
went back to Hattusa — and added them to the establishment. Deities arrived from
the Hattic substrate, from the Luwians, from the Hurrians of Syria in bulk after
Suppiluliuma, and from Mesopotamia.

Prayers survive that address a god by several names in several languages at once,
to be sure of reaching them. One royal prayer worries that some offence may have
been committed against a god whose rites the king does not know, and asks to be
told which one.

## Zero, twice, and why

This record's influence counts read 0 for religions carried and 0 for descendant
scripts, and both zeros are the honest result of a rule rather than an absence of
evidence.

Coding rule 2 counts propagation outward — mission, endowment, conquest followed
by durable religious institutions. The traffic here ran the other way for four
centuries. Hatti was the great importer of cults in the Bronze Age Near East and
exported none; there is no Hittite religion anywhere after 1180 that a source
attributes to Hittite propagation. The count is not zero because nothing
religious happened. It is zero because what happened was the opposite of what the
axis measures.

Coding rule 3 is the same shape. The Hittite scribes took Mesopotamian cuneiform
and adapted it to write an Indo-European language, which is precisely the act
rule 3 counts — and rule 3 counts it on the record of the polity whose script was
adapted, not on the adapter's. The Anatolian hieroglyphic script used on Hittite
seals and monuments is a separate system for Luwian, not derived from cuneiform,
and no source derives it from this state's writing. So: zero, with the actual
achievement sitting in a field on a page this corpus does not have.

Hard rule 3 says `null` renders as "no cited figure" and never as zero. These are
the other case. They are cited zeros, they are arguments, and a reader should be
able to tell them apart from a blank.

## What the archive actually contains

The thirty thousand tablets from Hattusa are overwhelmingly not narrative. They
are festival texts — the ritual calendar, in enormous detail, day by day, with
the offerings and the processions and the songs. They are oracle reports:
questions put to the gods about whether the king should campaign, whether an
illness has a cultic cause, whether a particular official has offended, answered
by bird-flight or by extispicy and recorded with the reasoning. They are
incantations, many of them by named practitioners, several of them women from
Kizzuwatna in the south-east.

And they are laws, treaties and letters, which is the part historians use.

It is worth saying plainly that this is an accident of what a fire preserves and
what a plateau climate does to clay. This corpus's Mitanni record is a great
power with one coded institution out of four, and the reason is that nobody has
found its capital. Wassukanni has never been located. Had the excavators of 1906
dug a different mound, the two records on this site might have been the other way
round — a legible Mitanni and a Hatti known only from other people's treaties —
and every judgement anyone has made about which of them was the more sophisticated
state would have been made in reverse.
