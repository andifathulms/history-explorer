Hattusili's successor Mursili I took Aleppo, which his predecessor had failed to
take, and then did something no one has satisfactorily explained. He marched
down the Euphrates to Babylon — eight hundred kilometres beyond anything the
Hittite state had an interest in — took the city, ended the dynasty of Hammurabi,
and went home.

He kept nothing. He installed no governor and left no garrison. The Kassites
moved into the vacuum within a generation and ruled Babylonia for four centuries.
The largest single military achievement in Hittite history transferred a great
kingdom to a third party who had not been present.

## Two consequences, both elsewhere

The first belongs to a record this corpus does not carry. The Old Babylonian
kingdom — Hammurabi's, the law code, the Babylon of the eighteenth century — is
not on this site, and one of the reasons its absence is felt is that its ending
is a turning point on this page. The Neo-Babylonian record entering alongside
this one is a different polity a thousand years later, and the two are as
distinct as Rome and the Papacy.

The second is the more consequential and it is not military at all. This site's
influence counts give this record zero descendant scripts, and the note in the
data explains that the interesting derivation runs the other way: Hittite
cuneiform is Mesopotamian cuneiform adapted to write an Indo-European language,
and under coding rule 3 that adaptation counts on the record of the polity whose
script was adapted. Hittite is the earliest attested Indo-European language in
the world, and it is attested because Anatolian scribes borrowed a Babylonian
writing system. The claim belongs to a page that does not exist.

## The date

1595 BC is the conventional date and it is doing more work than any other number
on this record, because Bronze Age Anatolian chronology hangs off it. The
absolute dates in this corpus for Hatti are Bryce's, following the middle
chronology; the competing high and low chronologies move the whole sequence by up
to a century in either direction, and the sack of Babylon is one of the pegs the
argument turns on.

This site's usual instrument for disagreement between sources is a `{ min, max }`
range with both citations, and it is the wrong instrument here. A range implies
two published figures for the same event. What exists instead is one event, three
frames, and a figure that is correct within whichever frame you have chosen. The
`span` field carries a note saying so. It is the only place in the corpus where
the schema is asked to hold a disagreement about the calendar rather than about
the fact.

## The murder

Mursili came home and was assassinated by his brother-in-law Hantili, who took
the throne. Hantili's own line was destroyed by Zidanta, whose line was destroyed
by Ammuna, and so on for the better part of a century — a sequence of palace
killings that reduced the kingdom to central Anatolia and lost everything Mursili
and Hattusili had taken.

The next chapter is about the king who wrote it all down and then legislated
against it.
