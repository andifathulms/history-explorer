Hattusili's successor Mursili I took Aleppo, which his predecessor had failed
to take, and then did something no one has satisfactorily explained. He marched
down the Euphrates to Babylon — eight hundred kilometres beyond anything the
Hittite state had an interest in — took the city, ended the dynasty of
Hammurabi, and went home.

He kept nothing. He installed no governor and left no garrison. The Kassites
moved into the vacuum within a generation and ruled Babylonia for four
centuries. The largest single military achievement in Hittite history
transferred a great kingdom to a third party who had not been present.

## Two consequences, neither of them Anatolian

The first is that the dynasty of Hammurabi ended. What the Hittites destroyed
was the state that produced the most famous law collection of the ancient
world, and they did not stay to replace it; the Kassites did that, and nobody
recorded how.

The second is not military at all, and it runs the other way. Hittite cuneiform
is Mesopotamian cuneiform adapted to write an Indo-European language — and
Hittite is the earliest attested Indo-European language in the world. It is
attested because Anatolian scribes borrowed a Babylonian writing system, and
the borrowing is worth more to us now than the raid.

## The date

1595 BC is the conventional date, and it does more work than any other number
in Hittite history, because the whole chronology of Bronze Age Anatolia hangs
off it. The dates used here follow the middle chronology; the competing high
and low chronologies move the entire sequence by up to a century in either
direction, and the sack of Babylon is one of the pegs the argument turns on.

It is worth being clear about what kind of uncertainty that is. It is not two
scholars proposing different dates for one event. It is one event, three
competing frameworks for the whole second millennium, and a date that is
correct inside whichever framework you have chosen. Nothing about the event is
in doubt; the calendar is.

## The murder

Mursili came home and was assassinated by his brother-in-law Hantili, who took
the throne. Hantili's own line was destroyed by Zidanta, whose line was
destroyed by Ammuna, and so on for the better part of a century — a sequence of
palace killings that reduced the kingdom to central Anatolia and lost
everything Mursili and Hattusili had taken.
