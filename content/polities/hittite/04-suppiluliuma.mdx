He came to the throne around 1350 by killing the designated heir, which is the
edict of the previous chapter being ignored by the greatest king who ever kept
it in the archive. He then spent thirty years making Hatti one of the two great
powers of the Near East, and he did it by destroying the one that was already
there.

Mitanni had held northern Syria for two centuries and had a marriage alliance
with Egypt, and no archive of its own has ever been found. In two campaigns
Suppiluliuma took Aleppo, Carchemish, Alalakh and Qadesh, stripped Mitanni of
its Syrian empire, and then intervened in the Mitannian succession: he backed a
claimant named Shattiwaza against a rival supported by Assyria, married him to
a Hittite princess, and installed him over a rump kingdom east of the
Euphrates.

The treaty that recorded this arrangement is also the single most important
document anyone has for Mitanni. Its list of divine witnesses includes Mitra,
Varuna, Indra and the Nasatya twins — Vedic gods, invoked in a Hittite chancery
document in northern Syria in the fourteenth century BC, which is the single
most important piece of evidence for the Indo-Aryan element in the Mitannian
ruling class. The most-cited document about Mitanni was written by this state's
foreign office.

## Carchemish

The structural achievement was not the conquest, which took two seasons, but
what followed it. Suppiluliuma put one son, Piyassili, on the throne at
Carchemish and another, Telipinu, at Aleppo, as viceroys with real authority
over the Syrian vassals. That arrangement held for a hundred and fifty years,
outlasted the central state. The kings of Carchemish, descended from Piyassili,
went on calling themselves Great King and their country Hatti long after
Hattusa stood empty.

<Figure id="ht-carchemish" />

Hatti fought with its own landholders and with troops owed by its clients, and
the unusual thing is that the obligation survives in the chancery's own words.
Client levies are normally known from a chronicler describing an army after the
fact. Here Beckman can print the treaty in which a named king promises a named
number of troops on named conditions, with the list of divine witnesses at the
bottom.

## The Egyptian widow

Near the end of his reign a letter arrived from Egypt. The queen —
Ankhesenamun, almost certainly, the widow of Tutankhamun — wrote that her
husband was dead, she had no son, she would not marry a servant, and would the
Hittite king send one of his sons to be her husband and king of Egypt.

Suppiluliuma did not believe it and sent an envoy to check. The envoy came back
with confirmation and an indignant second letter. He then sent a son, Zannanza,
who was killed on the road, and the affair ended in a war and, indirectly, in
something worse: Hittite prisoners taken in that war brought back a plague that
killed Suppiluliuma himself and his immediate successor, and that his younger
son Mursili II spent years addressing in a set of prayers asking the gods what
the kingdom had done wrong.

<Figure id="ht-plague" />

The episode is preserved only in the Hittite account, written by that son. It
is worth noticing what kind of document that is: the deeds of Suppiluliuma,
composed by Mursili II, is a work of history written about a father by a son
who had watched the plague arrive. This region's earlier records mostly cannot
supply anything of the sort, and it is a reasonable question whether the
Hittites look more interesting than Mitanni because they were, or because their
filing survived.
