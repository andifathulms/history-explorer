He came to the throne around 1350 by killing the designated heir, which is the
edict of the previous chapter being ignored by the king this record names as its
peak. He then spent thirty years making Hatti one of the two great powers of the
Near East, and he did it by destroying the one that was already there.

Mitanni had held northern Syria for two centuries and had a marriage alliance
with Egypt. This corpus carries it as a record with no archive of its own. In two
campaigns Suppiluliuma took Aleppo, Carchemish, Alalakh and Qadesh, stripped
Mitanni of its Syrian empire, and then intervened in the Mitannian succession: he
backed a claimant named Shattiwaza against a rival supported by Assyria, married
him to a Hittite princess, and installed him over a rump kingdom east of the
Euphrates.

The treaty that recorded this arrangement is the reason the Mitanni page on this
site exists in the shape it does. Its list of divine witnesses includes Mitra,
Varuna, Indra and the Nasatya twins — Vedic gods, invoked in a Hittite chancery
document in northern Syria in the fourteenth century BC, which is the single most
important piece of evidence for the Indo-Aryan element in the Mitannian ruling
class. The most-cited document about Mitanni was written by this state's foreign
office.

## Carchemish

The structural achievement was not the conquest, which took two seasons, but what
followed it. Suppiluliuma put one son, Piyassili, on the throne at Carchemish and
another, Telipinu, at Aleppo, as viceroys with real authority over the Syrian
vassals. That arrangement held for a hundred and fifty years, outlasted the
central state, and is the reason this record's `successor_claims` count is 1
rather than 0: the kings of Carchemish, descended from Piyassili, went on calling
themselves Great King and their country Hatti long after Hattusa was empty.

The `military_basis` here reads `land-grant, client-levy`, and the Syrian vassal
system is the second value. What makes this corpus's coding of it unusual is that
the obligation survives in the chancery's own words. Elsewhere on this site a
client levy is coded from a chronicler describing an army; here Beckman can print
the treaty in which a named king promises a named number of troops on named
conditions, with the god list at the bottom.

## The Egyptian widow

Near the end of his reign a letter arrived from Egypt. The queen — Ankhesenamun,
almost certainly, the widow of Tutankhamun — wrote that her husband was dead, she
had no son, she would not marry a servant, and would the Hittite king send one of
his sons to be her husband and king of Egypt.

Suppiluliuma did not believe it and sent an envoy to check. The envoy came back
with confirmation and an indignant second letter. He then sent a son, Zannanza,
who was killed on the road, and the affair ended in a war and, indirectly, in
something worse: Hittite prisoners taken in that war brought back a plague that
killed Suppiluliuma himself and his immediate successor, and that his younger son
Mursili II spent years addressing in a set of prayers asking the gods what the
kingdom had done wrong.

The episode is preserved only in the Hittite account, written by that son. It is
worth noticing what kind of document that is: the deeds of Suppiluliuma, composed
by Mursili II, is a work of history written about a father by a son who had
watched the plague arrive. This region's earlier records mostly cannot supply
anything of the sort, and it is a reasonable question whether the Hittites look
more interesting than Mitanni because they were, or because their filing survived.
