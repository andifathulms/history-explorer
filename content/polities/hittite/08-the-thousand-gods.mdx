Hittite treaties end with a list of divine witnesses, and the lists are long.
The formula that recurs in the state cult is *the thousand gods of Hatti*, and
it is not rhetorical: the pantheon was genuinely enormous, because it was
cumulative. When the Hittites took a town they took its gods, physically — the
cult statues went back to Hattusa — and added them to the establishment.
Deities arrived from the Hattic substrate, from the Luwians, from the Hurrians
of Syria in bulk after Suppiluliuma, and from Mesopotamia.

Prayers survive that address a god by several names in several languages at
once, to be sure of reaching them. One royal prayer worries that some offence
may have been committed against a god whose rites the king does not know, and
asks to be told which one.

## Zero, twice, and why

Hatti exported no religion at all, and the fact is worth stating because the
traffic ran the other way so hard for so long. It was the great importer of
cults of the Bronze Age Near East, and there is no Hittite religion anywhere
after 1180 that anybody attributes to Hittite propagation.

The same asymmetry holds for the writing. Hittite scribes took Mesopotamian
cuneiform and adapted it to record an Indo-European language, which is a
considerable achievement and is an achievement of borrowing rather than of
transmission — the credit for the script belongs to Babylon. The Anatolian
hieroglyphs used on Hittite seals and monuments are a separate system for
Luwian and are not derived from it.

So an empire that lasted four centuries spread neither its gods nor its
letters. It absorbed both.

## What the archive actually contains

The thirty thousand tablets from Hattusa are overwhelmingly not narrative. They
are festival texts — the ritual calendar, in enormous detail, day by day, with
the offerings and the processions and the songs. They are oracle reports:
questions put to the gods about whether the king should campaign, whether an
illness has a cultic cause, whether a particular official has offended,
answered by bird-flight or by extispicy and recorded with the reasoning. They
are incantations, many of them by named practitioners, several of them women
from Kizzuwatna in the south-east.

And they are laws, treaties and letters, which is the part historians use.

It is worth saying plainly that this is an accident of what a fire preserves
and what a plateau climate does to clay, not a measure of which kingdom was
the more sophisticated one — Mitanni's own record sets out exactly how much
of that asymmetry turns on a single unexcavated mound at Washukanni, and the
same caution applies here in reverse.
