Kadesh in 1274 is usually told from the Egyptian side, and the essential thing
about it is quickly said: Ramesses II covered the walls of Egypt with an
account of a victory that was at best a draw, and the Hittites kept Kadesh.
That version and this one are looking at the same two documents. What follows
is what the pair looks like from Hattusa, which is a different story, because
for Hatti the treaty was not a diplomatic tidying-up. It was a domestic
necessity.

## The man who needed it

Muwatalli II fought Kadesh. When he died the throne went to his son
Urhi-Teshub, and after seven years his uncle Hattusili deposed him and took it.
Hattusili III was therefore a usurper who had removed a legitimate king in
direct contravention of Telipinu's edict, and he knew exactly how that looked.

He wrote a defence of himself — the text conventionally called the Apology of
Hattusili — in which the goddess Ishtar of Samuha has favoured him since
childhood, his nephew persecuted him beyond endurance, and he acted only when
there was no alternative and even then spared the man's life. It is a
first-person political apologia by a reigning king, and there is nothing else
like it in this region for a thousand years.

And Urhi-Teshub, exiled, went to Egypt.

## What the treaty was for

That is the situation in which Hattusili III concluded a permanent mutual
defence pact with Ramesses II. The Hittite text, on a tablet from the Hattusa
archive, and the Egyptian text, cut on the walls at Karnak and the Ramesseum,
are not translations of each other — each side's version says the other party
sued for peace — but the operative clauses match: no aggression, mutual
military assistance against attack, and mutual extradition of fugitives, with
the extradition clause carefully qualified so that returned refugees are not to
be punished.

For Hattusili the extradition clause was the treaty. His deposed nephew was
sitting in Egypt with a claim, and this document turned Egypt from a place that
sheltered him into a state contractually obliged to hand him over. The peace
with the southern rival was, among other things, a solution to a succession
problem seventeen years old.

## What it freed

The rest of the logic is Assyrian. While Hatti and Egypt fought over Syria,
Assyria had taken the whole of Mitanni's remaining territory and reached the
Euphrates, and by the 1230s an Assyrian king would defeat a Hittite army and
carry off prisoners in numbers the inscription boasts about. Hatti's real
problem after 1259 was in the east, and the frontier with Egypt was closed off
so that it could be faced.

Thirteen years later Hattusili sent a daughter to Ramesses as a wife, with a
correspondence about the dowry and the arrangements that runs to a substantial
file, including letters between the two queens. It is the most fully documented
diplomatic relationship in the ancient world before the Greeks, and it consists
of two states that had been at war deciding, for reasons of internal politics
on one side and imperial overextension on the other, to stop.

## What the treaty was not

It was not a succession, an inheritance or a subordination, and that is worth
saying because those are the relationships that usually get recorded between
states.

Hatti and Egypt were contemporaries who fought, negotiated and intermarried for
two centuries, and neither ever governed the other or claimed to. Suppiluliuma
dismantled Mitanni without ending it, and it was the Middle Assyrian kings who
finished the job a century later. The only state Hatti destroyed outright was
Babylon, in a raid that it walked away from.

So the greatest of the Anatolian powers spent four hundred years at the centre
of a system of equals, and left no successor and no heir. Everything it was
actually connected to — Egypt, Mitanni, Assyria, Ahhiyawa — it was connected to
by correspondence, war and marriage, which is a different kind of relationship
and leaves a different kind of trace.
