This site's Egyptian record carries Kadesh in 1274 and the treaty of 1259 in a
single chapter, from the Egyptian side, and says the essential thing about the
battle: Ramesses II covered the walls of Egypt with an account of a victory that
was at best a draw, and the Hittites kept Kadesh. That chapter and this one are
looking at the same two documents. What follows is what the pair looks like from
Hattusa, which is a different story, because for Hatti the treaty was not a
diplomatic tidying-up. It was a domestic necessity.

## The man who needed it

Muwatalli II fought Kadesh. When he died the throne went to his son Urhi-Teshub,
and after seven years his uncle Hattusili deposed him and took it. Hattusili III
was therefore a usurper who had removed a legitimate king in direct contravention
of the edict this record's third chapter is about, and he knew exactly how that
looked.

He wrote a defence of himself — the text conventionally called the Apology of
Hattusili — in which the goddess Ishtar of Samuha has favoured him since
childhood, his nephew persecuted him beyond endurance, and he acted only when
there was no alternative and even then spared the man's life. It is a
first-person political apologia by a reigning king, and there is nothing else
like it in this region for a thousand years.

And Urhi-Teshub, exiled, went to Egypt.

## What the treaty was for

That is the situation in which Hattusili III concluded a permanent mutual defence
pact with Ramesses II. The Hittite text, on a tablet from the Hattusa archive, and
the Egyptian text, cut on the walls at Karnak and the Ramesseum, are not
translations of each other — each side's version says the other party sued for
peace — but the operative clauses match: no aggression, mutual military
assistance against attack, and mutual extradition of fugitives, with the
extradition clause carefully qualified so that returned refugees are not to be
punished.

For Hattusili the extradition clause was the treaty. His deposed nephew was
sitting in Egypt with a claim, and this document turned Egypt from a place that
sheltered him into a state contractually obliged to hand him over. The peace with
the southern rival was, among other things, a solution to a succession problem
seventeen years old.

## What it freed

The rest of the logic is Assyrian. While Hatti and Egypt fought over Syria,
Assyria had taken the whole of Mitanni's remaining territory and reached the
Euphrates, and by the 1230s an Assyrian king would defeat a Hittite army and
carry off prisoners in numbers the inscription boasts about. Hatti's real problem
after 1259 was in the east, and the frontier with Egypt was closed off so that it
could be faced.

Thirteen years later Hattusili sent a daughter to Ramesses as a wife, with a
correspondence about the dowry and the arrangements that runs to a substantial
file, including letters between the two queens. It is the most fully documented
diplomatic relationship in the ancient world before the Greeks, and it consists
of two states that had been at war deciding, for reasons of internal politics on
one side and imperial overextension on the other, to stop.

## Why there is no edge

This corpus records succession, and there is none here. Hatti and Egypt were
contemporaries who fought, negotiated and intermarried for two centuries, and
none of that is an edge in this site's vocabulary. Nor is there an edge to
Mitanni: Suppiluliuma dismantled it but did not end it, its `ended.by` field is
empty because the Middle Assyrian kings who finished it have no page here, and
in any case an edge runs from the earlier polity to the later one, and Hatti is
the older of the two.

This record entered the corpus with no edges in either direction, and this
paragraph said so. That is no longer true, and the way it stopped being true is
worth keeping rather than quietly editing away.

The First Dynasty of Babylon has since been added to this region, and its ending
is Mursili I's raid — the event this record's second chapter is about. So there
is now one edge, `old-babylonian -> hittite`, of type `conquered by`, running
into this page from a polity that Hatti destroyed and did not inherit. It is the
only edge Hatti has, it points backwards, and it records a conquest that led
nowhere.

The original observation survives it. Hard rule 7 is explicit that an edgeless
polity is an ordinary state and not a shortfall, and everything this record was
actually connected to — Egypt, Mitanni, Assyria, Ahhiyawa — is still connected
to it by correspondence, war and marriage, none of which this site draws. A
great power can be at the centre of its own world and at the edge of this data
model, and the model is right about that.
