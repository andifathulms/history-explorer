The city sits on a rock shelf in the bend of the Kizilirmak, in the central
Anatolian highlands, about a thousand metres up. The winters are severe and the
agriculture is marginal. It is not on a river route or a trade corridor. It is
ringed by gorges, which sounds defensible until you notice that the walls run for
six kilometres over broken ground and would need a garrison the kingdom could not
spare, and that in the course of its history the city was sacked at least twice
by people who simply walked in.

A king called Hattusili — "the man of Hattusa" — established himself there around
1650 BC and took the site's name as his own. His predecessor, or possibly the
same man under a different title, had burned it a generation earlier and cursed
anyone who resettled it. That is the founding act of this record: a capital
chosen against advice, on a cursed site, in bad country.

## Why there

Bryce's answer is that the Hittite state was never a river-valley state and could
not be one. Egypt is the Nile, Babylonia is the Tigris and the Euphrates, Assyria
is the northern Mesopotamian plain. Hatti is a plateau of scattered upland basins
separated by mountains, and a kingdom there is a kingdom of roads and vassal
towns rather than a kingdom of irrigated land. What the site offered was
distance: it was hard to reach from any direction, which mattered enormously to a
state whose most dangerous enemy for its entire existence was not Egypt or
Assyria but the Kaska, the unorganised hill peoples of the Pontic mountains to
the immediate north, who raided constantly and who burned Hattusa itself at least
once.

This is the reason this record has an unusual `revenue_basis` for an empire of
its size. There is no great agricultural surplus to tax. The state ran on dues in
produce and labour from land held in return for service, on the tribute of
vassals, and on the constant importation of people — which is the subject of the
sixth chapter and the reason the `plunder` value is a closer call here than the
coding note admits at first reading.

## What the archive is

Hattusa was excavated from 1906 and produced something like thirty thousand
tablets and fragments, mostly in Hittite, some in Akkadian, in cuneiform borrowed
from Mesopotamia. Among them are the treaties, the diplomatic letters and the
royal edicts that Beckman translates, and it is worth being blunt about what that
means for this corpus.

The Mitanni record on this site has one coded institution out of four and no
turning point that is not reconstructed from somebody else's document. Most of
that somebody else is this archive. The Egyptian New Kingdom record's treaty of
1259 exists in two versions and one of them was dug up here. This is the only
Late Bronze Age power in the region whose foreign ministry survived, and the
consequence is that Hatti is legible in a way that its exact contemporaries are
not — which is a fact about excavation, not about statecraft, and the reader
should hold the two apart.
