Ashurbanipal's account of what he did to Elam in 646 is the most detailed
description of a deliberate erasure written by the person who ordered it, and
it ends on an oddly tender note.

## The erasure

He lists it methodically. The treasure-houses opened and counted as spoil. The
ziggurat of enamelled brick destroyed and its bronze pinnacles broken off.
Inshushinak, the oracle god who dwells in seclusion, *the work of whose
divinity no one has ever seen*, carried off, along with twelve more named gods
and goddesses, their priests, their furniture and their vessels. Thirty-two
royal statues in gold, silver, bronze and marble. The guardian colossi lifted
out of the temple gates.

The sacred groves that no stranger had ever entered were entered by his
soldiers, who saw their mysteries and burned them. The tombs of the kings of
Elam, earlier and later, were opened and devastated and exposed to the sun,
their bones carried to Assyria — so that, he says, their shades would be denied
rest and cut off from food offerings and libations of water.

For twenty-five days' journey he scattered salt and thorn across the provinces.
The noise of people and the tread of cattle and the glad shouts of harvest he
banished from the fields, and left wild asses and gazelles to lie down there as
if at home.

## The goddess

Then, at the end of the same inscription, the reason for all of it.

The goddess Nana, he writes, had been angry for one thousand six hundred and
thirty-five years, and had gone away to dwell in Elam, *a place not suitable
for her*. Now, in these days, she had entrusted to him the return of her
divinity.

An Elamite king had taken her statue from Uruk sixteen centuries earlier. The
whole campaign is presented, in its closing lines, as a restitution — a goddess
brought home from an exile older than any state involved in the fighting.

Ashurbanipal's Assyria would itself be destroyed within forty years.

## What was not erased

The claim to have obliterated Elam is total and it was not true.

Within a century the country east of the Zagros was Persian, and the Persians
did not replace what the Elamites had built — they used it. Susa became one of
the capitals of the largest empire the world had yet seen. The Achaemenid
administration ran in Elamite, written by Elamite scribes; the archives of
Persepolis are in that language. When Darius wanted his account of his own
accession carved on a cliff for everybody to read, Elamite was one of the three
languages he had it written in.

The founder of that empire introduced himself, on a clay cylinder buried in the
wall of Babylon, as king of Anshan — an Elamite city, and an Elamite title,
taken up at the moment when Elam had been destroyed and the title was going
spare.

A kingdom whose fields were sown with salt supplied the next empire with its
scribes, its bureaucratic language and one of its capitals. The salt was
literal; the erasure was not.
