In 653 BC Ashurbanipal destroyed the Elamite army on the Ulai river near Susa,
and the king Te-Umman was killed in the rout. The battle is carved on the walls
of the North Palace at Nineveh in continuous narrative relief — one of the
earliest sustained attempts anywhere to tell a story in a single unbroken visual
field, with the river at the bottom full of bodies and horses, chariots
overturning, and the sequence of Te-Umman's death told in stages across the
panel: the chariot wrecked, the king wounded, the king seized, the king
beheaded.

The head then travels. It is shown being carried, and it appears again in the
most reproduced Assyrian image of all: the garden scene, in which Ashurbanipal
reclines on a couch under a vine with his queen sitting opposite, food and
musicians around them, and Te-Umman's head hanging from a branch of the tree
beside them.

## What that is doing on this record

This site does not carry battles that decided nothing — coding rule 4.2 makes the
filter consequence rather than documentation, precisely so that well-studied
polities do not look eventful while the rest look quiet. Til-Tuba passes on
consequence: Elam never fielded an army against Assyria again, and the sack of
Susa followed within seven years.

But the documentation problem is worth naming, because this page is where it is
sharpest in the whole corpus. The reason anyone can describe Til-Tuba in detail
is that the winner commissioned a picture of it for the wall of his own dining
room. There is no Elamite account of the battle, no Elamite figure for the army,
no Elamite name for the site. The one thing this record can say about its own
worst defeat is what the man who inflicted it wanted his guests to see.

## Interference from the inside

Te-Umman was not fighting only Assyria. He had taken the throne from a line whose
princes had fled to Nineveh, and Ashurbanipal was sheltering them; the campaign
of 653 was launched, in the Assyrian telling, to restore them. After Til-Tuba the
Assyrians installed one of these exiles as king in Susa and another in a second
Elamite centre, splitting the country in two.

The Neo-Elamite succession in this period is a sequence of rival claimants,
depositions and restorations, with Assyria backing one party and Babylon
sometimes another, and the sources for it are the courts doing the backing. This
record's `succession_rule` is null, and the honest reason is not that the throne
passed chaotically — it usually did — but that coding rule 7 asks for the rule and
what survives is a list of outcomes recorded by foreigners with an interest in
each one.

This corpus has now made that argument in five different shapes in this region.
On the Neo-Babylonian record the rule is documented and the outcomes were coups.
On the Ur III and Old Babylonian records the outcomes were perfect and there is
no rule. On the Middle Assyrian record the rule is documented and the peak reign
ended in patricide. Here neither is available. The field says the same word or
nothing in all five cases, which is the field working exactly as designed and
also the reason none of these pages could be written without chapters.
