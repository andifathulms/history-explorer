In 691 BC the king of Elam and the king of Babylon brought a coalition army to
the bank of the Tigris and blocked Sennacherib's road. Sennacherib's account of
the day is one of the longest battle narratives in Assyrian writing, and it is
worth reading for what it concedes rather than what it claims.

## The host

He lists who came, and the list is the achievement.

Umman-menanu of Elam had called to his side an enormous host of allies: the
cities of Bit-Salatutu-akki and Lahiru, and the tribes of Puqudu, Gambulu,
Halatu, Ru'a, Ubulu, Malahu, Rapiqu, Hindaru, Damunu. They took the road into
Akkad, came up to Babylon, exchanged courtesies with Shuzubu the Chaldean king
there, and halted.

Then, in Sennacherib's own image:

> Like the onset of the locust swarms of the springtime they kept steadily
> coming on against me to offer battle. With the dust of their feet covering the
> wide heavens, like a mighty storm with its masses of dense clouds, they drew up
> in battle array before me by the city of Halule, on the bank of the Tigris.
> They blocked my passage and offered battle.

An Elamite king had assembled Babylon, Chaldea and every marsh tribe of southern
Iraq into one army, marched it north, and chosen the ground. Nothing else in the
Neo-Elamite record comes close to that.

## What the victory looks like

Sennacherib describes his own preparation at length — the prayer, the coat of
mail, the helmet, the great battle chariot mounted in the anger of his heart, the
bow Ashur gave him, the voice raised rumbling like a storm, the roaring like
Adad. Assyrian royal writing does not usually need this much throat-clearing.

Then the result, in the later summary version:

> I went into their midst and accomplished the defeat of their armies... The
> chieftains of the king of Elam, together with Nabu-shum-ishkun, son of
> Merodach-baladan, king of Babylonia, my hands took alive in that battle. As for
> the king of Elam and the king of Babylonia, the dread of my terrible onslaught
> overcame them, they forsook their chariots, and they fled from their lands to
> save their lives. And they did not come back.

He took the officers and the Chaldean prince. Both kings escaped.

And then the sentence that gives the day away: *Thereupon Sennacherib became
violently angry.* A victorious king, writing in the third person about himself,
records that after the triumph he lost his temper and ordered the army turned
toward Elam.

## What he did instead

He did not conquer Elam. The Elamites, he says, left their land and took to the
inaccessible mountains like eagles, and to the day of their death did not come
out or make war.

So he went for Babylon, and what he did there in 689 is the most complete act of
destruction in the Assyrian record: the city invested and mined, the squares
filled with corpses, the king carried off alive with his household, the gods
smashed by Assyrian hands, the temples and ziggurats tipped into the Arahtu
canal, canals cut through the site and the ground flooded — *I made its
destruction more complete than that by a flood... I completely blotted it out
with floods of water and made it like a meadow.*

Babylon was destroyed because Elam could not be. The coalition Elam built at
Halule was beaten in the field and not broken, and the empire took its rage out
on the partner it could reach.

## The high-water mark

Halule is the peak of this kingdom by any measure that matters. Elam never held
more territory than it did in these decades and never needed to; what it had was
the ability to make the strongest state in the world fight for its own southern
provinces.

It is also the beginning of the reckoning. An empire that has flooded Babylon to
erase it has decided that this problem is to be solved permanently. Forty-five
years after Halule, an Assyrian army went into Susa and did to Elam what
Sennacherib had done to Babylon.
