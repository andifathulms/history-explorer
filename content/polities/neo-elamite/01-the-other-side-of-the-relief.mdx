Almost everything known about the Neo-Elamite kingdom is known because an
Assyrian king had it carved on a wall.

<Figure id="ne-hamanu" />

That is a common enough condition for a state of this period — Mitanni is in
it, so are the Medes — and what makes it worth stating here is the
completeness. The Neo-Elamite kingdom fought Assyria on and off for roughly a
century, is named constantly in Assyrian royal inscriptions and in the
Babylonian Chronicles, is the subject of some of the most elaborate narrative
relief sculpture surviving from the ancient world, and has left, from its own
side, building inscriptions and a modest number of administrative tablets from
Susa that nobody has been able to build a state out of.

So almost nothing can be said about how the kingdom was governed — which is
also true of Middle Elam three centuries earlier, for the opposite reason. That
kingdom is under-described because Elamite royal texts do not describe
administration. This one is over-described, by an enemy, in a genre that
records who was defeated and never how the loser paid his troops.

## What it was for

Strip the Assyrian framing and a shape emerges anyway, and it is a simple one.
Babylon could not beat Assyria alone. Elam sat immediately east of Babylonia,
behind the Zagros, out of easy Assyrian reach, with an army and a grievance,
and for a hundred years it was the thing that made Babylonian resistance
viable.

In 720 Humban-nikash I came west with Merodach-baladan of Babylon and met
Sargon II at Der. Sargon's inscriptions claim the victory; the Babylonian
Chronicle gives it to the Elamites; and the fact that decides between them is
that Merodach-baladan then held Babylon for eleven years, which he could not
have done if Sargon had won. Both sides' inscriptions survive and they cannot
both be right.

In 691 an Elamite and Babylonian coalition met Sennacherib at Halule and both
sides claimed it. Two years later Sennacherib sacked Babylon, demolished its
temples and diverted a canal over the site — the act that poisoned Assyria's
relations with Babylonia for good and, eventually, helped destroy it. He did it
because Babylon kept revolting, and Babylon kept revolting because there was
somewhere for it to get help.

That is the kingdom's structural role in the period, and it is invisible in any
account of what Elam itself achieved. It has no measurable extent, almost no
describable institutions, and it is load-bearing in the century that decided
who would rule Mesopotamia.
