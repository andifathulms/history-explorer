Almost everything on this page is known because an Assyrian king had it carved on
a wall.

That is not a complaint and it is not unusual — this corpus's Mitanni record says
something similar, and so does its Median record. What makes it worth stating
here is the completeness. The Neo-Elamite kingdom fought Assyria on and off for
roughly a century, is named constantly in Assyrian royal inscriptions and in the
Babylonian Chronicles, is the subject of some of the most elaborate narrative
relief sculpture surviving from the ancient world, and has left, from its own
side, building inscriptions and a modest number of administrative tablets from
Susa that nobody has been able to build a state out of.

So the institution fields on this record read: null, null, null, and one value.
Which is the same score as the Middle Elamite record three centuries earlier, and
the reasons are opposite. That kingdom is under-described because Elamite royal
texts do not describe administration. This one is over-described, by an enemy,
in a genre that records who was defeated and never how the loser paid his troops.

## What it was for

Strip the Assyrian framing and a shape emerges anyway, and it is a simple one.
Babylon could not beat Assyria alone. Elam sat immediately east of Babylonia,
behind the Zagros, out of easy Assyrian reach, with an army and a grievance, and
for a hundred years it was the thing that made Babylonian resistance viable.

In 720 Humban-nikash I came west with Merodach-baladan of Babylon and met Sargon
II at Der. Sargon's inscriptions claim the victory; the Babylonian Chronicle
gives it to the Elamites; and the fact that decides between them is that
Merodach-baladan then held Babylon for eleven years, which he could not have done
if Sargon had won. This record marks the turning point `contested: true` on
exactly that disagreement.

In 691 an Elamite and Babylonian coalition met Sennacherib at Halule and both
sides claimed it. Two years later Sennacherib sacked Babylon, demolished its
temples and diverted a canal over the site — the act this site's Assyrian record
calls the poison that eventually destroyed the Assyrian empire. He did it because
Babylon kept revolting, and Babylon kept revolting because there was somewhere
for it to get help.

That is this kingdom's structural role in the region, and none of it registers in
any field this site codes. It has no reach figure, no coded institutions, no
influence counts except one, and it is a load-bearing part of the century that
this corpus's largest Assyrian and Babylonian records are both about.
