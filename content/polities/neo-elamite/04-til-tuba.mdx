In 653 BC Ashurbanipal destroyed the Elamite army on the Ulai river near Susa,
and the king Te-Umman was killed in the rout. The battle is carved on the walls
of the North Palace at Nineveh in continuous narrative relief — one of the
earliest sustained attempts anywhere to tell a story in a single unbroken
visual field, with the river at the bottom full of bodies and horses, chariots
overturning, and the sequence of Te-Umman's death told in stages across the
panel: the chariot wrecked, the king wounded, the king seized, the king
beheaded.

The head then travels. It is shown being carried, and it appears again in the
most reproduced Assyrian image of all: the garden scene, in which Ashurbanipal
reclines on a couch under a vine with his queen sitting opposite, food and
musicians around them, and Te-Umman's head hanging from a branch of the tree
beside them.

## Whose battle it is

Til-Tuba decided something: Elam never put an army in the field against Assyria
again, and Susa was sacked within seven years.

But the documentation is worth naming, because it is about as one-sided as
evidence gets. The reason anyone can describe the battle in detail is that the
winner commissioned a picture of it for the wall of his own dining room. There
is no Elamite account of the battle, no Elamite figure for the army, no Elamite
name for the site. Everything that can be said about the worst defeat in
Elamite history is what the man who inflicted it wanted his guests to look at
over dinner.

## Interference from the inside

Te-Umman was not fighting only Assyria. He had taken the throne from a line
whose princes had fled to Nineveh, and Ashurbanipal was sheltering them; the
campaign of 653 was launched, in the Assyrian telling, to restore them. After
Til-Tuba the Assyrians installed one of these exiles as king in Susa and
another in a second Elamite centre, splitting the country in two.

The Neo-Elamite succession in this period is a sequence of rival claimants,
depositions and restorations, with Assyria backing one party and Babylon
sometimes another, and the sources for it are the courts doing the backing.
Whether there was a rule underneath all that cannot be established: what
survives is a list of outcomes recorded by foreigners with an interest in each
one.

Mesopotamia offers every possible combination of that. Babylon under the
Neo-Babylonian kings had a documented rule and three coups in five transitions.
Ur III and Old Babylon had flawless successions for generations and never wrote
a rule down. Middle Assyria had a clear rule and a king murdered by his son.
Here there is neither the rule nor a reliable account of the outcomes — only
what the neighbours chose to record about whom they had installed.
