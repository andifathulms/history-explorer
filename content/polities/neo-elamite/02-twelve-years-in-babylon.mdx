Elam's second life as a power did not begin with a conquest of its own. It began
when an Elamite king answered a Chaldean's call for help, and the two of them
held Babylon for twelve years against Assyria.

The account of it is Sargon II's, which is the only reason we have one.

## The alliance

Merodach-baladan of Bit-Yakin, whose people lived on the Persian Gulf shore, had
withheld his tribute — violated the oath and curse invoked in the name of the
great gods, in Sargon's phrasing.

Then: *Humbanigash, the Elamite, came to his aid.*

Six words, and they are the whole hinge of the period. An Elamite king intervened
in Babylonia on the Chaldean side, and the tribes of the marshes — Ru'a, Hindaru,
Yatburu, Puqudu, the desert Sutu — came over with them. Merodach-baladan
descended on Sumer and Akkad and took Babylon.

What Sargon then has to admit, in his own inscription, is the duration:

> For twelve years, against the will of the gods, he held sway over Babylon, the
> city of the lord of the gods, and ruled it.

Twelve years is not a raid. It is a reign. The greatest city in Mesopotamia was
governed for over a decade by a Chaldean chief with Elamite backing, while the
Assyrian empire at its height could not dislodge him — and the Assyrian record
has to explain that away theologically, as something Marduk permitted before
choosing Sargon to reverse it.

## The battle nobody will describe

The engagement that made it possible was fought at Der in 720, and the way Sargon
handles it is instructive.

He does not narrate it. What he does instead is put it in his titulary and repeat
it: *who brought about the defeat of Humbanigash the Elamite* — the same phrase,
in the same list, alongside the smashing of Karalla and Shurda, the destruction of
Kishesim and Harhar, the yoke laid on Ellipi, the devastation of Urartu, the spoil
of Musasir.

A king who names a victory five times in his formulaic epithets and never once
tells you what happened in it is a king making a claim rather than reporting an
event. Every other item in that list has a campaign narrative attached to it
somewhere in his inscriptions. This one does not.

What is certain from Sargon's own text is the outcome, whatever the battle was:
after Der, Elam and Chaldea held Babylon and Assyria did not.

## What Elam had become

The kingdom in these decades is not the Elam of Chogha Zanbil, building its own
monuments and carting other people's home.

It is something more modern and less grand: a state on the flank of a superpower,
whose weight came from being able to decide which way Babylonia went. Elam did not
need to take Babylon. It needed only to make Babylon expensive for Assyria — by
lending troops to whoever in the marshes was currently in revolt, and by being
close enough that Assyrian armies which went in after them had a second frontier
to worry about.

That is a policy, and Elam ran it for a century. It also explains why the Assyrians
eventually decided that the Elamite kingdom could not be left standing at all.
