Pachacuti gave command of the new expedition to his own illegitimate son
Topa Capac, assisted by two of Pachacuti's half-brothers and by Topa
Inca, the heir to the throne, sent along - nominally in overall command,
in practice to gain experience under more seasoned relatives. The army's
first task was subduing the province of Quechuas, which lay directly
across the road north; only once that and the surrounding highlands
were secured could the campaign reach Cajamarca, which it found still
standing despite repeated attacks Minchançaman had launched against the
garrison in the meantime.

## A kingdom invaded from its own highland flank

With Cajamarca firmly linked to Cuzco at last, Topa Capac turned to the
real objective. He divided his own forces, sending a smaller party to
explore the road toward Chachapoyas while the bulk of the army marched
on Chimor itself - descending the Moche river into the valley from the
highlands above Huamachuco rather than approaching from the coast.
Minchançaman resisted bitterly, but the outcome was never seriously in
doubt: the military gap between the two kingdoms, whatever its
particular source, was wide enough that Chimor could defend itself but
never seriously threaten Cuzco in return.

## A king carried off, a treasury emptied

After a further year spent campaigning in what is now Ecuador, the Inca
army returned to finish the conquest, looting Chimor of riches richer
than any previous Andean campaign had brought home - so much gold that
Pachacuti used part of it to cast statues of the Creator, the Sun, and
Mama Ocllo, the legendary ancestress of his own dynasty, and to gild a
band of gold along the wall of Cuzco's Temple of the Sun. Minchançaman
himself was captured and carried first to Cajamarca and then on to
Cuzco, where he lived out the rest of his life in honoured exile rather
than execution - a captured king, but a respected one, in a conquest
that treated the kingdom he had ruled with more care than its own final
defeat might have suggested.
