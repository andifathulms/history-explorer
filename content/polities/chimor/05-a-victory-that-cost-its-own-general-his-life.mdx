title: A victory that cost its own general his life

Capac Yupanqui sent word of his triumph ahead and marched south in
what he expected to be a hero's return, leaving a strong garrison behind
at newly conquered Cajamarca. Pachacuti's own reaction was not
gratitude. The general's unauthorized success had committed the Inca
state to defending a garrison five hundred miles from its own nearest
outposts, across territory that was, in large part, only loosely held or
not conquered at all - obligations Pachacuti had never intended to take
on so soon, and a war with Chimor he had actively hoped to postpone.

## A half-brother too dangerous to forgive

There was a further reason for Pachacuti's own anger that had nothing
to do with military logistics. He had come to his own throne by
displacing an older brother rather than through an uncontested
succession, and he was in no position to reward a half-brother whose
own claim to royal authority was nearly as strong as his, for
disobedience that happened, by chance, to succeed. Capac Yupanqui was
put to death on Pachacuti's own order before he ever reached Cuzco to
receive whatever honours he might otherwise have expected.

## A garrison that could not simply be abandoned

Executing the general did nothing to undo what his raid had set in
motion. Cajamarca's garrison still stood exposed, and the obligations it
created remained regardless of whether the man who created them was
alive to answer for them. The following year, Pachacuti made his own
choice: rather than withdraw and admit the whole venture had been a
mistake, he committed the Inca state fully, ordering a new expedition
not merely to relieve the garrison but to hold central Peru outright -
a gamble that would draw Chimor into a war for its own survival.
