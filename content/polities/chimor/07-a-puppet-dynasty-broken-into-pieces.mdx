Inca administration of the conquered kingdom was shrewder than outright
suppression would have been. In its first years, Chimor's own political
structure was left largely intact: Minchançaman remained an honoured
exile at Cuzco rather than a prisoner put to death, and one of his own
sons, Chumun-caur, was installed on the throne at Chan Chan as an Inca
client ruler, continuing the dynasty in form even as real authority now
answered to Cuzco.

<Figure id="ch-tunic" />

## A line kept, but slowly hollowed out

Chumun-caur was succeeded by his own son Guaman-chumo, whose name
already carried a Quechua element, a sign of the Cuzco education Inca
policy typically gave client heirs. His own son Anco-cuyuch succeeded
him in turn at Chan Chan - but by his reign, the unity of the old
kingdom had already been quietly dismantled. Rather than let one ruler
at Chan Chan continue to command the whole valley system Minchançaman
once had, Inca policy set up each of the old dynasty's own sons as
hereditary lord of a single town or valley, dividing what had been one
kingdom into many smaller, more easily managed lordships answering
directly to Cuzco rather than to a single Chimú king.

## A dynasty preserved by being taken apart

The valleys that received no new princely lord of their own continued
under the families the old kings of Chimor had themselves once
appointed to govern locally - so that Taycanamo's own descendants, even
as the kingdom that bore their family's name ceased to exist as a
single political unit, remained visible across the north coast in
office after office, a dynasty preserved everywhere in miniature rather
than surviving anywhere whole.
