The only connected account of how Chimor began comes from a Spanish
chronicle written in 1604, itself recording an oral tradition already
old by then: a man named Taycanamo arrived at the valley of Trujillo on
a balsa log raft of the kind used along the Gulf of Guayaquil, dressed
in a plain cotton breechclout and carrying with him certain yellow
powders he used in ceremonies. He told the people of the valley that a
great lord from across the sea had sent him to govern their land,
without saying more precisely where he had come from.

## A year of rites before a name was earned

Taycanamo spent his first year in the valley inside a building - likely
a shrine - performing rituals with his yellow powders. In that time he
learned the local language and won acceptance as ruler, taking for
himself the title King of Chimor. The dynasty that would govern the
valley for the next two centuries, well into the Spanish colonial
period, traced itself back to him.

## A founder more myth than man, and no less real for it

Modern scholarship treats the details of Taycanamo's own arrival with
real scepticism - a culture hero credited with introducing key
ceremonies and customs, assimilated over generations of retelling to
the figure of a dynasty's founder, in much the same way Inca tradition
would later treat Manco Capac. But a dynasty needs an actual first
member, whatever legend later grew up around him, and there is no
particular reason to doubt that a real man began the line the
chroniclers remembered under this name.
