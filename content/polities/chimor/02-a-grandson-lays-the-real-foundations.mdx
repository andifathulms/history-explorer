Taycanamo's son, Guacri-caur, expanded the family's authority
gradually, conquering the lower part of the valley his father had only
been accepted into by ceremony rather than by force of arms. What his
father had founded as a ritual lordship, Guacri-caur began turning into
a state with real territorial extent - though the chronicle that
preserves his name gives him only this one achievement before passing
the throne on.

## A hundred and twenty-five miles of coastline

It was Guacri-caur's own son, Ñancen-pinco, who did the work later
tradition credited with actually building the kingdom. He conquered the
upper reaches of the Chimor valley toward its mountain headwaters and
extended his family's rule along the coast in both directions - south
to the town the Spanish would later know as Santa, and north through
the valley of Chicama to Pacasmayo, near modern Saña. Altogether his
own conquests ran some hundred and twenty-five miles of coastline, by
the chronicle's own reckoning, a scale no earlier ruler of the line had
come close to matching.

## A date recovered from a dynasty's own arithmetic

No source gives an exact year for any of this, but a careful count
makes one possible. The same 1604 chronicle names ten rulers of
Taycanamo's line who governed in the roughly hundred and forty years
between the Spanish conquest and its own writing; applying that same
average reign length to the seven rulers who came between Ñancen-pinco
and the kingdom's last independent king places Ñancen-pinco's own
conquests somewhere around 1370 - and the true founding of the kingdom
of Chimor, as opposed to Taycanamo's original ceremonial lordship,
sometime in the first half of the fourteenth century.
