Chen Baxian had made his name fighting to restore order after Hou
Jing's rebellion tore apart the Liang dynasty he served, joining Xiao
Yi in driving the rebel from power before turning, within a few years,
on the Liang ruling house itself. In 557 he murdered the last Liang
emperor, Jingdi, and founded his own dynasty, naming it Chen after the
principality he had held as a semi-independent lord. He consolidated
his new throne by putting down several small uprisings and negotiating
terms with one of their principal leaders rather than fighting every
rebellion to the finish - a pragmatic beginning that gave the new
dynasty, briefly, every appearance of settling into a stable
existence. It did not last: Chen Baxian died suddenly within three
years of his own accession, and Boulger records that the loss "threw
things again into confusion," since the new emperor had no son and
had named a nephew, Wenti, as his successor instead.

## An uneventful reign and a deposed boy

Wenti's own eight-year reign proved, in Boulger's own description,
singularly uneventful - competent enough to defeat several attempts to
revive the fallen Liang and to punish his own general Zhaodi
(Chowti) for breaking his oath of loyalty, but marked by nothing
resembling real distinction. He earned his subjects' genuine
affection without any particularly brilliant act, and when he died in
567 his son Boqing (Petsong) succeeded him only to be deposed by his
own uncle after two years on the throne - the same pattern of short,
interrupted successions that had already ended the Song, the Qi and,
within a decade, would end the Liang as well. By the time Xuandi, a
nephew of the dynasty's own founder, took the throne, the Chen had
already burned through three rulers in a dozen years, and a
fourth-century-and-a-half pattern of division showed no sign of
resolving in the south's own favour.
