Xuandi gave the Chen their longest reign, fourteen years of continued
war against the northern kingdom of Zhou, the state that had emerged
from the final collapse of the old Northern Wei. It was, even by
Boulger's own account, a period of survival rather than achievement -
the dynasty held its own without ever meaningfully expanding it, one
ruler among several small, contending states in an age that had, by
this point, been fractured for more than a century and a half. The
throne passed on Xuandi's death to his son Houzhu, whose own short
reign would close out both his family's rule and the entire era of
division the Chen's four predecessor dynasties had shared with the
rival kingdoms of the north.

## An army the south could not stop

By the 580s a new power had absorbed the north entirely. Yang Jian, a
general who had taken the throne of the northern Zhou state and
founded his own Sui dynasty, spent years consolidating his authority
before turning south. In 589 his forces crossed the Yangtze, entered
the Chen capital, and subdued a country Boulger describes as having
welcomed its own conqueror rather than resisting him. Houzhu was
deposed and withdrew into private life, where he lived on for
twenty-four more years - long enough to outlast by decades the
dynasty whose own collapse he had presided over.

## The dynasty history forgot to praise

Boulger's own final verdict on the Chen is the bluntest judgment he
passes on any ruling house in this whole period: "certainly the
least notable of all the families which have ruled in China." Five
rulers in thirty-two years, none but the founder judged worthy of his
position, closing not in some singular catastrophe but in a
conquest the population itself seemed relieved to accept. What made
589 significant was never the Chen's own fall in isolation, but what
it opened: the reunion, under Yang Jian's new Sui dynasty, of an
empire that had been divided into hostile, independent states since
the collapse of the Han - the north and south finally answering, after
a century and a half apart, to a single throne again.
