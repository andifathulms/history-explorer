Tiglath-Pileser I ruled at the end of this state's life and was the furthest it
ever reached. His inscriptions are also the point at which Assyrian royal writing
becomes recognisably itself — the annual campaign, the count, the boast — and
they are strange in a way the later versions are not.

## To the Upper Sea

The claim is geographical and it is enormous: from beyond the Lower Zab to the
Upper Sea of the setting sun, meaning the Mediterranean.

The Nairi lands north in the mountains were campaigned in three times, and thirty
kings of Nairi were brought in submission, hostages taken, yoke-broken horses
received as gifts, tribute and toll imposed. Then Lebanon, for cedar for the
temple of Anu and Adad at Ashur — the logs cut and carried away, as every
Mesopotamian king who could reach that far had done and would do.

Then the coast. Byblos, Sidon and Arvad paid tribute. And Tiglath-Pileser went to
sea in Arvadite ships, from Arvad to Samuri, a journey he measures in the
overland unit — three double-hours by land — which is how a man from the Tigris
records a distance he has just travelled by water for the first time.

And in the middle of the sea he killed a *nahiru*, which he explains for his
readers: *which they call a "sea-horse."*

An Assyrian king went whaling. He thought it worth putting in the same
inscription as the thirty kings and the cedar, and he thought his audience at
Ashur would need the animal glossed.

## The count

The hunting records are kept with the same apparatus as the campaigns, and the
numbers are the point of them.

Four wild bulls, mighty and of monstrous size, killed in the desert in the
country of Mitanni near Araziki opposite the land of Hatti, with the bow, the
iron spear and sharp darts; their hides and horns brought to Ashur. Ten bull
elephants killed in the country of Harran and along the Habur, four more caught
alive; the hides, the tusks and the live elephants brought to Ashur.

Then: one hundred and twenty lions killed on foot, and eight hundred more from
the chariot.

Nine hundred and twenty lions is not a plausible personal tally and it is not
meant to be read as an afternoon's work. It is a reign-total, kept the way the
conquests are kept, because in this system killing lions and taking cities are
the same activity — the king imposing order on what is outside it — and both
belong in the ledger.

The elephants are worth pausing on for a different reason. There were elephants
on the Habur in the twelfth century BC, and Assyrian kings hunted them there.
They were gone within a few hundred years, and the hunting is part of why.

## What it was the peak of

Everything in these texts is about reach: the sea in the west, the mountains in
the north, animals brought back alive to be looked at in the capital, timber
carried the length of the Fertile Crescent for a temple roof.

None of it was held. There is no Assyrian province on the Mediterranean at the
end of this reign and no garrison at Arvad — there is tribute, a hunt, and a
record. What Tiglath-Pileser I established was not a frontier but a claim about
what an Assyrian king was for, and three centuries later a much larger state
would go back over exactly this ground and this time keep it.

He died in 1076, and what happened to Assyria immediately afterwards was not a
defeat.
