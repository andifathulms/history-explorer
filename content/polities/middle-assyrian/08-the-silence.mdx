Nobody conquered Assyria. That is the difficulty with the ending, and there is
no agent anywhere to name for it.

What happened, over the decades after Tiglath-Pileser I's death in 1076, is
that Aramaean groups moved across the country between the Assyrian core and the
Euphrates and stayed. There is no battle anybody names, no capital taken, no
king deposed. The provinces west of the Tigris stop appearing in the record;
the Habur is gone; the routes to the Mediterranean are gone. By the death of
Ashur-bel-kala in 1056 Assyria held not much beyond the triangle of Ashur,
Nineveh and Arbela — and then, for something like a hundred and forty years, it
stops writing.

Not entirely: there are king lists, there are eponym lists, there are scraps.
But the archives Postgate works from end, and the royal inscriptions become
brief and few, and the century and a half between this kingdom and the empire
that follows it is the darkest stretch in Mesopotamian history after the Bronze
Age collapse itself.

None of the ordinary ways of describing how a state ends quite fits what
happened here. The state did not break into pieces, which rules out the
plainest reading of a collapse. It was not absorbed into anybody else's
kingdom, since it was still there in 911, with the same god, the same title,
the same city and, when the documents resume, recognisably the same
institutions. It was not conquered, because a conquest needs a conqueror and
none is named anywhere in the record. What actually happened sits closest to
a state shrinking back onto its own core and going quiet — the least wrong of
the available descriptions, and no more than that.

## The same state, later

In 911 Adad-nirari II began the reconquest, and the empire everybody means by
Assyria starts there.

Nothing connects the two in the ordinary sense. Nobody claimed the earlier
kingdom's legitimacy, because there was no rival claim to answer; nobody
conquered anybody. What resumed in 911 was the same city, the same god, the
same royal title, the same provincial machinery and, as far as anyone can tell,
the same families.

The same thing is true of the two Babylons, separated by a thousand years
instead of a hundred and forty, and of the two Elams. In each case the ordinary
vocabulary fails: it is not a succession, not a conquest, not a restoration by
anybody's claim, and not a new state either. It is a place going quiet and then
starting again.

## Why that is hard to say

Because the relation has no date. There is no morning on which Assyria resumed
being Assyria — only a stretch of about a hundred and forty years during which
it was quieter, at the end of which somebody starts campaigning and writing it
down again.

And because the obvious test is the wrong one. Sharing a capital proves
nothing: plenty of unrelated states have governed from the same city after a
gap — the Umayyads and the Burids at Damascus, the Idrisids and the Marinids at
Fez, Han and Tang at Chang'an. This case does not even share a capital, since
the earlier kingdom ruled from Ashur and the later empire from Nineveh and
Nimrud.

The test that works is whether the sources treat the two as periods of one
continuous object. On that test Assyria is one thing with a long silence in the
middle, and the silence is the only reason it needs explaining at all.
