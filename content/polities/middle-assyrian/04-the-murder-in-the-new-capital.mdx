Tukulti-Ninurta I reigned thirty-six years and did two things that no Assyrian
king had done. He conquered Babylonia, and he left Ashur.

In about 1225 he took Babylon, deported the Kassite king Kashtiliash IV in
chains, and carried the statue of Marduk north. It is the first time Assyria
held Babylonia and it lasted roughly seven years. The relationship between the
two states that Sennacherib would poison for good in 689 — sacking the city,
demolishing its temples, diverting a canal over the site, and setting off a
chain that ended with his grandsons at war and, eventually, with Nabopolassar —
begins with this campaign. An Assyrian epic was written about it, and a
Babylonian text was written accusing Tukulti-Ninurta of sacrilege.

## Kar-Tukulti-Ninurta

Around 1233 he built a new capital directly across the Tigris from Ashur and
moved into it, naming it after himself: Port Tukulti-Ninurta.

A king of Ashur leaving Ashur is not a change of address. The city and the god
have the same name; the royal title is *iššiak Aššur*, the god's steward, and
the stewardship is of that place. Sargon II tried the same manoeuvre at
Khorsabad seven hundred years later. Both new capitals were abandoned within a
generation of their builder's death, and both builders died violently.

In about 1207 Tukulti-Ninurta was besieged in his own new city by a conspiracy
led by his son, and killed.

## The rule and the outcome

Assyria had a rule for the succession: the king designated an heir. Seven
hundred years later Esarhaddon would give that rule the most elaborate
instrument the ancient world produced — an oath sworn by every subject and
vassal of the empire — and the two sons it was written to protect fought a
four-year civil war anyway.

Rules and outcomes are different objects, and Mesopotamia keeps demonstrating
it in both directions. Babylon under the Neo-Babylonian kings had three coups
in five transitions. Ur III and Old Babylon had eleven and five flawless
father-to-son successions between them and never wrote down a rule at all. Here
the rule is clear and the greatest reign in the kingdom's history ends with the
king killed by his own son.

Four records, four different relationships between the rule and what happened,
and the field says the same word or nothing. That is the field working
correctly and it is also the limit of what a coded vocabulary can carry, which
is why the chapters exist.

## After

The empire came apart within a decade. Babylonia was lost, the west was lost,
the new capital was abandoned, and Assyria did not reach the same extent again
for a century — until Tiglath-Pileser I, the greatest of these kings, who
reached the Mediterranean, cut cedars in Lebanon, and hunted elephants in
Syria, all of which he recorded at length.
