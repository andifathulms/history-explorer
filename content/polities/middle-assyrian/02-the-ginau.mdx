Every province of the Middle Assyrian kingdom owed a fixed recurring delivery to
the temple of Ashur: grain, oil, honey, fruit, in specified quantities, on a
schedule. It was called the *ginā'u*, the regular offering. A central office
recorded what each province owed, what each province sent, when it arrived and
who brought it, and chased what was late.

Those records survive. Postgate works through them, and through nine other
government archives from Ashur and the provinces, and the resulting picture is
the reason this record's institutions are coded from him rather than from a
narrative of kings.

It is a tax in the strict sense — assessed in advance, scheduled, delivered,
audited, arrears pursued — and it is functioning in the thirteenth century BC.
Between this and the next thing in this corpus that can be described at the same
resolution lie the Neo-Assyrian provincial system, which is six hundred years
later, and the Neo-Babylonian temple accounts, which are eight hundred.

## What writing did

Postgate's larger argument, and the reason his book is called what it is, is that
the writing was not a record of the administration but a part of it. Middle
Assyrian officials wrote to each other constantly, sealed and archived what they
wrote, and structured their responsibilities around documents that could be
produced later. A governor's obligation was defined by what the tablet said. The
practice of government and the practice of writing were the same practice.

That has a consequence for this site's coding. The `military_basis` field here
reads `land-grant, conscript`, and the first value is the *ilku*: land held from
the crown against an obligation of service. Almost every polity in this corpus
has some version of that, and on most pages it is coded from a historian
describing the arrangement in general terms. Here the men owed, the equipment
owed and the substitutions permitted are itemised in archives kept for the
purpose, and the field is filled from the paperwork.

## Deportation, early

The other institution this kingdom is running three centuries ahead of schedule
is the resettlement of conquered populations. The Neo-Assyrian record on this site
has a whole chapter on deportation as a system — populations moved, mixed and
resettled as military colonists and as labour, deliberately and at enormous scale.
That record dates the systematisation to Tiglath-Pileser III's reorganisation
after 745.

The practice starts here. Tukulti-Ninurta I claims tens of thousands of people
moved out of Babylonia and the east, and the administrative texts show deportees
settled on state land and issued rations. The scale is smaller and the intent is
recognisably the same.

So the machinery this corpus credits to the Neo-Assyrian empire — provinces with
appointed governors, an assessed tax, service land, systematic resettlement,
government by archive — is substantially present six hundred years earlier in the
same city. What 745 did was not invent it. What 745 did was apply it to a territory
five times the size, and this record's `reach_km2` of 400,000 against the later
record's 1,400,000 is the whole of that difference in one line.
