Every province of the Middle Assyrian kingdom owed a fixed recurring delivery
to the temple of Ashur: grain, oil, honey, fruit, in specified quantities, on a
schedule. It was called the *ginā'u*, the regular offering. A central office
recorded what each province owed, what each province sent, when it arrived and
who brought it, and chased what was late.

Those records survive. Postgate works through them, and through nine other
government archives from Ashur and the provinces, and what comes out is a
description of how the state worked that no narrative of kings could supply.

It is a tax in the strict sense — assessed in advance, scheduled, delivered,
audited, arrears pursued — and it is functioning in the thirteenth century BC.
The next tax system anywhere that can be described at the same resolution is
the Neo-Assyrian provincial apparatus, six hundred years later, and after that
the Neo-Babylonian temple accounts, eight hundred.

## What writing did

Postgate's larger argument, and the reason his book is called what it is, is
that the writing was not a record of the administration but a part of it.
Middle Assyrian officials wrote to each other constantly, sealed and archived
what they wrote, and structured their responsibilities around documents that
could be produced later. A governor's obligation was defined by what the tablet
said. The practice of government and the practice of writing were the same
practice.

The army worked the same way. The *ilku* was land held from the crown against
an obligation of service, and versions of that arrangement turn up in most
premodern states — usually known from a historian describing it in general
terms. Here the men owed, the equipment owed and the substitutions permitted
are itemised in archives kept for the purpose.

## Deportation, early

The other institution this kingdom is running three centuries ahead of schedule
is the resettlement of conquered populations. Deportation as a system —
populations moved, mixed and resettled as military colonists and as labour,
deliberately and at enormous scale — is what the later Assyrian empire is most
notorious for, and it is usually dated to Tiglath-Pileser III's reorganisation
after 745.

The practice starts here. Tukulti-Ninurta I claims tens of thousands of people
moved out of Babylonia and the east, and the administrative texts show
deportees settled on state land and issued rations. The scale is smaller and
the intent is recognisably the same.

So the machinery usually credited to the Neo-Assyrian empire — provinces with
appointed governors, an assessed tax, service land, systematic resettlement,
government by archive — is substantially present six hundred years earlier in
the same city. What 745 did was not invent it, but apply it to about three and
a half times as much ground.
