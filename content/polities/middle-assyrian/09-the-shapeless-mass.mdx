The Middle Assyrian kingdom has two afterlives. One is the empire that resumed
its work three centuries later and is what everybody means by Assyria. The
other is what happened to its capital.

## Ashur

Ashur was the city, the god and the country, and the kingdom's kings called
themselves the god's steward of that place. It was where the archives were
kept, where the assessed tax was audited, where the king was crowned and
buried.

In 1840 Austen Henry Layard rode out to look at it, though he did not know that
was what he was looking at. He calls it Qalat Sherqat and describes it as a
vast, shapeless mass, covered with grass, showing scarcely any trace of the
work of man except where the winter rains had cut ravines down its almost
perpendicular sides and laid its contents open.

He and his companion spent the better part of a day going over the heaps of
earth and brick. There was an Arab tradition of strange figures carved in black
stone somewhere in the ruins; they looked and did not find them. What they did
find was a few fragments of pottery and some inscribed bricks — enough, Layard
noted, to prove that the mound had been built by the same people who built
Nimrud.

That is the whole of it. The capital of a kingdom that had run a provincial tax
system, a service-land army and a state archive in the thirteenth century BC
was a grassy hill with potsherds in the runoff, and the visitors could
establish nothing about it beyond that somebody Assyrian had made it.

## The paperwork

What survived instead was the writing, and it survived because clay does.

The archives Postgate works from — the tax records, the land grants, the
inventories of what each province owed — came out of the ground at Ashur when
the German expedition dug there from 1903. So did the Middle Assyrian Laws, the
fullest legal text of the Late Bronze Age, and the palace edicts regulating the
royal household.

The result is an unusual inversion. This kingdom is invisible in the standard
histories, is not the Assyria of the prophets or the museums, has almost no
famous objects, and is the best-documented administrative state of its century
anywhere in the world.

## The other afterlife

The same machinery did start again, under the ninth-century kings everyone
means by Assyria, for the reasons already given.

What it started again with is the point worth closing on. The empire of
Ashurbanipal is usually credited with inventing the provincial governors, the
assessed tax, the land held for service, the resettlement of conquered
populations, the whole apparatus of government by archive. It inherited all
of it. What it added was the scale, and the scale is the reason one of the
two Assyrias is famous and the other is a mound somebody walked over in 1840
without recognising it.
