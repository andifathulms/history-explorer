There are two Assyrian empires, and they are the same city, the same god, the
same royal title and very nearly the same institutions. This one runs from 1363
to 1056. The one everybody means by the word runs from 911 to 609, and begins a
hundred and forty years after this one ends.

Whether that makes them one state or two is a real question and not a
bookkeeping one. Some historians of Mesopotamia treat Babylon as a single
continuous object across seventeen centuries, on the reasonable ground that the
city, the gods and the language persist through every change of dynasty. The
other way of counting asks what the state was doing, and by that test the
interruption matters: in the years between these two Assyrias the country lost
every province, stopped campaigning and stopped writing.

Nothing passed between them in the ordinary sense. Nobody claimed the earlier
kingdom's legitimacy, because there was nobody to claim it against; nobody
conquered anybody. What happened is that the same place, under the same god,
started doing the same things again after a long silence — and there is no
ordinary word for that relation, which is why it keeps having to be described
rather than named.

## Out from under Mitanni

Ashur in 1400 was a city-state paying tribute to Mitanni. Seen from the other
side, Ashur was one of the dependencies that made Mitanni a great power, and
the Mitannian king treated its ruler as a subject.

Ashur-uballit I, who came to the throne around 1363, ended that. He took
advantage of the wars in which Suppiluliuma I was dismantling Mitanni from the
west to take the eastern half, and then he did something more consequential
than any conquest. He wrote to Egypt.

The letter is in the Amarna archive. Ashur-uballit addresses Akhenaten as a
brother, which is the term great kings used to each other, asks for gold, and
complains about the treatment of his envoys.

<Figure id="ashur-uballit-letter" /> It provoked a furious letter from
Burna-Buriash of Babylon to the same pharaoh, pointing out that the Assyrians
were his own vassals and had no business writing to anybody, and asking Egypt
not to receive them.

Egypt received them. That exchange is the moment Assyria enters the club, and
it is preserved because a heretic pharaoh's abandoned capital happened to keep
its foreign correspondence and be excavated in 1887. Almost everything known
about this century depends on accidents of that kind.
