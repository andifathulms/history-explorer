This site now carries Assyria twice. The Neo-Assyrian record covers 911 to 609
and is the empire everyone means by the word. This one covers 1363 to 1056, ends
a hundred and forty years before the other begins, and is the same city, the same
god, the same royal title and very nearly the same institutions.

That needs justifying rather than assuming, and the justification is sitting two
entries away in the same region: Babylon is here twice as well, as the First
Dynasty and as the Neo-Babylonian empire, separated by a thousand years. Beaulieu,
whose history this site's Babylonian records are drafted from, treats Babylon as
one continuous object across seventeen centuries, and the reason this corpus does
not is that it measures polities and not cities. Two states ruled from Babylon
with a millennium and four foreign dynasties in between are two polities. By
exactly the same test, two Assyrian states separated by a period in which Assyria
lost every province, stopped campaigning and stopped writing are two polities.

What the pairs also do is expose something this site cannot say. There is no edge
between the two Babylons and none between the two Assyrias, because succession is
not what happened: nobody claimed anybody's legitimacy, nobody conquered anybody,
and the vocabulary has no value for *the same polity, later, after an
interruption*. The last chapter of this record comes back to it.

## Out from under Mitanni

Ashur in 1400 was a city-state paying tribute to Mitanni. This corpus's Mitanni
record describes the arrangement from the other side: Ashur was one of the
dependencies that made Mitanni a great power, and the Mitannian king treated its
ruler as a subject.

Ashur-uballit I, who came to the throne around 1363, ended that. He took advantage
of the wars in which Suppiluliuma I was dismantling Mitanni from the west — the
subject of the Hittite record's fourth chapter — to take the eastern half, and
then he did something more consequential than any conquest. He wrote to Egypt.

The letter is in the Amarna archive. Ashur-uballit addresses Akhenaten as a
brother, which is the term great kings used to each other, asks for gold, and
complains about the treatment of his envoys. It provoked a furious letter from
Burna-Buriash of Babylon to the same pharaoh, pointing out that the Assyrians were
his own vassals and had no business writing to anybody, and asking Egypt not to
receive them.

Egypt received them. That exchange is the moment Assyria enters the club, and it
is preserved because a heretic pharaoh's abandoned capital happened to keep its
foreign correspondence and be excavated in 1887. This region's records depend on
that kind of accident more than any other region on this site.
