The reign after the furthest reach is documented by almost nothing, and the
almost-nothing is informative.

Assur-bel-kala, Tiglath-Pileser's son, came to the throne — Luckenbill's guess is
*probably after a long struggle*, which is the kind of sentence an editor writes
when the succession has left no record and the next reign has to be explained
somehow.

## Where the text is

His main surviving inscription is not on a stele, a temple wall or an obelisk. It
is written on the back of the torso of a female statue, now in the British
Museum.

<Figure id="ashur-bel-kala-statue" />

And Luckenbill's reading of who it was for is the whole chapter: the text *seems
to have been intended for his city governors, as a reminder of their allegiance
to him*.

It opens the way any Assyrian royal inscription opens, with the titulary — king
of the universe, the mighty king, king of Assyria — and the ancestry, his father
and his grandfather each given the same titles. Then a damaged passage about
statues and about the curses placed on them. Then the standard closing formula
against anyone who alters the inscription or the name: may the god Hanish and the
gods of the land of Amurru smite him in open battle.

## What has changed

Compare it with what his father had been writing a few decades earlier.

Tiglath-Pileser's inscriptions count thirty submitting kings, cedar cut in
Lebanon, a sea crossing, a whale, nine hundred and twenty lions. His son's
inscription counts nothing. It has no campaign, no tribute list, no building
programme of any scale. What it has is a genealogy and a curse, addressed to the
men who administered the cities of Assyria.

A king does not remind his own governors of their allegiance when it is not in
question. The document is a small piece of evidence, and it is nearly the only
piece, that the thing beginning to fail was not the frontier but the relationship
between the centre and the men holding the provinces for it.

## The shape of the evidence itself

There is a second lesson in it, and it is about what survives.

The material for this reign is a statue torso, a stele that may or may not be
his, and two tablet fragments that were catalogued as a religious text until a
scholar in 1917 noticed that one of them was historical. That is the whole
dossier for the son of the greatest king the Middle Assyrian state produced.

What the record is about to do is not stop because Assyria was destroyed. It is
about to stop because the apparatus that produced records — the scribes, the
provincial administration, the campaigns worth commemorating — was contracting
faster than the territory was. The silence comes first.
