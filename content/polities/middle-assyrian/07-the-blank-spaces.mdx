There is an object in the British Museum, said to have come from Kuyunjik, that
nobody has been able to assign to a king. It is called the Broken Obelisk, and it
has been furnishing scholars with arguments, in Luckenbill's phrase, ever since
its inscription became known.

## The problem

The obelisk carries a long introduction recording the conquests and hunting
exploits of an Assyrian king — in the third person. At the end there is a
building inscription, and that one is in the first person.

Two voices, one monument. The obvious reading has been that the introduction
belongs to Tiglath-Pileser I, whose campaigns and hunts it resembles, and the
first-person section to some later king who put the thing up.

Which later king depends on evidence that keeps moving. When eponym lists and the
annals of Adad-nirari II came to light, it seemed almost certain to Luckenbill
that the obelisk in its final form belonged to that reign: the building section
mentions a canal dug by Ashur-dan and restored thirty years later, which fits
Ashur-dan II and his son Adad-nirari II better than any other pair, and it
mentions the rebuilding of the city of Apku, which Adad-nirari's own annals
describe in detail.

Julius Lewy argued instead that the third-person narrative was Tukulti-Ninurta I,
three centuries earlier — plausible, because Adad-nirari II's own son was named
after that king. Luckenbill records the argument, says that until eponym lists
turn up containing the names in the obelisk any solution *must be received with
caution*, and then notes that the traces of a royal name in one damaged line
appear to be a contemporary of Tiglath-Pileser I, in which case Lewy's argument
falls.

## Where the numbers should be

Luckenbill's own view is the one worth keeping, and it rests on a physical
detail.

He believes the monument was inscribed with the annals and hunting exploits of
Tiglath-Pileser I, and that it was **never finished** — because blank spaces are
left in the stone where the numbers of slain beasts were to stand. Then, long
afterwards, Adad-nirari II *acquired merit* by setting the unfinished thing up
and adding a short inscription of his own to the end.

A monument cut for one king, abandoned with the kill-counts unengraved, and
raised generations later by a different king who signed it.

## Why it belongs here

Nothing states the condition of Assyria between Tiglath-Pileser I and its
recovery more exactly than that.

The state that could commission the stone could not finish it. Whatever
interrupted the work — the reign that ended, the scribe who was not replaced, the
campaign whose totals never arrived — the object sat with holes in it where the
achievements went. And when Assyria came back, a century and a half later, the
first thing one of its kings did with the abandoned monument was stand it up and
put his name on it.

He was not being fraudulent. He was doing exactly what Ur-Nammu did when he built
his ziggurat around Naram-Sin's: claiming the sequence. What makes this one
harder to read is that the object he claimed had never been completed, so the
argument about who it belongs to is an argument nobody in antiquity had settled
either.
