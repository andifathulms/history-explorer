Adad-nirari I, and then his son Shalmaneser I, took what was left of Mitanni and
turned it into Assyrian provinces under Assyrian governors. The country the
Assyrians called Hanigalbat — northern Mesopotamia between the Tigris and the
Euphrates, the Habur triangle, the Mitannian heartland — stopped being a kingdom
around 1260 and became administered territory.

That is the event this corpus's Mitanni record has as its ending and, until this
page existed, could not attribute. Its `ended.by` field was empty, with a comment
explaining that the conquerors were the Middle Assyrian kings and that this site's
only Assyrian entry began in 911, three and a half centuries later and, for the
purposes of the edges, a different polity. The field now names this record, and
`mitanni -> middle-assyrian` is an edge with both ends inside the region.

## What that resolved and what it did not

It is worth being exact, because the fix is smaller than it looks. Mitanni's
`ended.by` was empty rather than wrong: it was a correct refusal to name a
polity the site did not carry, and it has been a correct refusal for as long as it
stood. Nothing about Mitanni's history changed when this page was written. What
changed is that the corpus can now say who did it.

What did not change: Mitanni still has one coded institution out of four, no
capital that has ever been located, and a record reconstructed almost entirely
from other people's documents — Hittite treaties, Egyptian letters, and now
Assyrian provincial administration. Adding the polity that ended it does not make
it legible. It makes the illegibility attributable.

## Facing west

The strategic consequence is the one the Hittite record's fifth chapter is about.
With Hanigalbat absorbed, Assyria stood on the Euphrates facing Hatti directly,
and by the 1230s Tukulti-Ninurta I would defeat a Hittite army and boast in an
inscription about the number of prisoners taken.

That is why Hattusili III wanted a treaty with Egypt in 1259. The southern
frontier had been a war for two centuries and was, by then, the less dangerous
one. This site's Hittite and Egyptian records both carry the treaty; this record
is the reason it was worth having, and none of the three is joined to the others
by an edge, because none of it is succession.

Three great powers, two treaties, a marriage, forty years of correspondence, and
in this data model they touch at no point. Hard rule 7 says an edgeless polity is
complete, and the corollary this region keeps demonstrating is that a region full
of intense, well-documented, mutually determining relationships can still have
almost nothing to draw.
