Adad-nirari I, and then his son Shalmaneser I, took what was left of Mitanni
and turned it into Assyrian provinces under Assyrian governors. The country the
Assyrians called Hanigalbat — northern Mesopotamia between the Tigris and the
Euphrates, the Habur triangle, the Mitannian heartland — stopped being a
kingdom around 1260 and became administered territory.

<Figure id="adad-nirari-sword" />

That is the end of Mitanni, and it is worth noticing who ended it. Suppiluliuma
had broken the kingdom from the west two generations earlier and taken its
Syrian empire; what he left standing was a rump. The Assyrians finished it,
quietly, over about thirty years, and turned it into a province.

## What that leaves of Mitanni

Almost nothing of its own. A great power of two centuries with no capital that
has ever been located, and a history reconstructed entirely from other people's
documents — Hittite treaties, Egyptian letters, and now Assyrian provincial
administration. Adding the polity that ended it does not make it legible. It
makes the illegibility attributable.

## Facing west

The strategic consequence is the one the Hittite record's fifth chapter is
about. With Hanigalbat absorbed, Assyria stood on the Euphrates facing Hatti
directly, and by the 1230s Tukulti-Ninurta I would defeat a Hittite army and
boast in an inscription about the number of prisoners taken.

That is why Hattusili III wanted a treaty with Egypt in 1259. The southern
frontier had been a war for two centuries and was, by then, the less dangerous
one. The famous peace between Hatti and Egypt exists because a third power had
made the Hittite position in northern Mesopotamia untenable, and that third
power is not a party to it.

Three great powers, two treaties, a marriage and forty years of correspondence,
and not one of the three ever succeeded, inherited or governed either of the
others. They determined each other completely and are connected by nothing that
an account of successions would register.
