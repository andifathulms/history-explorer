Nectanebo II ruled with the threat of a Persian reconquest hanging over
every year of his reign, and used the resources of a kingdom under that
threat to build more extensively than any pharaoh of the preceding
century. The choice is worth pausing on separately from the war that
eventually ended it.

## Building against the odds

Restorations and new construction under this king are attested at temples
across the whole length of the Nile valley he controlled, from the Delta
to the First Cataract and, in one case, an oasis well beyond it: a temple
to Amun at Siwa, hundreds of kilometres into the western desert. The most
consequential single project, in hindsight, was a temple to Isis on the
small island of Philae near Aswan, where Nectanebo II built the first
pylon and colonnade of what would grow, under the Ptolemaic and Roman
rulers who followed him by centuries, into one of the last functioning
centres of the old Egyptian religion anywhere — a cult of Isis at Philae
that continued for roughly nine hundred years after this king's own reign
ended, into the sixth century AD.

## What building this much, this late, was for

A king whose own claim to the throne had come from a coup rather than an
orderly succession, ruling a restored kingship barely two generations old
and facing the same empire that had already conquered Egypt once within
living memory, had every practical reason to spend his revenue on defence
rather than stone. That he spent it on both at once says something about
what temple-building was actually doing for a king in this position: not
a luxury competing with the war effort, but a public assertion, renewed at
site after site, that the kingship itself was permanent even while the
man holding it knew exactly how contingent his own position was.

## The building nobody intended

None of the men who commissioned Philae's earliest stonework could have
known that a chapel raised there in an ordinary Late Period building
programme would still be receiving worshippers when a Roman emperor's own
religion had already displaced the gods Nectanebo built it for everywhere
else. Whatever this king expected to leave behind, an active fifth-century
cult centre one dynasty's ordinary provincial construction project away
from Nubia was not obviously the plan — and it is nonetheless what
outlasted every other part of his reign.
