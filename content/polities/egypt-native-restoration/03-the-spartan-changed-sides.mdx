Teos, Nectanebo I's son and successor, tried something no king of this
period had attempted before him: taking the war to Persian territory
rather than waiting to be invaded. In 360 he led an Egyptian army,
reinforced by a two-hundred-ship fleet and Greek mercenary contingents,
against the Persian-held cities of Phoenicia — and assembled, for the
purpose, two of the most famous soldiers in the Greek world. Chabrias, an
Athenian general, commanded the fleet. Agesilaus, king of Sparta and by
this point in his eighties, commanded a mercenary infantry force in the
field.

## The brother left at home

Teos left his own brother, Tjahapimu, as regent in Egypt while the
campaign was under way in Syria. It was a bad choice. With the king and
the army's most senior loyalists absent, Tjahapimu raised a rebellion at
home and offered the throne to his own son — who happened, by the
accident of the campaign's own command structure, to already be serving
under Teos as commander of the Egyptian infantry currently besieging
Syrian towns. The son's name was Nectanebo.

## The choice that decided it

What made the coup succeed rather than collapse the moment word reached
the army was a decision made by neither Egyptian brother. Chabrias and the
Egyptian fleet stayed loyal to Teos. Agesilaus, the Spartan king Teos had
paid to fight for him, judged the usurper the better bet and brought his
mercenary force over to Nectanebo's side. Deprived of the one contingent
actually capable of contesting Egypt by land, Teos had nothing left to
fight with. He fled to the Persian court at Sidon — the very power his
campaign had been raised to attack — and Artaxerxes II granted him refuge
there rather than treating him as an enemy still worth pursuing.

## A throne only as secure as its rented army

Nectanebo II inherited the kingship this way: not by defeating his uncle
and cousin in battle, but by being the candidate an aging foreign king,
already paid and already in the field, decided was worth switching
allegiance for. Every Egyptian dynasty of this period had leaned on hired
Greek soldiers for its wars; this coup is the moment the arrangement's
other edge showed itself plainly — a kingship's survival decided, in the
end, not by which brother Egypt's own people preferred, but by which one
a Spartan mercenary captain judged more likely to keep paying him.
