Egypt had been a Persian province for a hundred and twenty years, on and
off, when Darius II died in Susa in 404 and the empire's attention turned
to the succession dispute his death set off among his own sons. A Delta
rebel leader named Amyrtaeus, who had already been active against the
Persian garrison for a few years with Cretan mercenary support, used the
distraction to finish the job: Memphis fell, the Persian administration
was expelled, and Amyrtaeus proclaimed himself pharaoh of a country no
Persian army immediately came back to reclaim.

## A restoration that did not restore any one family

Whatever legitimacy Amyrtaeus claimed, he did not pass it to an heir.
Within five years he was overthrown and executed by a rival, Nefaarud, who
founded a new line ruling from Mendes in the eastern Delta rather than
continuing at Sais. That dynasty itself lasted less than twenty years and
ended the same way most transitions in this period would: not through
foreign conquest, but through an Egyptian general deciding an existing
king's own son was not worth deferring to.

## Sixty years, three capitals

By the time a general named Nectanebo seized the throne in 380 and founded
a third dynasty at Sebennytos, further west in the Delta, native Egyptian
kingship had changed capital twice in twenty-four years without any of the
changes being forced by an outside power. What held the restored kingship
together across those transitions was not a family's claim on the office —
none of the three houses that held it were related to each other — but
the office's own persistence: whoever won the latest contest for it simply
became pharaoh in the full traditional style, and the country's
administration carried on beneath him largely undisturbed by which
general currently sat above it.
