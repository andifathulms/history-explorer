A legend attached itself to Nectanebo II within a few centuries of his
death that is worth examining for what it reveals about later expectation
rather than for anything it says about the ninth-to-last decade of the
fourth century BC. The *Alexander Romance*, a work of fiction compiled and
expanded across the Hellenistic and Roman periods and eventually
translated into more languages than almost any other ancient text, claims
that Nectanebo did not simply flee to Nubia after his final defeat. He
fled, the story says, to Macedonia — disguised as an Egyptian magician —

where he seduced Queen Olympias in the shape of the god Amun and fathered
the son who became Alexander the Great.

## What the story cannot be

Nothing about the claim survives contact with the ordinary chronology.
Nectanebo's flight from Egypt is dated to 343 BC; Alexander was already
thirteen years old by then, born in 356 to a marriage that had taken place
years before Nectanebo lost his throne. No version of the legend attempts
to reconcile the dates, because reconciling them was never the point.

## What the story is actually doing

Read as history, the tale is worthless. Read as a document of what an
Egyptian audience under later foreign rule wanted to be true, it is
exactly the same move Egyptian religious institutions had made twice
before within living memory of this dynasty — Kushite kings taking the
pharaonic titulary, Saite kings reaching two thousand years back to Old
Kingdom precedent — pushed one step further, into outright invention: if
Egypt could no longer produce its own native pharaoh, at least the
conqueror who finally ended Persian rule over the country a decade later
could be quietly reclaimed as one. Alexander himself, on his own arrival
in Egypt, was welcomed as a liberator and crowned in the traditional
manner at Memphis — a coronation the story's authors had every reason to
want explained by more than politics.

The legend says nothing reliable about who fathered Alexander. It says a
great deal about how badly, within a few generations of this dynasty's
fall, an Egyptian audience wanted a native pharaoh's blood in the family
of the king who ended their occupation.
