Artaxerxes III had already crushed a wave of satrapal revolts across the
rest of the Persian empire by the time he turned to the one province that
had stayed independent longer than any other Persian loss: Egypt, still
ruled by the general who had taken the throne from his own uncle two
decades earlier.

## The invasion that did not stall

The 373 invasion had failed on the approach to Memphis, undone by a
commanders' quarrel and the Nile's own calendar. Artaxerxes' campaign,
arriving in the winter of 343 with the Nile flood already receded and
Mentor of Rhodes commanding a Greek mercenary contingent fighting for the
Persian side this time, did not make the same mistake. Pelusium and the
Delta fortresses fell in sequence rather than being bypassed, and
Nectanebo's own mixed army of Egyptians, Libyans and Greek mercenaries —
the same recipe that had built and repeatedly saved this restored kingship
for sixty years — was broken in the field before it could fall back on
Memphis in any organised way.

## A flight rather than a surrender

Nectanebo did not stay to negotiate terms or accept a reduced status as a
Persian client, the way some Delta kings during the fragmented centuries
before this one had. He gathered what treasure and following he could and
withdrew south, into Nubia, beyond the reach of the army that had just
beaten him — and, on the scattered later evidence, seems to have kept some
kind of following and local standing there rather than simply
disappearing. He never returned, and no source records him attempting to.

## What ended for good

Artaxerxes installed a Persian satrap at Memphis and this time the
administration held: Egypt was reincorporated into the Achaemenid empire
directly, without the sixty-year interruption that had followed Cambyses'
own conquest two centuries earlier, and stayed Persian territory until
Alexander took it from the Achaemenid empire in turn. No Egyptian-born
king ever again held the full traditional office this dynasty had spent
sixty years restoring, defending and finally losing — a fact later
generations found hard enough to accept that at least one of them wrote
Nectanebo back into history as the secret father of the man who conquered
Persia itself.
