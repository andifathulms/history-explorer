Long before Simuka rose to prominence, the Andhra country was a
patchwork of separate chiefly groups — powerful enough on their own
account that Ashoka's own inscriptions counted them, alongside the
hereditary Bhojas, among peoples still standing outside direct Mauryan
rule even at the height of that empire's reach. Bindusara had forced
earlier generations of these chiefs into subservience to Magadha, but
by the time Ashoka succeeded his father the Andhras remained a loose
confederation rather than a single kingdom answering to one leader.

## A leader who could not have known his own legacy

Simuka became, around 271 BC, the first to weld several of these
Andhra groups into recognizing him as their shared chief. He held that
position for twenty-three years without ever founding what could fairly
be called an independent state — the Mauryan empire, still formidable
under Ashoka, remained the region's nominal overlord throughout his own
lifetime, and the Andhras were content, for the time being, with a
semi-independent standing under its shadow. Later Puranic tradition
would remember him as a wicked king eventually dethroned and killed,
though the same tradition also credits him, if a Jain legend naming an
early Satavahana ruler refers to him, with patronizing Jain temples
during his earlier years.

## A brother who watched an empire crumble

Simuka's brother and successor, Kanha, continued to respect Mauryan
overlordship much as his predecessor had, commissioning a cave shrine
at Nasik in a style the source calls "Ashokan in inspiration and
Satavahana in execution" — physical evidence that the Andhra chiefs
still worked within a cultural and political order set from Pataliputra
even as they administered their own territory. It was only after
Ashoka's own death, and the disorder that followed at the Mauryan
capital, that Kanha found the opportunity to break fully from that
overlordship and claim independent status for the lands his family had
come to govern.
