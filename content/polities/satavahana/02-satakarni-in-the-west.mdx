Between Kanha's break from Mauryan overlordship and Gautamiputra's own
recovery of the family's lost western territory three centuries later,
the dynasty's history survives mainly in the boasts of other kings'
inscriptions and the name of one Satavahana ruler remembered less for
war than for poetry.

## A king named in a rival's own victory inscription

Kharavela, the Jain king of Kalinga on the eastern coast, left behind
one of ancient India's most detailed royal inscriptions, cut into the
rock of the Hathigumpha cave and recounting his own reign year by year.
In its second year, by the inscription's own account, Kharavela sent a
large army of horses, elephants and infantry into the west, in a
passage naming a king called Satakarni - almost certainly one of the
early Satavahana rulers, since the dynasty's kings routinely took that
title as a name in its own right. Later in the same reign Kharavela
marched against Magadha itself and claimed to have humbled its king;
whether his dealings with Satakarni in the west were alliance or
rivalry remains disputed, but the inscription is the clearest outside
evidence that a Satavahana king was already a power worth a rival
monarch's own attention a full century before Gautamiputra's
celebrated reign.

## A king remembered for a poem instead of a war

Later Puranic king-lists name Hala as the seventeenth Satavahana ruler,
placing his reign around the middle of the first century AD - squarely
inside the stretch of dynastic history that produced no monument, no
war record and no foreign inscription naming the family at all. What
Hala left behind instead was literary: he is credited with compiling
the Gathasaptasati, an anthology of seven hundred short verses in the
Maharashtri Prakrit dialect on rural and domestic life, composed at a
time when the more polished literary Sanskrit later used by poets like
Kalidasa had not yet displaced the vernacular tongues as a vehicle for
courtly composition. A grammar and a story-collection in a related
dialect are credited to ministers of the same court. Nothing about
Hala's reign survives in the register of conquest and administration
that the dynasty's early and late kings left behind - but the
anthology attributed to him did more to shape later Indian poetics
than most of his family's recorded battles.
