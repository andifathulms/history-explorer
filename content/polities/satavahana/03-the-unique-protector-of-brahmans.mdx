Between the early Satavahanas and the dynasty's own high point, the
family's control of its western territories in Maharashtra, Gujarat
and Malwa had slipped away entirely to foreign rule — Shaka satraps of
the Kshaharata line, chief among them Nahapana, governing lands the
Satavahanas had once held as their own. It took Gautamiputra Satakarni,
generations after Simuka's own founding, to recover that ground.

## An empire recovered by force

In his eighteenth regnal year, Gautamiputra defeated Nahapana in
battle and, in the source's own words, "put an end to the dynasty of
the Khakharatas" outright. A large hoard of coins bearing Nahapana's
own name, discovered restruck with Gautamiputra's own legend, stands as
physical evidence of the conquest even where inscriptions leave the
precise sequence of events uncertain. The victory recovered not just
territory but the prestige the family had lost across an entire
generation of foreign rule in its own former heartland.

## A eulogy that made a claim about more than conquest

Gautamiputra's own mother, Gautami Balashri, commemorated her son's
reign in a memorial inscription at Nasik that reaches well beyond a
simple list of victories. She styled him ekabamhanasa — a title the
source notes has been read two ways, either "the unique protector of
Brahmans" or, more provocatively, "the sole Brahman" himself, a claim
that would make the dynasty's own royal line religiously as well as
politically distinctive. Whichever reading is correct, the same
inscription credits him with restoring the proper mixing of the four
varnas, an order the eulogy implies foreign rule under the Shakas,
Yavanas and Pahlavas had disturbed. The Nanaghat inscriptions from
earlier generations of the dynasty already show Satavahana kings
sponsoring elaborate Vedic sacrifices, and Gautamiputra's own reign
extended that religious patronage into an explicit claim: that his
conquests had not merely expanded a kingdom but repaired a social order
his family considered themselves uniquely fit to guard.

## A reign whose end outlived its own certainty

Gautamiputra's own inscriptions confirm a reign of twenty-four years,
though scholars remain divided by decades over which calendar years
those actually were — competing equations place his accession anywhere
from the late first century to the early second century AD, none of
them resolved definitively by the evidence available. What is not in
dispute is the scale of what he achieved: no Satavahana king before or
after him is credited with recovering so much lost ground in a single
reign.
