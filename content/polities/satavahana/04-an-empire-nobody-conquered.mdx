Yajna Sri Satakarni, ruling generations after Gautamiputra's own
recovery of the west, is remembered as the last great king of the
dynasty — his ship coins, found in large numbers, testify to an
extensive maritime commerce that flourished under his reign, and his
inscriptions and monuments are scattered widely across the former
Satavahana domain. But even his own reign ended in reversal: pressed by
the Western Kshatrapa king Rudradaman, Yajna Sri lost ground his family
had held for generations, and the kings who followed him could not
recover what he himself had failed to hold.

## Three weak reigns and a fading empire

The successors who followed Yajna Sri — Vijaya, Chanda Sri and
Pulumavi III — ruled between them for only seventeen years, a span the
source dismisses as "of little historical significance," their reigns
marked by weakness rather than any fresh achievement. Pulumavi III, the
last king the dynasty's own records securely attest, is known chiefly
from an inscription placing him on the throne from at least 166 to 174
AD, administering a kingdom already visibly shrinking around him.

## A collapse with no single cause

What ended the Satavahana empire was not a conqueror but a slow
unraveling on every frontier at once. The Chutus rose to power in the
western and southern districts the dynasty had once ruled directly; the
Abhiras established themselves in the Nasik region; the Ikshvakus
carved out their own kingdom in the east; and the Shaka rulers of
Ujjain kept up relentless pressure from the northwest. No single one of
these rivals delivered a final, decisive blow — the empire simply
dissolved into the same patchwork of local powers it had once absorbed
four and a half centuries earlier, "through valour, greatness,
discord, degeneracy and decay," as the source puts it. What the
Satavahanas left behind was not a conquered territory claimed by a
single successor, but a Deccan whose stability, for over three
centuries, no other power before them had ever managed to provide.
