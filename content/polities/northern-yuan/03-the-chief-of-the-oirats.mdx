Esen's father held no khanship and needed none. What Toghon built in the
decade after the Ming humbled Arugtai is the reason his son could put an
emperor in a tent as a prisoner and still not be able to sit on the
Chinggisid throne himself.

## A rival khan, not a throne

By 1425 two men were contesting mastery of the eastern steppe: Adai Khan,
the Chinggisid the Chinese called Halutai, and his kingmaker Arugtai, who
had already fought the Yongle Emperor to exhaustion and buried the
Chinese dead from a failed pursuit with his own hands rather than let
them lie. Toghon, chief of the Oirats - the western Mongol confederacy
the Chinese called the Wala - was neither man's ally. In 1426 word
reached the Ming court that Toghon had raised a rival claimant, Toto
Timur, to the khanship: not a bid for the title himself, which his
descent could not support, but a second Chinggisid puppet set up to
divide whatever following Adai and Arugtai still commanded.

## The Khorchins driven to the Nonni

The clearer evidence of Toghon's strength was not a title he handed out
but a people he broke. In the same years, the Oirats fell on the
Khorchins - the eastern Mongol tribes descended from Chinggis Khan's own
brother Khassar - and scattered them. They fled east to the Nonni river,
in the old Jurchen borderlands, and stayed there afterward for as long as
anyone kept writing their history down. A confederacy that had camped in
the northeast of Mongolia for two centuries was pushed permanently off
its own ground by a single campaign, and the displacement is still what
explains, centuries later, why Khorchin princes are found so far from
every other Mongol tribe descended from Chinggis Khan's brothers.

## What the son inherited

Howorth's own sources lose the thread of Toghon's final years: how
Arugtai's resistance was finally ended, and exactly when the Oirat chief
himself died, go unrecorded in the chronicles Howorth drew on. What is
not in doubt is the shape of the state Esen took over: an Oirat
confederacy that had already humbled the Chinggisid line's own
kingmaker, absorbed or displaced the strongest of the eastern tribes, and
built a following wide enough that when Esen finally rode against the
Ming in 1449, he did it as taishi to a khan of his own installing rather
than as a subordinate testing power he did not yet have.
