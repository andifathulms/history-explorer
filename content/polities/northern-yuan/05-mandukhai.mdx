By 1470 the house of Khubilai had been reduced to one child, and he was a
hunchback, because of how he had been handled.

## What was left

Batu Möngke's father had been killed. His widowed mother married the man whose
slander had caused the quarrel. The boy stayed with his nurse, was taken from her
by force by a Mongol called Temür Khadagh, and had been so roughly used that he grew
up with a crooked back; Temür Khadagh's wife, Sanang Setsen says, tried to rub it
straight with a silver bowl.

He was five years old and he was the imperial line.

## The choice

Mandukhai Setsen Khatun was twenty-three and widowed, and Unebolod of the Khorchin
— a descendant of Chinggis's brother Khasar, and therefore of the blood but not of
the line — was courting her. She told him that if there had not been a descendant
of the imperial house in existence she would have listened, but that such a one
existed in the person of Batu Möngke.

She consulted two friends. One said it would be better for the people if she married
Unebolod at once and did not wait for the boy to grow up. The other, Saghan Agha,
said that if she married Khasar's descendant she would darken her path, divorce
herself from the people and lose the title of khatun; whereas if she protected the
boy she would be cherished by the gods, rule the whole people, and keep her title
with honour.

Mandukhai took the second argument, and hit the first adviser on the head with a
bowl of tea.

## The vow

Then she took the five-year-old by the hand and led him before the goddess-mother
of the royal house.

> I have wandered unconsciously into a place where one cannot distinguish black
> from white. The family of the Borjigin is nearly extinct.

She set out the case — Unebolod had wooed her; when he suggested the boy was too
young and insignificant she began to be nervous for his life — and made the bargain.
If she should despise the goddess's gates or accept Unebolod, let her be punished.
If she fulfilled her promise and protected the little descendant and in due time
became his wife, let there be born of that union seven sons and one daughter, and
she would name the seven the seven Bolods, the seven men of steel, and would fan
the flames on the altar.

Unebolod heard what had happened, reconciled himself gracefully, and stopped
courting her.

## The wagon

She married the five-year-old. She seated him before the goddess on a throne and
gave him the name Dayan Khagan, and expressed a hope that he would rule the united
people. Then she loosened her hair and gathered it on the crown of her head in the
manner of a married woman, put Dayan Khagan in a wagon, and marched at the head of
the army against the Four Oirats, from whom they took much booty.

## What it was worth, and what it cost

It worked. Dayan Khan grew up and did what she had named him for: he broke the
Oirat ascendancy, brought the eastern tümens under one authority, and restored a
Chinggisid khan who actually governed. Every Mongol prince of consequence for the
next two centuries — Ordos, Tümed, Khalkha, Chakhar — descends from him.

At the time she made the vow the khanate was so nominal that the Ming annals note
three or four Mongol raids a year from 1470, carrying off men and cattle in enormous
numbers, while the frontier commanders avoided contact and attacked the rearguards
to decapitate enough old and helpless people to claim the bounty.

And the restoration carried its own ending inside it. Dayan divided Mongolia among
his sons as appanages. Within three generations that left, in Howorth's comparison,
something like Russia in the thirteenth century: a disintegrated body of units whose
mutual jealousies were not reduced by their rulers being so closely related.

Mandukhai saved the line. Nobody could save the office.
