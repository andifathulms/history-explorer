The Mongols entered the Qing empire as its most privileged subjects and its most
carefully managed ones, and the terms were set in the years around Ligdan's death.

## The banners

The tribes that had come over were reorganised as banners — the forty-nine of Inner
Mongolia, with the Khalkha north of the desert added later — each under its own
hereditary prince, each with fixed pastures it could not leave, each answering to
Beijing rather than to any Mongol above it.

It was the appanage system Dayan Khan had created, frozen. What had been a
fragmentation problem for the khans became an administrative convenience for an
empire: a hundred princes of the blood of Chinggis, all of equal rank, none able to
combine.

The dynastic link was made and remade by marriage. Ligdan's son married a Manchu
princess; the Khorchin supplied empresses; the descendants of Dayan Khan became
imperial in-laws with court ranks, and their sons came to Beijing.

## The church

The other instrument was already in place. The conversion Altan Khan began put a
great share of the Mongols' herds, wealth and manpower into monasteries, and the
monasteries looked to Lhasa, and after the 1640s Lhasa looked to the Qing.

The Mongol pilgrims who went to Tibet in 1625 had reminded the nine-year-old Dalai
Lama that his own predecessor was born into one of their princely houses and asked,
through an interpreter, whether he would not come and visit the Mongol land. He did
not answer. He began to cry. Those present said to one another that this seemed to
be a prognostication, and was certainly not an accident.

## The arithmetic

Reckon from the abandonment of Dadu in 1368 to the surrender of Ligdan's son in
1635 and the Yuan, in its own reckoning of itself, lasted two hundred and
sixty-seven years after it stopped ruling China — three times as long as it ruled
it.

That is not a paradox, and it is the thing most easily missed about the Yuan. The
dynasty that lost China did not fall; it went home, and kept its name, its
genealogy and its claim, and was finally ended not by the state that expelled it but
by a different steppe power four centuries later, which then did to China exactly
what Khubilai had done.

What it never recovered was the ability to make Mongols obey a Mongol. Buyur Nor
took the seal; the Oirats took the century after it; Mandukhai and Dayan Khan
restored the line and divided the country; Altan Khan got the market, the seal and
the church, and none of the three for the khan; Ligdan tried to take them all back
and emptied his own camp.

The title survived every one of those. The authority went at the first blow and
never came back.
