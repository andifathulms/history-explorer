In 1449 the Mongols captured a reigning emperor of China alive, and the man who
did it was not the khan and could not have been.

## Esen

Esen was an Oirat — a western Mongol, of the Choros — and he was taishi, the
minister, to a Chinggisid khan he had installed. He held the western steppe and
much of the eastern one, and the Chinese frontier trade with it.

The Zhengtong emperor went out against him at the urging of a eunuch who overruled
every competent officer he had. The army was too large to manoeuvre, short of food,
soaked by rain; when the astrologer said the stars were unfavourable the eunuch
replied that if disaster came it would be by the decree of heaven. At Datong they
decided to retreat, and the eunuch insisted the route go through his own home
district so that his brother might have the honour of entertaining the emperor.

That took them through Tumu. Esen fell on the rearguard as the army crossed the
Qiminshan; the count of Gongshun and his brother were killed with all their men.
Forty thousand more came to the rescue and perished. The army dug wells at Tumu and
found no water. Esen sent a spy, learned the state of it, and attacked on all sides
as it moved off.

More than a hundred thousand Chinese were killed. Esen had ordered quarter for any
who did not resist and his men did not listen.

## The prisoner

The emperor, seeing himself surrounded, got off his horse, knelt facing south, and
then sat down on a cushion without any sign of agitation. The composure impressed
the Tatars. Their commander went to tell Esen, who did not believe it and sent two
Chinese to confirm it.

He asked his chiefs what should be done with him. One said there was no question:
the house of Ming had destroyed the house of Yuan and he must be killed. Bayan
Temür argued warmly that he should be treated as a lord, and that Esen would make
his name immortal by sending him home.

Esen had other plans. He kept him, honourably, and used him — sending him to the
gates of Chinese cities to be admitted; sending demands to a court that promptly
enthroned the prisoner's brother and stopped needing him. In the end Esen
entertained him in his own tent, served him himself, sent him home with three
thousand escorts and presents of game, and rode part of the way with him.

## Why it went nowhere

Because Esen could not convert a military triumph into a throne.

He was styled only taishi and jinong while a Chinggisid puppet lived. When he
finally killed the khan Toghtoa Bukha and had himself proclaimed khan, he broke
the one rule that held the Mongol world together, and it destroyed him within three
years. Two of his own commanders — Alag and Temür, very probably his brothers —
asked for the title of taishi. He refused, saying he had given it to his son. They
reproached him, raised an army, and drove him out. In flight he ran into the son of
a man he had put to death, who killed him and hung his body on a tree on the
mountain Kügei Khan.

Howorth's verdict is generous and correct: under Esen the whole Mongol world was
once more united, and he revenged the Mongols on the Chinese by capturing their
sacred ruler, a stroke of fortune that has seldom fallen to a nomad chief; with a
little more vigour abroad and a little more unity at home he might have rivalled
Chinggis, at least in Asia.

What he lacked was not vigour. It was a grandfather.
