The two things that changed the steppe most in the sixteenth century were both
achieved by a grandson of Dayan Khan who was not the khan, and neither of them
did the khanship any good at all.

## The war for a fair

Altan Khan of the Tümed spent nineteen years attacking China, and what he was
attacking it for was permission to trade.

The pattern is monotonous in the annals: an invasion, a town taken, a retreat; a
raid in 1557 and one in 1558; an entry into China in 1566 when the emperor died,
a town plundered, a withdrawal. Then in 1570 his own grandson defected to the Ming
and was invested with a mandarin's title and presents of silk, and Altan demanded
him back, and got him on condition that he return some Chinese deserters.

He was much pleased, and sent envoys asking to be admitted as a tributary. The
Chinese account and the Mongol chronicle describe the same settlement from opposite
sides — De Mailla's Mongols ask to submit, Sanang Setsen's have pressed the Chinese
so hard that they grant a title and a golden seal — and both agree on the terms and
the year.

In 1571 Altan received the title Shunyi Wang, a golden seal, and the frontier
markets. He then forbade the other Mongol princes to make war on China. In 1572 he
sent two hundred and fifty picked horses as tribute and his messengers brought home
two companions who had been prisoners for twenty years. In 1573 the Chinese
authorities engraved gold and silver seals for him and for the other chiefs. When
his son was refused a fair west of the Yellow River he moved to Kokonor and attacked
Shaanxi until he got one.

For the first time since 1368 the steppe had a legal economy. Horses for tea, at
places with names, on dates.

## The Lama

The other change came out of Tibet, and it was his great-nephew's idea.

In 1576 Setsen Khungtaiji of the Ordos came to visit him and urged him to make a
journey south and restore the religion that had prevailed among the Mongols in the
days of Khubilai and the Phagpa Lama. Altan agreed, took counsel with the chiefs of
the three western tümens, and sent an embassy to invite Sonam Gyatso, head of the
Yellow Hat order, to come to him. The embassy was well received; the lama sent back
a letter and presents and a promise to follow directly, and built a temple at
Tsabchiyal on Kokonor to receive him.

In 1577 Altan and the three tümens set out to meet him, and the chronicle Howorth
follows fills the approach with wonders. An advance party asked the lama for a sign
and he put his hand in the Ulaghan Muren and the water turned and ran the other
way. A second party drew near and a spring spouted out of dry ground. In the night
the dragons and evil spirits of Mongolia — with the heads of camels, oxen, sheep,
cats, hawks and wolves — were driven before him and subjected against their wills.
Where his horse walked, the hoofprints held the six sacred syllables.

*It was in 1578 the Allwise Bogda first showed himself in Mongolia.*

## Two men who had seen him before

At the first interview the khan and his great-nephew stared at the lama, and he
asked them why.

Altan answered first. He had long had a disease in his foot; he had been advised
to thrust it into the open breast of a freshly killed horse and had done so, and
the pain became insufferable; he looked up and there was a white man in the sky
who said *Khagan, how can you commit such a great sin?* and vanished. After that he
was constantly frightened until a Tangut lama taught him the six syllables and
another taught him the rosary, and he had said the six syllables a hundred and
eight times a day since. *I recognise you as the man who appeared to me.*

Setsen Khungtaiji answered second. Sitting at chess before his mother, he had been
given a fat piece of horseflesh; as he ate, the knife leapt out of his hand into
the air and came down past his knees, and when he looked for it there was a young
man in black clothes who asked him crossly why he was eating horseflesh, and also
became invisible. He had not eaten horseflesh since. *I saw you were the man who
had rebuked me.*

The lama said it was all true, and that this was not the first time they had met.
When Altan Khan had previously been born as Khubilai Setsen Khagan, he himself had
lived as the Phagpa Lama — and he reminded the khan, in the second person, of the
titles, the costly seal and the yellow signature that Khubilai had given him, and
of the place at the head of the religious administration. The interpreter standing
between them had done the same office between Khubilai and Phagpa, under another
name, and was now doing it after a third birth.

## The assembly

Altan put on a white dress, mounted a white horse and went back to the lama with
his wife and ten thousand men to bring him to the new temple at Tsabchiyal. His
presents are itemised: the seven noble precious things with the eight objects of
sacrifice, five hundred sidshirs in weight; a golden bowl of thirty sidshirs filled
with precious stones; silks of untold worth, ten pieces of each kind and a hundred
of each of five colours; ten white horses with saddles of gold and jewels; and five
thousand head of cattle.

More than a hundred thousand people were there — Chinese, Tibetans, Mongols and
Uyghurs, clergy and laity — and Setsen Khungtaiji made the speech that told them
what was being done and why. Chinggis had subdued the five banners and the four
nations at the command of Khormusta; his grandsons had put Sakya Pandita and
Phagpa at the head of the religious institutions; and since then, he said,
religion and justice among us have fallen very low — *we shed the blood and eat the
flesh of living beings.* From this day, with Shakyamuni reappeared in the Lama and
the lord of the earth in the Khagan, the piled-up waves of the tempestuous
blood-stream should be transformed into a deep, peaceful sea of milk.

Like the voice of the cuckoo in the first month of summer, Sanang Setsen says, so
these words sounded in the ears of the assembly.

## What was legislated

Then the khan and the lama put proposals to the assembly and they were accepted
unanimously.

The khan gave the lama the title *Vajradhara Dalai Lama* and raised him to the rank
Phagpa had held under Khubilai. The lama gave the khan the title of the thousand
golden wheel-turning Chakravartin Setsen Khagan.

The custom by which camels and horses were killed and buried with a dead man
according to his wealth — the *khoilgha* — was abolished, and the beasts appointed
for it were to be given to the priests instead. Slaughter and hunting were to cease
on the fast days. The priesthood was graded into four ranks with the legal standing
of princes and nobles, exempt from taxes, from the army, from the great hunts. A
monk who broke his vows was to have his face blackened with soot and be walked
three times backwards round the temple as a laughing stock, and then expelled. The
whole was collected under the title *the laws of the science of the ten meritorious
works*, and traced explicitly back to Khubilai.

Altan promised a Buddha of gold, silver and precious stones at Kökeqota; the Jinong
promised a copy of the hundred and eight volumes of the Kanjur in letters of gold
and silver; another prince promised a temple. Then the khan went home, taking with
him the monk Howorth calls the evangelist of the Mongols.

## What it did

Altan Khan died in 1583 aged seventy-seven, five years after the meeting, and the
Chinese court sent mandarins with presents to perform the ceremonies over his body.

The following year his son sent the funeral offerings to the Dalai Lama and summoned
him, and he came. He was feasted at Ganzhou, where the ash of the incense burning
before him arranged itself into letters and hardened; at Ningxia he showed himself
to the people with four hands and a rainbow round him; in 1585 he reached the Ordos
and gave the whole people the consecration; when he got to the Tümed he blamed them
for having buried Altan's body, had it exhumed and burned, and found relics in the
ashes.

He also stopped a custom. A son of Altan's had died and his mother had determined to
kill a hundred boys and a hundred camel foals to escort him to the next world. She
had killed more than forty when a tumult arose among the people and it stopped.

Within a generation the Mongols were Buddhists, the monasteries held a great part of
the herds and the manpower, and the next Dalai Lama but one was born into Altan
Khan's own family — a Mongol prince as the head of the Tibetan church, which a
Mongol pilgrim in 1625 would remind his successor of, in tears, while asking him to
come and visit.

## Where the khan was

Not in any of it. The title, the seal, the markets and the church all went to the
Tümed line. The great khan of the Chakhar, the actual heir of Khubilai, was a
spectator at the refounding of his own nation.
