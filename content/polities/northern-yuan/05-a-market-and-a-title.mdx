The two things that changed the steppe most in the sixteenth century were both
achieved by a grandson of Dayan Khan who was not the khan, and neither of them
did the khanship any good at all.

## The war for a fair

Altan Khan of the Tümed spent nineteen years attacking China, and what he was
attacking it for was permission to trade.

The pattern is monotonous in the annals: an invasion, a town taken, a retreat; a
raid in 1557 and one in 1558; an entry into China in 1566 when the emperor died,
a town plundered, a withdrawal. Then in 1570 his own grandson defected to the Ming
and was invested with a mandarin's title and presents of silk, and Altan demanded
him back, and got him on condition that he return some Chinese deserters.

He was much pleased, and sent envoys asking to be admitted as a tributary. The
Chinese account and the Mongol chronicle describe the same settlement from opposite
sides — De Mailla's Mongols ask to submit, Sanang Setsen's have pressed the Chinese
so hard that they grant a title and a golden seal — and both agree on the terms and
the year.

In 1571 Altan received the title Shunyi Wang, a golden seal, and the frontier
markets. He then forbade the other Mongol princes to make war on China. In 1572 he
sent two hundred and fifty picked horses as tribute and his messengers brought home
two companions who had been prisoners for twenty years. In 1573 the Chinese
authorities engraved gold and silver seals for him and for the other chiefs. When
his son was refused a fair west of the Yellow River he moved to Kokonor and attacked
Shaanxi until he got one.

For the first time since 1368 the steppe had a legal economy. Horses for tea, at
places with names, on dates.

## The Lama

The other change came out of Tibet, and Howorth prints two versions of how it
started.

The Mongol chronicle says Altan campaigned into eastern Tibet in 1573, took the
Yellow Uyghur country, and carried off a lama through whose influence he became
attached to the faith and began using the six-syllable formula. The Kalmuk account
Pallas took down says he suffered terribly from gout, that his shamans prescribed
burying his foot in the warm entrails of a man slit open for the purpose on the
evenings of the full moon, and that while he was doing this he dreamed of a lama in
the full moon who told him they would heal his pain without killing anyone and that
he should stop. He had the corpse removed, washed his feet, and asked the two lamas
at his court whose image it had been.

Either way he sent to Tibet for the head of the Yellow Hat order and brought him
north.

What happened at their meeting was the most consequential thing in Mongol history
after 1368. The khan gave the lama the title *Vajradhara Dalai Lama* and raised him
to the rank Phagpa had held under Khubilai. The lama gave the khan the title of the
thousand golden wheel-turning Chakravartin Setsen Khagan.

And they legislated. The old funeral practice by which cattle — the *khoilgha* —
were killed and buried with the dead was abolished and the beasts given to the
priests instead. Slaughter and hunting were to stop on the fast days. The priesthood
was graded into four ranks with the legal standing of princes and nobles, exempt
from taxes, from the army, from the great hunts. A monk who broke his vows was to
have his face blackened with soot and to be walked three times backwards round the
temple as a laughing stock before expulsion. The whole was collected under the title
*the laws of the science of the ten meritorious works*, and explicitly traced back
to Khubilai.

## What it did

Altan Khan died in 1583 aged seventy-seven, and the Chinese court sent mandarins
with presents to perform the ceremonies over his body.

The following year his son sent the funeral offerings to the Dalai Lama and summoned
him, and he came. He was feasted at Ganzhou, where the ash of the incense burning
before him arranged itself into letters and hardened; at Ningxia he showed himself
to the people with four hands and a rainbow round him; in 1585 he reached the Ordos
and gave the whole people the consecration; when he got to the Tümed he blamed them
for having buried Altan's body, had it exhumed and burned, and found relics in the
ashes.

He also stopped a custom. A son of Altan's had died and his mother had determined to
kill a hundred boys and a hundred camel foals to escort him to the next world. She
had killed more than forty when a tumult arose among the people and it stopped.

Within a generation the Mongols were Buddhists, the monasteries held a great part of
the herds and the manpower, and the next Dalai Lama but one was born into Altan
Khan's own family — a Mongol prince as the head of the Tibetan church, which a
Mongol pilgrim in 1625 would remind his successor of, in tears, while asking him to
come and visit.

## Where the khan was

Not in any of it. The title, the seal, the markets and the church all went to the
Tümed line. The great khan of the Chakhar, the actual heir of Khubilai, was a
spectator at the refounding of his own nation.
