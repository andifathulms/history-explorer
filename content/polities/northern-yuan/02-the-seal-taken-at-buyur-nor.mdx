In 1388 the Ming general Lan Yu asked permission to attack the Mongols at their
headquarters and scatter them thoroughly, and was given more than a hundred
thousand men to do it with.

## The march

Uskhal Khan — Tögüs Temür, the third of the exiled emperors — was camped near lake
Buyur, in the country full of reminiscences of the glorious days of Chinggis.

The Chinese came stealthily, lighting no fires, marching at night, the last stage
of the approach covered by a cloud of blowing sand. They were on the camp before it
knew. The Mongols struck their tents and fled after a short resistance.

## The tally

The khan got away with his heir. Almost nothing else did.

His second son was taken, with sixty-four of his suite; the wife of the crown
prince with fifty-nine of her household; many princesses; the Prince of Wu; the
Prince of Dai; Palan, one of the best Mongol generals; two thousand nine hundred
and ninety-four officers and seventy-seven thousand soldiers. A hundred and fifty
thousand head of cattle. An immense booty of gold and silver.

And the imperial seal.

## Afterwards

The khan made for Karakorum to join his minister, got as far as the Tula, and was
attacked there by one of his own relatives. His troops scattered. He was left with
sixteen horsemen, and a snowstorm came down on him, and the pursuers caught up and
killed him and his son.

The Ming divided the country into departments, appointed Mongol officers over them
and let them administer by their own customs.

Sanang Setsen does not mention any of it. The Mongol chronicle gives the khan's
accession and his death and passes over the worst day in the dynasty's post-Chinese
history in silence; the whole account comes from the Chinese side. Howorth notes
the omission, which is the sort of thing worth knowing about a source.

## What it meant

Two things, and the second is the one that shaped the next eighty years.

The line of Khubilai stopped being able to hold the eastern steppe. A Ming
expedition the following year marched to the north of the Gobi and came back
without seeing a single Mongol; the nation was disintegrated and scattered, and
whatever authority the next khan had was very local. Other chiefs set up on their
own account in other neighbourhoods, and Howorth's judgement is that their main
bond of union was the plundering of the Chinese frontier.

And the western Mongols — the Oirats, the Kalmuks of the European sources — became
supreme in Mongolia. They were not Chinggisids. They could not take the title. So
for the better part of a century they did what the Manghit did on the other side of
the continent: kept a khan, and ruled.
