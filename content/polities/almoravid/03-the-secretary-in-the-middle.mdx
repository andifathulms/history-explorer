Yusuf ibn Tashufin conquered Morocco, founded Marrakesh, crossed into Spain at
the invitation of its kings and took their kingdoms away from them, and he did
not know Arabic.

Ibn Khallikan, writing two centuries later, went looking for the fullest account
he could find and settled on a book whose author he could not name — only that
the man began it in 1183 and finished it at Mosul in the same year. What he
extracted from it is a story about translation.

## How he got the job

The Almoravids came north out of the country next to the land of the Blacks
against the Zanata Berbers, who could not resist them, and took everything from
the gates of Tlemcen to the Atlantic. Their leader was Abu Bakr ibn Umar — a man,
Ibn Khallikan's source says, accustomed to a simple life, virtuous in conduct,
preferring his own country to the Maghreb and having no inclination for the
luxuries of life.

Then somebody told him about an old woman.

> He was informed that an old woman in his own country had a female camel stolen
> from her in a foray and begun to weep, exclaiming: "Abu Bakr ibn Umar has
> ruined us by entering into the land of Maghreb!"

He went back south, and left as his lieutenant in the Maghreb a man called Yusuf
ibn Tashufin — brave, just and enterprising. Yusuf founded Marrakesh on a spot
where robbers used to lie in ambush, which belonged to an old Masmuda woman.

The empire that took al-Andalus was handed over by a man who went home because a
woman in the desert had lost a camel.

## The letter

When the taifa kings learned that Yusuf meant to cross, they were frightened, and
Ibn Khallikan's source is exact about the shape of the fear. They had been using
his name for years to hold off the Christians — letting it be understood that
they might ally with him whenever the Franks pressed too hard — while dreading
that he might come and, on seeing their kingdoms, want them. They had heard what
his men did in battle: that the best of them would cut a horseman in two with a
single stroke and run several through with one lance.

So they wrote to him, and a Spanish secretary drafted it for them. It is a small
masterpiece of the chancery art:

> If you let us alone, your conduct will be attributed, not to weakness but to
> generosity, and if we obey your orders, our conduct will be attributed, not to
> helplessness but to prudence. We therefore prefer the attribution which is the
> more honourable for ourselves, hoping that you will prefer the attribution
> which is the more honourable for you. The place which you hold is one in which
> you should not let yourself be surpassed in noble acts.

## What he was told it said

Yusuf did not know Arabic. His secretary, who had both languages, read it to him
in Berber, and what he said was not what the letter said.

He told him it came from the kings of al-Andalus, that they offered profound
respect, declared themselves partisans of his cause, and acknowledged his
authority. Then he supplied, in the kings' voice, a speech they had not made:
*We are Muslims, come of a noble race; let us not therefore incur your
displeasure. It is for us a sufficient misfortune to have close behind us a
people of infidels. Our country is much straitened and cannot support numerous
armies. Spare us therefore, as you have spared the people of Maghreb who
acknowledged your authority.*

The elaborate courtesy about attributions is gone. What is left is a frank
statement of weakness, an admission of overlordship, and a plea — which is the
version most likely to produce the answer the secretary wanted.

Yusuf asked him what he made of it, and got a lecture on kingship:

> Every time that he bestows an ample gift, he increases his influence; increase
> of influence consolidates his dominion, and when his dominion is consolidated,
> people think it an honour to obey him; when obedience is felt to be an honour,
> the people come unto him, and he is not obliged to encounter fatigues for the
> purpose of reaching them, and he thus inherits the kingdom without ruining his
> happiness in the next life. Know that it was said by a great king: "He who
> bestows may command; he who commands may lead, and he who leads an army
> becomes master of the land."

Yusuf, hearing this in his own language, understood its import and felt its
truth, and told him to draft the answer and read it back. The reply is four
sentences of warm and entirely non-committal goodwill — *as long as you hold to
your engagements towards us, we shall hold to ours towards you* — and it went off
with a present of Lamtuna shields, which Ibn Khallikan pauses to explain are made
only in one small town twenty days south of Sijilmasa and cannot be had anywhere
else.

The kings of al-Andalus, on receiving it, *were filled with love for Yusuf and
extolled him highly*, rejoiced at his friendship and at his having the Maghreb,
and resolved among themselves that if the king of the Franks did anything to
disquiet them, they would send for him.

## What the arrangement was

Ibn Khallikan's source states it without embarrassment: *It was thus that Yusuf,
by the good management of his secretary, obtained that which he wished for,
namely, the good-will of the people of al-Andalus; all that he wanted was an
opportunity of making war in their defence.*

The opportunity came. Alfonso overran the country, the taifa kings feared for
their states, and al-Mu'tamid of Seville — who understood perfectly well what
calling in the Berbers would cost, and said so — wrote the letter that brought
the army across.

None of the correspondence that arranged it was read by the man it was addressed
to. He heard it, in a different language, from an employee who knew what he
wanted to hear.
