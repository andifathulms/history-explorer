Ibn Khaldun sets the beginning of the end at a hearing. Ali ibn Yusuf, the
third Almoravid ruler, had governed fourteen years in unbroken good fortune
when a preacher out of the Anti-Atlas became a nuisance — a man who had studied
in the east, served for nothing as a legal consultant, and worked with
extraordinary zeal at the reform of morals. It had not gone well for him: he
had been roughly handled, Ibn Khaldun says, at Bougie, at Tlemcen and at
Meknes.

Ali summoned him to Marrakesh and put him in a room with the Almoravid doctors
of law. He argued with them, in Ibn Khaldun's phrase, and reduced them to
silence. Then he left for his own tribe, the Hargha.

Ali changed his mind about him afterwards and sent men after him. The Hargha
were told to hand their kinsman over and saw Almoravid troops behind the
demand; instead they made an alliance with the Hintata and the people of
Tinmallal, and all of them undertook to protect the Mahdi, to hold the true
religion and to spread it.

That is a state losing an argument in its own capital and then losing three
mountain confederations for the price of asking politely.

## What the state was doing while that happened

Governing, competently, in the other direction. In the same years Ali crossed
the strait himself and besieged Toledo, saw the Aragonese take Tudela, and lost
Mallorca to a Christian fleet — Genoese, in Ibn Khaldun's account — and had it
recovered for him by an Almoravid general, appointed
his son Tashfin to the western half of al-Andalus with Cordoba and Seville for
residences, gave the eastern half to a governor at Valencia whose minister was
the philosopher Ibn Bajja, and handed Denia and the Balearic islands to a
Massufa chief called Ibn Ghaniya.

None of it was foolish. All of it was the wrong problem. Two years after the
Mahdi died his chief disciple Abd al-Mu'min took command under the terms of his
will, the Masmuda tribes attacked Marrakesh again and again, and while the
Lamtuna were being worn down in Spain the Almohad cause was being carried
through the Maghreb to the other Berber peoples.

## Oran

Ali died in the 1140s and his son Tashfin succeeded him with both shores
hurrying to acknowledge him, at the moment when the Almohads had become
formidable and were bent on his destruction. When Abd al-Mu'min took his army
into the mountains, Tashfin followed on the flat below and reached Tlemcen.
The Almohads camped in the gorge that opens between the Two Rocks of the
mountain above the town and besieged him in it. A relief column sent by the
lord of Bougie charged them out of enthusiasm, was cut to pieces, and lost its
commander.

Tashfin got out to Oran, where he had arranged to meet his admiral and the
fleet. The Almohads followed and stormed the town. He was killed there — or,
Ibn Khaldun adds, by another account he disappeared and was not seen again. The
*Rawd al-Qirtas* gives a third version: that he came out at night with his
column to surprise the besiegers, was overwhelmed by their cavalry and
infantry, turned to flee across what he took for level ground in the dark, and
rode off a cliff above the sea by the ribat of Oran. His body was found on the
shore the next morning and his head was cut off and sent to Tinmallal.

Three accounts, one man, one night. Marrakesh fell within the year.
