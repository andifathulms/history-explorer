In 1086 the king of Seville sent across the strait for an army, and the army
came. Al-Maqqari, compiling in the seventeenth century from Andalusi
historians whose own books are lost, preserves a long account of what followed,
and almost every detail in it is about a man taking the measure of another man.

Yusuf ibn Tashfin landed at Algeciras and marched to Seville in order, army
after army, general after general, tribe after tribe. Al-Mu'tamid rode out with
a hundred horsemen to meet him; a short way from the camp he put his horse to
a gallop, the camp saw it and came out, and the two of them dismounted and
embraced. Every king in al-Andalus either came to Seville himself or sent
somebody to stand in for him. The Africans, says the account, saw enough of the
splendour of royalty in that city to make them glad.

## The camels

Ibn Khallikan, quoted in the same compilation, says Yusuf had camels shipped
over in such numbers that the country was filled with them and their cries
reached the sky. Nobody in al-Andalus had seen a camel. The horses would not
stand it: the sight of one of the animals, or the sound of it, was enough to
make a horse rear and throw its rider. Yusuf trained them to war and ringed his
camp with them, and in the battle they broke up the Christian cavalry.

That is a Saharan army solving an Iberian problem with the one asset a Saharan
army has, and it is a fair summary of why the crossing worked at all.

## The letter

Alfonso VI wrote to Yusuf first — a letter composed for him, al-Maqqari says,
by a Muslim traitor, full of abuse and of inflated figures for the forces and
stores at his disposal. Yusuf gave it to his secretary Abu Bakr ibn
al-Qusayra, a learned and elegant writer, who withdrew and came back with a
long epistle full of rhetorical beauties. Yusuf had it read to him and said:
that answer is too long, bring me Alfonso's letter. On the back of the letter
he wrote six words.

*He who shall live will see.*

Before the battle he wrote once more, formally offering the three conditions
the law prescribes — Islam, tribute, or the sword — and adding that Alfonso had
once wished to come over to Africa and had regretted having no ships, and that
his wish was now granted, since here they were.

## Sagrajas

The armies met near Badajoz — al-Maqqari, his translator notes, neglects to
give the date. Alfonso proposed, through al-Mu'tamid,
that since Friday was the Muslim holy day and Sunday the Christian one, the
battle should be fought on the Saturday between; al-Mu'tamid told Yusuf it was
a lie and that the attack would come on Friday, and it did.

It came at the Andalusians, and by design. Al-Mu'tamid's scouts had crept close
enough to the Christian camp to hear Alfonso explain his plan to his men: the
king of Seville understands Andalusian warfare and the Africans do not, so the
Africans are being guided by him, so break him first. They very nearly did.
Al-Mu'tamid was surrounded, took a sabre cut through his helmet into his skull,
another across his right hand and a spear in the thigh, had three horses killed
under him, and in the middle of it thought of a sick child he had left behind
in Seville and composed a verse about him. His own son gave way. He kept
looking towards the African camp and no troops came.

They came at the end, and not to him. Yusuf had told a general to wait until
al-Mu'tamid was fully engaged and then fall on Alfonso's camp and burn it. That
is what won the day.

Five years later Yusuf deposed him.
