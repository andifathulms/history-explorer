A movement came out of the western Sahara with a social order the settled
world found genuinely strange, and within thirty years was required to govern
the two most urbane provinces in the western Islamic world.

The strangeness was literal and contemporaries fixed on it. The Sanhaja of the
western Sahara were *al-mulaththamun*, the veiled ones: the men wore a face veil
and the women did not, which is the inverse of the practice in the cities they
came to rule and which Andalusi writers never stopped remarking on. Sanhaja
women moved and spoke freely, held property, and appear in the sources as
political figures — Zaynab al-Nafzawiyya, who married Yusuf ibn Tashfin, is
credited by the chroniclers with a substantial hand in the movement's early
politics and with the wealth that financed part of it.

The dynasty had no city, so it made one. Marrakesh was founded in the 1070s on
the plain below the High Atlas, at the point where the routes from the Sahara
reach the settled country — a garrison and a market rather than an inherited
capital, in the way Baghdad and Cairo had been built to order before it. It has
been a capital, on and off, ever since.

<Figure id="koubba" />

Then came al-Andalus, and the friction is the interesting part. The Almoravids
deposed the taifa kings on a legal argument — that rulers who paid tribute to
Christians and levied taxes the law did not permit had forfeited authority —
and they governed with the Maliki jurists of Cordoba and Fez at their shoulder.
That bought them legitimacy and cost them everything else. Andalusi poets and
courtiers, whose taifa patrons had been generous and cultivated, found their new
masters provincial; the jurists they deferred to were suspicious of speculative
theology and had al-Ghazali's *Ihya* burned in Cordoba and Marrakesh.

<Figure id="ihya" />

Within two generations the desert movement had become an urban dynasty with a
palace, an Andalusi chancery and a taste for the poetry it had arrived
disapproving of — and had lost the discipline that had made a few thousand
camel-borne Sanhaja able to take Morocco. That is the argument Ibn Tumart would
make against them from the Atlas, and he was not wrong about the facts.
