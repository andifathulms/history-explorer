The Almoravid state was destroyed by a doctrine, and the sequence took about
twenty-five years.

Ibn Tumart began preaching in the Atlas around 1120; his successor Abd al-Mu'min
spent the 1130s and 1140s reducing the Almoravid position mountain by mountain;
Marrakesh fell in 1147 and the last Almoravid ruler was killed. This site's
Almohad page describes what replaced them. What is worth adding here is what the
defeat was about, because it is a rare case in this corpus.

The charge was theological. Ibn Tumart held that the Almoravids, in their
attachment to the letter of Maliki law and their suspicion of speculative
theology, had ended up describing God in bodily terms — reading the
anthropomorphic language of scripture literally — and were therefore not
merely bad Muslims but unbelievers, against whom war was lawful. This corpus
records dynasties ending by conquest, succession, fragmentation and revolt. It
records almost none ending because somebody won an argument about the divine
attributes.

The Almoravids also did not entirely end in 1147. A branch of the family, the
Banu Ghaniya, held the Balearic islands, and from there they spent the next
seventy years attacking the Almohads from the sea — taking Ifriqiya's ports,
raiding the Maghrebi coast, and at one point holding much of the interior. They
were not finally suppressed until the 1230s, by which time the Almohads
themselves were breaking up. It is one of the longer afterlives in the corpus
and it was run from three small islands.

Two things for the record. The ending on this page is typed `conquest` with the
Almohads as agent, which is accurate for the state and silent about the reason;
the reason is in this chapter because the vocabulary has no value for it. And
the movement's own arc — desert austerity, urban success, doctrinal criticism,
replacement by a purer movement out of the same mountains — is the pattern Ibn
Khaldun set out three centuries later, in Tunis, watching the dynasties that
followed these ones. This site's Hafsid page has him.
