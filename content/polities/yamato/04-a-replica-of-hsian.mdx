In 710 the court laid out its first true city: a permanent capital at
Nara, built as a deliberate copy of the Chinese capital of Chang'an,
with seven great Buddhist temples, Shinto shrines, a palace and public
buildings enough to give it, almost at once, an appearance of real
magnificence. It held the court for seventy-four years, long enough
that the whole eighth century in Japanese history and literature is
simply called the Nara epoch.

## Writing the country down

It was near the start of that epoch that the court committed its own
origins to writing for the first time. The Kojiki was set down in 712;
the Nihon Shoki, more elaborate and more consciously shaped to fit
Chinese historiographical models, followed in 720. Between them the
two books gave Yamato what it had never had before - a fixed, written
account of its own gods, its founding emperor, and the line of
sovereigns descended from them, compiled from court tradition Prince
Shōtoku and Soga no Umako had already begun assembling nearly a
century earlier.

The compilers' own ambitions ran further than mere record-keeping.
Chinese theories of centralised, absolute imperial sovereignty were
worked back into the account of eras centuries before Yamato had any
contact with China capable of transmitting them - a court in 534
addressing a subject in language stolen wholesale from a Chinese
dynastic chronicle nobody in Japan could have read until decades
later. The Nihon Shoki's authors were not simply recording what had
happened; they were arguing that the centralised state Kamatari's
generation had only just built had, in fact, been Yamato's arrangement
from the beginning.

## The Great Buddha

The epoch's most physically ambitious undertaking came under the
Emperor Shōmu, whose own devotion to Buddhism was unmatched by any
sovereign before him. When smallpox, carried by a fisherman returning
from Korea, swept through the Nara aristocracy in 735 and killed four
Fujiwara brothers among its victims, Shōmu resolved on a colossal
Buddha to answer it - work interrupted by a revolt in Kyushu, resumed
in 743 with an imperial edict calling on the whole population to
contribute funds, and completed only after seven failed castings.

The eighth attempt succeeded in 749: a seated bronze Buddha fifty-three
feet high, built up in forty-one separate layers rather than cast in a
single shell, consuming five hundred pounds of gold, nearly seventeen
thousand pounds of tin, close to two thousand pounds of mercury and
almost a million pounds of copper. No country in contemporary Europe
outside Byzantium could have managed an undertaking on that scale. The
following month Shōmu himself, attended by his empress, his daughter
and the whole court, went to the temple and knelt before the finished
image - the sovereign of Yamato, in the position of a subject at an
imperial audience, professing himself the servant of Buddha, the Law
and the Priesthood.

## What the choice of Nara cost, and bought

A permanent capital was not free. Building it, and later moving away
from it, strained the treasury in ways a mobile court on campaign
never had to reckon with, and the era's own land and tax records show
a steady erosion: a district that had twenty thousand taxable adult
males around 600 counted fewer than two thousand by 767, the rest
absorbed into tax-exempt estates held by temples, courtiers and local
strongmen the central government could no longer reach.

But it was Nara, not a moving military camp, that made a standing
bureaucracy possible at all - officials who specialised, records that
accumulated in one place across reigns, and a literate court capable
of producing the Kojiki, the Nihon Shoki, and the Great Buddha within
the same two or three generations. Kyoto, when the court finally moved
there in 794, inherited an administration and a documentary tradition
that Nara had built first.
