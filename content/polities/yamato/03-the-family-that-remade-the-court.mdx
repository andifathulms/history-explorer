A century after Buddhism and continental learning first arrived, the
mikado Kotoku's regent Kamatari began reshaping the court's own
administration along Chinese lines. The reform's own name, still used to
mark the start of Japan's chronological year-period system, dates the
change precisely: 645, the first of the era-names Japanese court
records would use continuously for the rest of imperial history.

## A regent who founded a dynasty of his own

Kamatari's regency lasted only four years, but its consequences outlived
him by centuries. He is remembered as the founder of the Fujiwara
line - said to descend, through a servant of Jimmu's own grandfather,
from the earliest strata of the court's own mythology - and within a few
centuries ninety-five of the empire's hundred and fifty-five noble
houses would claim Fujiwara descent. The office of imperial regent
itself, the highest position a
subject could hold, would become the Fujiwara family's own hereditary
possession.

## An administration built to outlast any one reign

What Kamatari's reforms actually changed was less dramatic in the
moment than what they made possible afterward: a court organised well
enough to survive the ordinary business of governing an expanding
realm without depending entirely on one strong ruler's personal
authority. Where earlier government had been, in practice, whatever a
mobile military chieftain and his retinue could manage from camp to
camp, the reformed administration could function as a standing
bureaucracy - the same kind of institution that would, within another
century and a half, make a single permanent capital both possible and
necessary.

## The tool that outgrew its makers

The Fujiwara family's own rise from this reform is the clearest sign of
what had actually changed. A single regent's four-year tenure had
created an office durable enough that his descendants would go on to
staff it almost exclusively, eventually using it not merely to serve
the throne but to select who sat on it. The centralising reform meant
to strengthen the imperial house ended up building the instrument that,
within a few more centuries, would let a subject family govern in the
mikado's name more completely than the mikado governed himself.
