The office of Kuambaku, or imperial regent, became the Fujiwara
family's own hereditary property, and with it came a power more
durable than any single reign: the ability to decide who actually wore
the crown. Because the imperial succession ran on a wide and indefinite
basis rather than a single fixed rule, a family positioned to manage the
throne's vacancies could simply arrange to have one occur.

## Choosing an heir by choosing an abdication

Fujiwara regents compelled sitting emperors to abdicate whenever it
suited the family's own interest to install a more pliable successor,
and by long custom the throne passed to whichever candidate the
regental family judged most useful rather than to whoever inheritance
alone might have favoured. The mikado remained, in every formal sense,
the empire's only sovereign. In practice, for generation after
generation, the Fujiwara decided which mikado that would be.

## An arrangement no rival family broke - until one tried something different

What eventually ended the Fujiwara monopoly was not a rival claim to the
regency itself but a different kind of power emerging alongside it: the
military households the court's own earlier consolidation had helped
create. Two clans in particular, the Taira and the Minamoto, had risen
from comparative obscurity into the same social prominence long enjoyed
by the court nobility, and for decades the two lived alongside each
other in Kyoto without serious conflict, sometimes even as allies on
distant campaigns.

## The battle that changed what victory meant

That peace ended in 1156, when the two clans backed rival claimants to
the throne in an armed contest fought inside the capital itself. The
Taira won, and their victory delivered something the Fujiwara's own
regency had never quite required: physical possession of the imperial
palace, and with it, control of whichever policy the emperor now issued
in his own name. The Taira leader, Kiyomori, used that advantage to
fill court offices with his own relatives just as thoroughly as the
Fujiwara ever had - proof that the throne's own kingmakers had changed,
but the underlying arrangement of a nominal sovereign and an actual
one had not.
