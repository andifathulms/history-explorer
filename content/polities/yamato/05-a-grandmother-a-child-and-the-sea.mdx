Kiyomori's death in 1181 did not end the war his own rise had provoked.
The Taira, driven from the capital, regrouped in the south and west
while the Minamoto general Yoshitsune pursued them without pause -
burning one fortified retreat after another, driving the survivors from
Fukuwara to Yashima and finally to the straits at Shimonoseki, where
both sides committed their entire remaining strength to a naval battle
that would settle the war outright.

## A prearranged signal decides a fleet action

The battle, fought in the fourth month of 1185 at Dan-no-ura, turned on
treachery within the Taira's own ranks: a captain named Shigeyoshi, secretly
loyal to Yoshitsune, had agreed on a signal marking which ship in the
Taira fleet carried the prize Yoshitsune most wanted, and when the
signal appeared Yoshitsune's own vessels swarmed it at once. The Taira
commander Tomomori and six of his fellow leaders died fighting
hand-to-hand rather than surrender.

## A grandmother's last choice

Kiyomori's widow, a Buddhist nun, chose the sea over capture. Holding
her five-year-old grandson - the boy emperor Antoku, enthroned as a
Taira puppet since infancy - she leapt overboard with him in her arms.
The child's own mother tried and failed to save him; both drowned
together. The sacred regalia the boy emperor carried went into the
strait with him, and the head of the Taira house, along with a great
number of nobles and court ladies, was taken captive rather than
following his own family into the water.

## A throne that survived, and a war that decided who ruled beside it

The imperial line itself did not end at Dan-no-ura - Antoku's own
brother, Go-Toba, had already been recognised by the victorious
Minamoto and remained on the throne the court had never actually risked
in battle. What the war settled instead was which military house would
now stand over that throne. In 1192, Yoritomo received the title Sei-i
Tai Shogun from the same court his own family's victory had just
rescued and reshaped - an office that would outlast, in one house's
hands or another's, every dynasty that held it for the next six hundred
and seventy-six years. The mikado kept his throne. What ended in this
single generation, first by battle and then by title, was any
remaining question of who actually ran the country beneath him.
