For twenty-eight years the throne of Yamato belonged, in name, to an
empress, and in substance to her nephew - a regent who governed a court
he never sat at the head of, through a chief minister who could have
crushed him and instead deferred to him for the whole span of his life.

## An empress raised to keep the peace

The assassination of the Emperor Sujun in 592 - killed by an agent of
the Great Minister Soga no Umako, whose family's power the throne had
begun to threaten - left the succession in the hands of the very man
who had arranged the killing. Umako passed over a score of eligible
imperial princes and raised his own niece to the throne instead, as the
Empress Suiko. A few months later Suiko named her nephew Mumayado -
Prince Shōtoku - heir apparent, and with that nomination the claims of
her own seven children to the succession were set aside for good.

Shōtoku was twenty-one, already known for the zeal in which he had
fought, at fifteen, in the battle that annihilated the Mononobe clan -
the court's last serious internal opponents of Buddhism. From this
appointment he held, in the record's own words, "general control of
the Government, and entrusted with all the details of the
administration," for the rest of his life.

## Governing through a murderer

That Umako, described elsewhere in the record as a liar and a
murderer, submitted to a twenty-one-year-old regent's ascendancy for
twenty-eight years without open contest is itself the measure of what
Shōtoku had built. The prince is credited with a genuine scholarly
command of Buddhist doctrine and of Chinese classical and political
philosophy that outstripped his contemporaries, Umako included, and by
turning the older man's own energies toward the study of sutras and
the classics, he appears to have kept Yamato's most dangerous man
occupied with something other than mischief for nearly three decades.

## Seventeen articles, and one theory of the state

In 604 Shōtoku issued a set of seventeen articles of moral and
administrative guidance for officials - not a constitution in the
modern sense, but a direct importation of Chinese political theory
into a court that had never before had one written down. Article
Twelve states the claim plainly: "In a country there are not two
lords; the people have not two masters. The sovereign is the master of
the people of the whole country." Provincial officials, the article
goes on, had no standing to levy their own exactions on top of the
crown's.

It was an argument for a single, centralised sovereignty at a court
still organised as a confederation of clans under a first-among-equals
chieftain - and it went unchallenged only because the Soga's own
interests, tangled by marriage into the imperial house itself, were
not yet threatened by strengthening a throne their own family
effectively controlled. Two years after the prince's death, Soga no
Umako's own descendants would flout the article's plain words and
seize an imperial estate for themselves; two decades further on, his
grandson's contempt for it would help bring the whole Soga house down.

## What outlasted him

In 620, the year before his death, Shōtoku worked with Umako himself
to compile a history of the emperors, a history of the country, and a
record of the great clans and their subordinate houses - Yamato's
first attempt at a written account of its own past, and the direct
ancestor of the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki that would follow a century
later.

He died in 621. The obituary the record preserves for him describes a
mourning that reached every rank of society at once: "the old, as if
they had lost a dear child, had no taste for salt and vinegar in their
mouths; the young, as if they had lost a beloved parent, filled the
ways with the sound of their lamenting. The farmer ceased from his
plough, and the pounding woman laid down her pestle." Court obituaries
are conventionally suspect, written to flatter the dead man's
descendants - but Shōtoku left no dynasty of his own to flatter, and
the breadth of the grief recorded for him has outlasted every reason
to doubt it.
