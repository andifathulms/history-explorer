title: A court that stopped moving

For as long as the Yamato court remained, in substance, a military
chieftaincy ruling a few provinces and a scatter of tributaries, it had
no fixed seat. Government was conducted by the sword rather than the
sceptre, and a ruler who needed to be in camp or on the march as often
as on a throne had little use for a permanent capital. Nara, and
several sites in the Home Provinces before it, served the purpose only
as long as the purpose itself stayed modest.

## A choice made for the shape of the realm, not for a ruler's taste

Under Kammu, whose reign spanned the last two decades of the eighth
century, that calculation changed. As the court's territory grew and
settled, the sheer volume and complexity of government business made a
divided labour, and therefore a permanent seat, a practical necessity
rather than a luxury. In 794 the capital moved to the city that would
become known as Kyoto - chosen not for sentiment but for position,
sitting near the narrowest neck of land between the Sea of Japan and
the Pacific, close to the centre of the country's habitable and
populated area by any practical measure of geography.

## A name that outgrew its origin

The city was founded as Heian-kyō, but the more formal name fell out of
common use almost as soon as people started simply calling it the
miako - the capital - and from there, Kyoto. The name would remain
attached to the city as Japan's seat of government for the rest of the
imperial court's independent political life, through every later
century in which real power drifted elsewhere while the throne itself
never moved again.

## What permanence actually bought the throne

A fixed capital let the reformed administration Kamatari's regency had
begun a century and a half earlier finally operate at full capacity:
officials could specialise, records could accumulate in one place across
reigns rather than travelling with a mobile court, and the business of
governing an expanding realm no longer competed directly with the
business of campaigning to hold it. It was this same settled, layered
bureaucracy - built to run itself regardless of who occupied the
throne - that would make it possible, within a few more generations,
for a family of court officials to run it without occupying the throne
at all.
