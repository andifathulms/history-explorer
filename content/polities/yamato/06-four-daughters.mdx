For more than thirty years, one Fujiwara regent's word was law in the
capital, if not always in the provinces - and the arrangement he
perfected was simple enough to state in a single fact: Fujiwara no
Michinaga became the father-in-law of four successive emperors and the
grandfather of as many.

## Thrusting aside the rightful heir

The regency should have gone, in 995, to Michinaga's nephew Korechika,
the acknowledged head of the clan after his father's death. Michinaga,
the fifth son of the previous regent, set him aside and took the office
himself. He held it, and the office of chancellor after it, without
serious internal challenge for the rest of his life.

The method was marriage, applied with a thoroughness no earlier
Fujiwara had managed. In 999 his eldest daughter married the reigning
Emperor Ichijō. When Ichijō died in 1011, Michinaga raised his own
cousin to the throne as Emperor Sanjō and married him to his second
daughter. Sanjō went blind and abdicated in 1016; Michinaga's own
grandson succeeded as Emperor Go-Ichijō and had to marry his aunt, the
regent's third daughter. A fourth daughter went to Go-Ichijō's
brother, the future Emperor Go-Suzaku; a fifth was married to a prince
who had briefly stood heir presumptive to the throne himself - a prince
already married to a Fujiwara lady from a rival branch of the family,
who returned to her own father in such fury at being supplanted that
his hair is recorded as having turned grey from the shock. He is said
to have gone to work at once trying to destroy Michinaga by magic. It
did not work. No other noble family in the capital dared offer a
daughter as a candidate empress again while Michinaga lived.

## Nails and teeth

None of this would have held without force behind it, and the
imperial guard - by Michinaga's day a rabble of bribed placemen who
blockaded the palace gates over unpaid rice rations and had to be
bribed with drugged sake by an exasperated provincial governor before
they would disperse - could not supply it. Michinaga built his own:
the Minamoto general Yorimitsu and his retainers, known at court as the
regent's "nails and teeth," a private military household whose loyalty
did not depend on the crumbling machinery of state service at all.
It was an arrangement that solved Michinaga's own problem and created
a much larger one for his descendants - the same Minamoto and Taira
houses that would, within two centuries, stop taking Fujiwara orders
and start giving them.

## What Michinaga's court produced

The court Michinaga ruled from behind was, by the same tradition's own
account, also the court that produced the Tale of Genji. Murasaki
Shikibu, a provincial governor's daughter serving in the household of
Michinaga's own daughter, the Empress Shōshi, is said to have shut
herself in a tower overlooking Lake Biwa and, on a night when the full
moon lit the water, written two chapters of the tale in a single
sitting - the beginning of a fifty-four-chapter work she is said to
have finished within weeks and presented, through the empress, to the
throne itself.

Court life in these decades ran on the same currency throughout:
poetry composed on a subject the emperor himself set, a felicitous
verse or a graceful turn of phrase enough to make its author a
minister or a maid of honour, an empress or a councillor. It was a
society that had turned literary accomplishment into a form of
political capital, at exactly the moment one family had turned
marriage into the same thing - and the Fujiwara regency at its height
and the flowering of Heian court literature were not two separate
stories happening to share a generation. They were the same court,
doing the same kind of work, in prose and in politics both.
