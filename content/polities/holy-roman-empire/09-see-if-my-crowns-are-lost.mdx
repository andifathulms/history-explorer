Frederick's final war with the papacy escalated past excommunication
in 1245, when Pope Innocent IV convened a general council at Lyon,
far outside imperial reach, and had Frederick formally declared
deposed as both Emperor and King of Sicily. It was a sentence with no
means of enforcement behind it - Frederick still held his armies, his
kingdom, and most of Germany's loyalty - but it committed the papacy
to his destruction in terms no earlier excommunication had.

## A crown held up in defiance

Frederick heard the news of his own deposition at Turin. According to
the chroniclers, he scowled at his courtiers and demanded his treasure
coffers be brought and unlocked; lifting one of his crowns and placing
it on his own head, eyes flashing, voice shaking with rage, he
declared he had not yet lost his crown, and that it would cost the
Pope and his Council a bloody struggle before they took it from him.
The five years that followed were exactly that - a grinding war on
multiple fronts across Italy and Germany, fought by a king in his
fifties against an opponent who could depose him on paper but not
unseat him in the field.

## A death that ended the century's first half

Frederick died at Fiorentino in December 1250, still uncrowned by any
reconciliation with Rome. His body was carried to Taranto for shipment
to Sicily under guard of two hundred Saracen soldiers and six troops
of cavalry, mourned by wailing crowds in every town the procession
passed through, and buried at Palermo beside his father, grandfather
and first wife in a porphyry tomb still supported by four carved
lions. When the tomb was opened centuries later, his body was found
still crowned, a golden orb in one hand, a jewel-set ring on his
finger, and a red silk cross embroidered on his shoulder marking him
as the crusader-king who had taken Jerusalem without a battle twenty
years before.

## An emperor who would not stay dead

Frederick's death was so hard for his own contemporaries to credit
that followers of the prophet Joachim of Fiore insisted for months
that their prophesied Antichrist could not really have died, citing a
Sibylline verse that he was "alive, and yet is not alive." A beggar on
Mount Etna claimed to be the returned Emperor, grew out his beard, and
gathered noble backers before Frederick's own son Manfred had him
executed; a second impostor appeared in Germany decades later and was
burned alive by the reigning king, Rudolf of Habsburg. As late as 1290,
forty years after Fiorentino, Germans were still laying wagers that the
great Hohenstaufen would return with an army. No emperor before or
after him was mourned and feared in quite the same measure - the last
ruler, his own biographers judged, whose authority Germans and
Italians alike had once accepted without serious question.
