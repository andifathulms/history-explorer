Voltaire's line — that it was neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire — is the
best-known thing anybody has said about this polity and it is a joke made by a
man arguing for something else. It has also shaped the reading of eight hundred
and forty-four years, and Wilson's book is substantially an argument against
it.

The objection to the joke is not that the empire was holy, Roman and an empire.
It is that the test is wrong. Voltaire is measuring against the centralised
territorial state that France was becoming, finding this polity is not one, and
concluding it was nothing. What it actually was is a framework — a legal order
covering central Europe, with a head, a supreme court, a diet and a body of
constitutional law, inside which several hundred territories of wildly different
size and status governed themselves and mostly did not fight each other.

By the standard of not being conquered, it outlasted every centralised state
around it. By the standard of doing what a state does, it never began.

## Otto

Otto I was king of East Francia, one of the three pieces the Carolingian empire
had been divided into at Verdun in 843. He was a Saxon — from the people
Charlemagne had spent thirty-two years conquering and baptising at spearpoint —
and in 962 he was crowned emperor at Rome, seventy-four years after the last
Carolingian emperor had been deposed.

That was a claim rather than a continuation, and it is worth insisting on the
difference. Nothing had survived from 888 to be revived. There was no
institution in abeyance, no line of pretenders, no body of officials waiting.
What there was, was a title with two hundred years of prestige attached to it
and nobody currently using it in the west.

The doctrine that grew up around this was *translatio imperii*: the Roman empire
had passed from the Romans to the Greeks, from the Greeks to the Franks, and
from the Franks to the Germans, and the German king crowned at Rome held it.
That is a theory of transmission constructed after the fact to explain why a
Saxon in Aachen was the heir of Augustus. It convinced a great many people for
eight hundred years, which is a fair working definition of a successful
political idea.

## Why it was worth having

The title conferred no territory, no revenue and no additional power over
anybody. Wilson's answer to why men wanted it anyway is precedence: the emperor
was the senior secular ruler in Christendom, outranked nobody's army but everyone
else's protocol, and held a set of reserved rights — creating nobles, legitimising
bastards, chartering universities, granting the right to mint — that were worth
money and were sellable.

It was also, for most of its length, the property of one family. The Habsburgs
held it with two brief gaps from 1438 to 1806, and the honest account of why is
that a dynasty with its own large territorial base could afford an office that
paid nothing, and nobody else could.
