Frederick Barbarossa spent much of his reign after Besançon trying to
make the imperial claim over northern Italy something more than a
title. He destroyed Milan outright in 1162 after a long siege, holding
a diet at Roncaglia to reassert imperial rights over the Lombard
cities' own coinage, tolls and justice - rights the cities had spent a
century treating as their own.

## An army broken at Legnano

The cities did not accept it. Milan was rebuilt within years, and by
1167 a coalition calling itself the Lombard League - Milan, Brescia,
Bergamo, Cremona, and eventually more than a dozen others - had formed
against him, with the backing of a papacy already locked in its own
long quarrel with Frederick over a disputed election. Years of
grinding warfare in northern Italy ended in 1176 at Legnano, where the
League's own carroccio-anchored infantry and a reserve of mounted
knights broke Frederick's army so completely that the Emperor himself
was unhorsed and, for a time, given up for dead. It was the first
outright battlefield defeat of his reign, and it settled the argument
Roncaglia had tried to close by force: the Lombard cities could not be
governed as a Roman province by an army that could be beaten.

## A peace that wrote the defeat down as law

Frederick made his peace with Pope Alexander III at Venice the
following year, ending the papal side of the conflict, but the
Lombard cities held out for formal terms of their own. The Peace of
Constance in 1183 gave them what Roncaglia had denied: full
self-government, the right to make war and peace among themselves,
and control of their own regalia in exchange for a nominal oath of
fidelity to the Emperor and a fixed tribute. It was, in substance, an
admission that the empire's Italian claim would from now on be a
formality the cities purchased rather than an authority they obeyed.

## A crown married into Sicily

Frederick spent his final years securing the dynasty rather than
Italy directly. In 1186 he married his son and heir Henry to
Constance, the aunt and heiress of the Norman kings of Sicily - a
match that promised to fold the wealthiest kingdom in the
Mediterranean into Hohenstaufen hands, and that would, within a
decade, make a Sicilian childhood normal for a future emperor rather
than a German one. Frederick himself never saw the fruits of it: he
led a massive German army on the Third Crusade in 1189 and drowned
fording a river in Cilicia the following year, leaving Henry VI to
inherit both the empire and, after a hard-fought conquest of the
island, the Sicilian crown his father's marriage had won on paper.
