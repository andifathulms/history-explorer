In October 1157 Frederick Barbarossa was holding court at Besançon when two papal
legates arrived with a letter. By the end of the second day the German princes
were on the point of killing them, and the emperor had to put a stop to it
personally.

The quarrel was about a noun.

## The letter

Adrian IV's letter is mostly a complaint. An archbishop returning from Rome had
been seized in Germany by impious and godless men who rose against him with drawn
swords and took everything he had, and the emperor had let the outrage pass
without the vengeance that was fitting: the sword given him by divine concession
to punish evil-doers ought to have raged above the neck of the impious. The pope
is entirely ignorant of the cause of this negligence, having always loved the
emperor as a most dear and special son.

Then, working round to the reproach, he reminds Frederick what he owes:

> Thou should'st, oh most glorious son, bring before the eyes of thy mind how
> graciously and how joyfully thy mother the holy Roman church received thee in
> a former year; with what affection of heart she treated thee; what plenitude
> of dignity and honour she granted thee; and how, most willingly conferring
> upon thee the distinction of the imperial crown, she strove to cherish in her
> most bountiful lap thee at the summit of thy sublimity.

And he would rejoice, he adds, if the emperor had received from his hand even
greater *beneficia*, if that were possible.

*Beneficium* means a kindness. It also means a fief — land held of a lord, on
terms, by a man who is the lord's vassal. Frederick's chancellor read it out in
the second sense.

## The manifesto

The emperor's circular to the German church is the fullest statement anybody ever
made of what the imperial title was supposed to rest on, and it was provoked by
a translation dispute.

The legates, he writes, came asserting that they brought a message from which the
honour of the empire should receive no little increase; they were honourably
received on the first day; on the second, sitting with his princes, he heard what
they had brought — that he should always keep before his mind's eye how the lord
pope had conferred upon him the distinction of the imperial crown, and would not
regret it if his Highness were to receive from him even greater benefices.

> Of a truth at that word, blasphemous and devoid of all truth, not only did the
> imperial majesty conceive a righteous indignation, but also all the princes who
> were present were filled with such fury and wrath that, without doubt, they
> would have condemned those two unhallowed presbyters to the punishment of
> death had not our presence prevented them.

Searching the legates' baggage produced blank sealed forms to be filled in at
their discretion, which Frederick reads as a plan to strip the German churches;
they were sent back the way they came.

Then the constitutional claim, in one sentence:

> Inasmuch as the kingdom, together with the empire, is ours by the election of
> the princes from God alone, who, by the passion of His Son Christ subjected the
> world to the rule of the two necessary swords … whoever shall say that we
> received the imperial crown as a benefice from the lord pope, contradicts the
> divine institutions and the teaching of Peter, and shall be guilty of a lie.

He would rather incur danger of death, he ends, than sustain the shame of so
great a disaster in his own day.

## What was actually at stake

Both halves of that sentence matter, and the second half is the one that shaped
the next six hundred years.

*From God alone* is the answer to Canossa: the empire is not held of the papacy,
and nobody can depose its holder for disobedience. That argument had been running
since Gregory VII and would run until the Reformation.

*By the election of the princes* is the concession hidden inside the defiance. In
order to say that the crown did not come from the pope, the emperor had to say
where it did come from, and the only available answer was the men in the room.
Frederick's manifesto puts the electing princes between God and the emperor as a
matter of public doctrine, in a document circulated to every bishop in Germany,
and signed by a man arguing that his own authority was unlimited.

Two hundred years later the Golden Bull would write that principle down as
procedure, name the seven men who did the electing, and make the papal role
disappear entirely. The princes who nearly lynched two priests over a Latin word
at Besançon were defending an empire that, by defending it in those terms, they
were acquiring.
