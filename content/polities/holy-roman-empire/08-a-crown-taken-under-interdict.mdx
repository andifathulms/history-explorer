---
title: A crown taken under interdict
drafted_from: kington-1862

Frederick had sworn to go on crusade as early as 1215, and delaying it
repeatedly for reasons of state gave Pope Gregory IX the pretext to
excommunicate him in 1227. Frederick sailed for the Holy Land the
following year regardless - an excommunicated man leading a crusade,
a contradiction the papacy never let him forget and one he pointedly
ignored.

## A treaty instead of a battle

Rather than fight the campaign Christendom expected, Frederick opened
negotiations with Sultan al-Kamil of Egypt, who had his own reasons to
avoid a war on a second front. The resulting Treaty of Jaffa in 1229
handed Jerusalem, Bethlehem, Nazareth and a corridor connecting them
to the coast back to Christian control for ten years, in exchange for
Frederick's promise to help defend the Sultan against his own Muslim
rivals and to leave the Temple Mount's mosques under Muslim
administration. It restored more of the old Kingdom of Jerusalem than
Richard the Lionheart's or Philip Augustus's armies had managed by
force during the Third Crusade, and it did so without a single major
battle - a result that scandalized the Latin East's own establishment
as much as it satisfied Frederick.

## A coronation with no priest willing to perform it

Because Frederick was still under excommunication, no bishop would
crown him in Jerusalem, and the Patriarch placed the city itself under
interdict the moment he arrived. Frederick went to the Holy Sepulchre
anyway, and in a scene his own Teutonic Knights described in careful
detail, simply lifted the crown from the altar with his own hands and
carried it to his throne without any religious ceremony at all - the
Grand Master Hermann von Salza delivering a speech in Frederick's
honor in both German and Latin in place of a coronation Mass. Genoese
crossbowmen, Pisan sailors and German knights filled the church; a few
Templars and Hospitallers, loyal to the Patriarch's faction, watched
in open disapproval, storing up details to report to Rome. Frederick
had recovered Jerusalem for Christendom while formally cut off from
the Christian sacraments himself - the strangest coronation any
crusader king had staged in the city since Godfrey de Bouillon had
refused a crown there altogether more than a century before.

## A war waiting at home

News that a papal army under the exiled former King of Jerusalem,
John de Brienne, had invaded Frederick's own Apulian territory reached
him within weeks of the coronation, cutting short whatever advantage
the treaty might otherwise have built. Frederick spent barely a
fortnight more in the Holy Land before sailing home to fight the
Pope's own army on Italian soil - a war fought, on both sides, by
Christian forces while the crusader-king who had just reclaimed
Jerusalem without spilling blood was formally still an excommunicate
in his own Church's eyes.
