Napoleon beat the Austrians and Russians at Austerlitz in December 1805. Seven
months later sixteen German princes left the empire and formed the Confederation
of the Rhine under French protection. On 6 August 1806 Francis II announced that
he was laying down the imperial crown and releasing the estates from their
obligations to it.

He had already given himself a replacement. In 1804, watching Napoleon crown
himself emperor of the French, Francis had proclaimed himself hereditary emperor
of Austria — creating a title out of his own territories so that when the older
one went he would not be outranked.

The abdication was calculated to prevent Napoleon from taking the imperial title
himself, which he had considered. Eight hundred and forty-four years of an
institution were ended by a preemptive resignation.

## The field says `fragmentation` and the vocabulary has no better word

This site's `END_TYPES` are conquest, fragmentation, dynastic replacement,
gradual absorption, internal usurpation and still contested. A polity dissolved
by a unilateral act of its own head is none of them.

`conquest` would credit the French, who did not take it and whose pressure was
real but indirect. `internal usurpation` describes somebody seizing the throne,
not the man on it abolishing the throne. `gradual absorption` describes the
decades either side and not the act. `fragmentation` is coded because it
describes what the territory did — the member states became sovereign and stayed
that way — and `by` is empty because Napoleonic France is not in this corpus.

This is the third record in recent additions to sit awkwardly in that list, after
the Attalid kingdom left to Rome by will and the Venetian republic that voted
itself out of existence with an army on the mainland. Three cases with three
different problems is not a pattern in the sense that produced the `investiture`
value from nine records asking the same question, and no change is proposed. It
is worth noting that all three are polities ending by document rather than by
battle, which may eventually be a pattern.

## What it was, measured

This record's `reach_km2` is a million square kilometres, dated 1050 — before
anything on this page's list of turning points, and a measurement of the Salian
empire at its widest rather than of the thing the record is about.

That figure places it forty-second of the fifty-one records on this site that
carry one at all. The Roman record above it is five times larger and this site's own
frontier chapter for Rome says a reader arriving there expecting the largest
empire in history will not find it in that column either.

The measure this polity would score on, if the site had one, is duration and
membership: eight hundred and forty-four years, several hundred constituent
entities, and a legal order that a village-sized county could invoke against a
duchy. There is no column for that, and there is not going to be one, because a
site that adds a measure every time a record is unusual stops being able to
compare anything.

## What it left

The states. The German territories that emerged in 1806 as sovereign are the ones
the empire had kept in existence, and the map of nineteenth-century Germany is a
consolidation of them rather than a new arrangement.

And an argument. Wilson's book is written against two hundred years of German
historiography that treated the empire as a failure because it did not become a
nation-state, and against Voltaire's joke, which is the same judgement in one
sentence. This record does not settle that and does not need to. What it can do
is fill four institution fields and five turning points from a polity that
Voltaire said was nothing, and let a reader see that the fields are not empty.
