James Bryce opens his history of the Holy Roman Empire with the observation that
the people who read about its end in the English newspapers in August 1806
probably did not notice what they were reading:

> Of those who in August, 1806, read in the English newspapers that the Emperor
> Francis II had announced to the Diet his resignation of the imperial crown,
> there were probably few who reflected that the oldest political institution in
> the world had come to an end. Yet it was so.

He is entitled to the sentence. He also, several hundred pages later, has an
opinion of the document itself. The instrument of abdication, he says in a
footnote, is in no way remarkable — except for its ludicrous resemblance to the
circular in which a tradesman announces the dissolution of an old partnership,
solicits a continuance of his customers' patronage, and hopes by close attention
to merit it, the business henceforth to be carried on under the name of, and so
on.

That is what the end of the oldest political institution in Europe reads like on
the page: a change-of-management notice.

## The insignia in the shop window

There is a story about Napoleon that Bryce prints and then dismantles, and it is
worth both halves.

Napoleon said on one occasion that he had succeeded not to Louis XIV but to
Charlemagne. His secretary Bourrienne says that in 1804, shortly before his own
coronation, he had Charlemagne's imperial insignia brought from Aachen and
displayed in a jeweller's shop in Paris alongside the regalia that had just been
made for him.

Bryce points out that this cannot be right, because Austria had removed the
insignia from Aachen in 1798. So either Bourrienne misremembered, or — and Bryce
allows the possibility without pressing it — Napoleon put something in the shop
window and let people believe it was Charlemagne's.

Either way the anecdote is exact about the thing itself. What the empire had to
offer by 1804 was a set of objects and a genealogy, and a man building a new
throne wanted to be photographed next to them.

## The crown offered and refused

The empire's most curious afterlife is that people kept trying to restart it.

When Napoleon's own arrangements collapsed and the old landmarks reappeared, it
was widely assumed the empire would simply be put back — Hanover, speaking for
Britain, was among those who wanted it. What went up at Vienna instead was the
German Confederation, which Bryce calls a feeble shadow.

Then came 1848. The Federal Diet dissolved itself in favour of a national
parliament, which met at Frankfurt in May, appointed an Austrian archduke as
administrator of the empire — *Reichsverweser*, a title straight out of the old
constitution — and drafted a scheme for a federal Germany under a hereditary
emperor advised by responsible ministers. On 28 March 1849 it offered the
imperial crown to the king of Prussia.

He took a month, and refused it. Twenty-nine princes had already approved the
scheme; Frederick William IV feared the jealousy of the others, disliked parts
of the constitution, and by Bryce's reading was entangled in notions of divine
right and various other medieval whimsies. The refusal split the liberals and
destroyed their hope of material backing, and within two years the Confederation
was back on its old footing with its old powerlessness for good and its old
capacity for mischief.

An elected assembly offered a crown; a king declined it on the ground that an
assembly had no business offering one. It is the last time anybody was asked to
be a German emperor on those terms.

## The offspring

Twenty-two years later Bryce added a supplementary chapter to his book, because
there was a German empire again.

> In 1806 the Holy Empire died and was buried and to all appearance soon
> forgotten. No outworn shape of the past could have seemed less likely to be
> ever recalled to life … Then suddenly there arises from these cold ashes a
> new, vigorous, self-confident German Empire, a State which, although most
> different, as well in its inner character as in its form and legal aspect,
> from its venerable predecessor, is nevertheless in a very real sense that
> predecessor's representative.

He goes further: the new empire is, in a moral and intellectual sense, the
offspring of the old one, and but for the pre-existence of the other could never
have come into being at all.

It is a striking thing for a Liberal Englishman to write in 1871, and it is worth
saying plainly that the polity he was writing about did not share his account of
itself. What was proclaimed at Versailles was a Prussian king made emperor by
other German princes, in a hall belonging to France, at the end of a war. Its
constitution was Bismarck's and it owed nothing to the Golden Bull. The
continuity Bryce felt so strongly was a continuity of name and sentiment, which
is the kind that persuades people.

And the persuasion was the whole of the empire's own method for eight hundred
years. A Saxon king in 962 claimed to be resuming Charlemagne, who had claimed to
be resuming Augustus, on a doctrine invented afterwards to explain how the office
had travelled. A German empire in 1871 counting itself the second and this one
the first is the same operation performed one more time.

Bryce was not being sentimental. He was describing, from the inside and without
quite noticing, the one thing the Holy Roman Empire was unambiguously good at.
