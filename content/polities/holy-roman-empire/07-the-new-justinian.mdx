Frederick II grew up in Sicily rather than Germany, orphaned young and
crowned King of Sicily as a child under a series of regents while
German princes elected him King of the Romans in absentia. The
kingdom he came into as an adult, once he had finally subdued its
rebellious barons and Muslim strongholds in the island's interior,
became the testing ground for an idea of royal government that had no
real parallel anywhere else in thirteenth-century Europe.

## An army from an unlikely source

Having crushed a long Saracen revolt in Sicily rather than exterminate
its population, Frederick took an unusual path: he transported some
twenty thousand able-bodied Muslims from the island to the mainland
and resettled them at Lucera in Apulia, emptying the town of its
Christian inhabitants to make room for them, turning its cathedral
into a mosque, and ringing it with one of the largest citadels in
Italy. In Sicily the Saracens had been a chronic security problem,
reinforced from time to time by kinsmen from North Africa; transplanted
to the mainland and cut off from that support, they became some of
Frederick's most reliable soldiers - troops with no religious
scruples at all about being turned against the Pope himself when it
came to that.

## A code of law without sixty different customs

In 1231 Frederick issued the Constitutions of Melfi, a single
comprehensive law code for the whole kingdom that superseded local
custom outright and made royal authority the sole source of justice.
His own chroniclers contrasted the result directly with contemporary
France, where royal power still had to bargain with sixty-odd
different regional legal customs, nobles who coined their own money
and fought their own private wars, and towns still groaning under
episcopal lordship. Melfi's preamble styled Frederick the source and
guardian of law in language modeled on Justinian's own Roman codes,
and its substance backed the claim: distinct civil and criminal
courts, royal officials answerable to the crown rather than to local
lords, and a bureaucracy that ran on written record rather than
feudal custom - a state apparatus so far ahead of its northern
counterparts that later historians would call Sicily under Frederick
the first modern administrative kingdom in Europe.

## A war that outlasted popes

None of it reconciled Frederick with Rome. Innocent III's successors
regarded a single ruler holding both the empire and the kingdom that
surrounded the Papal States on two sides as an existential threat, and
the conflict between Frederick and a sequence of hostile popes -
excommunicated once by Gregory IX for delaying his crusade, and again
for making peace with the Sultan on terms Rome considered
insufficiently belligerent - ran for the rest of his reign. Frederick
answered papal propaganda with his own, styling himself in official
documents as a divinely appointed reformer of a corrupt Church rather
than its rebellious son. Neither side ever fully won the argument; it
outlasted Frederick himself and consumed his own dynasty within a
generation of his death.
