This record's `revenue_basis` field reads `tribute`, one value, and the data
comment says the value is not right and there is no better one.

What the empire had was matricular contributions: quotas assessed on the member
territories according to a register, voted by the Diet, expressed in units called
Roman Months because the original assessment was what it cost to keep an army in
Italy for a month. The imperial cities paid dues directly, and so did the Jewish
communities, both being under the emperor's immediate protection. There were
imperial tolls on the Rhine, most of which the princes along it had appropriated.

That is the whole of it, and it was never enough for anything.

## The gap this points at

`tribute` in this site's vocabulary means revenue received from another polity,
and these are contributions from members of this one. The value is doing the job
of a federal levy and there is no word for that in a list of seven.

The larger absence is the one this corpus's Carolingian record already named: the
money that actually paid for imperial policy came from the emperor's own dynastic
lands. A Habsburg emperor funded his wars out of Austria, Bohemia and Hungary,
which he held as a territorial ruler and not as emperor, and the fact that this
site's `revenue_basis` list has no value for a ruler's own domain means that the
single largest item in two of its records' finances is not in either of them.

That is now the second record to point at it. Nine records pointing at a missing
legitimation value produced a change to the vocabulary; two is not nine, and this
site's practice is to say so on the page and wait.

## What a state does instead of taxing

It litigates.

The Reichsreform of 1495, this record's third turning point, declared a perpetual
public peace outlawing private war and created the Reichskammergericht, a supreme
court staffed by the estates rather than by the emperor. It voted a general tax
at the same time, the Common Penny, which was not collected. The court worked.

For the following three centuries the empire's members largely sued each other.
A small imperial county with a grievance against a large neighbouring duchy did
not raise troops it did not have; it filed, and the case took decades, and the
duchy frequently lost. Wilson's argument, and the reason this record's turning
points are all documents, is that this was the empire's actual function: it kept
several hundred entities of wildly unequal power inside one legal order in which
the weak had somewhere to go.

It is also why an imperial county of four thousand people could exist at all in
the seventeenth century, in the middle of a continent of consolidating monarchies,
and why so many of them did.

## The army that was mocked

`military_basis` reads `client-levy, mercenary` and the first value is the
Reichsarmee: from 1681 a quota system in which each of the imperial circles owed a
fixed number of horse and foot, raised and paid by its own princes and assembled
when the Diet declared an imperial war.

It was slow to gather, unevenly equipped, commanded by whoever the circles could
agree on, and a standing joke. It was also the only army in Europe that a polity
with no revenue could put in the field, and it fought the Ottomans and the French
for a hundred and twenty years without ever being able to be used for anything
its members did not agree on — which, from the point of view of the smaller
members, was the entire point.
