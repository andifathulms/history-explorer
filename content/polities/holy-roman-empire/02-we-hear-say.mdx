Otto I was crowned emperor in Rome on 2 February 962 by Pope John XII, who was
about twenty-five and had inherited the papacy from his father along with the
city. Within a year the pope had made an alliance with Otto's enemy in Italy and
Otto had come back. Liudprand, bishop of Cremona, was present for what followed
and wrote it up, and it is the earliest detailed account of how the revived
empire actually worked.

Liudprand is not a neutral witness. He was Otto's diplomat, he wrote the account
for Otto's court, and the chapters are a brief for the prosecution. That is not a
reason to discard them. It is a reason to notice which details he thought helped
his case.

## The oath

When the emperor camped near Rome, the pope and his ally left the city together
and the citizens let Otto in — promising again to be loyal, and adding, under a
strong oath, that they would never elect or ordain a pope except with the consent
and approval of the emperor and his son.

That single sentence is the constitutional content of the revival. Whatever
*translatio imperii* meant in theory, in practice what a German king got by being
crowned in Rome was a veto on who crowned him.

## The synod

Three days later a synod met in St Peter's, and Liudprand lists the attendance
the way a man does when the length of the list is the argument: archbishops from
Milan and Ravenna, bishops from Saxony and France, the Italians, and then the
Roman bishops one after another — Albano, Ostia, Porto, Palestrina, Velletri,
Tivoli, Sutri, Narni, Anagni, Orte, and a dozen more. The Roman church was being
convened against its own head in its own basilica.

The charges were then given in order. A cardinal priest testified that he had
seen the pope celebrate mass without himself communicating. Two others said they
had seen him ordain a deacon in a stable, and at the wrong time of year. The
cardinal deacons said he had been paid for consecrating bishops and had made a
ten-year-old a bishop at Todi. On the sacrilege, they said, no inquiry was needed:
knowledge of it was a matter of eyesight, not hearsay. On the adultery they had
no visual information but knew for certain about four women including his own
niece, and that he had turned the palace into a brothel. He had hunted publicly.
He had blinded his spiritual father, who died of it. He had castrated a cardinal
subdeacon, who died of it. He had set houses on fire and gone about in sword,
helmet and cuirass. Everybody present, clergy and laity, accused him of drinking
wine for love of the devil; at dice he called on Jupiter and Venus; he did not
say matins or cross himself.

Then Liudprand records the thing that gives the whole scene away. The emperor had
to answer, and could not, because the Romans could not understand his native
Saxon and he had no Latin — so he told the bishop of Cremona to get up and say it
for him.

The heir of Augustus needed an interpreter to address a synod in Rome.

## The letter

The synod wrote to the absent pope, politely, saying that his sons the Roman
clergy had brought out such foul and filthy tales that they would be ashamed of
them even if told about actors, and begging his paternal highness to come and
clear himself, with an oath that nothing would be done contrary to the canons.

He replied. Liudprand gives the whole of it, and the whole of it is three
sentences:

> Bishop John, servant of God's servants, to all the bishops. We hear say that
> you wish to make another pope. If you do, I excommunicate you by Almighty God,
> and you have no power to ordain no one or celebrate mass.

The Latin is bad and Liudprand reproduces the badness deliberately; it is the
cheapest and most effective thing in his account. But the reply is not stupid.
It identifies, in one line, the only question that mattered — whether a synod
convened by a foreign king can unmake a pope — and answers it with the one weapon
the papacy had.

John was deposed and Leo VIII elected in his place. Otto went north. John came
back into Rome, held a synod of his own, and undid everything; he died a few
months later. The Romans then elected somebody else without asking the emperor,
who came back a third time, and Liudprand records the successful candidate being
asked in open synod by what authority he wore the pontifical robes, and whether
he denied swearing that no pope would be elected without imperial consent, and
answering: *Have mercy upon my sin*.

## What was founded here

Not a state. What Otto founded in these three years was a relationship, and its
terms were set in public: the emperor makes the pope, and the pope makes the
emperor, and neither can do his half without the other's city.

It worked for a century, in the sense that emperors appointed popes and nobody
successfully objected. Then somebody did.
