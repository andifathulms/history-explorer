title: An empire with no revenue

The Holy Roman Empire had no taxes worth the name.

What it had was matricular contributions: quotas assessed on the member
territories according to a register, voted by the Diet, and expressed in units
called Roman Months, because the original assessment was what it cost to keep an
army in Italy for a month. The imperial cities paid dues directly, and so did the
Jewish communities, both being under the emperor's immediate protection. There
were imperial tolls on the Rhine, most of which the princes along it had
appropriated.

That is the whole of it, and it was never enough for anything.

The money that actually paid for imperial policy came from somewhere else
entirely: the emperor's own dynastic lands. A Habsburg emperor funded his wars
out of Austria, Bohemia and Hungary, which he held as a territorial ruler and not
as emperor at all. The empire's finances and the emperor's finances were
different things, and only one of them worked.

## What a state does instead of taxing

It litigates.

The Reichsreform of 1495 declared a perpetual public peace outlawing private war,
and created the Reichskammergericht, a supreme court staffed by the estates
rather than by the emperor. It voted a general tax at the same time, the Common
Penny, which was not collected. The court worked.

For the following three centuries the empire's members largely sued each other. A
small imperial county with a grievance against a large neighbouring duchy did not
raise troops it did not have; it filed, and the case took decades, and the duchy
frequently lost. Wilson's argument is that this was the empire's actual function:
it kept several hundred entities of wildly unequal power inside one legal order
in which the weak had somewhere to go.

It is also why an imperial county of four thousand people could exist at all in
the seventeenth century, in the middle of a continent of consolidating
monarchies, and why so many of them did.

## The army that was mocked

From 1681 the Reichsarmee worked on a quota system: each of the imperial circles
owed a fixed number of horse and foot, raised and paid by its own princes, and
assembled when the Diet declared an imperial war.

It was slow to gather, unevenly equipped, commanded by whoever the circles could
agree on, and a standing joke. It was also the only army in Europe that a polity
with no revenue could put in the field, and it fought the Ottomans and the French
for a hundred and twenty years without ever being usable for anything its members
did not agree on — which, from the point of view of the smaller members, was the
entire point.
