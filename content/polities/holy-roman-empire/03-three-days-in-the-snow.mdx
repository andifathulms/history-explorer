For a century after Otto, emperors appointed popes and invested bishops with the
ring and the staff, which was how a state with no tax paid its officials: the
imperial church held a large share of the land in Germany, and the men who held
it were the emperor's men because the emperor had put them there.

In 1075 a pope decided to end that, and the documents of the quarrel survive
almost complete, which for the eleventh century is extraordinary.

## The claims

Gregory VII's position was set out in a memorandum of twenty-seven propositions,
each of one line. Among them:

> That the Roman pontiff alone can with right be called universal.
>
> That he alone may use the imperial insignia.
>
> That of the pope alone all princes shall kiss the feet.
>
> That it may be permitted to him to depose emperors.
>
> That he himself may be judged by no one.
>
> That the Roman church has never erred; nor will it err to all eternity.

The German bishops answered for the king. Their letter is addressed *to brother
Hildebrand* — the pope's own name, with no title — and accuses him of having
invaded the helm of the church, of tearing the members of the church with proud
cruelty and cruel pride, of taking from bishops the power divinely conferred on
them, and of filling the church with the ill odour of a grave charge concerning
his living with a strange woman: all the decrees of the apostolic see, they say,
have been set in motion by women, and the whole circle of the church is
administered through this new senate of women. Then the sentence the document
exists for: *we renounce the obedience which we never promised to thee.*

Gregory's reply, five weeks later, is addressed to somebody else entirely. It
begins *O St Peter, chief of the apostles, incline to us, I beg, thy holy ears*,
and it is a report to a superior — that the Roman church drew him to its helm
against his will, that he would rather have died a pilgrim than seized the
throne, and that the power of binding and loosing has been granted him. Then:

> I withdraw, through thy power and authority, from Henry the king, son of Henry
> the emperor, who has risen against thy church with unheard of insolence, the
> rule over the whole kingdom of the Germans and over Italy. And I absolve all
> Christians from the bonds of the oath which they have made or shall make to
> him; and I forbid any one to serve him as king.

That last clause is the weapon, and it was aimed past Henry at the German
princes. A king whose subjects' oaths have been cancelled is a king whose great
men may lawfully stop obeying him, and the German magnates had wanted a reason
for years.

## Canossa

Henry went to Italy in the winter of 1076–77 to get the sentence lifted before
the princes could act on it, and Gregory — who had been heading north to meet
those same princes — found himself at the castle of Canossa in the Apennines with
the king outside.

The account of what happened is Gregory's own, in a circular letter to the German
princes, and it has to be read as what it is: an explanation to the people he had
been about to join, of why he had let their quarry off.

> He came at length of his own accord, with a few followers, showing nothing of
> hostility or boldness, to the town of Canossa where we were tarrying. And
> there, having laid aside all the belongings of royalty, wretchedly, with bare
> feet and clad in wool, he continued for three days to stand before the gate of
> the castle. Nor did he desist from imploring with many tears the aid and
> consolation of the apostolic mercy until he had moved all of those who were
> present there, and whom the report of it reached, to such pity and depth of
> compassion that, interceding for him with many prayers and tears, all wondered
> indeed at the unaccustomed hardness of our heart, while some actually cried
> out that we were exercising, not the gravity of apostolic severity, but the
> cruelty, as it were, of a tyrannical ferocity.

Conquered, he says, by the persistency of the king's compunction and the
supplications of everyone present, he loosed the chain of the anathema.

Then he spends the rest of the letter telling the princes it changes nothing.
The whole question at issue is as yet so little cleared up that his coming and
their unanimity remain extremely necessary; they are to continue in the faith in
which they have begun; and he is not otherwise bound to the king except that, by
word alone as is his custom, he has said Henry might have hopes from him.

A pope who had just won the most famous submission in European history spends
three-quarters of his account of it reassuring his allies that he has not sold
them out. Which is the correct reading of Canossa: Henry, by performing penance,
had forced a priest to do what priests exist to do, and had thereby taken the
princes' legal ground away from under them. He was excommunicated again three
years later and this time it did not work at all.

## What it settled

The quarrel ran another forty-five years and ended in a compromise so narrow that
it reads like a drafting exercise. At Worms in 1122 the pope granted that
elections of German bishops and abbots should take place in the emperor's
presence, without simony and without violence, and that the man elected should
receive the *regalia* — the lands and secular rights of the office — from the
emperor, by the touch of a lance. The emperor for his part remitted to God and
the apostles all investiture through ring and staff, and granted canonical
election and free consecration in all the churches of his empire.

Two objects and two ceremonies. The ring and the staff, which are the bishop's
spiritual office, come from the church; the lance, which is the land, comes from
the emperor. The empire kept the estates and lost the men.

That was the end of the arrangement Otto had built. An emperor could no longer
staff his own church, and the imperial church had been the nearest thing to an
administration the empire had. Everything afterwards — the elective monarchy, the
princes, the empire that could not tax and had to litigate instead — follows from
a settlement about which of two objects a bishop receives from whom.
