Luther posted in 1517 and by 1530 a substantial part of the empire's princes and
imperial cities were Lutheran, in a polity whose head was Catholic and whose
central claim was that it was the secular arm of Christendom.

The Peace of Augsburg in 1555 settled it by
territorialising it: each ruler determined the confession of his own lands,
subjects who disagreed were free to emigrate, and the imperial framework held
above a religious division it had stopped trying to resolve. It is usually
summarised as *cuius regio, eius religio*, a phrase coined sixty years later by a
lawyer.

It lasted sixty-three years and then failed, and the failure was the Thirty Years'
War: a confessional civil war inside the empire that drew in Denmark, Sweden,
Spain, France and the Dutch, and killed something in the region of a fifth of
the German population.

## Westphalia

The settlement of 1648 is conventionally read as the empire's death certificate —
the moment the territories became sovereign, the emperor became a figurehead, and
the modern state system began.

Wilson reads it as a restoration, and the disagreement is real enough to leave
standing rather than settle. His case is that the treaties confirmed
the constitution rather than dissolving it: the territories got the right to
conduct their own foreign policy but not against the emperor or the empire, the
imperial institutions were repaired and went on working, Calvinism was added to
the recognised confessions, and the framework then held for another hundred and
fifty-eight years across two more general European wars.

An institution that survives the worst war in European history to that date, and
functions afterwards, is not obviously dead. What is true is that after 1648 the
empire was manifestly a framework and not a state, and had stopped pretending
otherwise.

## What the framework did

It is easy to describe an empire with no revenue, no army of its own, no capital
and no common language as an absence. What Wilson's account adds is what the
absence made possible.

Several hundred territories — electorates, duchies, bishoprics, abbeys, counties,
free cities, and about fifteen hundred imperial knights holding a village apiece
— existed inside it with legal standing. A ruler who attacked a neighbour broke
the public peace and was liable in a court that could and did find against him.
A subject with a grievance against his own prince could appeal past him to an
imperial court, and after 1648 they increasingly did.

Almost no other European polity was arranged that way, and the ones that came
after it were not. A ruler who attacked a neighbour anywhere else in Europe was
answerable to nobody but the other man's army. Inside the empire he was
answerable to a court, and the court took decades and frequently found against
him, and the decades were the point: a lawsuit that grinds on for thirty years
is a war that does not happen.

That is the whole case for the thing, and it is not a small one. Fifteen hundred
imperial knights holding a village apiece, and abbeys, and free cities, and
counties the size of a parish, survived into the age of Louis XIV and Frederick
the Great because there was a legal order above them that the large states had
agreed to be bound by. When it went in 1806 they went with it inside a decade.
