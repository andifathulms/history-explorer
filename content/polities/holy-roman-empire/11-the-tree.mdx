The empire's purpose, on the most favourable reading of it, was to stop its
members going to war with each other. Between 1618 and 1648 it failed completely,
and the best account of what that failure looked like at ground level was written
by a man who had been a boy on a farm when the soldiers came.

Grimmelshausen was carried off in the war and spent most of it in and about
armies. Twenty years after it ended he published a picaresque novel whose hero
begins as an illiterate peasant child in the Spessart, and its fifth chapter is
the most famous piece of German prose of the century. It is fiction, and a satire,
and it is also the only sustained account of that war from inside it by somebody
who was not an officer, a diplomat or a chronicler.

## The farm

The troopers come. They do not loot in the sense of taking valuables; they
dismantle a household.

> Houseware of copper and tin they beat flat, and packed such vessels, all bent
> and spoiled, in with the rest. Bedsteads, tables, chairs, and benches they
> burned, though there lay many cords of dry wood in the yard. Pots and pipkins
> must all go to pieces, either because they would eat none but roast flesh, or
> because their purpose was to make there but a single meal.

The manservant is tied down, gagged, and a pail of filthy water poured into him —
*by this, which they called a Swedish draught, they forced him to lead a party of
them to another place*, where more people and animals were taken and brought back
to the same farm.

Then the interrogation, and the boy narrating it has not yet learned what he is
looking at. The flints are taken out of the pistols so the peasants' thumbs can
be screwed in their place. One man is put in the baking oven and a fire lit under
him, *although he had as yet confessed no crime*. Another has a cord twisted round
his head with a stick until the blood comes out of his mouth and nose and ears.
*In a word each had his own device to torture the peasants, and each peasant his
several torture.*

The narrator's father gets what the boy takes for the best of it, because he is
the householder: bound before the fire, the soles of his feet smeared with wet
salt and a goat brought to lick it off, until he nearly bursts with laughing.

> And this seemed to me so merry a thing that I must needs laugh with him for
> the sake of fellowship, or because I knew no better.

In the middle of the laughing the father tells them where the money is, and it is
richer in pearls and gold than anyone would have expected of peasants. Of what
happened to the women the child says only that the soldiers would not let him
watch, and that he heard them screaming in various corners of the house.

That night, hiding in a thicket, he can hear the shrieks of the tortured peasants
and the song of the nightingales, *which birds regarded not the peasants either
to show compassion for them or to stop their sweet song for their sakes*; and he
lies down and falls asleep.

## The tree

Some chapters later, cold and hungry in the woods, he works out a theory. He can
see no explanation for the enmity between soldiers and peasants except that there
must be two races of men in the world and not one descended from Adam — wild and
tame, like unreasoning beasts, and therefore hunting one another.

Then he dreams the image the book is remembered for. Every tree around him turns
into an army. A trooper sits on each treetop; the trunks are hung, instead of
leaves, with pikemen, musketeers, halberdiers, flags, drums and fifes, *all
neatly distributed and each according to his rank*. And the roots:

> The roots, moreover, were made up of folk of little worth, as mechanics and
> labourers, mostly, however, peasants and the like; and these nevertheless gave
> its strength to the tree and renewed the same when it was lost: yea more, they
> repaired the loss of any fallen leaves from among themselves to their own great
> damage: and all the time they lamented over them that sat on the tree, and that
> with good reason, for the whole weight of the tree lay upon them and pressed
> them so that all the money was squeezed out of their pockets, yea, though it
> was behind seven locks and keys: but if the money would not out, then did the
> commissaries so handle them with rods (which thing they call military
> execution) that sighs came from their heart, tears from their eyes, blood from
> their nails, and the marrow from their bones.

That is a description of the war's actual financing. There was no treasury
capable of paying these armies. They were raised on credit against the promise of
contributions levied where they stood, and the technical term for collecting a
contribution by force was execution. The tree is not a metaphor for injustice.
It is an organisational chart.

## What the framework was for

The settlement that ended the war is usually discussed as a turning point in the
history of sovereignty, and there is a real argument about whether it dissolved
the empire's constitution or repaired it. Grimmelshausen's book is the other half
of that argument, and it is the half that explains why the repair was worth
making.

For the century and a half that the empire had left, its members did not do this
to each other again. They litigated instead, in a court that took decades and
frequently found against the powerful, and the tedium of that procedure is
usually offered as evidence that the institution had become absurd. The farm in
the Spessart is what the procedure was an alternative to.
