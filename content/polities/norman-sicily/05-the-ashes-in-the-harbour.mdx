In 1153 the kingdom's chief minister and admiral was tried for being a Muslim
and burned to ashes in front of the royal palace at Palermo, and his ashes were
thrown into the harbour where the fleet could see it.

He had won a campaign three months earlier.

## Philip of Mahdia

Philip was a eunuch, a Muslim by birth, a Christian in outward conformity, and
the king had raised him from childhood to be master of the whole royal
household. When George of Antioch died and the Almohads began pushing east
along the African coast, Philip was given the fleet.

He did the job. He reached Bona before the Almohads did and took it with the
help of Arab tribesmen from the interior, against the resistance of the
inhabitants themselves, who did not want Christian rule. Then he proclaimed the
peace and installed a governor who may well have been the man he had just
displaced. He collected a great deal of booty. And he went one step further than
his predecessor would have: he let the leading citizens leave the city with
their families and all their property.

He returned to Sicily by way of Mahdia, in triumph, in the autumn of 1153.

## The charge

Romuald of Salerno tells it with, as Curtis says, full Christian bias.

The accusation was not about Bona. It was that Philip was in heart and mind a
Saracen; that he was in the habit of visiting the mosques; that he sent
offerings to the tomb of Muhammad; and that he ate meat in Lent. Summoned before
the royal court, he tried to save himself by promising complete Catholic
orthodoxy in future.

Roger, according to Romuald, burst into tears. He told the assembled court how
it grieved him that a servant he had brought up from childhood as a Catholic had
practised the works of the infidel under cover of the faith, and then he said the
part that mattered:

> If he had wronged me I would have forgiven him, but he has sinned against God,
> for which I dare not forgive my own son. Now, that all the world may know I
> love the Christian faith with all my heart and do not cease to avenge her
> wrongs even on my servants, let the laws be evoked … let them be armed with
> the sword of justice … to strike terror into the infidels.

The court of justiciars, counts and barons convicted him of being *christiani
nominis delusor*, a mockery of the Christian name. He was dragged to the place
of execution at the heels of a vicious horse, burned at the stake before the
palace, and his ashes thrown into the harbour in sight of the fleet.

## Why

Nobody knows, and Curtis lists the attempts without choosing between them.

Romuald says Roger sacrificed him to demonstrate his own orthodoxy. The Arabic
writers think it was for exceeding his orders at Bona — Ibn al-Athir adds the
sentence that probably explains everything, which is that the people of Sicily
were murmuring that their own king was a Muslim. Modern authorities have
suggested a king prematurely old and failing, embittered by the deaths of his
wife and his three eldest sons and frightened by the two empires conspiring
against him, clearing an old scandal off his court. Or a king fallen under
clerical influence at the end. Or that Roger had nothing to do with it and his
son William, by then associated in the kingship, allowed it — Philip being the
first victim of a nobility that hated palace officials.

What is not in dispute is the shape. A state whose whole method was to employ
whoever was competent, in whatever language they worked, discovered that this
was a thing its own subjects could hold against it — and answered by burning its
most senior Muslim-born servant in public, three months after he had won it a
city.

## The reign of toleration

Curtis's own verdict is that the toleration had forty years left in it, and that
it is unlikely Roger meant to end it.

That is right as far as it goes. The Arabic bureau kept working, the chancery
kept issuing in three languages, and the next two kings were served by men out of
all three traditions. Nothing about the machinery changed in 1153.

What changed is that it had been demonstrated. Until then the kingdom's
arrangement could be described as simply the way things were done in Palermo.
Afterwards it was a thing a king could be accused of, and a thing he could prove
he was not guilty of by killing somebody — and the man he chose was the one who
had just handed him a province.
