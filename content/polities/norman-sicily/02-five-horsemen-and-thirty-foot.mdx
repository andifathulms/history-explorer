The fullest surviving portrait of the man whose family took southern Italy was
written by the daughter of the emperor he was trying to depose.

Anna Komnene wrote her father's life half a century after the events, and Robert
Guiscard is the villain of its opening books, because in 1081 he crossed the
Adriatic with an army and nearly took the Byzantine empire's western provinces
off it. She hated him. She also could not stop admiring him, and the two
impulses are visible in the same paragraph.

> This Robert was Norman by descent, of insignificant origin, in temper
> tyrannical, in mind most cunning, brave in action, very clever in attacking the
> wealth and substance of magnates, most obstinate in achievement, for he did not
> allow any obstacle to prevent his executing his desire.

Then the physical description, which she says she had repeatedly from many who
had seen him: taller than the tallest, ruddy, flaxen-haired, broad-shouldered,
his eyes all but emitting sparks of fire, well-proportioned from tip to toe. And
then Homer — Achilles shouted and his hearers thought a multitude was in an
uproar, but this man's cry is said to have put thousands to flight. *Powerful
natures are ever like this, people say, even though they be of somewhat obscure
descent.*

## How the family started

Anna's account of how Guiscard began is the reason to read her on this. She is
not describing a conquering prince. She is describing a bandit.

He would not endure any control, she says, so he left Normandy with five
followers on horseback and thirty on foot, all told. He then roamed the mountain
ridges, caves and hills of Lombardy as the chief of a robber band, acquiring
horses, weapons and other possessions by attacking travellers. *Thus the prelude
of this man's life was marked by much bloodshed and many murders.*

He came to the notice of a regional lord named Gulielmus Mascabeles, who admired
his strength and experience in war, attached him to his household, betrothed a
daughter to him, and gave him a city as a wedding present — *with a wonderful
lack of foresight*, Anna notes. Robert spent the next years quietly trebling his
cavalry and doubling his infantry, then manufactured quarrels, and then, since
his father-in-law far surpassed him in wealth and influence, decided against
meeting him in open battle and arranged a conference instead.

The scene she gives is a short story. Two hills of equal height facing each other
across swampy ground; four picked men hidden in the scrub; Robert on the wrong
hill with fifty-six foot and fifteen horse; a man detailed to hold his helmet,
shield and sword ready. The two lords meet on the slope, embrace, and talk. Robert
draws the conversation out, and then suggests that they are tiring themselves on
horseback and might sit on the ground and speak freely. Mascabeles dismounts;
his men, seeing the principals sitting, tie their horses to branches and lie down
in the shade, because it is high summer and they are hungry and thirsty. Robert
professes fealty for the future and calls his father-in-law his faithful
benefactor and lord.

And then, in Anna's words, *the sly fox* throws himself on him.

## The war

That is the method the family brought to Italy, and Anna gives an equally exact
account of how it was used against her own.

Robert needed a pretext to invade the empire. He found a monk called Raictor and
put it about that this was the emperor Michael VII, deposed, robbed of his wife
and son, and dressed in a monk's habit instead of a crown by a usurper — and now
come as a suppliant. Since a daughter of Robert's had actually been betrothed to
Michael's son and was shut up in Constantinople, the kinship argument had a real
foundation to stand on.

He then, in Anna's description, worked up the whole drama and put it in its
proper stage-setting: the monk given the best place at table and a higher seat;
the daily displays of respect; one day Robert lamenting his daughter's fate,
another day too delicate to mention his kinsman's misfortunes, another day
promising the ignorant masses heaps of gold out of the imperial treasury. *Thus
he led all by the nose*, and dragged the whole of Lombardy out with him.

He took Dyrrachium. He was still in the field, with the empire's Balkan provinces
open in front of him, when he died in 1085, in the twenty-fifth year of his rule
as duke and at the age of seventy. His son had to take the oath of allegiance
from the army on the spot, and the ship carrying the coffin back to Apulia was
half wrecked in a summer storm; the crew only just saved it. Anna records what
her father felt on hearing the news, which was relief at having such a burden
lifted from his shoulders.

## Why it belongs here

Because everything that came afterwards was built by these men.

The kingdom Roger II had crowned in 1130 was a state with three chanceries, a
Greek admiral, an Arabic fiscal bureau and a world geography commissioned from a
Muslim scholar, and it is easy to describe as a flowering of Mediterranean
civilisation. Its founders were a robber band out of the Cotentin who arrived
with thirty-five men and took southern Italy from the Lombards, the papacy and
the Byzantines in turn, largely by ambushing the people who had hired them.

Anna Komnene is a hostile witness and the hostility is the use of her. She had no
motive whatever to make Robert impressive, and she makes him impressive anyway,
which is worth more than anything his own side wrote about him.
