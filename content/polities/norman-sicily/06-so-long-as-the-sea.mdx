The dynasty lasted sixty-four years and the buildings are still there, which is
the ordinary way round for a state this short-lived — except that in this case
the buildings preserve the thing the state was actually for.

## Palm Sunday, 1140

The Cappella Palatina was consecrated on Palm Sunday 1140, and the sermon
preached in front of the king was given by Theophanes Cerameus, a Greek, the
court preacher, in terms Curtis notes were more adulatory than any western court
chaplain would have used:

> A joy day is this feast because of the numerous assembly of illustrious men,
> but its greatest adornment is the presence of the King who sits before us not
> in the glory of the Crown alone but also in the glory of high deeds … so long
> as the sea is bounded by the dry land, they will praise thy deeds to the end.

The building he was standing in was itself the argument. At Cefalù, at Monreale
and in the palace chapel the general plan is Norman and the decoration is not:
Arab and Greek craftsmen were called in to paint the roofs and cover the walls,
and what in the north would have stayed stately and austere was filled with
colour. At San Giovanni degli Eremiti in Palermo the plan is a Latin cross and
the domes above it are Oriental, because Muslim architects were given a free hand
with part of the building. In the cloister at Monreale two hundred columns carry
two hundred different capitals, and a Moorish fountain plays between Byzantine
arches as it might in Seville or Granada.

The kings did the same with their own houses. The emirs had built pleasure
villas in the open country outside Palermo without any apprehension about doing
so; their Norman successors imitated them, and Roger's Favara and William I's
La Zisa and William II's La Cuba are the result — open courts, Saracen columns,
honeycomb work on the ceilings. Curtis's phrase for them is vaulted pavilions of
half-Moslem princes.

## The mantle

The king's own coronation garments survive, and the principal one could be an
emir's.

It was woven at Palermo in the 1130s. Red silk, half-circular to sit round the
shoulders, embroidered in gold and pearl with floral work, and carrying an
inscription in Arabic in naskhi characters. On one side two great cats are
pulling down camels; the design is repeated on the other. There is nothing
Christian on it at all.

Roger's seal, meanwhile, would serve for any Byzantine emperor — the long
adorned tunic, the mantle, the globe surmounted by a cross, the staff, a Greek
inscription to either side — and his coins carry his name in Arabic. The mantle,
the seal and the coinage are three different self-presentations by the same man
in the same decade, each addressed to a different set of his own subjects, and
none of them pretending to be the others.

## What was lost and what was not

Both halves of that need saying.

The kingdom's Greek was going before the dynasty ended and its Arabic went
afterwards, and the Muslim population of Sicily was destroyed inside a century
and a half of Roger's death. The Martorana, founded as a Greek church by a Greek
admiral with an Arabic inscription round its dome, was celebrating in Greek only
until the pontificate of Honorius III. Nothing about the arrangement was
permanent, and nobody who wants to admire it should pretend the ending was
gentle.

What survived is the evidence that it existed. A Norman king's mantle with a
Muslim inscription, a Greek admiral's church with Kufic in the cupola, a Fatimid
ceiling over a Latin chapel, a world geography written in Arabic for a Christian
king by a man of the Prophet's kin, born at Ceuta and educated at Cordoba, whose
commission took its facts from Arab merchants on Africa and India, from Greeks at
court on the eastern empire, from returning crusaders, from the king's own
pardoned rebel barons on Apulia, and from the king's uncle on Piedmont, and set
down fact and fable together. And, at the far end of it, a word. The office
George of Antioch held was *amir al-bahr*, commander of the sea; it went into the
Sicilian chancery's Latin as *amiratus*, and out of the Sicilian chancery into
every language in Europe.

The kingdom that could not keep its own arrangement alive for a century
nevertheless gave the western navies the word for the man who commands them.
