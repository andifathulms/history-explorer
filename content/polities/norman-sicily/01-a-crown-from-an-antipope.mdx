The Hautevilles were minor Norman gentry from the Cotentin. Several sons of one
Tancred de Hauteville went south as mercenaries in the 1030s and 1040s, hired by
whoever was fighting in southern Italy — Lombard princes, the papacy, the
Byzantines — and by the end of the century their family held the whole of the
south, having taken it from all three employers in turn.

Robert Guiscard took Bari, the last Byzantine city in Italy, in 1071. His brother
Roger I took Palermo the same year and spent thirty more finishing the conquest
of Muslim Sicily, an island that had been under Islamic rule since an Aghlabid
army landed in 827.

Roger's son inherited a county in Sicily, acquired the mainland duchy when the
Guiscard line failed, and in 1130 wanted a crown.

## The grant

There were two popes in 1130. Anacletus II needed a protector in Italy and Roger
needed a title, and the bargain was made in the obvious way: Anacletus issued a
bull creating the kingdom of Sicily and Roger was crowned at Palermo on
Christmas Day.

The other pope, Innocent II, had the support of most of Latin Europe and spent
nine years refusing to recognise any of it. In 1139 he came south with an army to
settle the matter, was ambushed at Galluccio, and was taken prisoner. Roger
released him at Mignano in exchange for a bull confirming the royal title, the
duchy of Apulia for his son, and the principality of Capua for another.

So the kingdom of Sicily was created by a pope most of Europe did not recognise,
and confirmed nine years later by the pope who did, at swordpoint, as the price
of his release. Roger's title rested throughout on a grant from an outside
authority rather than on any claim of his own — which is the ordinary condition
of a conquering house with no lineage worth invoking, and which the Hautevilles
were unembarrassed about.

What they took care over was that somebody with the standing to confer it had
conferred it. Whether he had wanted to was a separate question.

## Why the papacy kept granting

Because the alternative was worse. The kingdom sat directly south of the papal
territories, it was the only power that could threaten Rome quickly, and it was
also the only one that could protect the papacy from the German emperors who
periodically came over the Alps to deal with it.

For most of the following sixty years the arrangement held: the Sicilian kings
were papal vassals in form, paid a token census, and were the papacy's military
guarantee against the German emperors. It broke in 1194, when the two crowns
ended up on one head.
