The man who made Sicily a naval power was a Greek from Antioch who had spent
his early career working for the enemy.

George of Antioch was the son of a Michael and a Theodula of Antioch. He and his
father served Temim, the Zirid prince of Mahdia on the Tunisian coast, and the
young George was put in charge of the finances — which meant that he learned
Arabic, learned the ports of the whole North African coast, and learned exactly
how weak the Muslim states along it were. Temim died in 1108. George was unsure
of the new prince's favour and left, and in 1112 he turned up in Sicily, where
he was well received.

Thirty-six years later he took Mahdia.

## What the fleet did

Roger II's power rested on ships and on two Greeks who knew how to use them —
George and Christodulus, whom the Arabic writers call Abd al-Rahman al-Nasrani,
the Christian. Between them they made the king master of the African coast and,
in Curtis's phrase, the dictator of the adjacent seas.

The African campaigns ran from the 1130s to 1148 and produced a string of
tributary ports along what is now the Tunisian and Libyan coast, the last and
largest of them Mahdia itself. It was not a settlement empire and was never
meant to be one: the kings installed or confirmed local Muslim governors, took
the customs and the grain, and left. It lasted about a decade before the
Almohads came east and took the whole coast back.

The title George carried says as much about the kingdom as any document it
produced. He was *amiratus amiratorum* — a Latin transliteration of the Arabic
for commander of commanders — and *protonobilissimus*, which is a Byzantine
court rank. A Greek serving a Norman king held an Arabic office under a
Constantinopolitan honorific. The first of those words is where English gets
*admiral*.

## The Martorana

What George built with the proceeds is still standing in Palermo, and it is the
best single object anybody has for understanding this kingdom.

Santa Maria dell'Ammiraglio, now called the Martorana, went up between 1139 and
1143. The plan is Byzantine: a square with a cupola and apses at the east end.
The walls are covered in Greek mosaic. Running round the inside of the cupola is
an inscription in Arabic, in Kufic characters. The deed of endowment survives in
the archive of the palace chapel; in it the admiral, styling himself with both
his titles, gives the church land and ten Saracen serfs attached to it.

Inside, on the left-hand wall, George himself kneels at the feet of the Virgin —
aquiline, white-bearded, and, Curtis notes, unmistakably Semitic in feature, the
head and hands being the original mosaic. Opposite him, on the right, King Roger
is being crowned by Christ.

He installed Orthodox clergy and the services were held in Greek. It went on
being a Greek church until the pontificate of Honorius III, and has been a Latin
one ever since.

## The shape of the thing

Curtis's summary of George is worth having whole, because the career is the
kingdom in one life: an obscure Greek Levantine who began as a finance official
for a Muslim prince, spent his life as the associate or the enemy of Muslims,
and ended as the right hand of a Norman king.

He died in 1152. Two years later Roger died too, and the man who had replaced
George as admiral had already been burned alive in front of the palace.
