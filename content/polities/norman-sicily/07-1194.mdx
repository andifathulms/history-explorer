William II died in 1189 at thirty-six, childless, having made his barons swear to
accept his aunt Constance as his heir. Constance was Roger II's posthumous
daughter, thirty-five years old, and married to Henry, king of Germany and heir
to the empire.

The barons and the chancellor elected Tancred of Lecce instead — an illegitimate
grandson of Roger II, competent, and not German.

## The one succession that mattered

The kingdom had a rule and it worked until the only occasion on which it
mattered.

Roger II to William I to William II is descent in the male line, three times, no
argument. 1189 is the first succession without a son, and the men who had to
decide chose the claimant they preferred over the claimant the rule produced —
which is what an inheritance rule is for, and is exactly the moment it stopped
being obeyed.

They were not obviously wrong to. The alternative was a German king in Palermo,
which is what they got five years later.

## What Henry did with it

Tancred died in 1194. Henry VI, by then emperor, came south with an army financed
by the ransom of Richard I of England — who had been captured on his way back
from the Third Crusade — took Palermo without much fighting, and was crowned on
Christmas Day.

He had Tancred's widow and children sent to Germany, had the surviving Norman
claimants blinded or mutilated, and by some accounts had Tancred's son castrated
and blinded. The Hauteville line ended there.

Constance, forty and pregnant, gave birth the following day in the market square
at Jesi in the Marche, reportedly in a tent open to the town so that nobody could
say the child was substituted. The child is Frederick II, who was raised in Palermo,
became Holy Roman emperor, and ruled Sicily and Germany together for thirty
years — a Sicilian by upbringing and self-description, governing the empire whose
army had killed his mother's family.

## A change of family

The kingdom was not dismantled, and the distinction matters. The chancery went on issuing in three languages
for a while yet, the registers went on being kept, the administrative structure
Roger II had built went on running, and Frederick II — who was born a Sicilian
and thought of himself as one — governed it from Palermo. What changed was the
family.

## What the barons were afraid of

They were right, and it did not help them.

The thing the Sicilian magnates would not accept in 1189 was a German king in
Palermo, and their remedy produced one five years later with an army behind him
and a grievance. Tancred, elected to keep the Hohenstaufen out, spent his whole
reign fighting them, died in the middle of it, and left a child who was
mutilated. Constance, who would have inherited peacefully, entered as a
conqueror's wife.

Whether a quiet accession in 1189 would have gone better for Sicily is
unknowable, and the barons had a real argument. What is certain is that the
kingdom's most consequential political decision was taken by men choosing the
outcome they preferred, and that it cost them everything they had chosen it to
protect.
