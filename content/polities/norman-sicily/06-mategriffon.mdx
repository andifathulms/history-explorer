In September 1190 the armies of the Third Crusade stopped at Messina to winter,
and one of them sacked the city. The kingdom of Sicily was not at war with
either of them.

Richard of Devizes, a monk of Winchester writing within a few years, is
entirely on the English side and reports the king's speeches as though he had
been in the room. What makes him useful is that he thinks the story reflects
credit on his subject, so he includes everything.

## The arrival

The French had come seven days earlier and King Philip had been received into
Tancred's palace inside the walls. Richard arrived by galley with the great ships
behind him.

> So great was the splendour of the approaching armament, such the clashing and
> brilliancy of their arms, so noble the sound of the trumpets and clarions, that
> the city quaked and was greatly astounded.

He camped outside the city and on the next day put up gibbets outside his camp to
hang thieves and robbers on, and his judges spared neither sex nor age, holding
strangers and natives to the same law. The French king, Devizes says, took no
notice of what his people did or what was done to them. *Wherefore the one was
called a Lamb by the Griffones, the other obtained the name of a Lion.*

Griffones is what the crusaders called the Greeks of the kingdom, and it is the
word that runs through the whole episode.

## The claim

Richard's sister Joanna had been William II's queen. Richard sent to Tancred
demanding her back with her dowry, and with the legacy William had left to Henry
II: a golden table twelve feet long, a silk tent, a hundred of the best galleys
fully found for two years, sixty thousand measures each of wheat, barley and
wine, twenty-four golden cups and twenty-four golden dishes.

Tancred sent back the sister with the ordinary furniture of her bed and a
million *terrini* for her expenses, and ignored the rest. Richard crossed the
strait, took a fortified town in Calabria and installed her in it, took a castle
called the Griffons' Monastery, and had the Greeks who resisted put to death by
various tortures and displayed to their friends.

## The tailed English

The Sicilians answered in the way a commercial city answers an occupying army.
All dealing with the English was forbidden by proclamation; unarmed Englishmen
were killed in the streets by forties and fifties, day and night. Devizes records
the insult too, and admits he does not know where it came from: the Greeks and
Sicilians followed the king about calling his men the *tailed* English.

Then he gives the king's speech to his court, which he did not hear and probably
invented, and which is worth having anyway because of what he thought a king
would say:

> Shall we vanquish Turks and Arabs? shall we be a terror to nations the most
> invincible? shall our right hand make us a way even to the ends of the world
> for the cross of Christ? … Shall we, subdued here in the confines of our own
> country, proceed further, that the sloth of the English may become a by-word
> to the ends of the earth?

The barons promise to penetrate mountains and walls of brass at a nod. Richard
then sets out the plan: Messina to be taken first, the Griffones to ransom
themselves or be sold, everything belonging to the inhabitants to be a prey for
anyone it falls to — *only with my lord the king of the French, who lodges in the
city, and with all his followers, shall perfect peace be preserved.*

## Five hours

The assault worked exactly as described. The walls were left unguarded because
nobody could look out of doors without taking an arrow in the eye; the gates went
down to a battering ram; the army took every hold in the city including Tancred's
own palace, sparing only the French quarters. Standards went up on the towers all
round the circuit. He took the sons of the nobility of the city and the
surrounding country as hostages against payment.

> He began to attack the city about the fifth hour of the day, and took it the
> tenth hour.

Tancred, terrified at the news, sent twenty thousand ounces of gold for the dowry
and twenty thousand more for the legacy and a perpetual peace. Devizes calls it a
small sum and says the hostages were kept anyway.

Then, having little confidence in the natives, Richard built a wooden fort of
great height by the walls of Messina and named it, to the reproach of the
Griffones, *Mategriffon* — Kill-Greek. The king's valour was greatly extolled,
Devizes says, and the land kept silence in his presence.

## What the winter shows

This was the kingdom that had employed Greeks, Arabs and Latins in three
chanceries and given its admiral an Arabic title under a Byzantine court rank. Thirty-seven years after the man who held that office was burned in front
of the palace, a passing crusade took the kingdom's second city in five hours,
executed the Greek population of two towns across the strait, extorted forty
thousand ounces of gold from its king, and put up a tower next to the walls with
an insult for a name.

None of it had anything to do with the crusade. The armies were en route to Acre
and stopped for the winter. What they found was a state that could not make a
succession work, whose king had been elected against the heir, and whose
population the crusaders had a word for.

Four years later the Germans arrived, and nobody had to besiege anything.
