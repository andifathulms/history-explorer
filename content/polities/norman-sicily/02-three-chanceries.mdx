The kingdom issued documents in Latin, Greek and Arabic, and the three were not
translations of each other. They were the working languages of three different
parts of the administration, inherited from three different states.

The Arabic came from the emirate. The *dīwān* — the fiscal bureau — kept the
land registers, the *defetari*, listing estates and the villeins on them, in
Arabic, using a Fatimid administrative apparatus that had been running in Sicily
for two centuries and that the Normans took over intact rather than replacing.
Roger II's own name appears on his coins in Arabic and on his mantle in Kufic
embroidery, and the mantle was later used at the coronations of Holy Roman
emperors.

The Greek came from Byzantium and from the Greek-speaking population of Calabria
and eastern Sicily, which was substantial. The chancery's Greek notaries drafted
for that population, the admirals of the fleet were Greeks, and the title admiral
reaches English from the Sicilian chancery's Arabic *amīr al-baḥr* by way of
Greek and Latin transliteration.

The Latin came with the Normans, and by the end of the century it was winning.

## Al-Idrisi

Roger II commissioned a geography. Muhammad al-Idrisi, a scholar from Ceuta,
spent fifteen years at Palermo interviewing travellers, collating what was known,
and produced in 1154 a description of the world with seventy sectional maps and a
silver planisphere, dedicated to the king and known as the Book of Roger.

It is the most accurate world geography produced anywhere for the following three
centuries, it was made by a Muslim scholar for a Christian king in Arabic, and it
sits in a corpus of Islamic geographical writing this site's Islamic records draw
on throughout.

## And what the coded fields make of it

`descendant_scripts` on this record is zero, and the data comment says why: the
kingdom wrote three existing scripts and coding rule 3 excludes using a script
unchanged. `religions_carried` is zero. `successor_claims` is zero.

So the record scores nothing on any influence axis, and the thing it is famous
for — a court where Arabic, Greek and Latin were administrative languages at the
same time, in the middle of the crusading century — is not registrable anywhere.

That is now a familiar result. The Carolingian record ends on manuscripts and
letterforms the rules cannot count, the Venetian one on double-entry bookkeeping,
the Nabataean one on the opposite problem. What this record adds is a case where
the uncountable thing is not an invention but a *practice*: three administrations
in three languages, kept running because they worked, by a dynasty with no
particular ideological commitment to any of them.

## How long it lasted

Not very. The trilingual arrangement was already narrowing under William II, and
the anti-Muslim riots at Palermo in 1161 killed a great many of the palace
eunuchs and officials who had staffed the Arabic bureau. Under the Hohenstaufen
the Muslim population of the interior revolted and was eventually deported
wholesale to Lucera on the mainland. By 1300 there were no Muslims in Sicily.

Sixty-four years of a state that did not require its subjects to be the same
thing, followed by a century of one that did.
