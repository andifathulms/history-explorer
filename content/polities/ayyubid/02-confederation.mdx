title: A family, not a state

Saladin divided his territories among his sons and brothers, and the Ayyubid
lands were never afterwards a single polity. Egypt, Damascus, Aleppo, Hama, Homs
and the Jazira were held by different members of the house, each with his own
army and revenue, acknowledging a senior sultan whose seniority was frequently
disputed and occasionally fought over.

This was a deliberate Turco-Iranian inheritance practice rather than a failure —
the same patrimonial logic that divided the Seljuk empire and would divide the
Mongol one — and it had a genuine advantage: it kept the family invested rather
than in revolt. It also meant that Ayyubid princes made separate truces with the
Crusader states, and that al-Kamil could hand Jerusalem back to Frederick II by
treaty in 1229 because it was his to hand.

The end was structural. The Egyptian sultan al-Salih Ayyub built his power on a
purchased regiment of Kipchak Turkish slave soldiers, the Bahriyya, quartered on
an island in the Nile. He died in 1249 during a Crusader invasion; his troops
defeated the Crusade, then killed his heir Turanshah in 1250 and took the
sultanate themselves. This site types that `internal usurpation`, which is the
third time the closed vocabulary has been asked to describe slave soldiers
becoming the state.
