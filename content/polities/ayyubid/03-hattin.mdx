On 4 July 1187 the field army of the kingdom of Jerusalem marched from Saffuriya
across waterless country towards Tiberias, was harassed all day, camped without
water, and was destroyed the next morning below the twin hills called the Horns
of Hattin. The king of Jerusalem was captured. So was the True Cross, which the
army had carried with it and which was never recovered.

The kingdom had almost no one left to garrison anything. Acre fell within a
week, then Sidon, Beirut, Ascalon; Jerusalem surrendered on 2 October after a
short siege. The terms were a ransom per head, generously and inconsistently
applied — Saladin's own brother bought a thousand of the poor their freedom, and
a substantial number who could not pay were enslaved anyway. Hillenbrand is
careful here, because the contrast with 1099 is the single most-repeated item in
the popular literature and it is usually made by people who want one of the two
events to be representative of a civilisation.

What Hattin did to the Ayyubids specifically was make Saladin's authority
unarguable inside his own house. He had spent twelve years being described by
Zangid partisans as a servant who had stolen his master's inheritance, and the
charge was hard to answer while he was fighting Aleppo instead of the Franks.
After 1187 nobody made it in public. The jihad rhetoric that his chancery had
been producing for a decade — his secretaries al-Fadil and Imad al-Din were
professionals at it — finally described something that had happened.

The one thing he did not do was take the coast. Tyre held, because Conrad of
Montferrat arrived in it by sea at exactly the wrong moment for Saladin, and
Tyre was the port through which the counter-attack came.
