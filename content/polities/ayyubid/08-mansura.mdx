title: Mansura, and the regiment that stayed

The last effective Ayyubid sultan of Egypt, al-Salih Ayyub, distrusted his
relatives with reason and built his power on men who had none: a purchased
regiment of Kipchak Turkish slave soldiers, quartered on the Nile island of
Rawda, and called the Bahriyya after it. He bought them in unusual numbers,
promoted them over his kinsmen's officers, and by the 1240s the Egyptian army
was theirs.

Louis IX's crusade landed at Damietta in June 1249 and took it without a fight.
Al-Salih Ayyub, already dying, was carried to Mansura and died there in
November; his widow Shajar al-Durr concealed the death for weeks and ran the war
with the senior emirs while an heir was fetched from the Jazira. In February
1250 the Franks tried to force the canal at Mansura, got a vanguard into the
town, and lost it in the streets. By April the army retreating towards Damietta
was destroyed and the king of France was a prisoner.

Then the winners turned round. Turanshah, the heir who had arrived from
Hisn Kayfa, began replacing the Bahriyya's commanders with his own men, and in
May 1250 they killed him — in front of the army, after a banquet, having chased
him into the Nile. Shajar al-Durr was proclaimed sultan, ruled for eighty days,
married the emir Aybak and handed him the title. The men who had just saved
Egypt from a crusade had taken it.

This site types the ending `internal usurpation`, which is the third time the
closed vocabulary has been asked to describe slave soldiers becoming the state.
The Syrian branch outlived the Egyptian one by ten years: al-Nasir Yusuf held
Aleppo and Damascus, claimed Egypt, failed to take it, and in 1260 rode away
from Hulegu's army without fighting. He was executed in Mongol custody, and the
Mongols who had taken his cities were beaten at Ayn Jalut by his father's
regiment.
