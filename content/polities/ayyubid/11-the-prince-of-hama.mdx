When Baybars was at the height of his power in the 1270s, his orders ran from
the fourth cataract of the Nile to the Pyramus in Anatolia, and eastward along
the Euphrates. The bedouin of the deserts were his auxiliaries, the sharifs of
Mecca and Medina were under his control, the king of Yemen sent him presents
and the ruler of Abyssinia asked him for a patriarch. All Syria obeyed him.

Lane-Poole lists two exceptions. The coastal cities the Franks still held — and
the principality of Hama.

Hama was Ayyubid. Saladin's family had been swept out of Egypt in 1250 and out
of Syria by the Mongols and then the Mamluks in 1260, and one branch of it was
still sitting in a Syrian city seventy years later, ruling as the Mamluk
sultanate's vassal and being left alone.

## A prince who wrote the books

The most interesting man that arrangement produced is Abu'l-Fida, prince of
Hama in the early fourteenth century.

He is one of the two great compilers of his generation: a universal history
that remained a standard reference in Europe into the nineteenth century, and a
geography of the known world that was still being translated and argued over
six hundred years later. He was also a working soldier of the Mamluk state, and
Lane-Poole notes in a footnote to his account of the last great battle against
the Ilkhanate that both Abu'l-Fida and the historian al-Nuwayri took part in it
personally.

So the description of the fighting at Marj al-Suffar in the standard sources
was written by two men who were in it — one of them the reigning prince of a
principality, holding the last piece of Saladin's inheritance, riding in
somebody else's army.

## What that is

It is not a survival in the heroic sense. Nobody in Hama was plotting a
restoration. The Ayyubid princes there took their orders from Cairo, sent their
troops when Cairo asked, and were rewarded with the thing their cousins in
Egypt had lost: a small state, governed in peace, for another three
generations.

The line ended in 1341, when the sultan removed Abu'l-Fida's son and put Hama
under a governor like everywhere else. That is ninety-one years after the
Ayyubids lost Egypt and eighty-one after they lost Damascus.

Saladin's dynasty is remembered for one campaign and one city. The longest that
any part of it actually lasted was in a town in central Syria that he never
fought for, under the protection of the slave soldiers who had destroyed it,
and its last notable member is remembered not for governing but for a geography
book.
