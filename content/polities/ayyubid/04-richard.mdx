The Third Crusade was the price of Hattin. Western Europe sent three kings; two
of them arrived. From August 1189 the Franks besieged Acre for two years while
Saladin besieged them in turn, an arrangement in which both armies starved and
neither could withdraw, and when Acre finally surrendered in July 1191 Richard I
had some two thousand seven hundred prisoners executed outside the walls
because the negotiated payments were late.

Richard beat Saladin in the field at Arsuf in September and again near Jaffa in
1192, took the coast from Acre to Jaffa, marched inland twice towards Jerusalem
and turned back twice without attacking it. The Treaty of Jaffa in September
1192 left the Franks a coastal strip, left Saladin Jerusalem, and guaranteed
Christian pilgrims access to it. Saladin died six months later in Damascus, aged
about fifty-five, having spent his personal treasury and leaving, by the count
of his secretary, one gold piece and forty-odd silver.

The five years after Hattin are the part of the story the romantic version has
no use for, and they are the part that explains the polity. Saladin could win a
kingdom in a summer and could not hold a siege line for two years, because the
army he was holding it with was an iqta' army — officers with revenue
assignments in Egypt, Syria and the Jazira who expected to go home for the
winter, and cousins with their own territories who could and did leave. The
force that took Jerusalem was a coalition of his relatives, and every campaign
after 1189 was negotiated with them again.

His reputation among his enemies survived all of it. Hillenbrand's point about
the European Saladin — the courteous knight, the Dante placement in Limbo, the
Walter Scott figure — is that it was already forming in Frankish writing during
his lifetime, and that it says considerably more about what the West needed a
noble Saracen for than about the man his own chroniclers argued over.
