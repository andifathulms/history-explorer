---
title: What Saladin did to Egypt
drafted_from: edde-2011
phase: formation
---

Ending the Fatimid caliphate took one Friday. In September 1171 the khutba in
Cairo was read in the name of the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad instead of the
Fatimid imam-caliph al-Adid, who was dying in his palace a short walk away and
is generally supposed not to have been told. There was no rising. Two centuries
of Isma'ili rule ended without a battle, which is itself the most interesting
fact about it.

What followed was slower and mattered more. The Fatimid palace libraries were
dispersed and sold. The Isma'ili teaching establishment — the dar al-ilm, the
missionary apparatus that had made Cairo the intellectual capital of a
transnational Shi'i movement — was shut down, and in its place Saladin and his
officers founded Sunni madrasas and khanqahs, endowed with agricultural revenue
in perpetuity. Eddé's point is that this is the durable conquest: within two
generations Egypt was a Sunni country with a Sunni legal establishment, and the
Fatimid two hundred years survived mainly as architecture.

The fiscal reorganisation ran alongside it. The Fatimid crown lands and the
enormous palace establishment were broken up and reassigned as iqta' — revenue
assignments to officers in return for service — which converted the wealth of
the Nile valley into cavalry. Saladin's family took the largest shares. The
customs revenue of Alexandria and the Red Sea ports came with it, and it was
Egyptian money, not Syrian, that paid for everything after 1174.

He also built. The citadel on the Muqattam spur, begun in 1176, moved the seat
of government out of the Fatimid palace-city and onto a rock above it, and a
single wall was projected to enclose Cairo and Fustat together. Later sultans
ruled from that citadel for six hundred and fifty years.
