Salah al-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub was a Kurdish officer in the service of Nur al-Din
of Damascus, sent to Egypt on his uncle's staff as part of a Syrian intervention
in a Fatimid vizierial quarrel. He inherited the vizierate when his uncle died,
ended the Fatimid caliphate in 1171, and on Nur al-Din's death in 1174 took
Damascus from his late master's heirs.

The order of operations matters and is usually reversed in the telling. Saladin
spent roughly twelve years fighting other Muslim rulers — Zangids in Aleppo and
Mosul, Fatimid loyalists in Egypt — before he fought the Crusader states
seriously. The unification of Egypt and Syria under one ruler is what made
Hattin possible in 1187, and Hattin is what delivered Jerusalem after
eighty-eight years of Frankish rule.

Hillenbrand's caution is worth carrying: the Saladin who reaches modern readers
is substantially a construction of nineteenth-century European romanticism and
twentieth-century Arab nationalism, both of which needed him for their own
purposes. The contemporary Muslim sources are more mixed, and several of them
are written by people who thought a Kurdish officer had usurped his employer's
family.
