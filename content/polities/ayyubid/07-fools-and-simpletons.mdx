In 1219 the sultan of Egypt offered a crusading army the entire kingdom of
Jerusalem as it had stood before Saladin took it, in exchange for one Nile port.
The offer was refused as inadequate.

## The siege

The Fifth Crusade had landed at Damietta and settled in for a siege that went on
for eighteen months. Reinforcements kept arriving — the counts of Nevers and La
Marche, the earls of Winchester, Arundel and Chester — while inside the walls the
garrison steadily dwindled until, of about fifty thousand men, four thousand were
still able to stand to arms.

The contest was too unequal to last, and al-Kamil could see it. So he asked for
terms, and named them: the whole of the kingdom of Jerusalem as it was before
1187, if Damietta were spared.

Lane-Poole's reaction to what happened next is one word long. *Incredible as it
appears*, the exchange was refused as inadequate, and further concessions were
demanded.

## Who refused it

The refusal had two constituencies and neither was military.

Cardinal Pelagius, the papal legate, had been elected commander-in-chief. Filled
with the exaltation of a pilgrim of the cross, he would have no traffic with the
infidels — not on these terms or any. The other party held that Damietta was too
valuable a commercial centre to give up, which is a merchant's objection to being
handed a kingdom.

Against them stood the king of Jerusalem himself and the northern knights, who
urged the advantages of the exchange and were overruled. The cardinal carried the
day and it was resolved to press the war to the uttermost.

The verdict that survives came from a man who had been at Acre with Richard and
knew what Saracen armies could do. When Philip Augustus heard that the crusaders
had refused to take a kingdom in exchange for a city, he said: **they are fools
and simpletons.**

## Then eighteen months of nothing

Damietta fell by assault and the remnant of the garrison was massacred. Muslim
alarm was such that the walls of Jerusalem and other Palestinian cities were
hurriedly pulled down, so they could not serve the enemy as strongholds.

And then the crusaders sat in Damietta for a year and a half quarrelling among
themselves, and did not move until July 1221.

They had also chosen the wrong door. Damietta was a valuable port but it was not
the base for an advance on Cairo. The road every invader from Cambyses and
Alexander to Amr and Amalric had used ran from Pelusium to Bilbays and, desert
march aside, presented no obstacles. Between Damietta and the capital lay a
network of canals and Nile branches offering a dozen obvious defensive positions
— a series of traps for an army that did not know the country.

## What the family did

While the crusaders argued, al-Kamil built. A little south of Damietta he threw up
strong fortifications at a village he afterwards enlarged into a city and named
al-Mansura, the Victorious.

And he summoned his relatives — which is the part that matters for this state.
The Ayyubid confederation spent most of its existence as a set of princes fighting
each other over Damascus, Aleppo, Homs and the Jazira, and the family quarrel is
usually the explanation for everything it failed to do. Faced with a Christian
army holding an Egyptian port, one and all laid aside their rivalries and rallied
to him. Al-Mu'azzam of Damascus had come from the first.

The confederation had exactly one thing that could unite it, and it was an
invasion of Egypt. That is the mechanism of Ayyubid survival and also its limit:
a family that combines only under attack cannot conduct a policy, and eight years
later al-Kamil would hand Jerusalem back to a Christian emperor by treaty, without
consulting the relative who thought he owned it.
