---
title: A family, not a state
drafted_from: humphreys-1977
phase: aside
---

Saladin divided his territories among his sons and brothers, and the Ayyubid
lands were never afterwards a single polity. Egypt, Damascus, Aleppo, Hama, Homs
and the Jazira were held by different members of the house, each with his own
army and revenue, acknowledging a senior sultan whose seniority was frequently
disputed and occasionally fought over.

This was a deliberate Turco-Iranian inheritance practice rather than a failure —
the same patrimonial logic that divided the Seljuk empire and would divide the
Mongol one — and it had a genuine advantage: it kept the family invested rather
than in revolt. Hama stayed in the hands of one branch for a century and a half
and outlived the sultanate that produced it. The arrangement also had a cost
that shows up in every chapter after this one: an Ayyubid prince could make his
own truce with the Franks, his own alliance with a cousin against another
cousin, and on two occasions his own alliance with the Franks against a cousin.

The site records this as `appanage` in the succession field, which is the same
coding the Seljuks and the Carolingians carry, and the comparison is the reason
the field exists. What distinguishes the Ayyubid case is that the confederation
was never reduced to a single line by a winner. Al-Adil and later al-Kamil came
close, each reassembling most of it for a decade or so, and each time the
reassembly lasted exactly as long as the man who made it.

There is a temptation to read seventy-nine years of cousins as decline from
Saladin, and the chronology does not support it. The dynasty's largest territory
and steadiest revenues came after 1200, not before, and the treaty that returned
Jerusalem to Christian rule and the treaty that had taken it were signed by men
of the same family forty-two years apart.
