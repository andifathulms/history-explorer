By the 1240s the Ayyubid confederation was at war with itself, and both sides
had allies of the other religion.

Jerusalem had been handed to Frederick II by treaty in 1229 and had stayed
Frankish, undefended, for fifteen years. The princes of Damascus and the
smaller Syrian appanages were fighting the sultan in Cairo, and had made common
cause with the kingdom of Jerusalem to do it. So the position in Syria was an
Ayyubid sultan of Egypt on one side, and Ayyubid princes of Syria plus the
Franks on the other.

## Who was available

Al-Salih Ayyub, in Cairo, needed soldiers, and there was a supply of them
wandering in upper Mesopotamia: the Khwarazmians, the remnant of an empire the
Mongols had destroyed, driven west with nowhere to go and nothing to do but
fight for whoever would pay.

He called them in.

In September 1244 they took Jerusalem, massacred seven thousand of the
inhabitants — Lane-Poole's word for them is helpless, and they were: the city
had no walls worth the name and no garrison — and the Holy City returned to
Islam, this time permanently.

The following month the combined armies of the Franks and the Syrian Muslims
met the Egyptians and the Khwarazmians near Gaza and were destroyed. It was the
worst Frankish defeat since Hattin, and it was inflicted on an army in which
Frankish knights and Damascene cavalry fought side by side.

## What the victory was worth

Al-Salih Ayyub recovered Damascus in 1245 and Ascalon in 1247, and by the end
of the decade he had restored his kingdom to the extent it had held under his
father and his grandfather.

It is worth pausing on that, because it is the last time an Ayyubid sultan
could be said to be winning. The instrument that did it was a stateless army of
displaced mercenaries hired to fight the ruler's own relatives, and the most
celebrated result — the recovery of Jerusalem — was achieved by men who had no
interest in Jerusalem and were paid to be there.

Then he fell ill. The despatch announcing that Louis IX had landed in Egypt
reached him while he was too sick to ride, and he had himself carried to the
war in a litter.

He died during the campaign, and what his household did next is the subject of
the next chapter.
