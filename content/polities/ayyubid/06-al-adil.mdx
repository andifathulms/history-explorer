Saladin's sons fought each other for seven years. Their uncle al-Adil —
Saladin's younger brother, who had governed Egypt for him and negotiated with
Richard's envoys — took Damascus in 1196, Egypt in 1200, and by 1202 held or
controlled almost everything the family had, having deposed or pensioned most
of his nephews without a general war.

Humphreys' Ayyubids of Damascus is largely a study of what he did with it, and
the answer is: as little fighting as possible. Al-Adil made truces with the
Frankish states and kept them, on the working assumption that the coast was not
worth the cost of taking and that Italian merchants in Acre and Alexandria were
worth more as customs revenue than as enemies. Egyptian trade with Venice and
Pisa grew under a ruler whose brother is remembered for the jihad. He divided
the realm again in his own lifetime, giving Egypt to al-Kamil, Damascus to
al-Mu'azzam and the Jazira to al-Ashraf — reproducing the structure rather than
ending it — but he did it while alive and able to arbitrate, which is why it
held.

This is the polity at its widest and richest: Egypt, most of Syria, the Jazira
as far as Mayyafariqin, the Hejaz with the two holy cities, and Yemen under a
separate Ayyubid branch that had been sent there in 1174 and rarely did what
Cairo said. Nobody has published a figure for how much ground the confederation
covered, and the figure would be misleading if anybody had, because the parts
of it obeyed Cairo to different degrees.

The reputational cost was immediate and has never quite been paid off. A ruler
who declines to fight the Franks and taxes their merchants instead was, to the
religious scholars whose writing survives best, a disappointment after 1187 —
and the modern nationalist reading, which needs Saladin and has no use for his
brother, has kept that verdict in circulation.
