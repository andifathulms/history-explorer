The peace was sealed the way peace was customarily sealed on that coast:
Goiti and the two chiefs cut their own arms and each drank the other's
blood mixed with wine, the Moros of Maynila agreeing to support the
Spanish settlement and pay no other tribute in return. Both sides kept
their weapons after the ceremony. Filipino oarsmen with relatives among
Soliman's people warned the Spanish that the Moros meant to fall on them
at the first rain, when wet powder would silence their arquebuses; Soliman
himself sent word that the lord of the allied town across the river had
asked him to join an attack and that he had refused it, professing
friendship even as, by every other sign, both towns armed further.

## A sky the men called blood-stained

The evening before the attack, witnesses recorded a sunset so red that
the soldiers said the sun itself looked blood-stained - the kind of detail
a chronicler keeps only because what followed made it feel like an
omen. The next morning brought it. Without warning, Maynila's fort
opened fire on the anchored Spanish ships, one shot piercing a hull and
scattering ashes among the crew. Prisoners taken afterward blamed young
Soliman by name, saying he had overruled his uncle and fired the first
shot himself.

## The town that cast its own guns, ended by them

The Spanish answer was immediate. Arquebusiers stormed the palisade
before its bombardiers could reload, captured thirteen pieces of Maynila's
own artillery, and turned them on the town as it burned - a fire that,
by report, killed or captured several hundred people and consumed the
cannon foundry, the trading quarter of resident Chinese and Japanese, and
Soliman's own house with everything in it. Two days later, with no word
from any surviving chief and the season's winds turning against a longer
stay, Goiti's ships withdrew, leaving the ruin of a town that had cast
bronze artillery of its own now standing only as ash and melted metal on
the riverbank.
