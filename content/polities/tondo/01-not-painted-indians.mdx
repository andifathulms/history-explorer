When Martin de Goiti's ships rounded the bar of the Pasig river in 1570,
what they found was not the undefended village Spanish officers in Mexico
had been led to expect. Maynila stood on a tongue of land between river
and sea, its front fenced by a palisade filled with earth, defended by
bronze cannon manned by bombardiers with lighted match-cord, its warriors
visible in the streets and crowding the shore beyond. Four Chinese trading
ships lay at anchor by the native houses, their crews rowing out in skiffs
to sell brandy, hens, rice and silk to the new arrivals before they had
even made contact with the town's own chiefs.

## An uncle and a nephew, both called raja

Two rulers governed the town jointly, both bearing the Malay title raja:
an older man, the first to greet Goiti's men, embracing the master-of-camp
"and appear[ing] to be a man of good intentions"; and his nephew, Raxa
Soliman, younger and prouder, who told the Spanish through an interpreter
that he was glad of their friendship but that "the Moros were not painted
Indians" - they would tolerate no abuse, and would answer any insult to
their honor with death. Across the river stood the allied town the
Spanish called Candola, whose own lord was reported, in the days that
followed, to have been invited by Soliman to join an attack on the newly
arrived fleet, and to have refused.

## A foundry casting cannon of its own

What most struck the men who later inventoried Soliman's burned house was
not the plunder alone - money, copper, iron, porcelain, wax, cotton, and
vats of brandy that report valued above five thousand ducats - but the
workshop next to it, filled with iron and copper and clay-and-wax molds
for cannon still being cast, the largest built for a seventeen-foot piece
resembling a culverin. Forty married Chinese and twenty Japanese lived
permanently in the town, part of a trade substantial enough that its loss,
when the town burned, struck the Spanish chronicler as a serious blow in
its own right - a river port that cast its own artillery and hosted
foreign merchants by the score, not the outpost of "painted Indians" later
Spanish rumor liked to imagine.
