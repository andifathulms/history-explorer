Where the father had ruled with talent, by every account that survives
him, his son ruled with the vices and none of the ability - cruel,
deceitful, hated by his own people. In 1796 the king from Saigon marched
on Qui Nhon by land; the young ruler mustered a hundred thousand men to
meet him, and lost anyway to a far smaller, better-disciplined force. One
episode from the fall of the city became, in the retelling, the measure
of the victor's own character: a captured officer who had fired on him
after the surrender was offered, in the old custom, his choice of poison,
cord or dagger rather than the headsman's blade - and instead of choosing,
declared he would rather die unbowed and raise an army against his captor
again if released. Struck by the defiance, the king freed him outright.
The following year that same officer commanded the defense of Qui Nhon
against a second siege, and died in it.

## The last court at Hue

With Qui Nhon fallen, only the court at Hue remained - held by Quang
Trung's own young heir, a boy who had inherited his father's throne but
none of the campaigns that might have kept it. For six more years the two
sides held an uneasy line at Turon bay while the king from Saigon prepared
a final campaign in the north. In 1802 that campaign reached Hue itself,
dislodging its last Tay Son ruler and driving him into Tonquin for good.
Cochinchina and Tonquin stood reunited under a single sovereign for the
first time since three brothers had risen against a sickly king at Qui
Nhon twenty-eight years before - the throne that rising had built now
entirely erased, and the throne it had once hunted through a forest fully
restored.
