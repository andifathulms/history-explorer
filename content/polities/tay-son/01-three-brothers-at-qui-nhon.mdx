The rebellion broke out in 1774, in the thirty-fifth year of the reigning
king's rule and the fiftieth of his own age, at his own capital of Qui
Nhon. Three brothers led it: the eldest a wealthy merchant who traded
with China and Japan, the second a general of high rank, the third a
priest. The king, sickly and indolent, had for years let the real work of
government slip into the hands of his own generals - mostly eunuchs - and
a fresh poll tax had already turned popular feeling against him. Against
that combination of wealth, military command and religious standing, his
resistance amounted to little.

## A capital emptied to make a point

The brothers moved first to secure the king's own person and to hunt down
every member of the royal family within reach, killing all who fell into
their hands. Saigon, suspected of favoring the deposed king, was singled
out for an army of its own: its walls were leveled and a reported twenty
thousand of its inhabitants put to the sword. Trials and executions
followed daily elsewhere, staged, the sources say, as much for their
effect on public opinion as for any real conspiracy uncovered. The
brothers balanced the terror with theater of their own - lavish feasts,
fireworks, a general who flattered the army into loyalty, and clergy
persuaded to tell the people that heaven itself had chosen these three men
to rule them.

## A kingdom split three ways

Having won the country, the brothers divided it. The eldest took the
southern provinces of Chang and Don-nai; the general took Hue, on the
border with Tonquin; the priest became high priest over the whole of
Cochinchina. It was, by design, an arrangement that put the general
between his own brother and Tonquin's still-independent king to the
north - a border Hue's new ruler would not leave undisturbed for long.
