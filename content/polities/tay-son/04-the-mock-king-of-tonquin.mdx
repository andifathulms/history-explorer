title: The mock king of Tonquin

While the exiled king fought Siam's wars and Bishop Adran carried a
treaty to Versailles, the Tay Son general who held Hue was building the
dynasty's own high point. He had barely settled into his new capital
before quarreling with the neighboring King of Tonquin, a tributary of
the Chinese emperor. Tonquin's forces, no match for hardened rebel
troops, broke after a single engagement; their king fled to Beijing to
beg the Qing emperor for an army to restore him.

## An army that starved before it fought

The Qing emperor obliged, ordering the Viceroy of Canton to march on
Tonquin at the head of a reported hundred thousand men. Warned well in
advance by his own spies, the Tay Son general laid the invasion route
waste ahead of the Chinese column, burning towns and villages in its
path; by the time the army neared the frontier it was already starving,
and the retreat that followed cost, by report, some fifty thousand men to
hunger, disease and a harassing rearguard action without a single general
battle ever being fought.

## A crown taken, a truth hidden

Facing ruin and disgrace rather than risk further losses, the defeated
Chinese viceroy opened secret talks instead, and the Tay Son general used
the opening to declare himself crowned by the will of heaven and the
voice of the people as king of a newly united Tonquin and Cochinchina. A
face-saving fiction followed: the viceroy reported a fabricated victory to
his own emperor, who in due course confirmed the arrangement and summoned
the new king to pay homage in person. Wary of a trap, the general sent a
trusted officer to Beijing in his place, posing as king; the impostor was
received with full honors and returned home confirmed in a title that was
never really his to receive - after which the real ruler at Hue had his
own friend and the whole of his suite put to death, the only sure way, it
seemed to him, of keeping the deception from ever reaching the ears that
mattered.
