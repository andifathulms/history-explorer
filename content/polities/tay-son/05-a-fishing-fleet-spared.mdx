The exiled king did not stay on Pulo Wai forever. After nearly two years
living on roots, he judged the moment right - the Tay Son brothers by then
exhausted by their own quarrels, each shut up in his own capital
expecting attack from the other - and landed once more in his own
country. His subjects flocked to his standard; he marched unopposed to
Saigon and spent a year rebuilding its defenses, training an army, and
gathering a fleet, joined in 1790 by Bishop Adran and his own grown son,
newly returned from the failed Versailles mission.

## Fire in the harbor

The chance came in 1791, when the Hue general - by then ruling as a
self-proclaimed king - died, leaving a twelve-year-old son to hold a
throne built entirely on his father's own military reputation. The
following spring, the king from Saigon put his fleet under two French
officers and struck the rival fleet at anchor in Qui Nhon harbor while its
own king and court were thirty miles inland, hunting. One French captain,
having done fearsome damage, ran his ship aground pressing the attack too
far; the king, watching, is said to have remarked only that he could not
complain of an accident that let him have a turn doing his own share of
the fighting. The rival fleet was all but destroyed. When his officers
pointed out the one grand yacht spared in the confusion, the king refused
to have it burned either - observing, with the same dry humor, that its
owner would need something to fish from once he tired of hunting.

## Death takes the merchant brother too

The eldest of the original three brothers, who had held Qui Nhon
throughout, did not long outlive the destruction of that fleet. He died in
1793 - of a disease of the brain, by one account, or of poison
administered to end an ungovernable rage, by another - leaving his own
son to inherit a capital that had already lost the navy meant to defend
it.
