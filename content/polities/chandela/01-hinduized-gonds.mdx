The Chandels of Jejakabhukti - the old name for the region later
called Bundelkhand, between the Jumna and Narmada rivers - did not
descend from the same Gurjara stock as the Pratiharas, Chahamanas and
other clans grouped under the Agnikula legend. Smith describes them
instead as Hinduized Gonds, closely tied to another indigenous tribe,
the Bhars, who acquired a small principality near modern Chhatarpur
and only gradually advanced their own frontier northward until it
reached the Jumna and the edge of Kanauj's own territory. The dynasty
first enters the historical record around 831, when a chieftain named
Nannuka Chandel overthrew a Parihar chieftain and made himself lord of
the southern parts of Jejakabhukti - the Parihars in question being a
branch of the same Gurjara clan then ruling Kanauj itself, displaced
here on a local scale generations before the main Pratihara line
would meet a similar fate.

## Builders in stone and water

What distinguished the Chandels early on was less conquest than
construction. Smith records the dynasty as great builders who
beautified their chief towns - Mahoba, Kalanjar and Khajuraho - with
temples and artificial lakes formed by damming the gaps between hills,
a practice they borrowed, by the source's own account, from the
Gaharwar rulers credited with some of the most attractive lakes in
Bundelkhand. For its first century the dynasty likely acknowledged the
suzerainty of Kanauj's own most powerful kings, Bhoja and
Mahendrapala; only in the first half of the tenth century did the
Chandels become unambiguously independent, at exactly the moment
Kanauj's own Pratihara dynasty began the long decline that would let a
once-subordinate hill kingdom start acting as an equal, and eventually
a superior.
