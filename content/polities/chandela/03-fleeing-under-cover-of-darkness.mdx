Vidyadhara, who came to the throne around 1015, was by his own
contemporaries' account the most formidable ruler the Chandels ever
produced. Not one inscription of his own reign survives - everything
known of him comes from later Chandel records and from Muslim
chroniclers - but those chroniclers alone are unambiguous: Dikshit
quotes them calling him "the greatest ruler of India in territory,
possessing the largest army," a reputation that reached as far as
Ghazni and unsettled the Sultan Mahmud himself. His killing of the
Kanauj king Rajyapala in 1019 spread that fame further still - Ibn
al-Athir writes that his name "spread throughout India" - but by the
same stroke marked him as a target worth punishing.

## Two campaigns, and a very different outcome each time

Mahmud marched against Vidyadhara that same autumn, either to avenge
a ruler he considered his own tributary or to punish Vidyadhara's
support for one of his own enemies, the Punjab Shahi prince
Trilochanapala. Vidyadhara rode out from Kalanjar at the head of a
large army to meet him - and then, by the Muslim chroniclers' own
account, fled the field under cover of darkness, abandoning his
elephants and baggage train to the invaders. It was the same
cowardice he had reproached Rajyapala for only months before.

The defeat did not cripple him. Three years later, in 1022-23, Mahmud
returned, took Gwalior, and laid siege to Kalanjar itself. This time
the Chandels held: the siege dragged on, by Dikshit's account, for "a
considerable time" before Vidyadhara sued for peace on terms Mahmud
was willing to accept, unable to storm a fortress that had withstood
what Kanauj's own defenses could not survive in a single day. Smith
records that the Chandel kingdom passed a hundred and eighty years
after this without another Muhammadan attack - the fortress Vidyadhara
had once abandoned a field beside was the one he held.
