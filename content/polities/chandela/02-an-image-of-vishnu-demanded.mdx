The reversal of fortune between Kanauj and its onetime Bundelkhand
subordinate played out across two generations. In 916, when the
Rashtrakuta king Indra III's army sacked Kanauj and drove the
Pratihara king Mahipala from his own throne, it was Chandel troops
under Harsha who helped restore him - the first recorded instance of
the Bundelkhand kingdom acting as Kanauj's ally rather than its
tributary. Harsha's son and successor Yasovarman completed the
reversal within a single generation. Strengthened by his own
occupation of the powerful fortress of Kalanjar, Yasovarman compelled
the reigning Pratihara king Devapala to surrender a prized image of
Vishnu that the Chandel king wanted for a new temple he was building
at Khajuraho - a demand a genuine subordinate could never have made of
its own overlord, and proof, in Smith's own reading, that the
Chandels had become the stronger power.

## A hundred years, and the grandest temples

Yasovarman's own son Dhanga carried that strength further than any
Chandel king before or after him. Reigning from around 950 to 999 and
living, by tradition, past the age of a hundred, Dhanga funded some of
the grandest of the temples still standing at Khajuraho and took an
active hand in the wider politics of Northern India - joining, in 989
or 990, the confederacy King Jaipal of the Punjab organized against
the Ghaznavid amir Sabuktigin, sharing in the confederacy's own
disastrous defeat between Bannu and Ghazni alongside the Rajas of
Ajmer and Kanauj. His son Ganda continued the same double pattern
after him: outward participation in failed pan-Indian resistance to
Ghaznavid raids, alongside a Bundelkhand kingdom secure enough at home
that Ganda's own heir apparent, Vidhyadhara, could march on Kanauj
itself in 1019 and kill its king for the humiliation of an unearned
surrender - the clearest possible sign that the balance of power
Yasovarman had first tipped a century earlier now ran entirely the
Chandels' way.
