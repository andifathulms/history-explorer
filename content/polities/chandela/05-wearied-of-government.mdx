Kirtivarman's own successors left little trace of distinguished rule,
and his grandson Jayavarman, who came to the throne around 1110, left
the least of all. The one dated act of his reign that survives is a
modest one: in 1117 he had a royal clerk re-engrave, "with clear
letters," a Dhanga-era inscription at Khajuraho whose text had become
illegible after more than a century - a king remembered, where he is
remembered at all, for preserving somebody else's monument rather than
raising his own. A Kalanjar inscription gives the reason his reign
ended: "wearied of government," Dikshit's rendering has it, Jayavarman
abdicated the throne and departed for the Ganges to wash away his
sins. He left no capable heir.

## An uncle's throne, and a nephew's revival

The throne passed to Jayavarman's uncle, Prithvivarman, the younger
brother of Jayavarman's own father Sallakshanavarman - later scholars
proposed a disputed succession or even a coup, but Dikshit finds
nothing in the inscriptions to support it beyond the ordinary
awkwardness of an uncle inheriting past a living line. No record of
Prithvivarman's own reign survives at all; his rule is known only
because his successors' inscriptions name him.

That successor was his son Madanavarman, whose accession around 1128
began what Chandel inscriptions of the next reign would remember as a
recovery of the initiative Kirtivarman had first restored and his own
descendants had let lapse. Coins and inscriptions from Madanavarman's
reign survive in unusual number, and one of them, raised by his
minister Gadadhara, credits the king with driving the Kalachuri
(Cedi) king to flight "before his name even," holding the ruler of
Kashi to careful deference, and destroying an arrogant king of Malwa
outright - very likely the Paramara Yashovarman, whose own dynasty
had already been broken by a Chalukya invasion from Gujarat and had
nothing left to answer a Chandel attack with. It was Madanavarman's
own son, Paramardi, who would inherit the kingdom he rebuilt.
