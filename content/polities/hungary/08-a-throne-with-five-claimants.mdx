Matthias Corvinus died in 1490 without a legitimate son to inherit
either his throne or the reforms that had made his own reign
exceptional. Five separate pretenders pressed claims to the vacant
crown, and the nobility - having chafed under Matthias's own strong
central authority - deliberately chose the weakest available candidate,
a Bohemian prince known afterward as Ladislaus the Good-natured for a
compliance that in practice meant abandoning nearly everything Matthias
had built.

## A crown crowned on the backs of the peasantry

The kingdom's underlying weakness became catastrophically visible in
1514. A crusade proclaimed against the Ottomans drew tens of thousands
of armed peasants under a Szekler soldier, George Dózsa; when the
nobility delayed and the campaign stalled, the same armed peasants
turned their grievances against their own landlords instead. Dózsa's
forces won an early battle before being crushed decisively near
Temesvár. His execution was designed as spectacle: seated on a
red-hot iron throne, crowned with a heated iron circlet, he was made to
watch starved followers tear flesh from his own body while he was still
alive, and by every account he never once cried out. The nobility that
crushed the revolt answered it afterward with a legal code binding
peasants to perpetual serfdom in terms the kingdom's own later
historians would compare to the harshest slave codes anywhere in the
world.

## A weakness the nobility inflicted on itself

The serfs who had fought and died under Dózsa were the same men who
would have filled Hungary's ranks against the Ottomans twelve years
later. A population bound by law to a servitude with no legal
recourse, brutalised in front of its own children as a matter of
routine, had no institutional stake in defending a state that
guaranteed its own oppression and nothing else. The nobility's harsh
answer to one crisis quietly manufactured the conditions for the next.

## Mohács

In 1526, an Ottoman army under Suleiman the Magnificent advanced
through the Balkans, and a Hungarian force - its ranks thinned by
exactly the social catastrophe of 1514 - met it on the plain of Mohács
rather than wait, as the more cautious among the king's own advisors
urged, inside the walls of Buda. Young King Louis II committed his
reserve at what seemed a moment of victory, only to find his own
finest troops broken by Suleiman's main force and three hundred pieces
of cannon. Seven bishops, dozens of the higher nobility, and more than
twenty thousand men died on the field. Louis himself, retreating from
the rout, attempted to cross a marsh on a horse too heavily burdened
with ceremonial trappings; the animal collapsed in the mud and fell
back upon its rider, and the king died within minutes of injuries no
one on the field was in a position to treat. No single ruler would
again hold the whole territory he had lost that afternoon.
