Coloman's own reign opened with a kingdom of pilgrims crossing his
frontier toward the Holy Land, and closed with a Croatian and Dalmatian
crown added to Hungary's own. His nephews and their sons then carried the
kingdom through a century of foreign wars, one blinding, a Byzantine
succession claim gained and lost, and a French queen's household before
the crown reached Andrew II and the crisis that produced the Golden Bull.

## A king nicknamed for his books

Coloman, whom Ladislaus had once forced into the church and who had fled
into exile rather than take orders, was recalled without opposition on
his brother Almos's abdication in 1095 and proved himself the opposite of
the warrior-kings around him — a scholar so devoted to learning that his
own subjects called him Bibliophilus, the book-lover. His own reign coincided
almost exactly with the First Crusade's passage through Europe. The
poorest and least disciplined bands went first: peasants so ignorant of
geography that, the chronicle records, they cried "Is that Jerusalem?" at
every town they passed, and a mob under Count Emico that massacred Jewish
communities across Germany before Coloman's own troops broke their assault
on the frontier fortress of Merseburgh. Godfrey of Bouillon's better
disciplined army fared differently: Coloman met the duke in person,
granted him a market and free passage on the security of Baldwin's own
hostage-taking, and both sides kept the bargain without incident.

Coloman's greater ambitions lay south. When Croatia's last independent
ruler was killed putting down a rival noble's claim, Coloman annexed the
kingdom outright rather than installing a client as Ladislaus had done,
and when Venice sought his help expelling a Norman incursion from
Dalmatia, he drove the Normans out himself and was crowned king of
Croatia and Dalmatia in 1102 — bringing the whole Adriatic coast the
kingdom would hold for centuries under one crown for the first time. The
years of intrigue that followed with his own younger brother Almos ended
in the same cruelty Coloman's own scholarly reputation might have seemed
to preclude: Almos and his son Bela were both blinded on the king's
order. Coloman died in 1114, and the throne passed to his son Stephen II.

## A minority, a rivalry, and a blind king restored

Stephen II ruled as a boy under a council of magnates for nearly eight
years, then asserted his own authority with a violence that earned him
the name "the Lightning" — campaigns against Venice in Dalmatia, against
Poland and Bohemia over old grievances, and against the Byzantine empire
itself after a marriage insult to his own sister. He died childless in
1131 and, in his last act, named as his heir the cousin his own father
had ordered blinded: Bela, Almos's son. Bela II proved himself capable
despite his blindness, incorporating Bosnia into the kingdom and driving
Venetian garrisons from Adriatic ports the republic had seized during
Stephen's reign.

Bela's own son Geza II succeeded him as a boy in 1141 and, within his
first year, routed an Austrian invasion that had expected an untested boy
king to fold — seven thousand German dead against three thousand of his
own, by the chronicle's count. His reign saw the Second Crusade pass
peacefully through Hungary under Louis VII of France, after Conrad of
Germany's own troops had extorted money from monasteries on their way
through, and German and Flemish settlers established in the kingdom's
north under their own counts, a colonization that would outlast every
king who reigned alongside it.

## A Byzantine claim, a French queen, and Bulgaria subdued

Geza's son Stephen III held the throne through the turbulence that
followed his father's death, and his own younger brother Bela spent years
as heir presumptive to the Byzantine throne itself — recognized formally
by the emperor and granted the frontier duchies of Sirmia, Sclavonia, and
Croatia to govern — until the birth of an imperial son ended the claim and
left him, on Stephen's own death, simply king of Hungary as Bela III.
Bela pressed Hungary's claim over Galicia against its own duke in the
1170s, taking the title king of Poland alongside his own crown for a
time, and married as his second wife Margaret of France, whose household
brought Parisian fashion and learning into the kingdom and inspired a new
university modeled on Paris's own at Veszprém. Margaret outlived Bela and
died in the Holy Land herself, leading a Hungarian contingent raised for
Henry VI's crusade.

Emeric, who succeeded his father Bela III, extended the kingdom's reach
further still, subduing Bulgaria and Serbia while his own brother Andrew
governed Croatia as the crown's vassal. It was this same Andrew, on
Emeric's death, who inherited the throne his brother had held — and whose
own reign, opening in years of foreign war and ending in his queen's
murder and his nobility's revolt, would produce the Golden Bull and the
constitutional settlement it secured.
