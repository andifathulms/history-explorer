The Árpád dynasty died out with Andrew III in 1301, and the nobility,
free again to elect their own king, took nearly a decade to settle on
Charles Robert of Anjou - not from any enthusiasm for him, but because
his two rivals had already been tried and driven out, and only the
Pope's own favored candidate remained. Charles spent his early reign
subduing magnates who refused to acknowledge an outsider at all,
chief among them Matthew Csák, whose Carpathian strongholds held out
through a long and costly war before finally submitting. In 1330,
campaigning against a Wallachian voivode who had stopped paying him
homage, Charles marched his army into a mountain pass on a promise of
peace negotiations and was nearly annihilated when his host revealed
the trap - three-quarters of the Hungarian force cut down among the
rocks before the king and a handful of nobles fought their way out.

## A crime the king's own justice made worse

Domestic tragedy proved just as damaging to his reign's reputation.
In 1330 a Hungarian noble, enraged after his daughter was assaulted by
a visiting Polish prince, burst into the royal family's dining hall
and attacked them directly, wounding both King Charles and Queen
Elizabeth before being cut down by the king's own guards. What followed
went well beyond punishing the attacker: his daughter was mutilated and
paraded through the streets as a warning, his son was dragged to death
behind a horse, and the family's collateral relatives were hunted down
for generations afterward. Charles nonetheless died in 1342 remembered,
on balance, as one of Hungary's ablest kings - not for any singular
triumph, but for steadily rebuilding a monarchy that had spent the
previous decades fought over by rival magnates.

## A brother's murder in Naples

His son Louis, who succeeded him amid genuine popular acclaim, inherited
one entanglement his father had arranged and never lived to see resolved:
a childhood betrothal between Louis's younger brother Andrew and
Giovanna, heiress to the kingdom of Naples. Giovanna's own resentment at
a marriage arranged rather than chosen curdled, over the years, into
open hostility, and on the night of 18 September 1344 Andrew was
strangled at Aversa in a conspiracy the Hungarian court was convinced
his own wife had organized. Louis demanded justice from a papal court
with every reason to protect Giovanna's family, got none, and answered
by marching an army into Italy himself - twice occupying Naples,
sparring by messenger with rival claimants, and eventually watching a
corrupt papal tribunal absolve the queen anyway on the pretext that she
had acted under diabolical enchantment. Disgusted with the whole affair
and unable to hold Naples permanently against nearer claimants, Louis
withdrew for good after six years of intermittent war, having avenged
his brother's honor without managing to punish anyone for his murder.

## A second crown, offered on Hungary's own terms

Louis found more durable success closer to home. Called on repeatedly
by his uncle, King Casimir the Great of Poland, to help fight Bohemia
and the Rus' principalities, Louis grew familiar with a kingdom that
would, on Casimir's death without a male heir in 1370, offer him its
own throne - conditioned on his accepting sharp limits on royal revenue
and a promise never to visit the country without the Polish diet's own
consent. Louis accepted the restrictions and the crown both, ruling
Hungary and Poland together for the rest of his reign and giving his own
daughter Jadwiga, born to that Polish inheritance, a throne of her own
to take up after him.
