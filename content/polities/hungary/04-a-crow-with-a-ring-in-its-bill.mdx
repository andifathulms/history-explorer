Louis the Great left no son, only two daughters - Mary, who inherited
Hungary, and Jadwiga, who took up the Polish throne their father had
also held. Mary's own betrothed, Sigismund of Luxembourg, spent years
securing his claim to her hand and her kingdom both: a rival Bosnian
ban had seized Mary's mother and briefly imprisoned Mary herself before
Sigismund marched in and forced her release, and the couple were
crowned joint sovereigns in 1386 only after that crisis was resolved
in blood - Mary, defying her own sworn oath of mercy, had her captor
tortured and quartered regardless.

## A crusade that ended in a small boat on the Danube

Sigismund's reign, fifty years long and among the most consequential
of any medieval Hungarian king's, was defined early by catastrophe. In
1396, besieging the Ottoman-held fortress of Nicopolis with an army
swollen by French, German and Bohemian volunteers eager for glory
against the Turks, Sigismund watched his own battle plan collapse when
impatient French knights charged ahead of the main Hungarian force and
were annihilated to a man. Panic spread through the rest of the army,
and Sultan Bayezid's forces cut down twenty thousand more in the
pursuit that followed. Sigismund himself escaped only by small boat
across the Danube, reaching his own kingdom again after a long detour
through Constantinople - a humiliation that hardened, rather than
chastened, his instinct for punishing anyone he judged disloyal at home.

## A legend attached to an unlikely name

It was in the years after Nicopolis, by the story Hungarian peasants
told for generations afterward and that Godkin repeats while plainly
doubting, that Sigismund fathered a son by a Wallachian boyar's
daughter during a military campaign - a child raised, unacknowledged,
under the name John, who grew up to bear a coat of arms showing a
crow with a gold ring in its bill, the token Sigismund was said to
have given the boy's mother as a promise of future favor. Whatever the
truth of his parentage, John of Hunyad's real career needed no legend
to recommend it: granted the domain of Hunyad and dozens of villages,
he fought at Sigismund's own side against the Ottomans and emerged,
over the following decades, as the one Hungarian commander who could
reliably beat them in the field.

## A boy king, and a regency built on results

Sigismund died in 1437 without a son of his own, passing his crowns to
his daughter Elizabeth's husband Albert of Austria - who died within a
year, leaving an infant heir and, eventually, the nine-year-old
Ladislaus III on Hungary's own throne after the disastrous 1444 defeat
at Varna killed the young king chosen to rule in the interim. Facing a
minority regency at the worst possible moment, the Hungarian diet
turned to the one man whose record against the Ottomans nobody could
match: John Hunyadi was chosen governor of the kingdom, and spent the
next several years reforming courts, untangling royal finances, and
rebuilding an army capable of doing what Sigismund's own crusade at
Nicopolis had failed to - holding the Ottoman advance at the Danube
rather than simply surviving it.
