John Hunyadi spent decades as Hungary's most effective defender against
Ottoman expansion, knighting his own fourteen-year-old son Matthias on
the field at Belgrade after a victory over an Ottoman commander, and
serving as regent and lieutenant-governor for a kingdom whose weak kings
depended on him far more than they ever openly admitted. His death left
his family exposed to enemies who had only ever tolerated Hunyadi
because removing him was too dangerous.

## An execution the king had sworn against

Count Cilly, appointed to government in Hunyadi's place, provoked his
own killing when he threatened and wounded Hunyadi's elder son Ladislaus
in a confrontation at Belgrade; Ladislaus's own men cut Cilly down on
the spot. King Ladislaus swore on the Eucharist that no harm would come
to the Hunyadi brothers for the killing, then broke that oath within
weeks under pressure from Cilly's allies. Ladislaus Hunyadi was
beheaded without trial in March 1457; his younger brother Matthias was
imprisoned.

## An assembly that would not accept a foreigner

When the king himself died soon after, three foreign claimants pressed
competing cases for the vacant throne at a diet convened in Pest.
Szilagyi, Hunyadi's own former lieutenant, surrounded the assembly with
an armed force - not, as many expected, to claim the crown for himself,
but to offer it to his imprisoned nephew instead. The gathered nobility
answered with open acclamation before any foreign embassy could press
its case further. Matthias was still a prisoner in Prague, held by a
Bohemian regent who refused to release him until he had extracted both
a substantial ransom and a promise of marriage to the regent's own
daughter, when news reached him that Hungary had elected him king.

## A reign that rebuilt the state from the ground up

Matthias governed with an ability that earned him the title Matthias the
Just, refusing an alliance offered by the Ottoman sultan and defeating
Ottoman forces repeatedly rather than accommodating them. He built
Europe's first standing army independent of noble levies, the Black
Legion, ending the nobility's own traditional military obligation and
its accompanying influence in the same stroke. A scholar in his own
right, fluent in Latin, French, German and Italian and a personal
correspondent of Lorenzo de' Medici, he assembled at Buda one of the
richest libraries in the known world, founded a university at
Pressburg, and began construction of an institution at Pest intended to
hold forty thousand students. No later Hungarian king would command the
same combination of military independence, administrative reach and
cultural ambition - and none would leave behind a son able to inherit
either.
