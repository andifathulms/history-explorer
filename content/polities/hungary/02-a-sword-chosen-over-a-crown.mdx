Stephen died childless in 1038, and the throne he had built passed for the
next forty years through nephews, a tyrant, an elected rival, a pair of
exiled princes, and finally a king who chose piety over conquest and still
managed to double the kingdom's reach.

## A nephew who insulted his own nobility

Peter, Stephen's grandnephew, owed his crown to the intrigues of the old
king's widow, and repaid the nobles who accepted him with open contempt.
He filled the highest offices with Germans, garrisoned Hungarian towns
with foreign troops against the kingdom's own laws, and dismissed every
protest with the boast that he would load his foreign favourites with
still greater honours. The magnates answered by deposing him and raising
Aba, a man of royal blood, to the throne in his place. Aba restored the
officials Peter had dismissed, expelled the Germans, and swore to govern
by the laws Stephen had established — but a purge of nobles suspected of
plotting Peter's return cost him the same support that had made him king,
and in 1044 the German emperor Henry III's army broke his forces in the
field. Aba fled across the Danube and was killed by his own followers
before he reached safety.

Peter's restoration lasted three years. He had learned nothing from his
first fall, and when three exiled princes of Stephen's own line — Andrew,
Bela, and Leventa — reappeared on the frontier, the nation that had
tolerated him once rose against him a second time. Peter was captured,
blinded, and died of his injuries within days. Andrew was crowned in 1047
with only three bishops able to attend, the rest having been killed or
scattered in the fighting that restored him.

## Two brothers, two-thirds of a kingdom

Andrew's own reign opened with a forced return to Christianity after
Leventa's pagan faction briefly threatened to undo Stephen's conversion
outright, and settled into a decade of border wars with Poland, Bohemia,
and a German emperor still determined to avenge Peter. A skilled swimmer
who scuttled the German river fleet at the siege of Pressburg, and a
forged letter that sent an entire imperial supply convoy home, turned back
two separate invasions without a decisive battle.

Andrew, fearing he would die without an heir, summoned his brother Bela
from Poland and divided the kingdom between them — two-thirds to the
crown, the rest to Bela's own dukedom — and for some years the brothers
ruled in genuine harmony. It did not last. Once Andrew had a son of his
own, Soloman, crowned as his successor, courtiers hostile to Bela
convinced the king to test his brother's loyalty: a sword and a crown
were laid before him, one symbolizing the dukedom he already held, the
other the kingdom itself, and Bela was told that his choice would decide
whether he lived or died on the spot. Warned in advance, he chose the
sword. Even so, the trap itself convinced him Andrew's court could not be
trusted, and he fled to his father-in-law's court in Poland to raise an
army of his own. The brothers' forces met on the banks of the Tisza;
Andrew's Bohemian and German reinforcements were routed, and Andrew died
a prisoner the following day. Bela ruled alone from 1060, reformed the
kingdom's coinage and weights, and died five years later when a collapsing
wall crushed him beyond a physician's help.

## A crown fought over three ways

Bela's death reopened the succession his own reign had settled. Soloman,
Andrew's son, called on his German brother-in-law to restore him, and
Bela's sons Geza and Ladislaus fled into Poland rather than fight
immediately — but a negotiated peace soon split the realm again, the
crown to Soloman and their father's old dukedom to Geza. The arrangement
did not survive Soloman's own ambition. Goaded by advisers who wanted his
cousins destroyed outright, he turned on them, was defeated in the field,
and driven from the kingdom. Geza was crowned in his place, with Ladislaus
as his palatine, and a German army sent to restore Soloman a second time
withdrew without a battle after its own officers concluded the Hungarian
forces could not be broken. Geza reigned only three years before his
death, and though the crown was legally Soloman's by birth, the assembled
nobility chose Ladislaus instead — the man whose services in war and
reputation for piety and prudence they trusted over a rival who had
already lost the throne twice through his own conduct.

## Ladislaus, and a kingdom doubled

Ladislaus's own reign, from 1077, answered the chaos before it with
sustained expansion and a reputation for sanctity that outlived him.
When Croatia and Dalmatia's queen, his own widowed sister, resigned her
throne to him for want of an heir, he absorbed the kingdom into Hungary's
own crown rather than ruling it as a separate title. Soloman never
accepted his own exclusion: freed after a failed plot to kidnap Ladislaus,
he raised the Cuman-allied Chuni against Hungary's frontier and, when that
army was broken, against the Byzantine empire itself, before vanishing
into the countryside after a final defeat — legend held that he lived out
his years in obscure penance, though no one recorded where or how he died.
Ladislaus turned back the Chuni again when they returned in his absence,
pushed a punitive campaign as far as the walls of Cracow, and restored the
city to its own people without ransom once it had submitted. He spent the
peace that followed founding churches, and died in 1095 on the road to
help a Bohemian nephew defend his own throne — bequeathing the crown to
his nephew Almos, who held it only days before yielding it, without a
contest, to his elder brother Coloman.
