Andrew II came to the throne already weakened by years spent campaigning
in the Holy Land rather than governing at home, and a court scandal
involving his own queen - murdered by an enraged palatine after she
schemed to arrange her brother's seduction of his wife - left him with
almost no standing left to resist his own nobility's demands.

## Concessions written down rather than merely promised

At a diet in 1231, mediated by his son Bela, Andrew confirmed in a
single written charter every political privilege the nobility and free
population claimed, going further than any previous king by adding a
clause permitting armed resistance, without penalty of treason, against
any future king who violated the same privileges. Thirty-one articles
were bound together in the document that became known as the Bulla
Aurea - the Golden Bull. Nobles were declared free of arbitrary
taxation and arrest, obliged to arm at their own expense only within
Hungary's own borders, and guaranteed an annual diet convened on Saint
Stephen's own day. Every king from Andrew onward swore an oath at his
coronation to uphold it.

## A charter answering a different threat

The bull did not eliminate crisis so much as give the kingdom a
constitutional language for surviving one. Andrew's son Béla IV inherited
a partially repaired kingdom and then faced a catastrophe the Golden
Bull had never anticipated: a Mongol invasion that swept first through
Poland and Russia before devastating the Hungarian plain itself,
massacring the population and carrying off thousands of captives before
withdrawing. Béla, unable to secure help from Austria, rebuilt what he
could - inviting German colonists to resettle the land - and, in the
years that followed, strengthened the county assemblies the Golden Bull
had already begun to formalise as the nobility's own institutional
check on royal power.

## The county assembly as the kingdom's real constitution

Those assemblies, meeting in public session in every county, became the
mechanism by which Hungary's nobility actually exercised the rights the
Golden Bull had put into writing - electing their own representatives,
watching the king's officers openly, and providing a check that no
single act of royal corruption could fully bypass. It was this
institutional habit, built county by county rather than legislated once
from the centre, that let the kingdom recover repeatedly from disasters
that might otherwise have ended it.
