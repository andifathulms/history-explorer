The Marinids answered their lack of a doctrine by building colleges. A hundred
and fifty years after the dynasty ended, a man who had grown up in Fez walked
through them and wrote down what he found.

## What was still standing

Leo Africanus counted two most stately colleges and a number of others, some
holding a hundred study-rooms, some more and some fewer, all built by various
kings of the Marinid family. The finest was the one Abu Inan put up. He
describes it in detail because it deserved it: a marble fountain with a cistern
of two pipes; a little stream running through in a channel edged with marble
and Majorcan tile; three cloisters on eight-sided pillars of different colours,
the arches between them overcast with gold and azure, the wooden roof
elaborately worked; sides built close enough that nobody outside can see who is
walking within; brass gates carved, wooden doors made by hand; and in the
chapel a pulpit nine steps high, the steps of ivory and ebony.

On the walls, in very large black letters that can be read from a distance,
verses giving the year the college was built and epigrams in praise of the
founder.

## Forty thousand ducats, and then four hundred and eighty thousand

Leo repeats the story that was told about the cost. Having finished it, the
king wanted to know what he had spent. He read a leaf or two of the account
book, found the sum of forty thousand ducats, tore it up and threw it into the
little river running through the courtyard, quoting an Arabic saying:

> Each pretious and amiable thing, though it costeth deere, yet if it be
> beautifull, it cannot choose but be good cheape: neither is any thing of too
> high a price, which pleaseth a mans affection.

A treasurer, Leo adds, later made a particular account of the whole expense and
found the building had cost his master four hundred and eighty thousand.

That is a dynasty's argument for itself in a single anecdote — the gesture of
not counting, and the clerk who counted anyway.

## The prize for the best poem on the Prophet's birthday

The other thing that goes when an endowment goes is the occasion.

Every year on the Prophet's birthday the poets of Fez went early in the morning
to the chief judge's palace, climbed onto his tribunal and read their verses to
a crowd, and the man whose verses were judged best was proclaimed prince of the
poets for the year. When the Marinids prospered, Leo says, they used to invite
all the learned men of the city to the palace instead, entertain them
honourably, and have each recite; and the one everyone thought the best poet was
given a hundred ducats, an excellent horse, a slave woman, and the robes the
king was wearing at the time. Everyone else got fifty ducats, so that nobody
went away without the king's liberality.

Then Leo dates the end of it, and dates the rest with it: *an hundred and
thirtie yeeres are expired since this custome, together with the maiestie of
the Fessan kingdome, decaied.*

## The part that did not survive

The buildings are the least of what a madrasa is. What makes one work is the
endowment: property settled on it in perpetuity, whose rents pay the professors
and keep the students.

In times past, Leo writes, the students of these colleges had their clothing
and their food found for them for seven years. By his own day they had nothing
free but their chamber. The wars had destroyed many of the possessions by which
learning was maintained, so that the greatest college of them all had two
hundred ducats a year for its professors and the second had one hundred.

Then he draws the conclusion himself, and it is the harshest sentence in his
book about his own country: *this perhaps may be one reason, among many, why
the gouernment not onely of Fez, but of all the cities in Africa, is so base.*

The colleges, he says, were now furnished with no scholars except strangers
living on the alms of the city; if any citizens lived there at all, not above
two or three.

## What the Marinids actually left

Not the scholarship, which was already going in the 1510s. Not the dynasty. Not
the empire, which lasted under a year.

What they left was the frame. They were the first rulers, Leo says, who ever
called Fez a kingdom — before them it was a city with a governor, and after
them Morocco has been a kingdom ruled from a capital that expects to be one.
They left the courtyard madrasa as the standard Moroccan building. They left
the Qarawiyyin at a level of prestige it had not had before their patronage and
has not lost. And they left a walled necropolis at Chellah, on a Roman site
outside Rabat, where a family with no sacred ancestry had arranged to be buried
in a sacred place.

Everything on that list is an institution rather than a claim. It was a dynasty
that could not tell anyone why it should be obeyed, and it dealt with the
problem by building things that were useful whoever obeyed whom.
