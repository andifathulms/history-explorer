The Marinids paid a secretary for a year to take down Ibn Battuta's travels. What
they got for the money is visible in a single paragraph near the end, where the
most travelled man alive describes arriving home.

He reached Fez in the autumn of 1349 — twenty-four years out from Tangier, and
with the Sahara crossing and Mali still ahead of him — presented himself to the
reigning sultan, and was honoured by a sight of him. Then this:

> The awe that surrounded him made me forget that of the King of Iraq; his
> elegance, that of the Emperor of India; his politeness, that of the King of
> Yemen; his bravery, that of the King of the Turks; his mildness, that of the
> Emperor of Constantinople; his religious carriage, that of the Emperor of
> Turkistan; his knowledge, that of the King of Sumatra.

Seven sovereigns, named in order, each outdone in the quality he was famous for
by Abu Inan of Fez. It is the most efficient piece of flattery in medieval
travel writing, and it works because the man delivering it had actually stood in
front of all seven. Nobody else in the world could have written that sentence,
and the sultan who commissioned the book had bought exactly the ability to write
it.

Then the traveller closes the account:

> In Fez, too, I terminated my travels, after I had assured myself that it is
> the most beautiful of countries.

## What the transaction was

This is what a court commission produces, and it is worth being precise about
what that does and does not damage.

Abu Inan did not get a true comparative assessment of the rulers of the
fourteenth-century world. He got a testimonial, from an unimpeachable source,
placing him at the top of a list he could not otherwise have appeared on at all.
The Marinids had no religious doctrine of their own, no descent from the Prophet,
and no claim to the Almohad caliphate they had replaced. What they had was money
and Fez, and they spent both on the things that confer standing — madrasas with
the founder's name in the plaster, a chronicle, a library, and a traveller.

What the commission also produced, incidentally, is the most useful narrative
source for the fourteenth century anywhere between Morocco and China. The
flattery cost a paragraph. The rest of the book is Delhi, Kilwa, the Maldives,
the Black Death at Damascus and the road to Mali, and it exists because a
Marinid sultan wanted a compliment.

## Gibraltar

Before settling down he went to see his father's tomb at Tangier, then to Ceuta,
and then, *it occurred to me that I should take pleasure in the warfare for the
faith*, so he crossed to Gibraltar.

His description of it is the other Marinid claim, stated the way the dynasty
wanted it stated. The rock is one of the greatest refuges of Islam and the place
where Tariq ibn Ziyad landed, from which it takes its name; a despicable foe had
held it for about twenty years until *our Lord the Sultan Abu'l-Hasan* reduced
him, sent his son with an army, won completely, rebuilt the fortifications and
stored it with cavalry, treasure and engines. *This was one of his good deeds,
the effects of which still remain.*

That is the whole of the Marinid case for existing: not descent and not doctrine,
but that they were the people paying for the defence of Islam in Spain. Ibn
Battuta says it in the voice of a man who had been away since 1325 and had every
reason to be impressed by whoever was in front of him.

He then spent some days with the scholars of the garrison, one of whom was his
own cousin, and went home.
