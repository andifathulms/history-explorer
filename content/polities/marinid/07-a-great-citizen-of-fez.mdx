The dynasty ended in a city riot, and the most compact account of it is four
lines in Leo Africanus, written by a man who grew up in Fez two generations
afterwards and had it from the people who remembered.

> Esserif a great citizen of Fez, hauing slaine Habdulac the last king of the
> Marin-familie, was by the fauour of the people aduanced vnto the Fessan
> kingdome.

That is the whole of it. In 1465 a notable of the city killed the reigning
sultan and the population made him ruler.

## Why a citizen could do that

By that year the sultans had been ornamental for a century. After the failure
of the eastern campaigns the throne mostly held children, the Wattasid kinsmen
of the family ran the government as hereditary regents, and the effective
authority in Morocco was divided between the regency, the Arab tribes and the
towns.

Fez had one thing the sultan did not: a reason for people to obey it. The
sharifian families — the descendants of the Prophet through Idris, whose
shrine stands in the middle of the city — were the recognised aristocracy of
the place, and the man Leo calls Esserif was one of them. When a government
that ruled on possession alone was seen to be losing possession, the city
transferred its allegiance to the credential it already respected.

## What happened next was not stability

The sharif did not keep it. A Wattasid whom Leo calls Saic Abra, pricked
forward with ambition, went about to conquer Fez and make himself king; he was
beaten and put to flight to his great disgrace, and then returned while the
sharif's best counsellor was away pacifying Temesna, besieged New Fez for a
whole year with eight thousand men, took it by the treason of the townsmen, and
compelled the sharif and his whole family to flee to Tunis.

So the sequence is: a dynasty with no doctrine is killed by a man with the best
doctrine in Morocco, who is then thrown out by the regents the dynasty had
been carrying. Seven years of that, and the family that had actually been
governing since 1420 was on the throne under its own name.

## The bill for the whole century

Leo puts the two things in the same sentence, and the juxtaposition is the
point. In the same season that the Wattasid was besieging Fez, the king of
Portugal sent a fleet and took Arzila. Among the prisoners carried to Lisbon
was a boy of seven, the son of the man who would be sultan, along with his
sister.

He spent seven years in Portugal and learned the language exactly. His father
ransomed him for a great sum. When he came to the throne he was known as
Muhammad the Portuguese.

A dynasty that had crossed the strait four times to fight in Spain ended with
its successor's heir being brought up as a Portuguese hostage, and nobody in
Fez that year was in a position to think about it.
