The Marinids took Morocco over about twenty-five years, and Ibn Khaldun's
account of the two decisive acquisitions is a study in method. One was bought.
The other was reached in a day.

## Sijilmasa, 1253

Sijilmasa was the northern terminus of the western gold road and the reason
anyone in the Maghreb was rich. The Almohads still held it, with an officer of
their own in command.

Abu Yahya went south in 1253 intending to take Sijilmasa, the Draa and the
country round them. He succeeded in corrupting a man inside, was handed the
Almohad commander, and got possession of the town. He subdued the Draa with the
rest of the southern region and gave his son the government of it. When the
Almohad caliph sent an army the following year to take it back, the general got
as far as hearing that Abu Yahya and his son were coming to meet him, and
hurried back to Marrakesh.

Four years later Yaghmurasan of Tlemcen listened to some people inside
Sijilmasa who wanted a change and marched on the town hoping to seize it. Abu
Yahya set off with his troops at once and entered Sijilmasa on the morning of
the very day Yaghmurasan appeared before it. It was with extreme chagrin, Ibn
Khaldun writes, that the emir of Tlemcen gave up the hope of taking the place,
where he found his adversary already installed.

Two attempts on the same town, seven years apart, both defeated by arriving
first.

## Salé, 1260

The other acquisition is a warning about what came with the coast.

A rebellious nephew of the sultan's had recovered Salé for himself and wanted
arms to hold it against his uncle, so he contracted with European merchants for
a supply. In 1260 the port filled so thoroughly with their ships that the
number of sailors exceeded the number of townspeople.

The opportunity was too good to pass up. At the end of Ramadan, while the whole
city was celebrating the feast that breaks the fast, the crews took the town,
carried off the women and put everything to the sack. The nephew shut himself
in the ribat across the river and sent a courier to the sultan.

Abu Yusuf was at Taza, watching Yaghmurasan. He gathered his fighting men,
rode at a full gallop, and was under the walls of Salé within twenty-four
hours. Volunteers and paid troops came in from every side; he besieged the town
for fourteen days, carried it by assault, and killed every one of the raiders
his men could reach.

Then he ordered the breach in the western wall through which they had entered
to be closed with masonry — and, to deserve more of the divine favour, worked
at it with his own hands.

## What the two stories have in common

Neither is a battle. One is a bribe and a forced march; the other is a rescue
of a city that had sold itself the means of its own sack. Both are about
speed, and both are about the two things the Marinids had understood before
anyone else in Morocco: that the country was held from the roads, and that the
coast was now a place where somebody else's ships could decide matters.

Three centuries later Portuguese ships were doing at Safi exactly what those
merchants did at Salé, and no Moroccan sultan rode fast enough.
