---
title: What they built instead
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987
phase: peak
---

The Marinid answer to having no doctrine was the madrasa, and they built them
in numbers nobody in the Maghreb had before.

The Saffarin at Fez in 1271, then the Attarin, the Sahrij, the Misbahiyya, the
Bou Inaniya at Fez and another at Meknes — a programme of foundations running
through the whole dynasty, each endowed with property to fund teachers and
lodge students, each carrying an inscription naming the sultan who paid for it.
They are among the most elaborately decorated buildings in the western Islamic
world: carved plaster, cedar, zellij tilework, and a courtyard plan that became
the standard Moroccan form.

The reasoning is legible and the sources make it explicit. A madrasa produces
scholars and judges; scholars and judges staff the legal system; a dynasty that
trains, houses and employs the religious establishment does not need to argue
with it. It is also a way of reaching past the tribes to the towns. This site's
Great Seljuk page has Nizam al-Mulk doing the same thing two centuries earlier
and further east, for the same reason and with the same instrument, and the
Almohad page has a movement that made its own doctrine the qualification for
office. The Marinids took the Seljuk approach rather than the Almohad one.

They also patronised the Qarawiyyin, the mosque and school this site's Idrisid
page records being founded at Fez in 859, which reached its greatest prestige
under their sponsorship. And they built for themselves at Chellah outside Rabat
— a walled necropolis on a Roman site, with a mosque, a minaret and the tombs
of the sultans, which is what a dynasty with no sacred ancestry does about
wanting a sacred place.

The people who passed through are the measure of it. Ibn Battuta was a Moroccan
of this generation who returned to a Marinid court and dictated his travels to
a secretary the sultan provided. Ibn Khaldun, whose *Muqaddima* this site's
Hafsid page describes, served the Marinids at Fez among his other employers and
wrote about them at length — including the judgement, from someone who had
worked for them, that a dynasty which rests on paid loyalty rather than
solidarity is already in its third generation.
