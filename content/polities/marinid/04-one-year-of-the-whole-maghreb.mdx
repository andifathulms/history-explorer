---
title: One year of the whole Maghreb
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987

Abu al-Hasan, who ruled from 1331, came closer than anyone since the Almohads
to putting North Africa back together, and the attempt destroyed his dynasty in
about eight years.

The sequence is quick. He took Tlemcen from the Zayyanids in 1337, which gave
him the central Maghreb. He crossed into Spain to support Granada and was
destroyed at Rio Salado in 1340 by a Castilian and Portuguese army — the battle
this site's Nasrid page records as the end of North African intervention in
Iberia — and lost Algeciras four years later. Then in 1347 he took Ifriqiya
from the Hafsids, and for something under a year he was master of the Maghreb
from the Atlantic towards Tripoli.

In 1348 an Arab tribal revolt broke his army near Kairouan. The Black Death
arrived in the same year. His son Abu Inan declared himself sultan at Fez while
his father was still alive and still in Ifriqiya; Abu al-Hasan came back,
fought him, lost, and died in the Atlas in 1351. Abu Inan then tried the same
eastern campaign himself, got as far as Tunis, and was strangled by his own
vizier in 1358.

Two things this leaves.

The first is that nobody tried again. The Maghreb had been one polity under the
Almohads and was one for less than a year under Abu al-Hasan, and after 1358 the
three-way division into Morocco, Tlemcen and Tunis was permanent — the shape
North Africa has kept, in outline, ever since. This site now has a page for each
of the three.

The second is what the failure did to Fez. After Abu Inan the sultans were
mostly children or nonentities and the government was run by viziers and by the
Wattasid family, who were Marinid kinsmen and became hereditary regents. The
dynasty lasted another century in that condition. Portugal took Ceuta in 1415
and kept it, which is the first European foothold in North Africa; Castile had
taken Tetouan in 1399. A dynasty that had crossed the strait to intervene in
Spain eighty years earlier was being landed on.

In 1465 a rising in Fez killed the last Marinid sultan, and the Wattasid
regents stopped pretending and ruled in their own name. This site types that
ending `internal usurpation` with no agent named, because the people who took
the state were the people who had been running it for forty years.
