---
title: A dynasty without a doctrine
drafted_from: abun-nasr-1987
phase: formation
---

The two dynasties before this one in the Maghreb had programmes. The Almoravids
came out of the Sahara enforcing Maliki law and deposed rulers by legal opinion;
the Almohads came out of the Atlas behind a mahdi and declared their opponents
unbelievers. Each of them ruled on a claim about God.

The Marinids had nothing of the kind, and that is the first thing to say about
them.

They were Zenata Berbers, pastoralists from the country near Biskra who moved
west into Morocco in the early thirteenth century under pressure from Arab
bedouin migration. They began as a nomad confederation raiding a collapsing
Almohad state, took Fez in 1248 and Marrakesh in 1269, and at no point produced
a religious claim of their own. There was no mahdi, no new reading of the law,
no charge of unbelief against their predecessors. They simply took the country
because it was available.

That left them with a problem the previous two dynasties did not have, and the
next chapter is their answer to it. What a ruler in the thirteenth-century
Maghreb needed was a reason for the scholars and the towns to accept him, and
the Marinids could not claim to be more Islamic than anybody. So they argued
instead that they were a *correct* government: Maliki, orthodox, deferential to
the jurists, and — this is the part that shows — willing to spend enormous sums
on institutions of learning.

So the honest answer to what entitled a Marinid to rule Morocco is: he held it.
Very few medieval dynasties anywhere were willing to rest on that, and almost
none of them lasted. What the Marinids had was the country, and what they did
was govern it in a way the religious establishment could endorse without having
to believe anything unusual.
