Ibn Battuta was born at Tangier in 1304, left in 1325 to make the pilgrimage,
and came back in 1354 having been away for twenty-nine years. What he had done
in the interval is the largest documented journey of the pre-modern world: the
Hijaz, Iraq, Persia, Yemen, East Africa as far as Kilwa, Anatolia, the Golden
Horde, Constantinople, Central Asia, India — where he was a judge for the Delhi
Sultanate on this site's Indian Sultanates page for several years — the
Maldives, Ceylon, Bengal, Sumatra, China, and on the way home the Black Death
at Damascus. Afterwards he crossed the Sahara to Mali, which is on this site's
Africa page, and came back again.

The sultan Abu Inan had him dictate it. A young Andalusi man of letters at the
Marinid court, Ibn Juzayy, was assigned to take it down and worked it into
literary form, finishing in 1356. Without that commission there would be no
book: Ibn Battuta had no manuscript, had lost his notes in the Indian Ocean,
and was working from memory thirty years on.

That is why this is a chapter on the Marinid page. The *Rihla* is not a Marinid
achievement in any ordinary sense — the dynasty did not make the journey and
does not appear in most of it. What the dynasty did was recognise that a man
who had seen the Islamic world end to end was worth an official scribe and a
year of his time, which is exactly the posture the previous chapter describes
them adopting toward scholarship generally, and which cost about as much as a
week of the war with Tlemcen.

The book that resulted is the single most useful narrative source in this
corpus. It is a first-hand description, by one observer with consistent
interests, of the Delhi Sultanate, the Golden Horde, the Mali empire, the
Anatolian beyliks, Mamluk Egypt and Yuan China, at a moment when this site has
pages for all of them. Where the chronicles tell us what rulers claimed, Ibn
Battuta tells us what the roads were like, who fed travellers, what a judge in
the Maldives was paid, and how the Mali court seated its officials.

None of that registers on this page's four axes, which give the Marinids a null
reach, a null population and three empty influence counts. A hundred dinars for
a secretary produced the best single window this corpus has onto the century it
covers.
