---
title: Throwing off one overlord and finding another
drafted_from: bosworth-1968
phase: expansion
---

Between taking the Seljuk sultanate in 1194 and meeting the Mongols in 1219,
Khwarazm assembled the largest empire in the eastern Islamic world, and it did
it in about twenty years by working through three relationships in sequence.

The first was the Seljuks, dealt with in the previous chapter. The second was
the Qara Khitai — the Khitan power that had come west out of north China,
destroyed the Seljuk sultan Sanjar on the Qatwan steppe in 1141, and held
Transoxiana as a suzerain thereafter. This site's Karakhanid page describes what
that arrangement looked like from underneath: a Muslim dynasty collecting taxes
for a Buddhist overlord that was largely indifferent to what its subjects
believed. Khwarazm had been paying tribute to them too.

Ala al-Din Muhammad stopped. Through the 1200s he pushed the Qara Khitai back,
and by about 1210 he had beaten them, taken Transoxiana and Samarkand, and
extinguished the Karakhanids in the process — the western line went in 1212. An
empire that had begun as a Seljuk washing-bowl appointment now ran from the Aral
Sea to the Persian Gulf and from the Punjab frontier towards Anatolia.

The third relationship is the one he could not close. Every polity in this
thread has eventually applied to Baghdad, and Muhammad applied: he wanted
investiture as sultan, and he wanted it from a caliph who was not disposed to
give it. Al-Nasir, whose forty-five years this site's Abbasid page calls the
period when the arrangement nearly worked, was reconstructing genuine Abbasid
authority in Iraq and had no interest in licensing a rival. So Muhammad did
something nobody in this corpus had tried. He had a rival caliph proclaimed, of
Alid descent, declared al-Nasir deposed, and in 1217 marched on Baghdad to
install his own candidate.

Snow in the Zagros passes turned the army back. It is the last thing the
Khwarazmian empire attempted before the embassy arrived from the east, and it
matters for what happened next: the state that met the Mongols in 1219 had
recently quarrelled with the caliphate, destroyed the Qara Khitai buffer that
had stood between Transoxiana and the steppe, and made itself the only power
between the Oxus and the Euphrates with nobody left to appeal to.
