The empire ended in 1220 and the war went on for another eleven years, because
Muhammad's son would not stop.

Jalal al-Din Mingburnu is the reason the dynasty has two ending dates. What he
did in those eleven years is remarkable, comprehensively unsuccessful, and much
stranger than the one episode everybody knows.

## The Indus

In 1221 he was overtaken on the bank of the Indus by a Mongol army much larger
than his own. He fought from dawn until midday. When the battle was
irretrievably lost he made a final charge, turned, stripped off his armour, and
put his horse into the river and swam it, with the survivors of his army
following him and many of them drowning or shot in the water.

Chinggis Khan is said to have watched and admired it.

What happened to his family at the same crossing is reported two irreconcilable
ways, and the disagreement is worth keeping rather than resolving. Juvayni, who
wrote for the Mongols, says his mother, his wife and the other women with him
fell into Mongol hands. Nasawi, who was his own secretary, says that Jalal
al-Din could not save them and had them drowned in the river at their own
request, rather than let them suffer worse.

One of those accounts was written by a man on the conquerors' payroll and the
other by a man on his. Neither is disinterested and both were in a position to
know.

## The eight years

Then he rallied what was left, and Browne's summary of what followed is a list
because it can only be a list.

He beat off an Indian prince, then threatened the rulers of Sind and Delhi and
held ground on their territory until the Mongol pursuit slackened enough to let
him back into Iran. In 1223 he stormed through Kirman, Fars and Isfahan to
Rayy. In 1225 he defeated and killed the caliph's general, pursued the army
almost to the gates of Baghdad, took Tabriz, and attacked Georgia. In 1226 he
reduced Tiflis, then had to hurry to the far south-east to punish the ruler of
Kirman for intriguing with the Mongols. In 1227 he chastised the Turkmens and
the Assassins, beat the Mongols at Damghan and executed four hundred prisoners,
defended Isfahan, and turned back to Georgia to kill four of their greatest
champions in single combat and rout them. In 1229, while trying to organise a
league of Muslim princes against the Mongols, he was surprised and put to flight
by thirty thousand of them, and took Ganja anyway.

Browne's verdict is exact: his hand was against every man. He was fighting the
Mongols, his own brother's faithlessness, and the disloyalty of his vassals, and
he found time besides to attack the caliph, the Turkmens, the Isma'ilis and the
Georgians.

That is why the league was never assembled. Nobody in the Islamic east could
tell the difference between Jalal al-Din arriving and the Mongols arriving,
because both of them burned Tiflis.

## The village

After 1229 the fortune fails and so does the man. Browne, following Nasawi,
records it without embroidery: he took to drink, and grew purposeless,
melancholy and even maudlin, and his grief over the death of a favourite named
Qilij was exaggerated and unreasoning.

On 15 August 1231, fleeing from the Mongols, he was murdered in a Kurdish
village.

Whether the empire ends in 1220 with the state or in 1231 with the man is not a
question with a right answer, and both dates are worth keeping. A state that
existed for twenty-six years was destroyed in two, and then a single man
conducted its foreign policy for eleven more, badly, alone, against everybody.
