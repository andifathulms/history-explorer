---
title: The man who wrote it down worked for them
drafted_from: morgan-2007
phase: aside
---

Almost everything known about the destruction of this polity comes from
Persians employed by the people who destroyed it.

Juvayni was born into a family of Khurasani administrators who had served the
Khwarazmshahs, entered Mongol service as a young man, travelled to Mongolia
twice, and wrote his history of the conquests — the *Tarikh-i Jahangusha*, the
history of the world-conqueror — in the 1250s while working for Hulegu. He was
present at the reduction of Alamut in 1256, which this site's Nizari page
describes, and he salvaged part of its library before the rest was burned. He
was later governor of Baghdad.

His position is therefore impossible and he knows it. He is a Muslim writing
about the destruction of Muslim cities for a patron whose grandfather ordered
it, in Persian, for readers who had lived through it. What he produces is not
propaganda and not lament but something stranger: a work that gives the
casualty figures and the sack of Merv and Nishapur in full, calls the invasion
a punishment God visited on Islam for its sins — which relieves the Mongols of
agency without excusing them — and then argues that Mongol rule is now
restoring order and that men like himself should serve it.

Two things follow for this page. The first is that the enormous numbers this
period is famous for come substantially from him and from writers using him,
and modern historians treat them as literary rather than statistical. The
second is subtler. A history written by the losers' administrators for the
winners will register the destruction of a state and not the state itself, and
the Khwarazmian empire accordingly survives mainly as the thing that was
destroyed — which is part of why this page has no cited extent for an empire
that briefly ran from the Aral Sea to the Gulf.

This corpus keeps arriving at the same problem from different angles. The
Umayyad record was written under the dynasty that overthrew them; the Nizari
archive was burned and replaced by a traveller's tale; the Rashidun conquests
are known from compilations made a century and more later. Khwarazm is the
version where the account is contemporary, detailed, by a compatriot — and
written on the conquerors' payroll.
