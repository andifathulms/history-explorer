When Muhammad took Firuzkuh in 1215 and finished the Ghurids, he found their
papers, and in them a correspondence between the caliph al-Nasir and the house of
Ghazna in which the Commander of the Faithful had been encouraging them to revolt
against their overlord.

What he did about it was the single most consequential decision of his reign,
and it had nothing to do with the Mongols. He denied that the Abbasids had any
valid title to the spiritual leadership of Islam at all, produced a sayyid of
his own and set him up as a rival claimant to it, and — in 1217, with a steppe
frontier he already knew to be unsettled — marched an army on Baghdad. The
campaign achieved nothing. It was broken by a winter of a severity almost
unknown in those mountains, and what came back was not an army.

Al-Nasir's side of it is the part that has never been settled. Ibn al-Athir
hints and al-Maqrizi states outright that the caliph, finding his overmighty
vassal beyond the reach of ordinary intrigue, encouraged the Mongols to invade
Khwarazm. Browne, reporting this, cannot help noting what followed: it was at
Mongol hands that al-Nasir's own posterity perished and his house fell, forty
years later, in the same city he was defending.

Whether or not the invitation was ever sent, the quarrel did the work on its
own. Ibn al-Athir's summary of Muhammad's career is unsparing and is the best
short account of why the collapse of 1219 went the way it did. By his greed, he
says, the Khwarazmshah had weakened or destroyed most of the neighbouring Muslim
states in order to build himself an unstable and unwieldy empire; so that when he
fled before the Mongols and abandoned his people, there was no Muslim prince left
anywhere who could unite the forces of Islam against the invaders. The Ghurids
were gone. The Karakhanids were gone. The Qara Khitai were gone. The Seljuk
sultanate had been gone for twenty years. And the one authority that might still
have called the rest of them together had been publicly told that it was not the
caliph.

There is a smaller illustration of the same thing at the other end of the
quarrel. The caliph had been making a conspicuous friend of the Nizari grand
master at Alamut, who had converted to Sunnism, sent his mother on the
pilgrimage and invited the jurists of Qazvin to burn his heretical books —
honours pointed enough that Browne dates the estrangement between Khwarazm and
Baghdad partly to Muhammad's jealousy of them. When Chinggis Khan crossed the
Oxus, the first Muslim ruler to send envoys offering submission was that same
grand master.

Muhammad had spent twenty years removing every power in the east that could have
stood beside him, and two years insisting that the only remaining source of
common authority was a fraud. When the thing he had not prepared for arrived, he
was the only ruler in the eastern Islamic world, and there was nobody to ask.
