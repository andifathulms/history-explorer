Mac Dang Dung's usurpation did not go unanswered. A former Le
general named Nguyen Kim, fleeing Mac rule, took refuge with the
king of Laos, who granted him a district's own revenues to support
himself; there he gathered the scattered loyalists of the fallen
dynasty and brought with him a surviving son of the murdered King Le
Chieu Tong, building the nucleus of a restoration movement in exile
from the kingdom Mac Dang Dung now ruled at Thang Long.

## The Trinh inherit a cause

When Nguyen Kim died, his own son-in-law inherited both his army and
his restoration cause, not only because he was a capable commander
but because Nguyen Kim's own sons were still too young to lead in
their father's place. That son-in-law was Trinh Kiem, and under him
the Le restoration turned from an exile's cause into a standing
rival government: by 1551 Trinh Kiem's forces were strong enough to
attack Dong Kinh, the old Le capital, directly.

## An honest governorship, a hidden intention

Trinh Kiem kept Nguyen Kim's own sons at arm's length as far as their
family name allowed him to. One of them, Nguyen Hoang, won his
permission to go govern the provinces of Thuan Hoa and Quang Nam as
a feudatory governor - territory still held by Mac troops, which
Trinh Kiem judged a convenient, deniable way to be rid of a rival
brother-in-law. Nguyen Hoang had intentions of his own. He left at
once with a thousand carefully recruited men aboard twenty armed
junks, landed at the port of An Viet, and settled first at Ai Tu. The
country he explored there struck him as magnificent, and he resolved
to make his future in it; he lived there in peace for ten years,
until 1572, when a Mac expeditionary force of ten thousand men on
thirty junks arrived to drive him out. Against them he had only
twenty junks and no army worth the name - until, by his own account,
a miraculous dream sent him the means to hold his new country and
kept the line of Nguyen lords his descendants would build there out
of Trinh hands for two centuries to come.
