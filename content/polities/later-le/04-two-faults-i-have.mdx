Le Thanh Tong's reign, which ran thirty-eight years from his
coronation to his death, was not exhausted by the Champa campaign
that made his military reputation. He gave the same vigorous
attention to administration, public instruction, finance and law
that he had given to war, and it was under his direction that a new
legal code, later known by his reign name as the Hong Duc code, took
shape - a code that did away with the older practice of allowing
punishments to be bought back with a payment, up to and including
the death penalty itself. Only eight categories of case were
reserved thereafter for the king's own personal judgment; everywhere
else, a sentence handed down was a sentence that had to be carried
out.

## A son's grief, a king's confession

Le Thanh Tong's private life closed in sorrow before his public one
did. In 1496 the queen mother fell fatally ill, and the king and his
younger brother kept watch at her bedside without leaving her side
day or night until she died. Before the year was out Le Thanh Tong
himself fell ill in turn. On the twenty-ninth day of the first month
of 1497, though he was already dying, he propped himself up on a
table long enough to see his own son formally take the throne before
him and to deliver his last instructions; he died the following day.

In his final days he had spoken with real bitterness about his own
reign, listing what he judged to be his own two failings: that he
had sometimes acted against justice simply to win men's praise, and
that at other times he had sat on his throne as inert as a corpse,
drawing down heaven's own displeasure on his kingdom rather than
governing it. He turned the same unsparing eye on the men who had
served him, naming ministers he judged mere decorations at court -
wooden statues in official dress - who had never once opened their
mouths to advise him on the business of the state, or lifted a
worthy man up, or turned an unworthy one away. He left behind
thirty-four children, and a code of law that would outlast every one
of them.
