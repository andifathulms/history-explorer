When the Ming occupation of Vietnam finally broke, the general who
had led the uprising against it, Le Loi, did not simply take the
throne outright. A figurehead had been placed on it during the
fighting, a Tran claimant named Tran Cao, kept in place as a
concession to Vietnamese loyalty toward the old dynasty even as the
real power in the kingdom belonged entirely to Le Loi. Once the
country was at peace, Le Loi issued a general amnesty and called on
the people to return without fear to their farms and their trades.

## An unnecessary flight

The following year, 1428, with calm restored across the country, Le
Loi's generals and the court's own mandarins unanimously demanded
Tran Cao's death and Le Loi's coronation. Tran Cao had no real
following left - Le Loi was already revered as the saviour of the
country and its independence - but the frightened young king fled
by sea toward Nghe An regardless. A mandarin loyal to Le Loi caught
him and brought him back to the citadel at Dong Quan, where he
poisoned himself on the tenth day of the first month of 1428. His
disappearance changed almost nothing in practice, since Le Loi had
already been running the state in every respect that mattered.

## A dynasty announced

By the second month Le Loi had distributed rewards to his army and
organized his court, and on the eighteenth day of the fourth month
he sent an embassy to the Ming imperial court to announce Tran Cao's
death and the extinction of the Tran line. It was the formal
beginning of a dynasty that would rule Vietnam, in one form or
another, for the next three hundred and sixty years.
