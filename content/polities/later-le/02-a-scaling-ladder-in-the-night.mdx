Le Loi's own son and successor, Le Thai Tong, and his grandson Le
Nhan Tong governed the kingdom Le Loi had founded without incident
for three decades between them - Le Nhan Tong's own reign secure
enough that by 1456 he was receiving the homage of Lao chieftains on
the kingdom's western frontier. Not every claimant to the throne had
accepted being passed over, however. Nghi Dan, Le Nhan Tong's own
elder half-brother, had been set aside from the succession years
earlier on account of his mother's rank, and he had never stopped
believing the throne was rightfully his.

## A palace taken by scaling ladder

On the third day of the tenth month of 1459, Nghi Dan struck. He had
won over a number of mandarins to his cause and gathered his own
partisans in secret; that night, they scaled the palace walls and
broke in. Le Nhan Tong, nineteen years old, was killed in the attack,
and the queen mother was put to the sword the following day. Nghi
Dan proclaimed himself king under the name Thien Hung, and moved
immediately to secure recognition: he sent the triennial tribute
embassy to the Chinese court without delay, followed three weeks
later by a second embassy asking that his own kingship be formally
invested.

## Eight months, and a chosen death

The reign did not last. Nghi Dan's rule leaned entirely on the
adventurers who had helped him seize the palace, and the kingdom's
senior mandarins, worn down by eight months of his government,
conspired against him in turn. They caught his followers by
surprise and put them to death; Nghi Dan himself was spared at first,
then stripped of his rank and ordered to choose the manner of his own
death. In his place, the court raised to the throne Tu Thanh, prince
of Binh Nguyen - the eighteen-year-old who would be crowned that
summer, on the eighth day of the sixth month of 1460, and remembered
afterward as Le Thanh Tong.
