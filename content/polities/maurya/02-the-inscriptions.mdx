title: Reading a king who explains himself
drafted_from: thapar-2002

Ashoka's edicts are cut into rock faces and polished sandstone pillars from
Kandahar to Karnataka, and they were unreadable for two thousand years. Brahmi
had been forgotten; James Prinsep deciphered it in 1837, and a king known only
from Buddhist legend became the best-documented ruler of ancient India.

What the edicts contain is unusual for any period. They use the first person.
They give a policy — *dhamma*, glossed as consideration for others, restraint in
spending, care for animals, respect for all religious communities — and they
instruct officials to tour and enforce it. They record the Kalinga campaign and
say plainly that the king felt remorse. The Kandahar edicts are in Greek and
Aramaic, addressed to populations left behind by Alexander and the Achaemenids,
both of whom are in this site's pre-islamic-iran region.

The historiography has swung about what to make of them. A nationalist reading
made Ashoka a pacifist unifier; a sceptical reading points out that he did not
give Kalinga back, kept the army, and that *dhamma* served an empire needing a
common ethic across a hugely diverse population. Thapar's position, followed
here, treats the edicts as a genuine and unusual political programme that was
also useful.

The Mauryan wheel is on the Indian flag and the Sarnath capital is the national
emblem. Under this site's coding rules that is a modern invocation and is
excluded from the successor-claim count, which is accordingly null.
