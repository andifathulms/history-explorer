Chandragupta died in 297 BC and handed the throne to his son Bindusara, who
ruled for twenty-five years and left behind, so far as anyone has found, not
a single inscription or monument bearing his own name. Between the
conqueror who founded the empire and the son who covered it in edicts, the
middle reign is close to a blank.

## A letter to a Greek king

What survives of Bindusara is almost entirely anecdotal, and the best of it
is a piece of court correspondence too small to have been invented for
effect. The Greek epithet by which Greek writers knew him, Amitrochates,
transcribes a Sanskrit title, Amitraghata — "Slayer of Foes" — and under
that name he kept up the same diplomatic relationship his father had opened
with the Seleucid kings, receiving an ambassador at his own court exactly as
Chandragupta had received Megasthenes.

Bindusara wrote to Antiochus I Soter asking for a shipment of figs and
raisin wine — nothing, he said, being sweeter than figs — and added, as an
afterthought that outlived the fruit by twenty-two centuries, that he would
also like Antiochus to buy him a sophist and send him along with the rest.
Antiochus's reply survives too: he was glad to send the figs and the wine,
but regretted that Greek custom did not permit the sale of a philosopher.

## What the silence probably covers

Bindusara's own dominions are known only backward, from what his son Ashoka
is independently attested to have ruled. Ashoka's empire reached south as
far as the latitude of Madras, short only of the deep south and of the
coastal kingdom of Kalinga, which he took by force in a campaign of his own.
Chandragupta's twenty-four years look, on the balance of probability, too
crowded already with the conquest of Magadha, the expulsion of the Macedonian
garrisons, the war with Seleucus and the annexation of Ariana to have left
much room for a separate campaign into the Deccan as well. What that leaves,
by elimination rather than by any direct record, is Bindusara: a reign with
no edict of its own, in which the empire most likely grew to nearly the full
southern extent his son would inherit and then stop growing at.

## A shadow between two well-lit reigns

Bindusara's twenty-five years pass, in what survives of them, without a
single named battle, a single surviving decree, or a single building anyone
has since dug up and attributed to him. What is left is a courtesy visit
from a Greek ambassador, a request for fruit and wine and a philosopher, and
a kingdom that was, by the time it reached his son, most of the way to the
edges Ashoka would spend his own reign explaining rather than conquering.
