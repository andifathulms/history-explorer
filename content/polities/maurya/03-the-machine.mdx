What Megasthenes describes at Pataliputra is not a court with a countryside
attached. It is an administrative apparatus, and the parts of his account that
his Greek readers found least interesting are the ones that matter.

## The boards

City government, he says, was run by thirty men in six committees of five.

One looked after the crafts. One after foreigners — assigning them lodgings,
watching them, escorting them, sending their property home if they died and
burying them. One registered births and deaths, for taxation and so that the
information should not be lost. One supervised trade and commerce, and weights
and measures. One inspected manufactured goods and enforced the separation of
new goods from old. One collected the tax of a tenth on sales.

A committee for the registration of births and deaths, in the fourth century
BC, whose stated purpose includes making sure the record is not lost. There is
nothing comparable anywhere in the Greek world he came from.

The army was administered the same way: another thirty men in six committees,
one each for the infantry, the cavalry, the chariots, the elephants, the navy
and the commissariat — a department for bullock carts, porters, grooms, and the
men who beat drums and led horses.

## The roads

He records a royal road running from the north-western frontier to
Pataliputra, with pillars set up every ten stadia to show the distances and the
turnings.

Milestones, on a trunk road, across an empire — the same infrastructure the
Achaemenids had built and the Romans would, arrived at independently or
borrowed from a Persian model the Mauryas inherited through the north-west,
which had been Achaemenid territory a generation before Chandragupta took it.

## The tax

The land tax was a quarter of the produce, and the land belonged to the crown.
The cultivator paid rent to the king and a tax on top of it, and the fragments
say a good deal about the officers who measured the land and inspected the
irrigation sluices to see that everybody got a fair share of the water.

That is a state reaching down to the level of the individual watercourse. It is
also the fiscal basis of everything else: the elephants, the roads, the wooden
walls, the thirty officials in the city and the thirty in the army camp.

## The seven classes

The one part of the account that is famously wrong is the description of Indian
society as seven hereditary classes: philosophers, farmers, herdsmen, artisans,
soldiers, inspectors and councillors.

There are four varnas, not seven, and the categories he lists mix occupation
with function. What he seems to have done is describe the administrative
categories the state used — the groups it taxed and conscripted differently —
and take them for the society's own divisions.

That mistake is itself evidence. A Greek observer at Pataliputra encountered a
government that classified its subjects thoroughly enough that he took the
classification for the thing itself.
