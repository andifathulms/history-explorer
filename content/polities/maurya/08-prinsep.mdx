Ashoka's edicts stood in the open air for two thousand years, on roads people
used, and nobody could read them. When they were finally deciphered, the king
they revealed did not match the king anybody had been expecting.

## The script that was lost

Brahmi went out of use in its early form and the knowledge of it went with it.
By the medieval period the inscriptions were simply marks: Firuz Shah Tughluq
moved two of the pillars to Delhi in the fourteenth century because they were
magnificent objects, had the writing examined, and was told nobody could read
it. Jahangir added his own inscription to the Allahabad pillar in the
seventeenth, alongside a text he could not decipher.

James Prinsep, assay master of the Calcutta mint, worked it out in 1837, partly
from the short donative inscriptions at Sanchi where the repeated formula
*dānam*, gift, gave him a purchase on the letters.

What emerged was a king who called himself Devanampriya Priyadarsin — Beloved of
the Gods — and who was not obviously anybody. The identification with the Ashoka
of Buddhist legend came from the Sri Lankan chronicles, and was confirmed when
an inscription turned up using the name outright.

## Two Ashokas, and neither is the edicts

The Ashoka the tradition had preserved was a Buddhist saint: Ashoka the Fierce
in his youth, who killed ninety-nine brothers and built a torture chamber, then
converted, built eighty-four thousand stupas in a single day with the help of
spirits, and gave the whole earth to the order.

Faxian, walking through Pataliputra around 405, was shown the ruins of a palace
he was told Ashoka had built by employing spirits to pile up the stones and carve
the walls — *no human hands of this world could accomplish this*.

The edicts are a different man. He is preoccupied with administration, with
whether his officials are doing what they are told, with getting the message
into the local languages, and with religious concord as a public order problem.
He never mentions the Four Noble Truths. He does not use the word Buddhism.

So the two records that survive are a legend that made him a saint and an
archive that shows him governing, and they were reunited by an English
metallurgist working from mason's marks on a stupa railing.

## What was done with him afterwards

The rediscovered Ashoka arrived exactly when he was needed.

Indian nationalism in the twentieth century had a use for an indigenous
emperor who had unified the subcontinent, renounced violence, guaranteed
religious tolerance and left the evidence in his own words. He was, on that
reading, the answer to the claim that India had never been a nation and had
always needed governing from outside.

In 1947 the Sarnath lion capital became the state emblem of the Republic of
India and the wheel from the same pillar went on the flag.

That is a modern government adopting a third-century-BC king as its
constitutional ancestor, on the strength of documents that had been unreadable
until a hundred and ten years earlier — and it works, because the documents are
genuine and say what they are quoted as saying.

The part usually left out of the flag is the clause about the forest peoples
being reminded that the king can still kill them. Ashoka put both on the same
rock, and the state that took his wheel took the half that suited it, which is
what states do with ancestors.
