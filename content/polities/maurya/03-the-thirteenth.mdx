The Thirteenth Rock Edict is the most famous inscription in Indian history and
the most commonly misquoted, because the passage everyone knows is followed by
one that nobody quotes.

## What it says about Kalinga

> When king Devanampriya Priyadarsin had been anointed eight years, the country
> of the Kalingas was conquered by him. One hundred and fifty thousand in number
> were the men who were deported thence, one hundred thousand in number were
> those who were slain there, and many times as many those who died.

Then: *This is the repentance of Devanampriya on account of his conquest of the
country of the Kalingas.* He finds it painful and deplorable that when an
unconquered country is being conquered, slaughter, death and deportation take
place there.

Notice what is being counted and by whom. This is a king publishing his own
casualty figures — including the deportations, which is the part a conqueror
would ordinarily leave out — and describing the whole operation as a source of
remorse, in an inscription cut on rock faces across his own empire.

## What is deplored, exactly

The edict is more specific than the summaries make it. What Ashoka says he
minds is not death in the abstract.

He minds that the Brahmins, ascetics, sects and householders living there — among
whom are practised obedience to elders and to parents, courtesy to friends and
relatives, and proper treatment of slaves and servants — suffer injury or the
slaughter or deportation of the people they love. And that even those not
directly harmed suffer through the misfortune of friends and relatives they are
attached to. *This is shared by all men.*

It is an argument from social bonds rather than from sanctity of life, and it is
addressed to a very wide audience: *there is no place where men are not indeed
attached to some sect.*

## The sentence nobody quotes

Two clauses later:

> And even the inhabitants of the forests which are included in the dominions of
> Devanampriya, even those he pacifies and converts. And they are told of the
> power to punish them which Devanampriya possesses in spite of his repentance,
> in order that they may be ashamed of their crimes and may not be killed.

The forest peoples are to be reminded that the king can still kill them.

That is not a later interpolation or a sceptical historian's gloss. It is in the
edict, four sentences after the remorse, in the same voice: *in spite of his
repentance*. Ashoka is telling one population that he is sorry and another that
he retains the capacity and they should bear it in mind.

## How to read it

The pacifist Ashoka and the cynical Ashoka are both readings of a text that
contains the material for each, and the edict is more interesting than either.

He did not give Kalinga back. He kept the army. He went on governing an empire
that had been assembled by force and was held by the threat of it, and he
warned the frontier populations accordingly.

He also, in the same document, published his own atrocity figures, argued that
suffering is shared by all men regardless of their sect, and declared that *this
conquest is considered the principal one by Devanampriya, viz. the conquest by
morality*.

Both halves are the policy. A ruler who has decided that further conquest is not
worth what it costs, and who wants that decision understood as a principle
rather than as exhaustion, still has to hold what he already took — and this
edict is what it looks like when somebody tries to say all of that at once, on a
rock, in the third century BC.
