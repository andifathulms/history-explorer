---
title: What the edicts were for
drafted_from: thapar-2002

The renunciation is not the whole of what Ashoka had cut into rock, and the rest
of it is a governing programme.

*Dhamma* as the edicts define it is not Buddhism. It is a civic ethic: obedience
to parents and elders, liberality to Brahmins and ascetics, proper treatment of
slaves and servants, restraint in spending, care for animals, and — repeatedly
— respect for the beliefs of others. The Twelfth Rock Edict says that whoever
honours his own sect and disparages another's from devotion to his own does his
own sect the greatest possible harm, and that concord is best.

That is an argument aimed at a specific problem. Ashoka governed a subcontinent
of Brahmins, Buddhists, Jains, Ajivikas and forest peoples, in several
languages, through officials he could not supervise. What a state in that
position needs is not a religion but a shared minimum, and the edicts are an
attempt to legislate one.

The machinery is there too. He created a class of officers called
*dhamma-mahamatras* to tour and enforce it. He instructed officials to hear
petitions at any hour. He ordered wells dug, trees planted and rest-houses
built along the roads, and medical provision for men and animals — the Second
Rock Edict claims it for his own dominions and for those of the Greek kings
beyond them.

He also sent missions outward, to Sri Lanka and to the Hellenistic kingdoms, and
named the kings: Antiochus, Ptolemy, Antigonus, Magas and Alexander. It is the
earliest attested attempt anywhere to propagate a religion by diplomacy, and it
is the reason Buddhism became something other than a north Indian school.
