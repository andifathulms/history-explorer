The Mauryan empire begins with the only thing in ancient Indian history that
can be dated from both ends: a treaty between an Indian king and one of
Alexander's generals, in which the Indian side paid in elephants.

## The bargain

Seleucus Nicator, having taken the eastern half of Alexander's empire,
campaigned into the Punjab around 305 BC against Chandragupta Maurya, who had
taken Magadha in the disorder after the Macedonian withdrawal.

<Figure id="mr-seleucus" />

Nobody records a battle. What is recorded is the settlement: Seleucus ceded the
satrapies of Arachosia, Gedrosia and Paropamisadae — roughly southern
Afghanistan and Baluchistan — and received five hundred war elephants.

He used them four years later at Ipsus, where they were decisive in the battle
that broke Antigonus and settled the shape of the Hellenistic world.

So the largest single transfer of territory in the early Hellenistic period was
paid for in animals, and the animals went on to determine who ruled Anatolia.
It is the clearest evidence anywhere of how the two worlds valued each other's
assets: Seleucus wanted the elephants more than the provinces, and Chandragupta
agreed.

## The ambassador

The other term of the settlement was an embassy, and Megasthenes was sent to
Pataliputra and stayed for years.

Not one word of what he wrote survives directly. Everything known of it comes
from later Greek and Latin authors quoting him — Strabo, Arrian, Diodorus,
Pliny — which means every sentence has passed through somebody with different
interests. They preserved the administration and they also preserved the ants
that dig gold and the men with no mouths who live on smells, in the same
register, because a marvel and a customs regulation were equally reportable.

Read with that allowance, he is the only outsider who saw the Mauryan state
working.

## The city

His measurements of Pataliputra are the most checkable thing in the fragments,
and excavation has broadly borne them out.

The city stood where the Ganges met another great river, eighty stadia long and
fifteen broad — something like fifteen kilometres by three — laid out as a
parallelogram. It was girded with a wooden wall pierced with loopholes for
archers, crowned with five hundred and seventy towers, and had sixty-four
gates. A ditch ran round it, six plethra wide and thirty cubits deep, serving
for defence and for the city's sewage.

Timber palisades on that scale sound implausible until you dig them up, which
is what happened at Kumrahar and Bulandi Bagh in the twentieth century: massive
sal-wood beams, set as a double palisade, exactly where he said.

<Figure id="mr-kumrahar" />

A wooden city fifteen kilometres long, with five hundred and seventy towers, in
the fourth century BC. That is the capital of the state Ashoka inherited, and it
is the reason the Greeks who saw it went home describing the Prasii as the most
distinguished people in India.
