Around 185 BC the last Mauryan emperor was killed by his own army commander at
a review of the troops, and the empire that had held most of the subcontinent
for a hundred and forty years stopped existing.

## The killing

Brihadratha was reviewing the army. Pushyamitra Shunga, his senapati —
commander-in-chief — killed him there, in front of it, and took the throne.

That is the whole account. No siege, no invasion, no succession war among
princes: the man who commanded the army used it, in the one setting where the
emperor was surrounded by soldiers who answered to somebody else.

It is the most ordinary way for an empire to end and the least explicable
without knowing what had happened to it in the fifty years before, because a
functioning Mauryan state does not put itself in that position.

## What had already gone

The empire had been shedding provinces since Ashoka's death in about 232 BC.
Kalinga went. The Deccan went. The north-west was lost to the Greeks of Bactria,
who came back over the mountains in the 180s and reached deep into the Punjab.
Bengal appears to have gone its own way.

The reasons argued over are the ones argued over for every large ancient
empire, and there is no consensus. A succession of short reigns after Ashoka.
An administrative apparatus expensive enough that the revenue could not sustain
it once the campaigning stopped. Provincial governors who had always been sent
out from the centre and who, in a weak reign, stopped being recalled.

There is also a specifically Mauryan argument, and it belongs to whoever finds
it convincing: that Ashoka's programme itself had a cost. A state that renounces
further conquest keeps the standing army and loses the plunder that helped
justify it, and a *dhamma-mahamatra* touring the provinces to enforce a civic
ethic is another salary on the same revenue.

That case has been made and it is thin, because empires with no such programme
came apart on the same timetable.

## Ashoka's reputation, first version

The Shungas were Brahmins, and the later Buddhist tradition says Pushyamitra
persecuted Buddhists, destroyed monasteries and put a price on the head of every
monk.

The tradition is Buddhist and late, the number of destroyed monasteries grows
with the retelling, and the archaeological record does not show a rupture — the
stupa at Sanchi was substantially enlarged during the Shunga period, and the
famous gateways are Shunga work.

So the best-attested statement about the fall of the Mauryas after the event
itself is a piece of religious polemic which the buildings contradict. That is
worth knowing before reading anything about what the dynasty meant, because
almost everything written about Ashoka for the next two thousand years came
through the same tradition.

## What was actually lost

Not the idea of an Indian empire, which was attempted again by the Guptas five
hundred years later and by others afterwards.

What was lost was the apparatus, and it did not come back in that form. The
Mauryan state had governed directly through appointed officials over an
enormous area — the committees Megasthenes describes, the land measurement, the
registers, the trunk roads with distance markers. What replaced it, and what
Indian empires largely ran on afterwards, was a looser arrangement of
subordinate kings paying tribute and keeping their own governments.

The Guptas at their height held less ground than Ashoka and held most of what
they did hold through other people's dynasties, and they were the most
successful thing anybody built in the interval.
