Faysal ibn Turki ruled, in two stretches interrupted by five years in an
Egyptian prison, for about thirty years, and the emirate under him is the second
Saudi state at its most stable.

The interruption is the formative event. In 1838 Muhammad Ali sent a second
expedition, took Riyadh, and carried Faysal to Cairo as a prisoner; he escaped
in 1843 and was back in Riyadh within months. The lesson, and it was learned,
was that Egypt could reach the Najd whenever it was worth paying for — and that
what made it worth paying for was the Hejaz.

<Figure id="hufuf" />

So Faysal did not go near the Hejaz. He consolidated the Najd, took and held
al-Hasa on the Gulf coast, exacted zakat from the tribes, corresponded
respectfully with the Ottoman authorities in Baghdad and Basra, and stayed
inside the one part of Arabia nobody else wanted enough to garrison. The
emirate's relations with the outside world in this period are conducted by
letter and by the payment of small sums, and its army fought nobody who had
artillery.

<Figure id="baghdad-1890" />

That is a strategy, and it shows in what the state lived on: the tax of the
oases it governed, and nothing at all from raiding, which is what had paid for
the first state's expansion.

<Figure id="gulf-map" />

It was also a strategy with an expiry date, because it depended entirely on
there being an imam. The emirate had no administration to speak of, no standing
force, no revenue that did not require collection by somebody the tribes would
obey, and no rule about who that somebody should be after the current one died.
Faysal died in 1865 leaving four sons.
