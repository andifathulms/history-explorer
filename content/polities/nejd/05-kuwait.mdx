Abd al-Rahman ibn Faysal, the last imam of the second state, left the Najd after
Mulayda and settled with his family in Kuwait, as a pensioner of the Al Sabah.
He had a son, then about fifteen, called Abd al-Aziz.

<Figure id="ibn-saud-kuwait" />

The state ends there, in 1891, conquered by the family that had begun as its own
appointed governors at Ha'il — a reversal about as complete as one gets.

Eleven years later the boy came back over the desert with about forty men and
took Riyadh in a night attack, and the third Saudi state begins — the one that
became the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in 1932.

<Figure id="hafar-wells" />

What the two failed Saudi states left the third is worth setting out, because it
is not obvious and it is most of the answer to why the third worked.

They left the compact, which had survived a demolition and a civil war and did
not have to be renegotiated. They left a doctrine with an established scholarly
class in the Najd, teaching continuously through both collapses. They left a
capital, Riyadh, which had been the seat of the state for sixty-seven years and
was recoverable in a single night by forty men because everyone knew what it
was. And they left a precise diagnosis of what had gone wrong twice: the first
state was destroyed because it took the Hejaz and provoked a power that could
reach it, and the second because it had no rule about succession.

<Figure id="masmak" />

The third state took the Hejaz anyway, in 1925, at a moment when no power was
in a position to send an army for it.
