Abdullah, the eldest, succeeded. Saud, the second son, disputed it, and the two
of them fought over the imamate for a decade — taking and losing Riyadh several
times, drawing in the tribes of the Qasim and al-Hasa, and appealing outside for
help, which is what quarrelling claimants in small states have always done and
which reliably costs more than it buys.

Abdullah appealed to the Ottomans, who took the opportunity to occupy al-Hasa in
1871 and kept it for forty years — so a Saudi imam's request for help cost the
state its only coastline and its only customs revenue. Both sides appealed to
Muhammad ibn Rashid of Ha'il, an amir whose family had been installed by Faysal
and had served the Saudi state as governors, and who arbitrated with increasing
authority and decreasing deference.

<Figure id="hufuf-suq" />

By the 1880s the imam in Riyadh was Ibn Rashid's dependant. In 1887 Ha'il took
the city outright and installed a governor; in 1891 the last serious Saudi
attempt to recover it was beaten at Mulayda, and the family left for Kuwait.

<Figure id="hufuf-mosque" />

There was no rule of succession, and this is what the absence of one cost. The
emirate had a doctrine that gave it a claim on
every Muslim in Arabia, an alliance with a scholarly house that had held for a
century and a half, and no answer at all to the question of which of four
brothers should be imam. Twenty-six years after Faysal's death the state was
gone, and nobody outside Arabia had had to do anything about it.

It is the most reliably repeated lesson in the history of states this size: a
polity whose cohesion is one man does not survive him, and a doctrine is not a
constitution.
