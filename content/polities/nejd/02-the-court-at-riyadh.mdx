In 1862 a European travelling under a false name as a Syrian doctor reached
Riyadh and stayed for weeks, treating patients and being entertained. He is the
only detailed outside witness of the second Saudi state, and he is a hostile
one: he loathed the doctrine, wrote for a London audience, and has been accused
since of embroidering. His judgements of people are frequently vicious. His
descriptions of how things were arranged are another matter.

What he found was a working capital. Rich householders receiving in
well-carpeted rooms with an ostentatious pile of coffee pots — two for use and
ten for show — and poor ones in low, ill-lit ground-floor rooms; strangers in
town on business; gardens a mile or more outside the walls where people went for
the day. A place with enough commerce to attract visitors and enough surplus to
keep gardens.

## The other half of the compact

The figure who mattered most to him is the one who shows the state's
constitution still running a century after it was made.

Abd al-Latif was the great-grandson of the preacher who had made the compact
at Diriyah in 1744, and he was qadi of Riyadh and head of the religious
establishment. Palgrave describes a handsome, fair-spoken man with a palace, wide
gardens and a throng of slaves, who after the king was unquestionably the first
personage in the capital and in the whole state — and in many respects, he adds,
more powerful than Faysal himself.

The bargain of 1744 was that the amir would enforce the doctrine and the
preacher would supply it, and that neither would take over the other's function.
Here it is, still operating: two families, two functions, and the religious half
strong enough that a visitor thought it the stronger.

There is one more detail and it is the sharpest thing in the account. Abd
al-Latif had been carried off to Egypt as a child with the rest of his family by
the pasha who destroyed the first state, and was educated in Cairo. He came back
with an ease of conversation Palgrave found startling in a qadi of Riyadh, and
used it to run the strictest religious establishment in Arabia.

Palgrave's own explanation of him is a long, spitting comparison to Ottoman
officials who had seen Paris and hated it, and it says more about Palgrave than
about the man. The fact underneath is simply reported: the head of the Wahhabi
ulama in 1862 had been educated by the empire that had demolished Diriyah, and
came home to rebuild what it had knocked down.

## What the state reached

The second state did not go back to the Hejaz — that lesson had been taught
in 1818 and again in 1838 — but it did not stay inside the Najd either.

By the time Palgrave was travelling, Riyadh's authority ran east to al-Hasa and
the Gulf coast, where he went on afterwards, and tribute was being taken from
further round the coast still. The pattern is the opposite of Diriyah's: not a
doctrinal expansion towards the holy cities, but a commercial one towards the
sea, into the districts with dates, pearls and ports.

It is the same family, the same doctrine and the same constitution, pointed
ninety degrees away from where the first state had pointed. That was a deliberate
choice, and it kept them alive for sixty-seven years.
