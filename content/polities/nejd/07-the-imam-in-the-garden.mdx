The last ruler of the second Saudi state did not die with it. He lost Riyadh to
the Rashidis, went into exile in Kuwait with his family, watched his son take
the town back in 1902 with about forty men, and then lived on in that town for
another thirty years while the son built a third state around him.

In the summer of 1918 a British political officer spent forty-two days as a
guest in the palace at Riyadh, and Abd al-Rahman is in his diary — not as a
former sovereign, and not as a pensioner, but as a presence.

He is called the Imam. Not the former imam: the Imam, in the ordinary way, as
the man's name. The best garden in the whole oasis belonged to him, and the
party went out to picnic in it — thin date clusters that year, but peach trees
loaded with fruit just ripening and an enormous vine trellis hanging with
grapes, and they sat by an irrigation runnel in the shade of a sidr tree eating
fruit and talking.

## What he had become

The clearest picture of his position is an incident three weeks later.

On the morning the expedition was to leave, a heavy haze hung between earth and
sky, dark clouds were about, and a curious yellow light lay over Riyadh. It was
odd enough that the Imam Abd al-Rahman sent round to the Englishman to ask
whether the sun was eclipsed.

The visitor knew it was the twenty-fifth of the lunar month and no eclipse was
possible, but made a show of consulting the Nautical Almanac before pronouncing
that there was no cause for nervousness — and his verdict, he notes, was
accepted as conclusive.

That is what the imamate of the second Saudi state had come to in its last
holder: a very old man with the finest garden in the capital, addressed by the
title, sending a servant across town to ask a foreign guest whether the sky
meant anything.

## The point of keeping him

None of this reads as humiliation, and there is no sign that anyone intended it
as one. It is closer to the opposite.

Abd al-Rahman had abdicated the imamate in his son's favour in 1902 rather than
resume it himself, which is not the usual behaviour of a displaced ruler with an
adult heir. What he retained was the title and the standing, and what his son
got was a claim that ran back through him to Faysal, to Turki, to the men who
had rebuilt the state in 1824 — and behind them, through the same family and the
same compact with the same clerical house, to the oasis in 1744.

The third Saudi state's whole legitimacy is that continuity, and it was walking
around Riyadh in 1918 in the shape of an old man in a garden.

The second state failed in the way small states fail — a succession fought
between brothers, clients who became patrons, a capital lost to a neighbour. It
transmitted nothing institutional, because it had almost no institutions to
transmit. What it handed on was a title, a family, an arrangement with a clerical
house, and a boy in Kuwait who had been told what his grandfather had held.
