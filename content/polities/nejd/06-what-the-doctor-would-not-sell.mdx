title: What the doctor would not sell

The war that destroyed the second Saudi state was fought between two sons of
Faysal after his death in 1865. It can be dated earlier than that, because in
November 1862 one of them tried to buy poison from a visiting doctor, and the
doctor wrote it down.

Palgrave was at Riyadh under a false name, practising medicine, and had got
close enough to the heir apparent to be given amateur lectures in pharmacy — from
which, he says, Abdullah had learned enough to know the poisonous qualities of
various drugs, and of strychnine in particular. When Palgrave announced that he
was leaving the capital, Abdullah asked him to leave behind some useful medicines
for the public benefit, and in particular to entrust him with the powerful drug
whose effects everyone had been admiring.

Palgrave said what a doctor says about pharmacy in unlearned hands. It was
treated as a pretext. The prince said he could leave out the other ingredients if
he liked, but the strychnine he must have, at any price Palgrave cared to name.

He asked twice more. At the third meeting, on the nineteenth or twentieth of the
month, he called Palgrave to sit beside him at the far end of the coffee hall,
out of earshot of the attendants, dropped the pretences entirely, and stated the
reason he wanted it — though not, Palgrave says, the name of the person it was
for. He would accept no excuse, conscientious or otherwise.

Palgrave refused, and a few nights later a rap came at the door after dark. It
was the prince's confidential retainer. *The king sends for you; come with me at
once.* Could his companion come? The king wants you alone. Should he bring a
book? There is no need. Palgrave delayed long enough to have coffee made for the
messenger and to tell his companions to stay awake, and then walked through the
dark streets to the palace, where he was left standing in the courtyard while
word was taken in.

The room he was shown into had no light but the fire on the hearth. Abdullah sat
silent at the far end. Opposite him was Abd al-Latif — the great-grandson of the
preacher who had made the compact at Diriyah, head of the religious
establishment — and a few others of the Zealots' party. At the other end of the
hall stood a dozen armed men. Palgrave gave the greeting; Abdullah did not
return it.

Nothing was done to him in the end, and he got out of Najd. That is not what the
episode is for.

The name Abdullah would not say was his brother Saud's. Their father was still
alive and still holding the two of them apart, and Palgrave, watching the nobles
and even the visiting strangers take sides, thought Faysal's life was the only
thing restraining them — a silken thread round two tigers. Faysal died three
years later and the thread went. Saud and Abdullah fought each other across the
Najd for the rest of their lives, one or the other holding Riyadh at intervals,
the emirate shrinking with every round, until in 1891 the Rashidis of Ha'il took
what was left of it and the Saudi family went into exile in Kuwait.

A foreign doctor turned down a request for strychnine in a coffee hall in 1862,
and the state still had twenty-nine years to run. What he had actually been shown
was the mechanism: a succession with no rule in it, two candidates who both had
followings, and a religious establishment already sitting on one side of the
room.
