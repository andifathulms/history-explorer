Tarnovo sits in a loop of the Yantra with rock on three sides, and the Asens
fortified two hills of it — Tsarevets for the palace and the patriarchate,
Trapezitsa for the aristocracy and the churches. It was the capital for two
hundred and eleven years and it was, for the last fifty of them, the most
productive literary centre in the Orthodox Slav world.

## The last school

Patriarch Euthymius, who held the see from 1375 until the city fell in 1393, ran
a school at Tarnovo that did something narrow and consequential: it revised the
Slavonic texts.

Four hundred years of copying since Preslav had produced drift — variant
spellings, variant grammar, variant readings of scripture, and translations made
in the tenth century that no longer matched the Greek anybody could check them
against. Euthymius and his circle went back to the Greek, retranslated,
standardised the orthography, and issued corrected texts.

Then the Ottomans took the city, and the school scattered.

## Where it went

Its members went to Serbia, to Wallachia and Moldavia, and to Russia. Cyprian and
Gregory Tsamblak, both trained in that circle, became metropolitans of Kiev and
of all Rus; Constantine of Kostenets went to Serbia and wrote the first Slavonic
treatise on grammar and orthography for a Serbian despot.

What they carried was the Tarnovo revision, and it became the standard. The
Russian church books of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries are in the
orthography Euthymius fixed. The historiography calls this the second South
Slavic influence, to distinguish it from the first — the alphabet, the liturgy
and the books that went out of Preslav and Ohrid to Serbia and the Rus four
hundred years earlier, in the same direction, from the same country.

## What that was and was not

It is worth being exact, because the achievement is easy to overstate and easy to
miss.

Euthymius did not invent an alphabet; the letters are the letters Preslav had
settled four hundred years earlier. He did not convert anybody; Serbia and Russia
had been Orthodox for centuries. What his school did was editorial — go back to
the Greek, retranslate where the old version had drifted, fix the spelling, and
issue a text that could be copied without compounding the errors.

That is unglamorous and it is the work that keeps a literature usable. A
scriptural and liturgical corpus copied by hand for four hundred years decays;
somebody has to sit down with the originals and stop the decay, and if nobody
does, the tradition slowly becomes a set of local variants that cannot be checked
against anything.

The circle at Tarnovo did that for the whole Orthodox Slav world in the last
twenty years before the Ottomans took the city, and then carried the corrected
text out with them when it fell.

Bulgaria did this twice, four centuries apart, and on both occasions the export
happened because the state producing it was being destroyed. The books left
because the scholars had to.
