Tarnovo sits in a loop of the Yantra with rock on three sides, and the Asens
fortified two hills of it — Tsarevets for the palace and the patriarchate,
Trapezitsa for the aristocracy and the churches. It was the capital for two
hundred and eleven years and it was, for the last fifty of them, the most
productive literary centre in the Orthodox Slav world.

## The last school

Patriarch Euthymius, who held the see from 1375 until the city fell in 1393, ran
a school at Tarnovo that did something narrow and consequential: it revised the
Slavonic texts.

Four hundred years of copying since Preslav had produced drift — variant
spellings, variant grammar, variant readings of scripture, and translations made
in the tenth century that no longer matched the Greek anybody could check them
against. Euthymius and his circle went back to the Greek, retranslated,
standardised the orthography, and issued corrected texts.

Then the Ottomans took the city, and the school scattered.

## Where it went

Its members went to Serbia, to Wallachia and Moldavia, and to Russia. Cyprian and
Gregory Tsamblak, both trained in that circle, became metropolitans of Kiev and
of all Rus; Constantine of Kostenets went to Serbia and wrote the first Slavonic
treatise on grammar and orthography for a Serbian despot.

What they carried was the Tarnovo revision, and it became the standard. The
Russian church books of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries are in the
orthography Euthymius fixed, and this transmission has a name in the
historiography — the second South Slavic influence — to distinguish it from the
first, which is this corpus's First Bulgarian record's fourth chapter, four
hundred years earlier and in the same direction.

Bulgaria supplied the Rus with an alphabet and a liturgy in the tenth century and
supplied them with a corrected edition of it in the fifteenth, in both cases at
the moment its own state was being destroyed.

## And the fields count none of it

This record's three influence counts are zero, zero and zero.

`descendant_scripts` is 0 because an orthographic reform is not a script. The
letters are the same letters. Coding rule 3 counts derivation and adaptation of a
writing system, and standardising how an existing alphabet is spelled is neither
— it is the same distinction the Attalid record makes about parchment, where a
writing material is not a writing system.

`religions_carried` is 0 because coding rule 2 counts propagation beyond a prior
confessional boundary, and Serbia and Russia were already Orthodox. The First
Bulgarian record counts 1 for carrying the tradition to them in the first place;
carrying them a better edition of it four centuries later is maintenance, which
that rule excludes in terms.

Both zeros are correct and both are unsatisfying, and this site's practice is to
say so on the page rather than to loosen the rule. What the two Bulgarian records
have between them is one influence count and two chapters explaining what the
axes cannot hold — the alphabet that belongs to Byzantium under rule 3, the
Bogomils who spread in spite of the state, and this.
