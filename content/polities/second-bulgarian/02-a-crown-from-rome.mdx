Kalojan, the youngest of the Asen brothers, ruled from 1197 and wanted two things
his brothers had not got: recognition, and Constantinople.

The recognition he obtained from a direction nobody in Tarnovo had used before.
In 1199 he opened a correspondence with Innocent III, and the letters survive on
both sides. Kalojan asked for an imperial crown and a patriarch, citing the
precedent of Simeon and Peter and pointing out that his ancestors had held the
title.
Innocent offered a royal crown and a primate, not an imperial one and not a
patriarch, in exchange for recognising papal authority. Kalojan took it, and a
legate crowned him at Tarnovo in November 1204.

Both sides understood they were describing different transactions. The papal
register calls him king; his own charters call him emperor.

## Why a lapsed union was worth five years

The union with Rome lasted about a generation. The patriarchate was restored on
Orthodox terms in 1235 and the papal connection was dropped, and it would be easy
to file the whole episode as a manoeuvre that came to nothing.

Kalojan plainly did not think so, and he was the one paying for it. He conducted a
five-year negotiation, accepted terms he then reinterpreted, and got the two
things a European ruler needed — a crown and a church — conferred by the one
office in the west whose authority to confer them nobody in Latin Europe would
dispute.

That is the whole point of the transaction and it does not depend on the union
surviving. An Orthodox ruler in the Balkans could get a crown from
Constantinople, which would attach conditions and consider it revocable, or from
Rome, which was a thousand miles away and had no means of enforcing anything. The
second was a better deal, and Kalojan took it, and then kept the crown and
returned the obedience.

## 1205

Six months after the coronation, the crusaders who had taken Constantinople the
previous year marched on Adrianople, and Kalojan destroyed them.

The Cuman horse archers feigned flight and drew the Frankish knights into a
pursuit; the pursuit was broken up and ridden down; the emperor Baldwin I was
captured and died in a Bulgarian prison. The Latin empire of Constantinople never
rebuilt a field army capable of offensive war, and it had been in existence for
sixteen months.

A state twenty years old, founded by two provincial brothers over a wedding tax
and a slap, destroyed the army that had just taken Constantinople — in the same
year its ruler was crowned by a papal legate, and using cavalry hired from the
steppe.

Kalojan then spent three years taking Thrace and was murdered outside
Thessalonica in 1207 by his own commander, probably at his wife's instigation.
The state he had made was the strongest power in the Balkans and had no rule
whatever for who came next.
