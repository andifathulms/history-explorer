Kalojan, the youngest of the Asen brothers, ruled from 1197 and wanted two things
his brothers had not got: recognition, and Constantinople.

The recognition he obtained from a direction nobody in Tarnovo had used before.
In 1199 he opened a correspondence with Innocent III, and the letters survive on
both sides. Kalojan asked for an imperial crown and a patriarch, citing the
precedent of Simeon and Peter — this corpus's First Bulgarian record's fifth
chapter is those men — and pointing out that his ancestors had held the title.
Innocent offered a royal crown and a primate, not an imperial one and not a
patriarch, in exchange for recognising papal authority. Kalojan took it, and a
legate crowned him at Tarnovo in November 1204.

Both sides understood they were describing different transactions. The papal
register calls him king; his own charters call him emperor.

## `investiture`

This record's `legitimation` field carries `investiture` on that crown, and it is
worth setting out why a lapsed union with a pope who called him something else
still fills a coded field.

Coding rule 8 says the value records a public claim resting on a grant of
authority from an external sovereign, and it says explicitly that a grant which a
later source calls a formality still counts — the same reasoning coding rule 1
uses for unrecognised successor claims. What is coded is that the ruler thought
the grant was worth having.

Kalojan plainly did. He conducted a five-year negotiation for it, accepted terms
he then reinterpreted, and got what he actually wanted, which was that the two
things a European ruler needed — a crown and a church — had been conferred by an
authority Latin Europe could not argue with.

The union lasted about a generation. The crown did not lapse and the patriarchate
was restored on Orthodox terms in 1235.

This is the third record in a Latin or Balkan context to carry the value, after
Ostrogothic Italy and Norman Sicily, and all three are the same manoeuvre by
rulers in very different positions: a title that is contested at home, confirmed
by the one office in the west whose confirmation everybody could read.

## 1205

Six months after the coronation, the crusaders who had taken Constantinople the
previous year marched on Adrianople, and Kalojan destroyed them.

The Cuman horse archers feigned flight and drew the Frankish knights into a
pursuit; the pursuit was broken up and ridden down; the emperor Baldwin I was
captured and died in a Bulgarian prison. This corpus's Latin Empire record
carries the same day as its first turning point and concludes that the empire
never rebuilt a force capable of offensive war.

From this side the conclusion is different. A state twenty years old, founded by
two provincial brothers over a wedding tax, destroyed the army that had just
taken Constantinople, and did it in the same year its ruler was crowned by a
papal legate.

Kalojan then spent three years taking Thrace and was murdered outside
Thessalonica in 1207 by his own commander, probably at his wife's instigation.
The state he had made was the strongest power in the Balkans and had no rule for
who came next, which is the fourth chapter's subject and this record's null.
