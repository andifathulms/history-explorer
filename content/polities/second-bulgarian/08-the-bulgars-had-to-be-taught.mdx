Five centuries is long enough to forget.

By the beginning of the nineteenth century the Bulgarian people, in Forbes's flat
description, consisted entirely of peasants. There was no Bulgarian upper class,
no middle class, no professional class. Such educated Bulgarians as existed lived
somewhere else — in Bucharest, in Odessa, in the Russian and Romanian cities
where money and printing presses were. And the church, which under the Ottoman
settlement of 1393 answered to the patriarch of Constantinople, was in Greek
hands, and its hierarchy competed with the Turkish administration in suppressing
any suggestion that Bulgarians were a nation.

Then Forbes writes the sentence that says what a national revival actually
involves:

> not only was nothing known at that time about Bulgaria and its inhabitants in
> other countries, but the Bulgars had themselves to be taught who they were.

## Taught by whom

By émigrés, in other people's countries, with other people's money.

A history of Bulgaria was published by a Bulgarian in Moscow in 1829. The first
school inside Bulgaria opened in 1835 and others followed quickly. Two committees
did most of the work and did not agree about the method: the one at Odessa wanted
educational and religious reform and a gradual, peaceful regeneration; the one at
Bucharest wanted immediate political emancipation by violence if violence was
required.

Both were funded by merchants. A nation with no aristocracy left had to be
reassembled by its traders, from outside its own borders, starting with a
schoolbook.

## The church came first

The ecclesiastical question was settled before the political one, and by the
people themselves.

The Porte had promised in 1856 that there would be Bulgarian bishops and that the
Bulgarian language would be recognised in church and school, and had not
delivered. So in 1860 the Bulgarians simply stopped recognising the patriarch of
Constantinople. There was an attempt the same year to place the Bulgarian church
under Rome instead — the exact manoeuvre Boris had tried in 866 and Kalojan in
1204 — and Russian opposition killed it.

In 1870 the Turkish government, alarmed by the agitation, established the
Bulgarian Exarchate: a free and national Bulgarian church under an Exarch who
would reside in Constantinople, Bulgaria itself still being a Turkish province.
The Greeks staved it off for two years and in 1872 the Exarch was installed in
Constantinople anyway.

So the church was restored eight years before the state was, in the capital of
the empire that ruled the country, by permission of a sultan. Five hundred years
after Constantinople finally won the argument about who ran the Bulgarian church,
it lost it again — and this time to a decision of the Ottoman government.

## What the church defined

The Exarchate mattered more than a jurisdictional detail should, because in the
Ottoman system a religious community was the unit that existed. Nationality had
no administrative meaning; confession did.

That is why the boundaries of the Exarchate became the boundaries of the claim.
Where a village went over to the Bulgarian church it was, for practical purposes,
a Bulgarian village, and where it stayed with the patriarch it was Greek. The
Macedonian question — which occupied Bulgaria, Serbia and Greece for the next
forty years and produced two Balkan wars — was in large part an argument about
which parishes belonged to which hierarchy, conducted afterwards with rifles.

A nation reconstituted through a church ends up with a national question shaped
like a diocese.

## Tarnovo, 1908

Risings in 1875 and 1876 were put down with the massacres that turned European
opinion; Russia went to war in 1877; and the Congress of Berlin the following
year produced a Bulgarian principality considerably smaller than the one the
Russians had drawn, still under Ottoman suzerainty.

Thirty years later that formality was ended, and where it was ended is the point.

On 5 October 1908, at Tarnovo — the ancient capital, the Asens' city, the place
the Ottomans had taken after a three-month siege in 1393 — Ferdinand proclaimed
the complete independence of Bulgaria under himself as king. The Bulgarian word
he used for the title was *tsar*.

It is the same word Simeon spent thirty years at war to have recognised in 927
and Kalojan negotiated five years with a pope to obtain in 1204, resumed in the
city where the second empire had been crowned, by a Saxe-Coburg prince, five
hundred and twelve years after Vidin fell.
