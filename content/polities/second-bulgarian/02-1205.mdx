In April 1205, outside Adrianople, Kalojan of Bulgaria destroyed the Latin field
army with Cuman horse archers who feigned flight and drew the knights into a
pursuit, and captured the emperor Baldwin I, who died in a Bulgarian prison.

This corpus's Latin Empire record carries the same day as its own first turning
point and says the empire never rebuilt a force capable of offensive war —
everything after it being a state holding a capital it could not supply, kept
alive by Venetian shipping. That record was written before this one, and the
sentence it needed was the one this page supplies: the power that did it.

## A crown from Rome

The other thing Kalojan did is this record's most unexpected coded value.

In 1204 — the year the crusaders took Constantinople, and before Adrianople — he
obtained from Innocent III a royal crown and a primate for the Bulgarian church,
in exchange for recognising papal authority. A papal legate crowned him at
Tarnovo in November.

That is `investiture` in this site's `legitimation` vocabulary: a public claim
resting on a grant of authority from an external sovereign. It is the third
record in this corpus to carry the value in a Latin or Balkan context, after
Ostrogothic Italy and Norman Sicily, and all three are the same manoeuvre — a
ruler with a contested title getting it confirmed by the one authority in Latin
Europe whose confirmation was universally legible.

The union with Rome lapsed within a generation and the Bulgarian church went back
to Orthodoxy. The crown did not lapse. That is precisely why rule 1 and rule 8
both count claims without filtering on whether they were honoured: what is being
recorded is that the grant was worth having.

## Klokotnitsa

Ivan Asen II, this record's peak ruler, destroyed the army of Epirus in 1230 and
captured and blinded Theodore Komnenos Doukas, who had crowned himself emperor at
Thessalonica and was the likeliest man in the Balkans to retake Constantinople.

Bulgaria absorbed Macedonia, Thrace and Albania and was, for about a decade, the
dominant power between the Danube and the Aegean. Ivan Asen styled himself
emperor of the Bulgarians and the Greeks, minted, endowed, and had his victory
carved on a column in the church at Tarnovo where the inscription still is.

It is also the reason this corpus's Nicaean record's account of its rivals stops
mentioning Epirus after 1230. The Greek claimant Nicaea most feared was removed
by a Bulgarian tsar, and Nicaea took Constantinople thirty-one years later.

## The shape of the peak

One reign.

Ivan Asen II died in 1241 and the Mongols raided the Balkans the following year.
His son was a child, the throne changed hands repeatedly, and by 1300 Bulgaria
was a middling power paying tribute to the Golden Horde. This record's
`succession_rule` is null and its data comment is blunter than most: something
over twenty transfers in two hundred and eleven years, four tsars assassinated in
the first forty, and no source stating a rule.

A state whose best decade is its fifth and whose next hundred and sixty years are
a decline is not unusual in this corpus. What is unusual is how completely the
peak depended on one man, and the next chapter is what happened to what he left.
