Ivaylo's fall did not end the chaos his rising had been a verdict on. It
took Bulgaria another half-century, a Tatar alliance, a run of forgotten
rulers, and one genuinely capable nobleman from the western frontier to
reach the battlefield that handed the Balkans to Serbia for a
generation.

## A Tatar alliance nobody could undo

Constantine, the aristocrat installed after Ivaylo's death, called on
the Tatars of southern Russia - then at the height of their own power -
to beat back a combined Hungarian and Byzantine attack, and won. The
victory came at a lasting price: from this point on the Tatars of the
Golden Horde had a permanent hand in Bulgarian affairs, the same power
whose unstopped raids had put a swineherd on the throne in the first
place. Constantine married a Byzantine princess as his second wife,
giving Constantinople its own voice in Tarnovo's court as well, and by
1277 - the year of his death in the same battle that had crowned Ivaylo
- Bulgaria answered to Tatars, Greeks, and whatever faction could hold
the capital for a season.

## A parade of rulers nobody remembers

What followed Constantine's death was, by its own historian's account,
a series of upstart rulers whose reigns left no mark beyond their
sequence - a chaos serious enough that Serbia's King Uroš II found the
opening to conquer all of Macedonia outright and end whatever remained
of Bulgarian influence there. In 1285 the Golden Horde's own Tatars
swept across Hungary and Bulgaria alike, a reminder that the alliance
Constantine had bought still came with a cost attached. Further south, a
different danger was gathering unnoticed: Turkish forces reached the Sea
of Marmara in 1308 and took Bursa in 1326, decades before Bulgaria's own
rulers would have reason to treat them as more than a distant power's
problem.

## Svetoslav's order, and the chaos that followed him

From 1295 the throne passed to Svetoslav, a nobleman from Vidin on the
western frontier, who held it for twenty-seven years and did what none
of his immediate predecessors had managed: keep the peace. Left
unmolested by a Byzantium with troubles of its own, and already alert to
the Ottoman threat gathering to the south, Svetoslav gave Bulgaria a
generation of order its subjects had not seen since before Ivaylo. It
did not survive him. His death in 1322 reopened the chaos his reign had
only suspended: his successor, married to the daughter of Serbia's King
Uroš II, abruptly abandoned that alliance for one with the Greeks
against his own brother-in-law, Stephen Uroš III, sending his wife home
in the process. The war that followed ended in total defeat. At
Kustendil in Macedonia in 1330, the Serbs destroyed the allied
Greek-Bulgarian army outright - the defeat that handed Bulgaria's fate
to Serbia for the next thirty years.
