In 1277 a peasant called Ivaylo, who had kept pigs, raised the countryside of
north-eastern Bulgaria against the Mongol raids the government was not stopping,
beat the Mongols in the field, then beat the royal army, killed the tsar in the
battle, and was crowned tsar himself at Tarnovo.

He married the dowager empress, Maria, who was a Byzantine princess and the
previous tsar's widow, in order to make the thing legal. He reigned about two
years. Byzantium sent an army and a candidate of its own against him, he beat
that too, and he was finally undone by a siege at Silistra in which he was
blockaded by Mongols for three months while a rival was installed behind him. He
went to the Mongol khan Nogai to ask for help and was killed at a feast.

## Why it is a turning point

Coding rule 4.1 asks what an event altered rather than what it was, and rule 4.2
excludes events that were merely well documented. A two-year peasant reign that
ended with the throne going back to an aristocrat looks at first like an
interruption rather than a hinge.

What it altered is what the state was for. Ivaylo's rising was a response to a
specific failure — the Mongol raids across the Danube were annual, the government
paid tribute and did not fight, and the villages that were burned had no
protection from a state whose entire justification was that it protected them.
A peasant beating the Mongols the government would not fight is a verdict on the
government, and the aristocracy that removed him did not fix the thing he had
risen about. Bulgaria went on paying the Golden Horde for another twenty years.

Men from outside the ruling house take thrones elsewhere in this corpus — the
Mamluk record is a slave soldiery that took a sultanate, and the Abbadid record
opens on a mat-maker installed as a caliph — but both of those are the military
or political class doing the installing. This one is not. A man who kept pigs
raised a countryside, won two wars, and was crowned in the capital, and the
record would be poorer for leaving it out on the grounds that it did not last.

## What it says about the null

This record's `succession_rule` is null, and its data comment is the bluntest on
the site: something over twenty transfers in two hundred and eleven years, four
tsars assassinated in the first forty, no source stating a rule.

Ivaylo is the extreme case and he is also, oddly, the one that most clearly shows
why the field is null rather than `factional`. `factional` — the value this
corpus gives the Mamluk sultanate — describes a throne that goes to whichever
faction inside the political class can impose its man, which is a rule of a kind:
everyone knows what the competition is and who is allowed to enter it.

Bulgaria did not have that either. It had a hereditary principle nobody wrote
down, honoured about half the time, and a court in which a swineherd could be
crowned because he had an army and had married the right widow. Coding rule 7
takes the rule in force and there was not one, which is a different answer from
having a rule that produced disorder.

## The Mongols

The tribute to the Golden Horde is not in this record's `revenue_basis`, which
reads `land-tax, trade-toll`, because that field records where a polity's income
came from rather than where it went.

There is no field on this site for what a polity paid out, and this is the second
time the absence has been worth noting — the First Bulgarian record's own
`revenue_basis` carries `tribute` for the subsidy Byzantium paid *to* Bulgaria,
which is the same relationship seen from the receiving end three centuries
earlier and does get a value.

One record in this region collecting tribute from an empire and the next paying
it to one, with only the first of the two visible in the coded fields. Two cases
is not a pattern and no change is proposed. It is worth having on the page that
the asymmetry exists.
