title: A tax and two brothers

In 1185 the emperor Isaac II Angelos levied a special tax across the Balkans to
pay for his wedding. Two brothers from the Tarnovo region, Theodore and Asen,
went to him to ask for a grant of land and were refused; Asen was slapped in the
face. They went home and raised a revolt.

It failed. They crossed the Danube, came back with Cuman cavalry, and by 1189
had a state with a capital, a crown and a patriarch.

## What they said they were restoring

Not a new kingdom. The brothers took the title *tsar*, were crowned at Tarnovo,
and had the relics of the First Bulgarian Empire's saints — Ivan of Rila among
them — moved to the new capital, which is the medieval way of saying that this
is the same country.

This record carries `resumes: first-bulgarian` for that reason, and it is the
first use of the field outside the Ancient Near East, where it was built for the
two Babylons, the two Assyrias, the two Elams and the two Egypts.

The field says two records are one object seen twice across an interruption. It
is not an edge, and the reason is the one the field's own documentation gives:
every edge in this corpus is an event with a year, and there is no year on which
Bulgaria resumed being Bulgaria — 1185 is a revolt against a tax, not a
restoration ceremony. And an edge would be drawn by a thread as though the First
Empire had been succeeded by the Second, which is not the claim.

The build enforces the distinction. It refuses a pair that has both a `resumes`
and an edge, on the ground that a pair is either a succession or the same object
resuming and this site does not assert both. That is also why the First Empire's
`successor_claims` is zero rather than one: a polity does not claim its own
legitimacy.

## The Cumans

The brothers' decisive asset was cavalry from across the Danube, and this record
codes `mercenary` in its `military_basis` because of it.

The Cumans were a Turkic steppe people occupying the plain north of the Black Sea
— this corpus's Golden Horde record will eventually displace them — and for the
first thirty years of the Second Empire they are the arm that wins its battles.
They were paid, married into the Asen family, and eventually settled inside the
state, which is roughly what the Bulgars had done to the Slavs five centuries
earlier and is a fair description of how states in this region were made.

The other value is `land-grant`: the *pronoia*, grants of revenue in return for
service, taken over from the Byzantine administration the revolt displaced. This
corpus's Nicaean record describes the same instrument being arrived at
independently by the Greek government in exile in the same decades, and its own
chapter counts thirty-one records across the corpus carrying `land-grant` from
Ur III to the Ottomans. This is one more.

## What it took off Byzantium

The Balkans north of the Balkan range, permanently, twenty years before the
Fourth Crusade took Constantinople.

This corpus's Byzantine record spends its last two centuries losing things and
its ninth chapter dates the collapse to 1204 and the civil wars of the 1340s.
This is the first substantial piece, and it went before either.
