In 1277 a peasant called Ivaylo, who had kept pigs, raised the countryside of
north-eastern Bulgaria against the Mongol raids the government was not stopping,
beat the Mongols in the field, then beat the royal army, killed the tsar in the
battle, and was crowned tsar himself at Tarnovo.

He married the dowager empress, Maria, who was a Byzantine princess and the
previous tsar's widow, in order to make the thing legal. He reigned about two
years. Byzantium sent an army and a candidate of its own against him, he beat
that too, and he was finally undone by a siege at Silistra in which he was
blockaded by Mongols for three months while a rival was installed behind him. He
went to the Mongol khan Nogai to ask for help and was killed at a feast.

## Why it matters more than two years suggests

A two-year peasant reign that ended with the throne going back to an aristocrat
looks like an interruption rather than a hinge. It is a hinge because of what it
was a verdict on.

The Mongol raids across the Danube were annual. The government paid tribute to
the Golden Horde and did not fight. The villages that were burned had no
protection from a state whose entire justification for taxing them was that it
protected them — and then a man who kept pigs raised those villages and beat the
Mongols that the tsar's army had not engaged.

The aristocracy removed him and did not fix the thing he had risen about.
Bulgaria went on paying the Golden Horde for another twenty years.

## The company he is not in

Men from outside the ruling house take thrones all over the medieval world, and
almost always with a faction behind them. A slave soldiery makes one of its own
sultan; a general deposes a king; a governor's son is proclaimed by the army.
What is unusual here is that nobody in the political or military class installed
Ivaylo. He assembled his own following out of the countryside, won two wars with
it, killed the reigning tsar in a battle, and was crowned in the capital — and
then married the dead man's widow, a Byzantine princess, because he understood
perfectly well that winning was not the same as being legitimate.

He was right to worry about it. The marriage did not save him, and the
aristocracy waited him out.

## What it says about the throne

Bulgaria in these two centuries had a hereditary principle that nobody had
written down and that was honoured about half the time. The throne changed hands
something over twenty times between 1185 and 1396, and four tsars were
assassinated in the first forty years alone.

That is not the same as an openly factional succession, where the competition has
rules and everybody knows who is entitled to enter it. This was a court in which a
swineherd could be crowned because he had an army and had married the right widow
— which is what happens when there is no rule at all, rather than a bad one.

## The Mongols

The tribute Bulgaria paid the Golden Horde ran for decades and shaped everything
in this chapter, and it is worth noticing that it is the exact reverse of the
arrangement the first Bulgarian state had enjoyed.

Byzantium had paid Bulgaria an annual subsidy for three centuries to keep it
quiet, and the withholding of it started wars. Three hundred years later Bulgaria
was paying a steppe power to be left alone, and failing to be left alone, and a
pig-keeper had to do the fighting.
