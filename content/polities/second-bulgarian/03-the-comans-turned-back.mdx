A year after the crusaders took Constantinople, the Bulgarian tsar destroyed
their army outside Adrianople and took the Latin emperor alive. The fullest
account of it was written by a man who was in the camp that day, and who spent
the rest of the chronicle explaining what had gone wrong.

Villehardouin calls him Johannizza, King of Wallachia and Bulgaria. He is Kaloyan,
the younger of the two brothers whose revolt had made the state twenty years
earlier.

## How he was invited

The Latins had spread themselves across Thrace, each baron attending to his own
new lands. Villehardouin's account of what the local population did about it is
brief and bitter:

> Now the Greeks, who were very disloyal, still nourished treachery in their
> hearts … They took envoys therefore privily, from all the cities in the land,
> and sent them to Johannizza, the King of Wallachia and Bulgaria, who was still
> at war with them as he had been aforetime. And they told Johannizza they would
> make him emperor, and give themselves wholly to him, and slay all the Franks.
> So they swore that they would obey him as their lord, and he swore that he
> would defend them as though they were his own people.

The Greek cities of Thrace, having just lost their empire to a crusade, offered
the imperial title to the Bulgarian king they had been fighting, on condition
that he get rid of the Franks. He accepted.

## Two days

The military part is the clearest description anywhere of what happened when
western heavy cavalry met steppe horse-archers, and it is clearer because the
Frenchman telling it knew exactly what his own side had done wrong.

On the Wednesday of Easter week Kaloyan sent his Cumans running past the Latin
camp. The Latins poured out after them for a full league, *very foolishly*; when
they turned to come back, the Cumans began shooting, and wounded a great many
horses. The barons were summoned to the emperor's quarters that evening, agreed
that they had dealt foolishly in chasing people so lightly armed, and resolved
that next time they would form up in front of the camp and wait, and not move. It
was proclaimed through the host that nobody was to leave his post *for any cry or
tumult that might come to his ears*.

On the Thursday the Cumans ran up to the tents again. Count Louis of Blois went
out first and followed them, sending word to the emperor to come after him. *Alas!
how ill did they keep to what had been settled the night before!* They pursued for
at least two leagues, closed with the Cumans, and chased them a long way. Then the
Cumans turned back on them and began to cry out and to shoot.

Louis was wounded twice and unhorsed, and a knight dismounted to give him his own
horse. His men told him to get away. He said: *The Lord God forbid that ever I
should be reproached with flying from the field, and abandoning the emperor.*

Baldwin recalled his people and told them he would not fly either. Villehardouin,
who was guarding the camp and not in the fighting, says that those present bear
witness that never did knight defend himself better with his hands. Then:

> There on the field remained the Emperor Baldwin, who never would fly, and Count
> Lewis; the Emperor Baldwin was taken alive and Count Lewis was slain.

He follows it with a list of the dead by name — a bishop, two brothers of counts,
a dozen knights — and notes that there were many more the book does not mention.

## What it established

The Latin emperor of Constantinople died in Bulgarian captivity at Tarnovo. The
manner of it was disputed for centuries and Villehardouin does not know; what he
knows is that Baldwin never came back.

Nothing else in the Second Bulgarian Empire's history matches it. Within fifteen
months of the Fourth Crusade's triumph, the state the crusaders had built was
beaten in the field by a kingdom most of them had not heard of, its emperor was a
prisoner, and the Greek cities that had invited the victor in were finding out
what that cost — Kaloyan turned on Thrace and depopulated it, and the same
chronicle records him doing it.

What the chapter is evidence of is not Bulgarian greatness. It is that the balance
in the Balkans after 1204 was not between a Latin empire and a Greek one. There
was a third power in it, it had Cuman cavalry on contract, and for a few years it
was the strongest thing on the peninsula.
