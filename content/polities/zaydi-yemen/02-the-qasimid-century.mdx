---
title: The century it ruled everything
drafted_from: dresch-1989
phase: peak
---

For most of its history the imamate was a highland thing. The coast, the
Tihama, and the southern uplands belonged to whoever was strong that century —
Ziyadids, Sulayhids, Ayyubids, Rasulids, and from the 1530s the Ottomans, who
took Yemen as a province and held the ports because the Red Sea route to India
mattered to them.

Then the imamate did something it had never managed. From 1597 al-Mansur
al-Qasim led a rising against Ottoman rule that his successors continued, and
by 1636 the Ottomans were out of Yemen altogether. For roughly the following
century the imams ruled the whole country: the highlands they had always held,
the Tihama coast, Aden, and eastward towards Hadramawt. Coffee went out through
Mocha to Europe on Yemeni terms, and the imamate collected the customs.

The family that did it, the Qasimids, then held the office continuously enough
to look like a dynasty, which is exactly what the doctrine was supposed to
prevent. An imamate open to any qualified Alid who rose had become, in
practice, hereditary in one house — and the tension between the practice and
the doctrine is a recurring subject of Yemeni scholarly argument thereafter,
because there were always other qualified candidates entitled to rise, and
sometimes they did.

The century of ruling everything did not last. The state fractured through the
eighteenth century into effectively separate districts; the Ottomans came back
to the Tihama in the nineteenth and to Sana'a in 1872, and were only finally
gone in 1918 when their empire ended elsewhere. The imamate that emerged from
that, under Yahya and then his son Ahmad, governed an independent Yemen that
kept foreigners out and kept almost everything else out with them.

In 1948 a conspiracy killed Yahya, and failed; his son took the office back
within weeks. In September 1962 army officers shelled the palace a week after
Ahmad's son succeeded, declared a republic, and this time it held. The imam
fought on from the northern mountains for years, which is where al-Hadi had
begun.
