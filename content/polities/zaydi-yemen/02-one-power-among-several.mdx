---
title: One power among several, for seven hundred years
drafted_from: dresch-1989
phase: expansion
---

For the first seven centuries the Zaydi imamate was not the government of
Yemen. It was one power in a country that usually had three or four, and the
others are on this site's own pages: the Sulayhids held Sana'a and the southern
highlands in the eleventh century, the Ayyubids took most of the country in the
1170s, the Rasulids ran a wealthy state from Ta'izz and Zabid for two hundred
years afterwards, and the Ottomans arrived in the 1530s and took the ports.

Through all of it there were imams. Sometimes several at once, which the
doctrine permits and the politics encouraged: an office open to any qualified
Alid who rises will attract more than one riser when the moment looks
favourable. Sometimes the imam held Sa'da and a few districts and no more.
Sometimes there was no claimant at all for decades.

What kept the office alive when it was not governing anything is the thing
Dresch's book is about, and it is not the imamate. It is the tribes.

The northern highlands were organised in confederations — Hashid and Bakil,
principally — with their own customary law, their own mechanisms for settling
disputes and guaranteeing truces, and a structure that did not depend on any
state and did not much change when states came and went. The imam sat above that
system rather than inside it. He was an outsider by definition, an Alid and a
scholar rather than a tribesman, and that was the qualification: a man with no
stake in any tribe's quarrel could arbitrate it. He had no standing army worth
the name and no bureaucracy; what he had was the ability to convene, to judge,
and to call for a rising when there was something worth rising against.

Two consequences run through the rest of this page. The imamate could not be
destroyed by defeating it, because it was not an army or a capital — the
Ayyubids, the Rasulids and the Ottomans each took Yemen and each found the
office still there when they left. And it could not build a state either, for
the same reason, because the constituency that answered an imam's call was the
same constituency that would decline to be taxed by him.

The other output of those centuries is the law. Zaydi imams were expected to be
jurists and many of them were serious ones; the *madhhab* has a large literature
and a good deal of it was written by men who were simultaneously conducting a
war in the mountains.
