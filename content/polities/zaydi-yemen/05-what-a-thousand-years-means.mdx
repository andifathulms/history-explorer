---
title: What a thousand years means here
drafted_from: dresch-1989
phase: aside
---

This page carries a span of 897 to 1962. That is one thousand and sixty-five
years, which makes it the longest-lived polity on this site — longer than
Byzantium, longer than the Abbasids, longer than anything else in the corpus.
The endurance board in this site's rankings will put it first.

The number is real and it is not what it looks like, and saying so is the
purpose of this chapter.

What persisted for a thousand years was an office, not a government. The imam
was a qualified Alid who had risen and been answered; when no qualified
claimant rose, or when the man who had risen held only a few districts, or when
an Ayyubid or Rasulid or Ottoman administration governed most of Yemen from the
coast, the imamate did not cease to exist as an institution but was not
governing a state in any sense the other pages here use the word. There were
periods of decades in which it amounted to a family in the northern mountains
with a claim, and periods — the Qasimid century — when it ruled from Sa'da to
Aden.

The site's own machinery cannot say that. `span.start` and `span.end` take a
range where two sources disagree about a date; they have no way to record that
the thing between them was intermittent. So the record here gives the dates the
sources give, and this chapter carries the caveat that the data model cannot.

Two things follow, and the second matters more than the first.

The narrow point is that the longevity figure on this page should be read as
the duration of an institution rather than of a state, and compared with
Byzantium's ten centuries only with that in mind.

The wider one is that this is what the site's own rules produce when they meet
a shape they were not designed for. Reach was built for territorial empires and
reads as a gap for Srivijaya, which held a strait rather than an area. Longevity
was built for polities that begin, continue and stop, and it overstates an
office that recurred. Neither is a fault in the polity. The measures are
narrower than the world, which is a reason to publish them with the argument
attached rather than to quietly exclude whatever does not fit them.
