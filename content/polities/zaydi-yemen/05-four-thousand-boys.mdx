title: Four thousand boys
drafted_from: rihani-1930

The Ottomans left in 1918 and the imamate got the country back — more of it than
any imam had held since the seventeenth century. Four years later an
Arab-American writer travelled up from Aden to Sana'a, was received by Imam
Yahya, and wrote down what an independent Yemen actually looked like while it was
working.

Ameen Rihani is a sympathetic witness, which is what makes his account useful. He
wanted Arab states to succeed, he thought Yahya one of the few genuine rulers in
the peninsula, and he describes the machinery anyway.

The machinery, at Yarim, came out to meet him: fifty-odd boys, brought by the
local jurist to welcome the visitor. They were hostages. Rihani says he had heard
in Lahej that the imam took a son or a brother of every official in his service,
civil and military, as a guarantee of honesty and loyalty, and that he had not
believed it. There were, he was told, four thousand of them, all living away from
home and deliberately circulated — Yarim's hostages sent to Sana'a, Sana'a's to
Ibb, Ibb's to Dhamar. Some were under military supervision and taught to read and
write; some were simply imprisoned; a few, on satisfactory bail, could move about
the town they were held in.

That is a revenue and loyalty system with no administration underneath it, and
Rihani noticed the hole. At an inn on the road he asked for milk and was told
there were neither cows nor sheep, and nobody to herd them if there were, because
the imam's agents took them.

The rest of his portrait is of a state improvised out of the wreckage of the one
before it. The last Ottoman governor had handed over power at Sana'a in person
and then stayed on as the imam's adviser; three hundred-odd Ottoman soldiers and
officers were retained to leaven the regular army; an Austrian Christian ran the
cartridge factory. Yahya was sixty-three, of the line of the first Zaydi
missionary to Yemen, with four wives, thirty-four children of whom sixteen lived,
and four sons serving as officers. Rihani counts twenty-seven years of reign of
which twenty were continuous war — a chain of campaigns and truces — and observes
that making children and making war would seem to be the business of an imam.

And he identifies, precisely, the thing that would eventually end the imamate.
Yahya governed as a Zaydi and invoked the Zaydi creed against the Shafi'is of the
Tihama as well as against his rivals: *these are worse heretics than the Turks,
and the jihad must continue.* Rihani calls it a jihad of one party of the
Prophet's followers against another, and argues that if the rule were civil
rather than sectarian the imam would get everything he wanted without a war,
because the Shafi'is would stop being a weapon in his enemies' hands and become
his strongest supporters. As it stood, he writes, whatever might be said of the
imam's impartial justice, they were discontented.

He also notices the country's arithmetic. A population of three million that is
not growing; about a third of Yemen under cultivation; and a state whose answer
to both is a larger hostage list.

Rihani published this in 1930. Imam Yahya was assassinated in 1948 in a
constitutionalist coup that his son Ahmad put down from the north; Ahmad ruled
until 1962 and survived several attempts on his life; and a week after Ahmad's
death the army abolished the imamate. Everything that happened in those
thirty-two years is in this chapter in outline, written by a man who liked the
imam and could see it coming.
