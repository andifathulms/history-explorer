The Ottomans came back to the Tihama in the nineteenth century and to Sana'a in
1872, and were finally gone in 1918 when their empire ended elsewhere. What Imam
Yahya inherited was something the office had never had: an independent state
with recognised borders, a coast, and a seat at international negotiations.

He governed it by keeping almost everything out. Foreigners, foreign capital,
foreign schools, roads, and — as far as possible — the twentieth century.
Hostages from tribal families were held at Sana'a as surety for their
confederations' good behaviour, which was traditional practice conducted on a
larger scale than tradition had required. Yemen in the 1930s had a handful of
motor vehicles and almost no modern schooling, and a generation of Yemenis
educated abroad, in Egypt and Iraq, came home and found that intolerable.

In 1948 a conspiracy of those men and dissident sayyids killed Yahya and
proclaimed a constitutional imamate under a different Alid. It was doctrinally
coherent — a qualified candidate had risen — and it lasted a few weeks. Yahya's
son Ahmad raised the northern tribes, took Sana'a back, and executed the
leaders. What the episode established is on this record as a turning point: the
question had stopped being which Alid would be imam and had become whether
Yemen would have one.

Ahmad ruled until 1962, survived several attempts on his life, and died in bed.
His son Muhammad al-Badr succeeded him and a week later army officers under
Abdullah al-Sallal shelled the palace and declared a republic.

This time it held, at a price. Al-Badr got out and raised the same northern
tribes his grandfather had, and the civil war that followed ran for eight years
with Egyptian troops on the republican side and Saudi money on the royalist one
— a proxy war that cost Egypt heavily and is one of the reasons the Egyptian
army was in the state it was in 1967. The republicans kept Sana'a. The imamate
was not restored.

The office had begun in 897 with tribes near Sa'da sending to Medina for
somebody to arbitrate, and it ended with a claimant fighting from the mountains
above Sa'da with foreign subsidies. This site types the ending `internal
usurpation` with an empty `by`, which is right in the narrow sense — army
officers took the state — and thin in every other. What ended in 1962 was not a
government but a way of constituting one, and the corpus has nothing else like
it to compare with.
