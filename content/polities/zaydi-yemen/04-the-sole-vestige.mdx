The imamate that had driven the Ottomans out of Yemen in 1636 and ruled from
Sa'da to Aden spent the following two centuries losing everything it had gained,
and the losses came from every direction at once.

The account followed here was written by a British political agent who lived in
Yemen and Aden and knew the country well. He is useful for the sequence and
should not be borrowed from for anything else: he was writing wartime
propaganda, his opinions of Yemenis and of Islamic law are contemptuous, and
they can be left where he put them.

## The pieces coming off

Aden and its hinterland stopped obeying Sana'a in 1728, and the British
bombarded and occupied the port in 1839.

In 1801 the Wahhabi movement swept across central Arabia and took the holy
cities. A chief of Asir joined it and took the Yemeni coastal towns as far south
as Mocha in 1804. Muhammad Ali of Egypt, ordered by the sultan to deal with the
Wahhabis, used the opportunity to seize several towns on the Yemeni coast for
himself — and then handed them back to the imam, on tribute. When he broke with
the Porte in 1832 he sent a pasha to occupy the coast again, and the Egyptians
left only because of pressure at home and from four European powers.

Then the dismemberment turned into a family matter. Sharif Husayn of Abu Arish
in Asir, a relative of the imam's, tried to bring the whole of Yemen under
himself, found it beyond him, and acknowledged Ottoman suzerainty instead. The
Porte made him governor of Yemen in 1843 with the title of pasha. He held only
the coast; the interior went on recognising the imam at Sana'a.

In 1848 the imam took his relative prisoner in battle. The prisoner ransomed
himself, and afterwards showed such energy that he reduced the imam, in the
phrase of the account, to the city of Sana'a as the sole vestige of his
sovereignty.

## Beheaded by his own subjects

The next year the Ottomans landed at Hodeida to take the country back properly.

The imam recognised Ottoman suzerainty immediately. But when the Turkish column
reached Sana'a the townspeople rose and attacked it in the streets; its commander
was wounded and pulled back to the coast with what was left of his men. And then
the people of Sana'a beheaded their own imam.

That is the position the office had reached. An imam is a qualified descendant of
Ali who rises, calls for allegiance, and is answered; he is not a hereditary
sovereign and his authority is conditional on his conduct. Two centuries after
the imamate held the whole country, the holder of the office had been reduced to
one city, had submitted to a foreign empire to keep it, and was killed by the
inhabitants for doing so.

The Suez Canal opened in 1869 and made Yemen matter again to whoever held the
Red Sea. The Ottomans sent a serious army in 1872 and stayed for the next
forty-six years.

## Winning by losing

The last act of the contraction is the strangest, because it looks like a
recovery.

An imam driven out of Sana'a by Ottoman neglect at the end of the century was
taken up by the tribes on the eastern border as a persecuted leader of Islam.
His son established himself at Khamir in the central highlands, and in his name
the tribes were always ready to have a go at the Ottoman government. In 1904 and
1905 they took Sana'a itself.

The Ottomans then did the cleverest thing available to them. Rather than
continue a war they could not finish, they recognised him as imam — and after
the rising of 1911 they conceded his central demand, that Yemen be administered
under the sharia rather than the Ottoman civil code, with the imam nominating the
judges who would apply it. A decree proclaiming his authority and his alliance
with the empire was read out at Sana'a in September 1913 to celebrations in the
streets.

He had won the thing the office existed for: religious authority over the
country, formally acknowledged. He had won it by becoming the junior partner of
an occupying empire, with Ottoman troops stationed at his own capital — soldiers
whose European-cut trousers his tribal supporters considered immoral.

The observer who recorded this thought the arrangement fragile and expected it to
break. It did, five years later, though not for any of the reasons he had in
mind: the empire lost a world war, went home, and left the imam holding a
country.
