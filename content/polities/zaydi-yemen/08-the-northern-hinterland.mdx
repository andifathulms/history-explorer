The imamate was abolished in a week and then fought for eight years.

Army officers shelled the palace at Sana'a in September 1962 and declared a
republic. The imam got out, went north, and did what every claimant to the
office had done since 897: called on the tribes of the northern highlands, and
was answered.

What followed is described, a year in, by an American intelligence memorandum
written for people who needed to know whether it was going to end. It is a
useful document precisely because it is not a history — it is a working
assessment, hedged where the analysts were unsure, and it describes the imamate's
last campaign in the terms the office had always operated on.

## A continuing stalemate

The overall picture in Yemen, the memorandum begins, is that of a continuing
stalemate.

Egyptian and Yemeni republican forces, it goes on, remained unable to cope
effectively with the guerrilla tactics of the royalist tribes in the northern
hinterland, while holding the major population centres and the coastal lowlands
firmly. The Egyptians were bearing almost the entire brunt of the actual
fighting on the republican side and keeping up indiscriminate daily bombing and
strafing attacks on royalist areas. As long as those troops stayed in the field
the royalists could not win a major victory — but they had arms, ammunition and
money enough inside Yemen to go on with hit-and-run attacks indefinitely.

And this: the pattern of shifting centres of hostile action and of shifting
tribal loyalties, in response to changes in the tactical situation and to new
offers of money to individual tribes, remained a constant.

Read that against the thousand years behind it. Highlands that no lowland army
could hold; a lowland power that could take every town and none of the country;
tribes whose allegiance moved with the tactical situation and the money. It is
the same description that fits the Ayyubids in the 1170s, the Rasulids, the
Ottomans in the 1870s. The republic had inherited the Yemeni problem entire, and
so had the Egyptian army sent to solve it.

## The line that did not move

The other thing the memorandum records is the confessional geography, and it had
outlived the office that produced it.

The republican regime's main support was the southern Shafi'i tribes. Its
president had alienated some of them by appointing a number of northern Zaydi
tribesmen to important posts, and his own position looked doubtful in
consequence. Some Shafi'is, unhappy with all of it, apparently favoured a
separate state free of Zaydi domination altogether.

So within a year of abolishing an office that had been Zaydi for eleven
centuries, a republic founded to replace it was already quarrelling about
whether northerners were getting too many of the jobs, and southerners were
already talking about leaving.

## What was actually abolished

The war ran for eight years. The republicans kept Sana'a, the Egyptians
eventually went home, and no imam was proclaimed again.

But it is worth being exact about what stopped in 1962, because the answer is
narrower than it looks. What ended was the proposition that a qualified
descendant of Ali who rose and called for allegiance thereby became the
legitimate ruler of Yemen. What did not end was any of the material the
proposition had been built on: the sayyid families, the Zaydi law and learning,
the tribal confederations of the north, the fact that Sa'da is a long way from
anywhere an army wants to be, and the readiness of those tribes to answer
somebody.

The imamate's afterlife is not a memory. It is a set of conditions that were
still there when the office went, and that produced, within twelve months, a
republic with a northern problem.
