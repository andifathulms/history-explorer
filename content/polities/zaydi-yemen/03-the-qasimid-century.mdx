---
title: The century it ruled everything
drafted_from: dresch-1989
phase: peak
---

The previous chapter described seven centuries of the imamate as one power among
several. From the 1530s the strongest of the others was the Ottoman empire,
which took Yemen as a province and held the ports because the Red Sea route to
India mattered to it.

Then the imamate did something it had never managed. From 1597 al-Mansur
al-Qasim led a rising against Ottoman rule that his successors continued, and
by 1636 the Ottomans were out of Yemen altogether. For roughly the following
century the imams ruled the whole country: the highlands they had always held,
the Tihama coast, Aden, and eastward towards Hadramawt. Coffee went out through
Mocha to Europe on Yemeni terms, and the imamate collected the customs.

The family that did it, the Qasimids, then held the office continuously enough
to look like a dynasty, which is exactly what the doctrine was supposed to
prevent. An imamate open to any qualified Alid who rose had become, in
practice, hereditary in one house — and the tension between the practice and
the doctrine is a recurring subject of Yemeni scholarly argument thereafter,
because there were always other qualified candidates entitled to rise, and
sometimes they did.

The century of ruling everything did not last, and the coffee did not either:
the Dutch and then the French took plants out of Yemen and established the crop
in Java, Ceylon and the Caribbean, and by the middle of the eighteenth century
the monopoly that had paid for the imamate's one period of real revenue was
gone. The state fractured into effectively separate districts, and the Ottomans
came back to the Tihama in the nineteenth century and to Sana'a in 1872.

What the Qasimids left behind was not the territory. It was the expectation that
the imam would be one of them, which is the subject of the next chapter.
