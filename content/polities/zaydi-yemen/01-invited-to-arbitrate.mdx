title: Invited to arbitrate

Almost every polity on this site begins with somebody taking something. This
one begins with an invitation.

In 897 tribes in the country around Sa'da, in the far north of the Yemeni
highlands, sent to Medina for a learned descendant of Ali named Yahya, and
asked him to come and settle their disputes. He came, was acknowledged as imam,
took the regnal title al-Hadi ila'l-Haqq — the guide to the truth — and ruled
what he could hold for fourteen years. The state that followed him lasted, with
long interruptions, until 1962.

The Zaydi conception of the imamate is what makes this different from the other
Shi'i polities in this corpus, and the difference is doctrinal rather than
circumstantial. The Fatimids and the Nizaris held that the imam is designated
by his predecessor — *nass* — which makes the office hereditary in a specific
line and makes a disputed designation catastrophic, as this site's Fatimid page
records for 1094. Zaydism rejects designation. The imam is any descendant of
Ali who satisfies a demanding list of conditions — religious learning, piety,
courage, physical soundness — and who then *rises*, calls for allegiance, and
is answered.

That has three consequences visible in everything this polity did. It is
open: an imamate can be claimed by anyone qualified, so the office survives the
extinction of any particular family. It is contested: more than one claimant
could rise at once, and often did. And it is demanding in a way inherited
offices are not, because the qualification is scholarly and has to be
demonstrated — an imam was expected to be a jurist, and many of them wrote.

It also explains the geography. The imamate held the northern highlands and
repeatedly failed to hold anything else. Its authority rested on arbitration
between tribes who had asked for a judge and had not asked for a government,
and who could withdraw their recognition as readily as they had extended it.
Dresch's account is of an office that sat above the tribal system without ever
absorbing it, for a thousand years, which is why both survived.
