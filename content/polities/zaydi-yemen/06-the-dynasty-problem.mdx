---
title: An open office that became a family's
drafted_from: haykel-2003
phase: aside
---

The Qasimid century created a problem the doctrine had no clean answer to.

Zaydism holds that the imamate belongs to any descendant of Ali who meets a
demanding list of conditions and who rises and calls for allegiance. It is
explicitly not hereditary: designation is rejected, and the whole point of the
qualifications is that they have to be demonstrated in each generation by the
man claiming the office. It is not designation, not inheritance and not
election, and the initiative lies entirely with the claimant.

After 1636 the office stayed in one family for three centuries.

The Qasimids had expelled the Ottomans and ruled the whole country, and the
prestige of that made their descendants the obvious candidates; and once an
imam's son could expect to succeed, an imam could govern like a king. Haykel's
account traces what followed. The eighteenth-century imams accumulated the
apparatus of a dynasty — a court, a treasury, a standing guard, tax farms — and
found the classical Zaydi doctrine increasingly inconvenient, because the
doctrine gave every qualified Alid in the country a standing right to rise
against them, and some did.

So the doctrine was revised, and the revision came from inside. Muhammad
al-Shawkani, chief judge under three imams from 1795 until his death in 1834,
argued against the accumulated Zaydi legal tradition and for direct recourse to
the Qur'an and hadith — a position that reads as scholarly purism and had a
precise political effect. It undercut the school's own jurisprudence, including
the parts of it that made the imamate contestable, and it moved Yemeni legal
practice towards the Sunni schools.

Haykel's argument is that the sunnification of Yemeni society over the
following two centuries and the imams' need for an uncontestable throne are the
same story. It is an uncomfortable finding and it is hard to argue with: an office that had
been elective for seven hundred years spent its last three centuries dismantling
the grounds of its own election, and did so by degrees, in good faith, one
reasonable accommodation at a time.

The shape is not peculiar to Yemen. The Ibadi imamate in Oman split over whether
a deposition had been lawful and never entirely healed. An office constituted on
a principle has to keep paying for the principle, and the usual way of stopping
is to reinterpret it rather than to abandon it.
