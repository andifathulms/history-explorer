Sultan Abdul Kahar, crowned in 1615, was the first ruler of Jambi to
hold the title Sultan outright - a formal marker of the kingdom's own
transition into an Islamic sultanate on the same model other Malay
river states of the period were adopting. His own reign coincided
almost exactly with the arrival of the Dutch at Jambi: the same year
he took the throne, the VOC's Governor-General sent merchant ships to
establish the first Dutch trade presence there, and within that same
period Abdul Kahar permitted the construction of a "logi" - a
combined trading post and fortification that functioned, in practice,
as much like a small fort as a warehouse.

## A wealthy court, briefly

By the following year Jambi was already being described as the
wealthiest city in Sumatra after Aceh itself, its ruler drawing profit
from trade passing through the river mouth that gave the kingdom its
own strategic value. That wealth did not translate into lasting
independence from outside pressure. Abdul Kahar's own alliance with
the Javanese kingdom of Mataram angered the Dutch enough that the
Governor-General personally tried to court him away from that
relationship with gifts and warnings, an early sign of how closely
Dutch interests would come to shadow Jambi's own foreign policy for
the rest of the sultanate's existence.

## A court divided against itself

What followed Abdul Kahar's own reign was a long pattern the Dutch
learned to exploit rather than create: internal rivalry within the
Jambi court itself, between the reigning sultan and the crown prince's
own circle, and among competing aristocratic factions more generally.
Dutch administrators took advantage of these disputes repeatedly,
sometimes covertly and sometimes openly, at points going so far as to
seize a sitting sultan and exile him to Batavia or the Moluccas rather
than let internal Jambi politics settle the question on its own terms.
