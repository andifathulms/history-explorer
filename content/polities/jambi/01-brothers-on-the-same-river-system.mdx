Jambi's Islamic sultanate governed a river that had carried a great
power once before. The Batang Hari had been the heart of old Malayu, the
kingdom that inherited Srivijaya's own position as the paramount trading
power of the strait centuries earlier, and that a Javanese king's own
expedition had once been sent to bring under control. The later
sultanate was a new institution on old, well-used ground - a river
system whose downstream mouth had been worth fighting over for the
better part of a thousand years before any sultan sat there.

## A relationship framed as kinship

What Jambi's own rulers made of that inheritance in the seventeenth and
eighteenth centuries was defined above all by one relationship: with
Palembang, the sultanate on the neighboring river system to the south,
competing for the same upstream pepper and the same tin from Bangka
island. The two courts described their own connection to each other in
the language of family - "to live as brothers" - even while competing
hard for the same trade and, more than once, going to open war over it.
Kinship and rivalry were not opposites in this framing; they were the
same relationship described from two different angles.

## A prosperity built on a chokehold

Jambi's own leverage came from geography as much as from politics. Both
it and Palembang sat at river mouths the interior's own produce had to
pass through to reach any outside buyer, which gave each court real
control over trade neither could simply route around the other. It was
this position, held for generations without any European power's
direct involvement, that eventually made Jambi wealthy enough to take
on a Malay neighbor considerably larger than itself.
