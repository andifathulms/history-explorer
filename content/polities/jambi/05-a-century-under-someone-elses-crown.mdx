Minangkabau migration into the Jambi interior, already underway when
Pangeran Ratu crowned himself at Muara Tebo with Minangkabau backing,
only deepened over the following century. By the close of the
eighteenth century the balance the 1690 split had struck was gone
entirely: Hilir, the old downstream seat, never recovered the standing
it lost in the split, and the Jambi sultanate as a whole had become a
vassal of the Minangkabau king at Pagaruyung rather than an
independent court answering to nobody. The Dutch presence shrank to
match. The Company vacated its own trading office in Jambi in 1724,
concentrating its resources on Palembang instead, and though it drew
up plans to reopen a post in 1791, the plan was never carried out; for
more than a century the Sultanate of Jambi had no direct relationship
with the Dutch at all.

## A favor that came with a price

That distance ended in 1833. Sultan Muhammad Fachruddin asked the
Dutch East Indies government for help clearing pirates entrenched at
the mouth of the Batang Hari and for assistance asserting control over
the Rawas district, then under Palembang's own sultanate. The mission
succeeded - and the Dutch collected their fee for it. On 4 November
1833, at Sungai Baung, Fachruddin was made to sign an agreement placing
Jambi under Dutch influence and protection and granting the Dutch the
right to station personnel wherever they judged it strategically
necessary. The Dutch judged the terms incomplete, and on 21 April 1835
a fuller agreement followed: the Dutch government took the right to
collect excise on all imports and exports and to monopolize the sale
of salt, promised in exchange not to interfere in Jambi's own
government or customary law beyond that excise, and paid the sultan
and his crown prince an annual stipend of 8,600 guilders for their
compliance.

## An heir who watched the terms tighten

Fachruddin died in 1841, succeeded by Abdurrahman Nazaruddin, with
Fachruddin's own son - the future Sultan Thaha Syaifuddin - installed
as crown prince (Pangeran Ratu) under the title Pangeran Ratu
Jayadiningrat. Even before he held the throne himself, the younger
prince chafed at what the 1833 and 1835 agreements had actually
cost his family: without his uncle's knowledge, he opened an arms deal
with an American trading mission in Singapore, a move the Dutch
authorities in Batavia learned of and answered by sending their own
Assistant Resident to Muara Kumpeh to remind the court of the
agreements already in force. The stipend and the protection Jambi had
purchased in 1833 had, within a decade, become a leash - and the prince
who would inherit both the throne and the resentment was already
refusing to wear it quietly.
