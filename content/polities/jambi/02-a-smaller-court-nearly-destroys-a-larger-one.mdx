---
title: A smaller court nearly destroys a larger one
drafted_from: andaya-b-1993
phase: peak
---

In 1673 Jambi did something that fits poorly into the way the
seventeenth-century strait is usually told. The standard narrative
frames the period as a contest between Asian courts and European
trading companies - Portuguese Melaka, then Dutch Melaka, monopolies
imposed from outside. Jambi's own actions that year belong to a
different story entirely: its forces sacked Johor Lama, the capital of
a Malay sultanate whose own dynasty had ruled continuously since the
fall of Melaka, and drove its court into flight.

## A quarrel with no outsider in it

The war was fought over precedence and trade between two Malay
polities of the same broad kind, with no European company involved on
either side. Johor was in the middle of what its own chroniclers would
later call a recovery from earlier troubles; Jambi's attack came in
the middle of that recovery and very nearly ended it outright. Johor's
own court fled its river capital entirely and re-established itself in
the Riau islands - a relocation that would define the geography of its
own later history.

## A rivalry the record does not resolve simply

The sack of Johor Lama stands as the clearest evidence of how far
Jambi's own prosperity, built on its command of the pepper and tin
trade passing through its river mouth, could translate into real
military reach. It did not permanently settle anything between the two
courts, any more than Jambi's parallel rivalry with Palembang ever
settled into a stable, one-sided outcome. What it demonstrates is
simply how seriously a Sumatran river sultanate, wealthy enough and
determined enough, could threaten a Malay peninsula rival many times
its apparent size.
