By the middle of the nineteenth century, Jambi's own resistance to
Dutch control had a name attached to it that would come to define the
sultanate's final decades: Sultan Thaha Syaifuddin, who took an
openly uncooperative stance toward the colonial government and
refused to accept the terms the Dutch pressed on Jambi's own rulers.
His resistance did not begin as an organized military campaign. In its
earliest phase it took the same shape resistance had long taken across
much of the archipelago - localized, sporadic, and only gradually
hardening into something more coordinated.

## A capital moved to fight from

Rather than remain in a position the Dutch could pressure directly,
Sultan Thaha eventually withdrew to Muara Tembesi, further up the
Batang Hari, to organize his own strategy and tactics of resistance
away from the reach of Dutch forces at the coast. The move did not
cost him his own authority. He continued to command real loyalty and
respect even after relocating, evidence that his standing among his
own people rested on more than simple control of the traditional
capital.

## A death that closed the sultanate's own history

The resistance ended not in negotiation but in violence. On 21 April
1904, at a place called Bedaro, Sultan Thaha Syaifuddin was killed
still pursuing the same goal that had defined his reign - driving the
Dutch out of Jambi altogether. His death effectively closed nearly
three centuries of sultanate history that had begun with Sultan Abdul
Kahar's own coronation in 1615: a kingdom that had opened its
relationship with the Dutch through trade contracts and courtly
diplomacy ended it with its own ruler dying in armed resistance rather
than accepting the colonial order that had, by degrees, closed in
around it.
