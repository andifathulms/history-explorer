Caesar led a single legion across the Rubicon, the small stream marking
the boundary between his own province and Italy proper, in January 49 BC -
a deliberate act of war against his own state, since a general was
forbidden to bring troops into Italy under arms. Plutarch has him
hesitating at the river and then quoting a Greek proverb about the die
being cast before he crossed; whether or not the words are authentic, the
act itself was unambiguous.

## The war against Pompey

Pompey and most of the senate, judging Rome itself indefensible against
Caesar's veterans, abandoned the city rather than fight for it, gathering
their own forces in Greece instead. Caesar, moving with the speed that had
made his Gallic campaigns effective, secured Italy and Spain first and then
crossed to face Pompey directly. The decisive battle came at Pharsalus in
Greece in 48 BC, where Caesar's smaller, more experienced army broke
Pompey's larger one built substantially from newer recruits. Pompey fled to
Egypt, expecting shelter from a kingdom his own earlier eastern settlement
had helped stabilise, and was murdered on the beach as he landed, on the
orders of the Egyptian court, who hoped the killing would please Caesar
when he arrived in pursuit. Caesar, by the ancient accounts, wept at the
sight of Pompey's head.

## Dictator

Caesar spent the following three years mopping up the remaining
Pompeian resistance across the Mediterranean - in Egypt, where he became
involved with Cleopatra and helped secure her hold on the Egyptian throne;
in North Africa; in Spain - while accumulating, in Rome itself, a series of
extraordinary honours and powers the constitution had no real precedent
for. He was made dictator repeatedly, and in February 44 BC accepted the
title dictator perpetuo, dictator for life, formally abolishing the
office's traditional time limit altogether.

He used the position for a genuine programme of reform: a reorganised
calendar, closely resembling the one still in use today with periodic
adjustment; new colonies founded to resettle veterans and the urban poor;
citizenship extended to more of Rome's provincial subjects; debt relief
for citizens in genuine distress, though less sweeping than some of his own
supporters had hoped for. None of it addressed the question that mattered
most to the senators who had once been his peers: whether Rome was still,
in any meaningful sense, a republic with two consuls and no permanent ruler.

## The killing

A conspiracy of more than sixty senators, led by Marcus Junius Brutus - who
some ancient sources claimed, probably wrongly, descended from the Brutus
who had expelled the kings four and a half centuries earlier, a connection
the conspirators themselves seem to have found rhetorically useful whether
or not it was genuine - and Gaius Cassius Longinus, stabbed Caesar to death
at a senate meeting on the Ides of March, 44 BC. Plutarch records that
Caesar, seeing Brutus among his attackers, said nothing further and simply
covered his face.

The conspirators had a plan for the killing and almost none for what
followed it. They had assumed, by most ancient accounts, that removing one
man would restore the republic's ordinary operation more or less
automatically. Mark Antony, Caesar's own consular colleague, delivered a
funeral oration that turned the Roman crowd against the assassins within
days, and the conspirators - who had taken no measures to secure the city,
arrest Caesar's supporters, or control his veteran soldiers - fled Rome
within weeks. Caesar's eighteen-year-old great-nephew and adopted heir,
Gaius Octavius, arrived in Italy from abroad almost immediately afterward
to claim his inheritance, and the republic the conspirators had killed a
dictator to save had, within a year, three more men fighting over exactly
the position they had killed him for holding.
