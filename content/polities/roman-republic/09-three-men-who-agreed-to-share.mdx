Sulla's reforms had strengthened the senate against individual popular
tribunes. They had done nothing to stop three ambitious men from simply
agreeing among themselves to override the senate together, which is what
happened in 60 BC.

## The arrangement

Gnaeus Pompeius, called Pompey, had won a string of major military
commands - against pirates infesting the Mediterranean, against
Mithridates of Pontus, in the settlement of the whole eastern
Mediterranean afterward - and returned to Rome expecting the senate to
ratify his arrangements and grant land to his veterans. It refused,
suspicious of a general who had shown he could raise armies the senate did
not fully control. Marcus Licinius Crassus, the wealthiest man in Rome,
wanted political influence to match his money. Gaius Julius Caesar, a
patrician with plebeian sympathies and heavy debts, wanted a major military
command of his own and the wealth and prestige that came with one.

The three men - none of whom held any formal joint office, since nothing
in the constitution provided for one - agreed privately to pool their
influence: Pompey and Crassus would use their standing to get Caesar
elected consul for 59, and all three would support each other's
subsequent interests against the senate. It worked. Caesar, as consul,
pushed through Pompey's eastern settlements and land grants for his
veterans over senatorial objection, and secured for himself a five-year
military command in Gaul - later extended to ten - that no ordinary
consular term would have granted him.

## Gaul

Caesar used the command to conquer the whole of Gaul, roughly modern France
and Belgium, in a series of campaigns between 58 and 50 BC that his own
published commentaries on the war presented, deliberately, as a defensive
response to threats against Rome's existing allies - a framing later
readers have found considerably more convenient for Caesar than the actual
sequence of events supports. He crossed briefly into Britain twice and
built a bridge across the Rhine to raid Germanic territory beyond it,
neither campaign producing lasting conquest but both producing exactly the
kind of dramatic report that kept his name prominent in Rome while he was
away from it.

The conquest also gave him what Pompey and Sulla before him had both had:
an army of many battle-hardened legions personally loyal to him, funded in
large part by Gallic plunder, and a fortune to match it.

## What broke the arrangement

Crassus, seeking a military reputation to match his wealth, led an invasion
of the Parthian empire in 53 BC and was killed along with most of his army
at Carrhae, removing the third leg of the arrangement and leaving Pompey
and Caesar as two men of comparable military standing with no remaining
reason to cooperate. Pompey, increasingly courted by a senate that had
come to see him as the lesser danger, drifted toward its side; Caesar's
Gallic command, and the immunity from prosecution it gave him, was due to
expire, and his political enemies in Rome made clear they intended to
prosecute him for actions taken during his own consulship the moment he
became a private citizen again.

In January 49 BC the senate declared Caesar a public enemy unless he
disbanded his army by a fixed date. Caesar chose the alternative, and what
he did next is the chapter that follows.
