Rome's own tradition gave it seven kings, from Romulus, a supposed founder
suckled by a wolf, to Tarquin the Proud, driven out in 509 BC. Livy, writing
under Augustus more than four and a half centuries later, says plainly in his
own preface that events before the city's founding and immediately after it
are better suited to poetry than to reliable history, and that he will not
undertake to guarantee them. What follows here keeps that distinction: the
kings are legend the Romans told about themselves, not a documented
government.

## What the legend was for

Whatever the historical kernel, the tradition gave later Romans something to
define themselves against. Each king added a fixed institution to the city
in turn - Numa the religious calendar and priesthoods, Servius Tullius the
census and the military classes it organised citizens into - so that by the
time the tradition reaches its final king, Rome already has, in outline, the
machinery a republic without a king would go on using: an assembly organised
by property class, a priesthood, a senate of elders advising the ruler.

The last king is where the legend turns openly moralising. Tarquin the Proud
is remembered as a tyrant who ruled without consulting the senate and put
opponents to death without trial - the charge sheet, not coincidentally,
against exactly the kind of one-man rule the Romans spent the next five
centuries organising their state to prevent.

## Lucretia

The story the tradition gives for the actual expulsion is specific rather
than general. Tarquin's son raped a Roman noblewoman, Lucretia, who told her
husband and father what had happened, made them swear to avenge it, and
killed herself rather than live with the disgrace. Her body was carried into
the Forum. Lucius Junius Brutus, one of the men who had sworn the oath,
pulled the knife from her wound and swore on it that he would drive out the
whole house of Tarquin by every means in his power, and called on Rome to
join him.

It worked. The king and his family fled the city, and the Romans - according
to Livy, in the same year - voted to abolish the monarchy and replace it with
two annually elected magistrates, holding equal power, each able to block
the other's actions. Brutus was one of the first two.

## What the story is actually evidence of

Whether Lucretia existed, and whether the mechanics happened in a single
year rather than across a longer and messier transition modern archaeology
would find harder to date so precisely, is not something the surviving
record can settle. What can be said with more confidence is what the Romans
themselves chose to remember about their own founding moment: not a
constitution debated and drafted, but a single act of intolerable abuse by a
king's household, answered by an oath and a public killing that made
monarchy itself the thing to be ashamed of.

Every later Roman who accused a rival of aiming at kingship - and it
happened repeatedly, across five centuries, to men who had done nothing
resembling what Tarquin's son was said to have done - was invoking exactly
this story. The republic's whole political vocabulary of suspicion toward
concentrated personal power starts here, whether or not the woman at the
centre of it ever lived.
