Rome's conquests had produced a problem its own institutions had no settled
answer for: what to do with the enormous quantity of land - the ager
publicus, public land technically owned by the Roman state - that
conquered enemies had forfeited, most of it in Italy itself.

## What had actually happened to it

In principle the state assigned public land in small parcels to citizens or
leased it out at a nominal rent. In practice, over generations, wealthy
families had occupied vastly more of it than the legal limit permitted,
farming it with slave labour - itself supplied in growing numbers by the
same wars that had produced the land - rather than the free peasant
smallholders whose military service the republic's whole army system
depended on. Plutarch's account, drawing on now-lost earlier sources, has
Tiberius Gracchus travelling through Etruria on his way to a military
post and being struck by the absence of free farmers working the land at
all, and the presence instead of imported slave labour gangs.

## Tiberius

Elected tribune for 133 BC, Tiberius Gracchus proposed enforcing the
existing legal limit on public land holdings and redistributing the excess
in small parcels to landless citizens - not a new law so much as
enforcement of an old one the wealthy had been ignoring for generations. It
was popular with the citizens it would benefit and understood immediately
by the senate as an attack on property its own members held, however
irregularly.

When a fellow tribune, acting - by the more sympathetic ancient
accounts - at senatorial instigation, vetoed the bill using exactly the
constitutional power the tribunate had been created to exercise, Tiberius
did something no tribune had done before: he had the assembly vote to
depose his colleague from office mid-term, on the argument that a tribune
who worked against the people's own interest had forfeited the office's
protection. The bill passed. It was a procedural innovation with no clear
precedent either sanctioning or forbidding it, and it convinced a
significant part of the senate that Tiberius intended to rule by the
assembly's votes rather than by the constitution's ordinary checks.

## The killing

Tiberius stood for a second consecutive year as tribune, itself widely
read as a further break with custom, campaigning on rumours - Plutarch is
explicit that the truth of them was never established - that he intended
still more sweeping changes. On the day of the election, a group of
senators led by Tiberius's own cousin, convinced the sitting consul would
not act against him, armed themselves with clubs and broken furniture,
marched into the assembly, and beat Tiberius and some three hundred of his
supporters to death. Their bodies were thrown into the Tiber rather than
given proper burial.

## What actually changed

The land question itself was not resolved by the killing - Tiberius's own
younger brother Gaius, elected tribune a decade later in 123, revived and
extended the programme along with a wider set of reforms before he too was
killed in renewed political violence in 121, the first time the senate
authorised the consuls to use whatever force was necessary against a
citizen, a precedent invoked repeatedly in the civil wars that followed.

What mattered more than either brother's specific programme was the
precedent the first killing set. Rome had fought wars, endured betrayals
and survived Hannibal without its own political class killing each other
in the Forum over a domestic policy dispute. After 133 it did that
routinely, for the rest of the republic's life, and every subsequent
generation of Roman politicians who reached for violence against a
domestic rival could point to precisely this moment as the one that had
made it thinkable.
