Octavian spent the six years after Actium consolidating a position no
Roman had ever held without a formal title to match it: sole command of
every legion that mattered, sole control of Egypt's grain, and a Roman
public exhausted enough by twenty years of intermittent civil war to want
almost anything that promised it would stop.

What he did with that position - the powers voted to him individually and
by name, a bronze copy of a near-identical later grant to one of his
successors that survives to prove the form was maintained rather than
merely claimed once, the senate meeting and the consuls being elected for
three more centuries afterward - is the story of what the settlement
built, and it belongs with the empire that grew out of it rather than with
the republic it replaced.

## What ended here

What can be said from the republic's own side, rather than the empire's,
is narrower and worth stating plainly. In January 27 BC Octavian formally
resigned the extraordinary, effectively unlimited powers the civil wars
had left concentrated in his hands, and the senate, in response, voted him
a specific, itemised set of ordinary constitutional powers instead, along
with the name Augustus. No law declared the republic abolished. No office
called king, or dictator for life, was created or claimed.

And none of it changed the fact underneath the form. The senate voted what
Octavian's own army made it prudent to vote. The consulship, elected
annually for four hundred and eighty-two years by citizens choosing between
genuinely competing candidates, continued to be filled - by men Octavian's
own influence determined, in practice, whenever it mattered to him which
men they were. The republic's institutions did not stop existing in 27.
They stopped being the place where the Roman state's actual decisions got
made, which is a different and quieter kind of ending than a conquest or a
collapse, and is exactly why the date is hard to defend as clean: nothing
visible broke on the day it happened.

## What four hundred and eighty-two years had actually produced

By any reasonable accounting the republic that ended this way was, by
27 BC, unrecognisable as the state a Latin League of client farmers and
merchants had governed through annual elections in the fifth century BC.
It had gone from a single city defending itself against its immediate
neighbours to the government of the entire Mediterranean basin, using
institutions built for a city-state and never redesigned for an empire -
which is, on the reading several of the preceding chapters have set out,
close to the whole explanation for why it could not survive its own
success. A system of checks built to stop one man from ruling alone
worked, with real interruptions, for most of five centuries. It failed,
in the end, not because an enemy defeated it, but because the wars that
made Rome dangerous to everyone else eventually produced generals more
powerful than the state that had sent them out to fight.
