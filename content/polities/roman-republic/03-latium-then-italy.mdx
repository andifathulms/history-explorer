While the plebeians and patricians were negotiating their own arrangement
inside the city, Rome was becoming the dominant power in the country
around it, and the two processes ran on the same timetable for a reason:
a city that needed its citizen-soldiers to keep winning wars could not
afford to let a third of them sit out a campaign in protest indefinitely.

## The neighbours

Rome spent the fifth and early fourth centuries fighting the other Latin
cities of the plain around it, the Etruscan city of Veii to the north, and
assorted hill peoples to the east and south, in a pattern common to every
city-state in the region: alliances, betrayals, and slow accumulation of
territory measured in individual towns rather than provinces. Veii fell
after what the tradition remembers, almost certainly with exaggeration, as
a ten-year siege. A Gallic raid sacked Rome itself around 390, a
humiliation the Romans never fully stopped referring to, and the city
rebuilt and kept expanding regardless.

## The Samnite Wars and the method that mattered more than any battle

The decisive conflicts were three wars against the Samnites, a confederation
of hill peoples controlling the mountainous centre of the peninsula, fought
intermittently between 343 and 290 BC. Rome lost badly at least once - an
entire army was trapped and forced to pass under a symbolic yoke at the
Caudine Forks in 321, a humiliation on the scale of the Gallic sack - and
still won the war.

<Figure id="paestum-warriors" />

What made the outcome durable was not the fighting but what Rome did with
each victory. Rather than annexing defeated peoples outright and ruling
them as subjects, Rome bound them as allies - socii - obliged to supply
troops for Rome's wars and forbidden an independent foreign policy, but
left with their own local government, their own laws, and in many cases
eventual paths to Roman citizenship itself. A defeated people became part
of the war machine that had defeated them rather than a resentful subject
population waiting for the chance to break away. By the time of the wars
against Pyrrhus of Epirus in the 280s and 270s - a Greek king whose
victories over Roman armies cost him so many men that "another such
victory and we are lost" became his own reported verdict on winning - Rome
could replace its losses from a pool of Italian allies no single defeat
could exhaust.

## What "all Italy" meant by 264

By the time Rome went to war with Carthage in 264 BC, it controlled,
directly or through the alliance system, the whole Italian peninsula south
of the Po valley - not as a single administered territory, but as a
network of scores of individual treaties, each specifying exactly what
each community owed in soldiers and owed nothing else. No other Iron Age
Mediterranean state had assembled manpower this way, on this scale, and it
is the reason Rome could absorb catastrophic defeats in the following
century and keep fielding armies when a state that ruled through direct
annexation and a single citizen body would have run out of men.
