The reform that did the most long-term damage to the republic's stability
was not aimed at the constitution at all. It was a practical fix for a
recruitment shortage, made by a general who needed soldiers and found the
old property-qualified levy could no longer supply enough of them.

## Marius opens the legions

Gaius Marius, elected consul an unprecedented seven times between 107 and
86 BC, responded to a war in North Africa and a looming threat from
migrating Germanic peoples by recruiting from the landless poor - men who
did not meet the old census property threshold and had therefore never
been eligible for legionary service - promising them pay, equipment
supplied by the state rather than themselves, and a share of land or
plunder on discharge.

It solved the manpower problem immediately and permanently changed what a
Roman soldier's loyalty rested on. A property-owning citizen levy of the
old kind had a farm to go home to regardless of how a campaign turned out,
and its loyalty ran to the state that had called it up for the season. A
landless volunteer whose livelihood depended entirely on his general
securing him land or money at discharge had every reason to want that
general, specifically, to stay powerful enough to deliver - and no
comparable reason to prioritise the senate's own institutional interests
over his.

## Sulla marches on Rome

The consequence arrived within twenty years. Lucius Cornelius Sulla, a
former subordinate of Marius's turned rival, was assigned command of a war
against Mithridates of Pontus in 88 BC; a political manoeuvre in Rome
transferred the command to Marius instead while Sulla's own army was still
mobilised nearby. Sulla did what no Roman general had done before: he led
his legions into Rome itself, by force, against a rival Roman politician's
faction rather than a foreign enemy, and his soldiers followed him rather
than refusing to march against their own city - the clearest possible
demonstration of exactly the loyalty shift Marius's reform had created.

Marius fled and later returned when Sulla left for the eastern war,
seizing Rome in turn and killing a number of his own political enemies.
Both men, in effect, had shown the same thing was possible; only the order
differed.

## The proscriptions

Sulla returned from the eastern war in 82 BC, defeated the faction that had
controlled Rome in his absence, and had himself appointed dictator - an
emergency office the republic's own constitution provided for, traditionally
limited to six months and a specific crisis, which Sulla held with no fixed
term at all. He then published proscription lists: names of citizens
declared outlaws, whose killers could claim a reward from the victim's
confiscated property and whose property, disproportionately, ended up in
the hands of Sulla's own supporters. Thousands died, by the ancient
accounts' own uncertain counts, in a process that made political killing an
openly administered state policy rather than a mob's improvisation.

## What nobody expected

Sulla used his dictatorship to push through a substantial programme of
constitutional reform, most of it aimed at strengthening the senate against
the kind of popular tribunate that had produced the Gracchi - stripping the
tribunate of much of its legislative initiative, enlarging the senate,
formalising the cursus honorum's age requirements. Then, in 79 BC, he
resigned every power he held and retired to private life, dying the
following year of natural causes.

No later Roman with comparable power ever did the same thing, which is
itself worth noting: Sulla's retirement was treated by his contemporaries
as remarkable precisely because it was not what the logic of the previous
decade suggested a man in his position would do. What his career had
demonstrated, and what every ambitious Roman after him had now watched
happen twice, was that an army loyal to its general rather than to the
state could take Rome, kill its political opponents by the thousand, and
rewrite its constitution - and that whether the man who did it chose to
give the power back afterward was now entirely a matter of his own
character rather than anything the constitution itself could enforce.
