Rome had no written constitution in the modern sense and no single document
that set out how power was distributed. What it had was a set of offices,
assemblies and customs that had accumulated since 509, each one a response
to a specific earlier crisis, held together by a habit of deference to
precedent - the mos maiorum, the way of the ancestors - stronger than most
of the individual rules it enforced.

## The ladder

An ambitious Roman climbed a fixed sequence of elected offices, the cursus
honorum: quaestor first, handling financial administration, usually in a
province; then aedile, responsible for the city's markets, festivals and
public buildings, an office with no formal power but a well-understood use
as free advertising for a future consular campaign; then praetor, holding
judicial authority and, increasingly, provincial military commands; and
finally consul, the senior magistracy, held jointly by two men elected for
a single year. Each rung had a minimum age attached, enforced with real
inconsistency the better a candidate's family connections were, and moving
from one rung to the next required a fresh election every time - no office
carried automatic promotion to the one above it.

Two further devices were built into the design specifically to prevent any
one part of it working the way a king's power had. Collegiality meant every
major office was held by at least two men simultaneously, each with power
to block the other. Annuality meant every term lasted exactly one year, with
a customary though not always observed expectation of a ten-year gap before
repeating a magistracy. A man who wanted to matter in Roman politics for
longer than a year had to keep winning elections, which meant keeping the
electorate persuaded - by military success, by public generosity, by
oratory, or by all three at once.

## The senate

The senate itself held no formal legislative power under most readings of
the constitution: it could not pass binding law. What it held instead was
auctoritas - a Latin word usually translated as authority but closer in
sense to accumulated institutional weight - expressed through formal
advice, the senatus consultum, that no magistrate in practice felt free to
ignore. Roughly three hundred men, mostly former magistrates who held their
seats for life once enrolled, met to debate foreign policy, finance, and
any matter a sitting magistrate chose to bring before them. It was not
elected and not accountable to the citizen assemblies in any direct sense,
and for most of the republic's life it ran the state anyway, because the
men who held the elected offices were, with rare exceptions, the same men
who sat in it.

## The assemblies

Formal legislative power, and the election of magistrates, belonged to the
citizen assemblies - several different bodies, organised on different
principles (by property class in one, by geographic voting unit in
another), each with its own jurisdiction over which kinds of law and which
elections it handled. In practice, attendance required a citizen to be
physically present in Rome on the day of the vote, which meant that
citizens living at a distance, farming their own land or serving in the
army, were structurally underrepresented relative to the urban population
and, disproportionately, relative to the wealthy who could afford to keep a
household in the city. A system that was formally popular in the fullest
sense - every free adult male citizen had a vote - was informally skewed
toward whoever could get their own supporters into the Forum on the right
day.

## What held it together, and what it assumed

None of this worked by force. There was no police force and no standing
army permitted inside the city itself for most of the republic's life.
What kept two consuls from simply refusing to leave office, or a tribune
from ignoring an unfavourable vote, was the expectation that everyone
else - the senate, the other magistrates, the electorate the next time a
candidacy came up - would treat the man who broke the rules as having put
himself outside the system that gave him standing in the first place. It
worked for a very long time because ambitious Romans kept finding it more
useful to win inside the rules than to break them.

The chapters that follow are substantially the story of what happened once
that stopped being true.
