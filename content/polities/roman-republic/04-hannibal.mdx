Polybius, an Achaean statesman deported to Rome as a hostage after his own
country's defeat, wrote his history explicitly to explain to a Greek
audience how Rome had conquered the known world in under a century - and
he treats the war against Hannibal as the hinge the whole story turns on.

## The first war, briefly

Rome and Carthage fought their first war, from 264 to 241 BC, over Sicily,
and Rome won it at sea - a land power that had never before maintained a
serious fleet built one, lost it repeatedly to storms and battle, rebuilt
it each time, and eventually forced Carthage to accept peace on Roman
terms. Carthage kept its African base and lost Sicily, its wealthiest
overseas territory, along with an indemnity it spent years paying.

## The second war

Hannibal, a Carthaginian general who had grown up in Spain under his
father's command and inherited both the army and, by his own later
account, an oath sworn as a boy never to be a friend to Rome, invaded Italy
directly in 218 BC by marching an army - including a contingent of war
elephants that mostly did not survive the crossing - over the Alps in
autumn, a route no army was expected to attempt.

He then destroyed three Roman armies in succession. At Trasimene in 217 he
ambushed a consular army along a lake shore in fog and annihilated it. At
Cannae in 216 he encircled and destroyed an army of a scale Rome had never
before committed to a single battle - Polybius's own casualty figures, and
those of other ancient sources, disagree on the exact count but agree that
it was the worst single defeat Roman arms ever suffered, killing a
meaningful fraction of the republic's entire adult male citizen body in a
single afternoon.

## What Rome did instead of negotiating

Polybius's own argument is that this is the moment the Roman system proved
what it was for. A Greek city that had just lost an army of that size to
an enemy loose in its own countryside would, in his judgment, have sued for
peace. Rome refused even to discuss it. The senate met the survivors at the
gates and thanked them for not despairing of the republic. New legions were
raised from citizens too young or too old for normal service, from freed
slaves, from whatever manpower the Italian alliance network - battered but
still substantially intact - could still supply.

Fabius Maximus, a dictator appointed after Trasimene, adopted a strategy of
avoiding pitched battle with Hannibal entirely, shadowing his army and
cutting his supplies while refusing the one kind of fight Hannibal kept
winning - a policy unpopular enough at the time that it earned him the
nickname Cunctator, the Delayer, meant as an insult and preserved as a
compliment. Hannibal spent the next thirteen years marching around Italy,
winning battles, and watching the Italian alliance system refuse to break:
some allied cities defected to him, most did not, and without a fleet
capable of resupplying him from Carthage or reinforcing him at the scale he
needed, he could win every battle he fought and still not win the war.

## Zama

Rome took the war to Carthage's own territory instead. Publius Cornelius
Scipio, who had survived Cannae as a young officer, invaded North Africa in
204 and forced Hannibal's recall from Italy for the first time in fifteen
years. At Zama in 202, Scipio - reinforced by Numidian cavalry that had
switched sides - defeated Hannibal decisively on ground of his own choosing
for once. Carthage sued for peace on terms that stripped it of its fleet,
its war elephants, and its independent foreign policy, and Scipio returned
to Rome with the name Africanus.

Polybius's own verdict, writing two generations later with access to
Carthaginian as well as Roman accounts, is that the war was decided less by
any single battle than by the structural fact underneath all of them: Rome
could lose army after army and field another, and Carthage, for all
Hannibal's own genius, never found a way to make that stop being true.
