The republic's institutions did not survive it. Its vocabulary, and a
theory of why its constitution had once worked, both did - and both
travelled much further than the state that produced them.

## Polybius's own theory

Polybius, writing while the republic he was analysing was still winning
its wars rather than losing its constitution, offered a specific
explanation for Roman success that later readers found more durable than
the republic itself: Rome's government was not a pure monarchy,
aristocracy or democracy but a deliberate mixture of all three, with the
consuls supplying the monarchic element, the senate the aristocratic, and
the citizen assemblies the democratic - each checking the others, so that
none could turn its own advantage into unaccountable power. Whether Rome's
actual founders had designed the system this deliberately is doubtful, and
Polybius's own account of the constitution's late stability did not survive
even one more century intact. The theory itself, though, entered Western
political thought as a template for how a mixed constitution could be
built on purpose rather than discovered by accident.

## Italian city republics

More than a thousand years later, the merchant republics of medieval and
early modern Italy - Venice, Florence, Genoa, and others - built political
institutions explicitly self-described in Roman terms: senates, consuls,
tribunes, doges whose title itself descends from the Latin dux. None of
them was a literal continuation of Rome's own institutions, and none
claimed to be; what they borrowed was a vocabulary and a set of structural
ideas - annual or limited terms, divided executive power, a council
recruited from a propertied elite - that Roman precedent made available to
draw on, and that centuries of surviving Roman texts kept in continuous
circulation for anyone who wanted to.

## The American founders

The clearest and most consequential later use of the republic's own
vocabulary is the constitutional design of the United States in the 1780s.

The upper house of its legislature is called the Senate. Its founders,
debating the new constitution in essays later collected as the Federalist
Papers, wrote under Roman pseudonyms - Publius, the name shared by the
Brutus who expelled the kings - and argued explicitly from Roman precedent
about the dangers of concentrated power, the value of checks between
separate branches of government, and the fate of a republic that let
military commanders accumulate personal followings loyal to themselves
rather than to the state. They had, in Caesar and Sulla, two documented
historical cases of exactly the danger they were trying to design against,
preserved in Latin texts educated Americans of the period read as a matter
of course.

## What was actually transmitted

None of these later republics inherited Rome's own citizens, territory or
continuous institutions - the empire that grew out of the settlement of 27
BC did that. What travelled instead, across two thousand years and from
Italian city-states
to a republic founded on a different continent, was a vocabulary for
describing shared power and a set of specific, well-documented failures -
Tiberius Gracchus, Marius's army, Sulla's proscriptions, Caesar's
Rubicon - that every later constitution-maker who studied Rome could point
to and try to design against. Most of them failed to design against it
completely. All of them tried, using words the Roman republic had coined
for exactly that purpose and then, in the end, could not save itself with.
