Where the first arrangement between Pompey, Crassus and Caesar had been a
private understanding with no legal standing, the second was voted into
formal existence. In 43 BC a law gave Octavian, Mark Antony and Marcus
Aemilius Lepidus joint dictatorial power for five years, with the explicit
authority to make law, appoint magistrates and put citizens to death
without trial - a constitutional recognition of the same kind of personal
military dominance that had previously operated outside the law entirely.

## Proscriptions again

The triumvirs published proscription lists on the same model Sulla had
used two generations earlier, and used them, among other purposes, to
remove personal enemies and to fund their own armies through confiscated
property. Cicero, Rome's most prominent orator and a persistent public
critic of Antony, was among those killed; Antony, by several ancient
accounts, had his head and hands displayed on the speaker's platform in
the Forum where Cicero had delivered his own speeches against him.

## Philippi

Caesar's assassins, Brutus and Cassius, had spent the two years since the
killing raising an army in the east from the provinces they controlled.
The triumvirs' combined forces met them at Philippi in Macedonia in 42 BC
in two engagements; Cassius, misreading the outcome of the first, killed
himself, and Brutus, defeated in the second, did the same rather than be
captured. The last organised resistance in Caesar's name to Caesar's own
killers was over, and the triumvirs divided the Roman world between them:
Antony took the wealthy eastern provinces, Octavian the west and Italy,
and Lepidus, steadily marginalised by the other two, was left with a
diminishing role that ended in effective retirement within a few years.

## Antony and Cleopatra

Antony's eastern command brought him into a political and personal
partnership with Cleopatra VII of Egypt, whose kingdom's wealth and grain
supply made her the most valuable ally available to a Roman general
planning further campaigns against Parthia. The relationship, and Antony's
increasingly open treatment of Alexandria as a second capital - distributing
eastern territories to Cleopatra and the children he had with her in a
ceremony called the Donations of Alexandria - gave Octavian, back in Rome,
exactly the material his own propaganda needed: not a war against a fellow
Roman, which the recent civil wars had made deeply unpopular, but a war
against a foreign queen who had, in Octavian's telling, seduced a Roman
general away from his own state's interests.

## Actium

The senate, worked by Octavian's own campaign, declared war on Cleopatra
alone rather than on Antony, allowing Octavian to present the conflict as
foreign rather than civil even though every soldier on both sides knew
which Roman it was actually fought against. The fleets met off the western
coast of Greece at Actium in September 31 BC; Antony and Cleopatra's ships
broke off the engagement and fled south, followed by a substantial part of
Antony's own fleet, and his remaining land forces surrendered without
further fighting within days.

Both Antony and Cleopatra were dead within a year, by their own hands, as
Octavian's forces closed on Alexandria - Octavian had Caesarion, Caesar's
son by Cleopatra and the one rival with a plausible blood claim to Caesar's
own name, hunted down and executed separately, on the reported grounds
that too many Caesars was one too many. Octavian was now the last man
holding an army that mattered anywhere in the Roman world, twenty years
almost to the month since Caesar's assassination had left three men
fighting over the position one dead dictator had held alone.
