Rome spent the six decades after Zama fighting the Hellenistic kingdoms of
the eastern Mediterranean, and finished the process in 146 BC by destroying
two of the Mediterranean's oldest and wealthiest cities within months of
each other, on opposite ends of the sea, for two different reasons that
converged on the same outcome.

## Macedon and the Greek cities

Rome fought three wars against Macedon between 214 and 168 BC, the last of
which ended at Pydna with a Roman legion breaking a Macedonian phalanx in
open battle, a genuine turning point in how heavy infantry was used in the
ancient Mediterranean. Macedon's monarchy was abolished outright and
the kingdom broken into four client republics forbidden to trade or
intermarry across their new borders.

The Greek cities further south, nominally free after Rome's earlier
declarations that it fought to liberate Greece from Macedonian rule, found
that liberation had limits. The Achaean League, a federation of Greek
cities that had at various points been a Roman ally, rose against Roman
authority in 146 over a dispute originating in Sparta's own status within
the league. Rome crushed the revolt in a single campaign and destroyed
Corinth, one of the wealthiest and best-positioned trading cities in
Greece, as an example: the male population was killed or enslaved, the
city's famous art was shipped to Rome, and the site was left deliberately
unoccupied for a century.

## The third Punic War

In the same year, on the other side of the Mediterranean, Rome finished
what Zama had left unfinished. Carthage had rebuilt itself as a prosperous
trading city under the terms of the peace of 201, without its former
empire or its fleet, and a faction in the Roman senate - Cato the Elder is
remembered for ending every speech on any subject with the same sentence,
that Carthage must be destroyed - argued for decades that a wealthy
Carthage was a Carthage that would eventually threaten Rome again, treaty
terms or not.

Rome manufactured a pretext, besieged the city for three years, and razed
it in 146 after fighting through its streets house by house. The
population that survived the siege was sold into slavery and the
territory was organised as the new province of Africa.

## What the same year means

Appian, who structured his own history of Rome by writing a continuous
narrative for each conquered people rather than a single year-by-year
account, makes the comparison explicit: two of the oldest and richest
cities on the Mediterranean, one Greek and one Punic, destroyed within
months of each other by the same power, for grievances that in neither
case required annihilation as the remedy. Rome had spent two and a half
centuries fighting wars it could plausibly present as defensive or
retaliatory. By 146 it no longer needed the pretence to be convincing. No
Mediterranean power remained that could contest Rome for anything, and the
generation that grew up after 146 inherited a republic with no serious
external rival left to organise its politics around - which the following
chapters treat as part of the explanation for what that generation did to
itself instead.
