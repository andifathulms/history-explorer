Abolishing the monarchy did not make Rome's citizens equal. It left a
hereditary aristocracy, the patricians, holding the senate, the priesthoods
and the new consulship between them, and everyone else - the plebeians,
the great majority of the citizen body - excluded from all three. The two
centuries after 509 are substantially the story of the second group forcing
its way into the first's institutions, by a method Rome used nowhere else in
its history: withdrawing its labour and its soldiers rather than fighting
in the streets.

## The first secession

In 494, according to the tradition, the plebeians who made up most of the
army simply walked out of the city and camped on a hill outside it,
refusing to return until their grievances - chiefly debt bondage, which
could turn a citizen who owed money into another citizen's unfree labourer
- were addressed. A city that depended on those men to fight its wars could
not function without them, and the senate negotiated.

What came out of it was a new office found nowhere else in the ancient
world in quite this form: the tribune of the plebs, a magistrate elected by
the plebeians alone, sacrosanct - anyone who harmed a tribune was
accursed and could be killed without penalty - and holding the power to
veto any act of any other magistrate, including a consul. It was a
constitutional brake built for one purpose: to let the excluded half of the
citizen body stop the included half from acting against it.

The plebeians repeated the tactic, by the later tradition's count, several
more times over the following two centuries, each secession winning a
further concession.

## The Twelve Tables

One of those concessions deserves a word here without repeating in full what
its own fuller telling, and its afterlife in the classical jurists and in
Justinian's later codification, already covers in detail. Around 451
and 450 BC, plebeian pressure produced Rome's first published law code, ten
tables of rules posted in the Forum and later extended to twelve - a fixed,
public text that ended the patrician priesthood's exclusive, unwritten
knowledge of what the law actually was. It did for Rome's own citizens what
the tribunate did for their magistrates: replaced an arrangement resting on
one group's discretion with one either side could read and cite.

## The Lex Hortensia

The conflict's formal end came in 287. A law named for the dictator
Quintus Hortensius made resolutions of the plebeian assembly binding on the
whole Roman people, patrician and plebeian alike, without requiring the
senate's prior approval - closing a loophole that had let the senate
disregard plebeian votes it disliked. By any strict reading of the
constitution, Rome was now a state in which the majority of citizens could
make binding law without the old aristocracy's consent.

## What it did and did not change

It did not abolish the aristocracy. Wealthy plebeian families had been
marrying into patrician ones and standing for the consulship since the
fourth century, and by 287 the two groups had substantially merged into a
single governing class - patrician birth remained a mark of prestige, but a
plebeian noble family's wealth and political standing were no longer
meaningfully different from a patrician one's. What the conflict produced
was not economic equality among Roman citizens, which never arrived, but a
set of institutions - the tribunate, the plebeian assembly, published law -
that stayed in place for the rest of the republic's life and that the next
generation of ambitious Romans learned to use for their own purposes rather
than for the plebeians' original ones. The seventh chapter is about what
happened when they did.
