Long treated as a matter of legend and reconstructed genealogy, the
Chalukyas' own founding came into much sharper focus in 1941, when an
inscription was discovered on a boulder in the fort at Badami itself.
It records that by Saka 465 — 543 or 544 AD — the hill fort at Vatapi
had already been fortified, above and below, by a ruler styled the
Chalukya Vallabhesvara, who performed several sacrificial rites there
including the Ashvamedha, the horse sacrifice long recognized across
India as the ritual symbol of sovereign, undisputed power. This ruler
is identified with Pulakeshin I, and the source treats him plainly as
"the real founder of the Chalukyan kingdom" — the man who established
Chalukya independence from prior Kadamba overlordship by fortifying
Vatapi and making himself master of the surrounding country.

## A capital chosen for its defenses

Vatapi's own selection as capital reflected clear strategic thinking:
the site stood on a defensible eminence within three miles of the
Malaprabha river, and its surrounding neighborhood — Mahakuta a short
distance to the east, Pattadakal and Aihole further along the same
river — would in time become studded with the dynasty's own temples
and inscriptions, physical testimony to two centuries of Chalukya rule
radiating outward from this one fortified stronghold.

## A name and a reputation that outlasted the man

Later Chalukya inscriptions, some composed centuries after his own
death, continued to recall Pulakeshin I's horse sacrifice as proof of
the dynasty's own legitimate claim to paramount sovereignty — a
foundation myth grounded not in legendary origin stories but in an
actual ritual act his own descendants kept invoking as late as the
twelfth century, long after the Chalukyas of Badami themselves had
ceased to exist.
