Having rebuilt his own inheritance, Vikramaditya I turned the same
campaign south, toward the kingdom whose invasion had cost his own
father's life a generation before.

## A camp outside the enemy's own capital

By 670-671, sixteen years into his reign, Vikramaditya had advanced a
large military camp to Malliyur, west of Kanchipuram itself, in what
a contemporary land grant describes as an active subjugation of the
Pallava dynasty already under way. A further grant, issued four years
later from a victorious camp south of the Kaveri, credits him with
having crushed three successive Pallava kings in turn - a rhetorical
flourish playing on the divine names each Pallava ruler had carried -
and with capturing Kanchi itself, "the city which was her girdle," in
language triumphant enough to leave no doubt the grant's own scribes
believed the war already won.

## A king who fled the way his own father had died

The Pallava side of the story survives too, and it does not agree.
A charter of the Pallava king Paramesvaravarman I claims that he
forced Vikramaditya's own army - "several lakhs of men," in the
grant's own exaggeration - to flee the field, the Chalukya king
himself covered by nothing more than a rag. Paramesvaravarman had
escaped the fall of his own capital, rallied forces from the rest of
his kingdom, and sent a diversionary force under the general
Siruttondar into Chalukya territory to split Vikramaditya's attention
before the decisive engagement. That battle came at Peruvalanallur,
near Trichinopoly, around April 674 - and by every later Pallava
account, it was Vikramaditya who was driven back into his own
territory, not the Pallavas who lost their capital for good.

## A war neither side's own inscriptions could end cleanly

The truth sits somewhere between the two boasts: Vikramaditya
plainly took Kanchi at some point in the campaign, and just as
plainly did not hold it, driven out by a Pallava counterattack he
had not fully anticipated. Through the years of this southern
campaign, Vikramaditya left the Chalukya homeland in the hands of his
own son, Vinayaditya - a delegation of authority that meant the
kingdom kept functioning even while its king spent years fighting an
inconclusive war on someone else's frontier, one round in a
Pallava-Chalukya rivalry that had already claimed one king's life and
would go on claiming ground back and forth for another century.
