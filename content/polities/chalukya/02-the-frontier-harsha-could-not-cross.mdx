---
title: The frontier Harsha could not cross
drafted_from: yazdani-1960
phase: peak
---

Pulakeshin II inherited a kingdom in ruins. A civil war during the
closing years of his predecessor Mangalesha's reign had brought the
Chalukya empire to the edge of collapse, and when Pulakeshin took the
throne around 609-610 the world was, in a contemporary court poet's own
words, "encompassed by the darkness of enemies." He held Vatapi and
little else, and had to rebuild Chalukya power from that single
foothold outward.

## An empire rebuilt from one fortress

Pulakeshin proved equal to the task. Within a few years he had brought
the whole of the Deccan back under Chalukya control, launched the
eastern branch of his own line into the Telugu country, and made his
own name respected as far away as the Persian court of Khusrau II. It
was against this rebuilt power that Harshavardhana, whose own conquests
had brought most of northern India under his control, turned south —
and met the one reversal his otherwise unbroken career would ever
suffer.

## A battle that fixed a frontier

Harsha's army lost heavily in the fighting, particularly in elephants,
and the Narmada river became the fixed boundary his forces could never
push past — the single check on an emperor whose campaigns elsewhere in
India met no comparable resistance. The victory, won sometime before
612, stood as the defining achievement of Pulakeshin's own reign: proof
that the kingdom he had rebuilt from a single fortress could turn back
the most powerful ruler in India.

## A debt collected by a different rival

The empire that had stopped Harsha could not stop everyone. Some three
decades later, a Pallava army under Narasimhavarman I advanced rapidly
on Vatapi itself and took the city and its fortress by force —
retribution, Pallava inscriptions make clear, for Pulakeshin's own
earlier advances that had twice threatened Kanchipuram with the same
fate. Nothing more is heard of Pulakeshin II after the fall of his own
capital, and the source treats his death in the fighting as the only
reasonable reading of that silence. The king who had stopped an
emperor at the Narmada could not, in the end, stop the revenge of a
neighbor he had humiliated twice.
