Kirttivarman II, the last Chalukya king of Badami, took the throne
around 744-745 to inherit an empire that had recovered from
Pulakeshin II's own death a century earlier but was already showing
signs of renewed strain. Feudatory chieftains bearing the Chalukya name
had spread across the Deccan by this point, some loyal, some
increasingly independent in all but title — among them a rising family
descended from one Indra, whose son Dantidurga would prove the most
dangerous of them all.

## An heir who married his way into the family he would destroy

Dantidurga's own rise combined military ambition with calculated
alliance-building. He married a Chalukya princess he had taken by
force on the battlefield at Kaira, tying his own line to the imperial
family even as he prepared to supplant it. Rather than move directly
against Kirttivarman, he spent years building strength on the margins
of Chalukya power — subduing the Gurjaras of Malwa, campaigns against
Kosala and Kalinga, and, most consequentially, marching all the way to
Kanchipuram, where he struck an alliance with the Pallava king
Nandivarman Pallavamalla by giving him his own daughter in marriage.

## An empire isolated before it was ever attacked

By the time Dantidurga turned against Kirttivarman directly, the
Chalukya king had been stripped of his outlying provinces and
diplomatic support through years of patient maneuvering rather than
open war. The source is explicit that this was no sudden catastrophe:
"a rapidly progressing attenuation of Chalukyan power" preceded
Dantidurga's final assault rather than any single decisive battle.
Dantidurga's own Samangad copper-plate grant, dated 753, already
describes the overthrow of Chalukya sovereignty as an accomplished
fact, declaring that the once-invincible Karnataka army that had
defeated the lords of Kanchi, Kerala, Chola, Pandya and Harsha himself
was "now easily routed by Dantidurga with the aid of a few followers."

## A lingering reign with nothing left to rule

Kirttivarman did not vanish immediately. Records from his own camp
show him still issuing grants in his eleventh regnal year, and his
conqueror's own successor, Krishna I, still found it worth claiming
further victories against him — evidence, the source suggests, of "a
belated and futile struggle" rather than any real continuation of
Chalukya power. That eleventh-year grant is the last trace history has
of the imperial line Pulakeshin I had founded two centuries before at
Vatapi, a dynasty that ended not in one battle but in the slow,
deliberate isolation a former subordinate had spent years arranging
before he ever moved against it.
