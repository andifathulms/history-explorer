Pulakeshin II's death in the fighting at Vatapi did not simply cost
the Chalukyas a battle - it opened thirteen years in which no one
could say for certain who actually ruled the empire he had built.

## An empire that could not agree who inherited it

Feudatories who had answered to Pulakeshin began declaring themselves
independent within months of his death, issuing land grants that made
no mention of Chalukya overlordship at all. Worse, his own sons could
not agree on a single successor. A copper-plate grant survives from
the first regnal year of one son, Adityavarman, who took the full
royal titles and claimed to have brought "the whole earth" under his
own sway; a second grant, from a different year, was issued by Queen
Vijaya-Bhattarika, ruling in the name of her late husband Chandraditya
- Adityavarman's own brother, evidently already dead by the time of
her grant, and clearly succeeded by no adult son of his own. Later
Chalukya court genealogies, confused by the whole episode, tried to
paper over it by inserting two invented generations between Pulakeshin
II and the eventual restorer.

## A fortune "concealed by three kings"

That restorer was Vikramaditya I, whose own early inscriptions
describe him as having gained back his father's "regal fortune, which
had been concealed by three kings" - Adityavarman, Chandraditya, and,
by most readings, the Pallava king Narasimhavarman I himself, whose
sudden invasion had triggered the whole crisis in the first place.
Vikramaditya did not do it alone. His maternal grandfather, the
Western Ganga king Durvinita, intervened on his behalf - later
inscriptions credit Durvinita with defeating a Pallava threat and
personally establishing his own grandson in his hereditary kingdom,
motivated as much by loyalty to his own daughter's dead husband's
line as by any formal obligation to the Chalukyas as overlords.
Vikramaditya's own war-horse, Chitrakantha, is remembered by name in
the tradition as having carried him through the battles that followed.

## A throne restored, piece by piece

Vikramaditya dated his own reign from 654-655, and spent its early
years doing exactly what his own inscriptions claimed: restoring
lapsed temple grants his predecessors had let fall away, and
recovering, district by district, territory that had slipped from
Chalukya control during the interregnum. Copper-plate grants from his
third and sixth regnal years show Chalukya authority reasserted in the
Ceded Districts and along the Nellore coast; feudatory families who
had drifted toward independence, or even toward the rival Pallavas,
were brought back or confirmed in a looser but still recognizably
Chalukya orbit. It was not a swift recovery, and it was not complete -
but within a decade of an empire that could not settle who ruled it at
all, Vikramaditya had rebuilt something recognizably continuous with
what his father had lost.
