By the end of the eighteenth century, Fulani communities had grown
large within the pagan Hausa state of Gobir, organized under their own
chiefs and religious teachers whose independent doctrines increasingly
unsettled the ruling authorities. In 1802 King Bawa of Gobir summoned
their imam, Usman dan Fodio, along with the principal Fulani chiefs,
and administered what Shaw calls a severe public reprimand for the
religious and political pretensions they had begun to display. It was,
in Shaw's own phrase, "a spark to the tinder." Indignation spread
through the Fulani community, and Usman dan Fodio fed it directly,
preaching that his own people should no longer submit to rule by a
pagan king. The Fulani chiefs raised the standard of revolt, elected
Usman their Sheikh, and declared a holy war under his leadership.

## A war whose own start date nobody could fix

Shaw is candid that even contemporaries disagreed about exactly when
the jihad began - accounts vary between 1800 and 1804 - though it
evidently broke out around the same time the Tuareg conquest of
Timbuktu was reshaping power farther west, and Usman dan Fodio's own
uprising in Hausaland ran roughly parallel to a second Fulani jihad led
by Ahmadu in Masina. Gobir itself resisted stubbornly: the province
was eventually subdued, but its capital, Alkalawa, held out for the
whole of Usman dan Fodio's own lifetime and was not taken until after
his death. Elsewhere in Hausaland, where Fulani communities were
already established inside half the towns before a shot was fired,
the conquest spread far faster - Zamfara fell in the war's first
year, and town after town across the savanna changed hands as the
jihad's own momentum built toward an empire nobody involved at the
1802 reprimand could have predicted.
