By the end of the nineteenth century Sokoto's own authority had
become mostly ceremonial rather than administrative - Shaw describes
the Sultan as the recognized religious and political head of
Hausaland, from whom every Fulani emirate not subject to Gando took
its formal investiture, even as Britain's own colonial administration
expanded steadily around it. The British High Commissioner tried
conciliation first: a friendly letter announcing the new British
administration went to Sokoto in 1900 and was never answered, its
messenger treated with open contempt; a 1901 request that Sokoto
nominate a successor to a deposed emir went unanswered too. In May
1902 Sokoto finally replied, in terms Shaw quotes in full: "I do not
consent that any one from you should ever dwell with us... Between us
and you there are no dealings, except as between Mussulmans and
Unbelievers - war as God Almighty has enjoined upon us."

## Kano falls without a real fight

War came the following year. A British force of under a thousand rank
and file left Zaria in January 1903 under Colonel Morland, and found
Hausaland's supposedly impregnable stronghold, Kano - a walled city
eleven miles in circumference, its walls forty feet thick at the
base - defended half-heartedly at best. The city's own emir had
already withdrawn to Sokoto with his army and the bulk of the ruling
family a month before the assault, leaving Kano's defense to two
trusted slaves; when the emir tried to return and retake the city, his
own brother the Wombai refused to fight and split off a large part of
the army, and the emir himself fled in disguise toward the French
frontier rather than face the British force in open battle. Kano fell
on 3 February 1903, and within three days its great market had
resumed full activity, caravans moving in and out as though the
country were at peace.

## The battle that ended a century of Fulani rule

Sokoto alone stayed defiant. Conciliatory letters to the Sultan went
unanswered, and the British column pressed westward through brutal
Harmattan heat and salt-poisoned water toward the capital. On 15
March 1903 a battle was fought outside Sokoto itself, and the
Sultan's own army was defeated and put to flight. The city that
Muhammad Bello had founded less than a century earlier as the eastern
capital of his father's jihad state passed, in a single afternoon,
under British colonial rule - the last independent seat of a religious
and political authority that had once commanded holy war across the
whole of Hausaland, undone not by a rival Muslim power or an internal
Hausa revolt, as its own history had weathered before, but by an
army its own defiant letter had explicitly, and unsuccessfully,
invited.
