Gobir itself resisted the jihad it had provoked. The province was eventually
subdued, but its own capital, Alkalawa, held out for the whole of Usman dan
Fodio's lifetime and did not fall until after his death. Everywhere else in
Hausaland the war moved far faster, because Fulani communities were already
established inside half the towns before a shot was fired: Zamfara fell in
the war's first year, and Zaria was either conquered outright or allied
itself with the conquerors within a month of Zamfara's own submission. Kano's
conquest followed shortly after; Katsina was taken in 1807; and by 1808 the
jihad's armies had been carried into Bornu itself.

## Cities that were already Muslim

The Fulani justified their war as a struggle to hold the courage of their
armies against infidels, but the central Hausa provinces they were
conquering - Katsina, Kano and Zaria - were themselves already governed on
Islamic lines, their society organised under law based on the Quran. In
Zaria the reigning dynasty was itself Fulani; in Katsina and Kano the
sovereigns were of Hausa dynasties that had been Muslim for generations. The
jihad drove large bodies of Muslim Hausa townspeople into the hills
alongside the pagan population they were nominally at war to convert, and
the Fulani themselves later adopted the existing Hausa legal, judicial and
tax systems largely unchanged once the conquest was won - a holy war whose
own conquerors ended up governing through the institutions of the people
they had just displaced.

## The limit of it

Bornu is where the westward push stopped. Muhammad al-Kanemi, a scholar
turned military leader, met the Fulani advance with a force only four
hundred strong and is said to have broken an army of eight thousand in his
first encounter with it; reinforced by the victory, he won forty battles in
ten months and drove the Fulani out of Bornu entirely, restoring - rather
than claiming for himself - a sultan of the old dynasty. It was the jihad's
first defeat by a fellow Muslim power rather than a pagan one, and Shaw's
own account is direct about what it did to the war beyond Bornu's own
border: the news of al-Kanemi's victories, arriving in Hausaland shortly
after 1808, gave new courage to the Hausa states still resisting Fulani rule
- courage that would gather, by 1816, into the confederation Sultan Bello
spent the opening years of his own reign putting down at the battle of Dagh.
