The fighting did not end with Sokoto's own fall in March 1903. The deposed
sultan fled east with a body of followers, was pursued to the frontier of
Bornu, and was killed that July in an engagement near Burmi alongside a
rebellious district chief who had joined him - the last armed resistance of
any consequence the conquest produced.

## An installation borrowed from the throne it replaced

What followed was not the dismantling of the caliphate's own institutions
but their adoption. The British High Commissioner, following the same
principle already used to reconstruct the southern emirates, turned to the
Council of Notables that had traditionally elected the Sarikin Muslimin -
Commander of the Faithful, the title the Sultan of Sokoto had always
carried - and asked them to choose between recalling the fled ruler's
predecessor or naming someone new. The installation itself was staged with
the same royal trumpets that, by custom, could only be sounded for a duly
accepted emir: troops drawn up with Maxim guns mounted, a carpet spread for
the new Sultan and his officers, a public assurance that the administration
would not interfere with Islamic worship, and the gown and turban of
office presented as the new sultan accepted British terms in front of an
assembled crowd. Shaw records that the office continued to work "in the
utmost cordiality" with its British Resident from that point on.

## What outlasted the conquest

The caliphate's own religious geography survived its political one almost
entirely intact. Sokoto's investiture of the lesser emirates - the same
chain of authority that had been quietly breaking down for two decades
before 1903 - became, under colonial indirect rule, the actual machinery
the British used to govern the north: the same councils, the same emirs,
the same religious hierarchy, now answering to a Resident instead of
functioning independently.

That machinery outlived the colonial administration that adopted it. The
Sultanate of Sokoto continues in Nigeria today, its holder recognised as
head of the country's Supreme Council for Islamic Affairs and, by long
tradition rather than any constitutional office, the spiritual authority
most Nigerian Muslims look to - a religious position now held by the
twentieth man to carry a title Usman dan Fodio's own jihad created, more
than two centuries after the reprimand at Gobir that started it.
