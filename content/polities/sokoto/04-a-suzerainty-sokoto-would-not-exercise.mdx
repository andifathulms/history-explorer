Sultan Bello died in 1837, and the caliphate his father had founded settled
into the shape it would keep for the rest of the century: a religious
authority radiating outward from Sokoto and Gando over dozens of Fulani
emirates, each governed by its own emir and Council of Notables, each taking
its own formal investiture from one of the two capitals. The empire that had
once put a hundred thousand men in the field stopped expanding. What it had
already conquered, it now administered - or, increasingly, simply presided
over.

## A company's treaty, not a government's

The first European power to reach an understanding with Sokoto was not a
government at all. In 1884 a trading concern sent Joseph Thomson to
negotiate its first treaty with the sultanate, and in 1886 that company
received a Royal Charter that made it, for international purposes, the
equivalent of a recognised government: the Royal Niger Company's own flag
carried the standing of the British flag wherever it flew, its treaties with
local rulers earned Great Britain's protection, and it was given the power
to levy taxes and raise a small native force to pay for its own political
work. Sokoto and Gando, among the more important signatories, agreed to deal
with no other European power, to give Britain jurisdiction over foreigners
in their territory, and to cede sovereign rights along a strip of the Niger
and Benue - while formally keeping their own internal rule intact.

## Emirates that stopped listening to Sokoto

That internal rule was where the empire actually came apart. As Company and
then colonial pressure grew through the 1890s, emirates further from the
centre began acting entirely on their own account. The Emir of Yola ordered
the Company's traders to haul down their flag despite treaty rights
protecting them; in Bautchi the town of Guarram was destroyed and its people
carried off; Kontagora and Nupe moved to outright attack on the British
administration; other emirates raided British-protected populations for
slaves regardless of what Sokoto or Gando had signed. Sokoto's own response
to this fragmentation was not to reassert control but to withdraw from the
question entirely - refusing, again and again, to exercise its own
traditional right of nominating successors when the British deposed an emir
who had defied them, leaving every one of those southern emirates to take
its investiture from the colonial administration instead, by conquest
rather than by the old religious chain of authority. The high command that
had once bound Hausaland's Fulani rulers to a single centre was, emirate by
emirate, ceasing to function years before Sokoto itself was ever attacked.
