Usman dan Fodio died in 1816, dividing a conquest still not fully
secured between two heirs: his son Muhammad Bello, who had already
founded the city of Sokoto for himself during his father's lifetime,
received the eastern provinces; his brother Abdullahi, based at Gando
to the south and west, took the western half. Shaw calls the period
of Bello's own reign, which ran until 1837 and coincided closely with
the great era of Bornu under its own reforming ruler, "the great
period of Fulani rule in Haussaland." Bello inherited a war far from
finished - Kano, Katsina and Zaria had already fallen to his father's
armies, but the western provinces, including Gobir, Zamfara and Gando
itself, remained only imperfectly subdued, and around 1816, the very
year of his own accession, a confederation of Hausa states formed to
fight the Fulani for their independence.

## The battle of Dagh

The confederation's decisive push came around 1820, reinforced by an
alliance with Moorish forces and the sympathy of Bornu's own ruler.
Bello assembled his army at Wurno, where Sheikh Omar, head of the
Fulani church, addressed the troops and enjoined them to keep their
hearts pure and commit no atrocities. The march that followed crossed
genuinely hostile terrain in the dry season - Shaw describes the
army's suffering from thirst as terrible enough that Bello halted the
column and asked Omar to seek divine guidance on whether to advance
or retreat. Omar, after a night in prayer, reported a vision of water
running underground; Bello drove a spear into the earth and ordered
his men to dig, and water rose wherever they broke ground. The army
that reached the confederated forces at the resulting battle - known
afterward as the battle of Dagh - won decisively: the king of Katsina
was killed, the king of Gobir taken prisoner, and, by the account
Shaw quotes, twenty-five thousand of the enemy were killed. Bello kept
his own soldiers under strict discipline and permitted no slaves to
be taken from the field.

## Victory that settled nothing for long

Dagh broke the confederation's momentum without ending its
resistance. The defeated states continued fighting Fulani rule
piecemeal for years afterward, and Bello spent much of the rest of
his reign reducing them one at a time - conquering Zamfara outright,
marching against a revolt in his uncle Abdullahi's own territory of
Gando while scrupulously respecting his father's division of the
realm by forbidding his own troops to enter the town itself. Even in
victory, the wars exposed an uncomfortable truth the Fulani generals
could not fully ignore: many of their opponents were themselves
practicing Muslims, and prisoners taken in these campaigns were tested
on their recitation of the Quran before being freed or sold into
slavery - a holy war, in practice, fought as often against fellow
believers as against genuine unbelievers.
