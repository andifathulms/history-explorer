By 1900 European scholarship had catalogued the temples of Java and the
inscriptions of Cambodia, and had no name at all for a six-hundred-year
maritime power in between.

It had the evidence. It just had it in three unconnected piles: Chinese records
of a kingdom called Shilifoshi that the southern trade came through; Arabic and
Indian references to a place called Zabag or Sriwijaya; and Old Malay
inscriptions from around Palembang that nobody had linked to either.

In 1918 George Cœdès, a French epigrapher, put the three together and showed
they were one polity. Paul Wheatley later called it "possibly the most
significant contribution ever made to the progress of Southeast Asian history"
— a large claim for a short paper, and not obviously an overstatement, given
that it produced an empire.

## What had actually been forgotten

The name, and only the name. It is worth being exact about this, because "a
kingdom nobody remembered" is the sort of phrase that runs away with itself.

Palembang was never forgotten. Malay court tradition went on routing its
dynasties back to it for centuries — and the most consequential of those
genealogies is the one that produced Melaka.

The founder of Melaka was a prince called Parameswara who was, on Albuquerque's
account, a native of Palembang, and who was married to a princess of Majapahit.
He revolted, probably after the death of Hayam Wuruk in 1389, and took refuge
at Tumasik — modern Singapore, and of all the peninsular possessions of the
kingdom of Palembang the one furthest from Ayutthaya's reach. He killed its
chief, reigned there a few years, was driven out by Ayutthaya, fled to Muar and
then Bertam and finally to Melaka, where a Chinese eunuch envoy found him
established in 1403.

The *Sejarah Melayu*, the Malay Annals, traces his line back through Palembang
to the kings of Srivijaya, and then onward to Alexander the Great. It was
composed a century and a quarter after the last Srivijayan ruler anyone can
name, by a court writing in exile after its own city had fallen — so it is a
claim rather than a record, and the second half of it is plainly a claim.

But the first half is a memory of something real. A man out of Palembang
founded a port on the other side of the strait that did exactly what Srivijaya
had done — sold safe passage, bound the sea peoples, took the tolls — and his
descendants said so, in those terms, on the ground that they came from the
place that had done it first.

## What the rediscovery gave back

So the sequence is stranger than either "forgotten" or "remembered" allows.

Srivijaya's institutions were carried on at Jambi, and its position was rebuilt
at Melaka by a family who knew where they had come from. What was lost was the
word: the polity's own name for itself, which survived in Chinese
transcriptions, Arabic travel accounts and Sanskrit-Malay inscriptions that
nobody had thought to read alongside each other.

For six hundred and forty-three years the largest maritime power in the history
of the region existed in the record under three different names in three
different languages, and belonged to nobody. Cœdès gave it back its own.
