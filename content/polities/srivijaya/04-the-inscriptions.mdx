Srivijaya left few monuments and no chronicle. What it left is a small set of
seventh-century inscriptions in Old Malay, and they are unusually direct about
how the polity worked.

The Telaga Batu stone carries an oath. It lists the people bound by it —
princes, commanders, judges, scribes, naval captains, merchants — and specifies
what happens to them if they conspire against the ruler: an elaborate curse,
invoked over water poured across the stone and drunk by those swearing it.
Those who stay loyal are promised prosperity.

## A state describing its own weakness

That is the political technology of a thalassocracy set out in its own words,
and what is striking is how frankly it admits the problem.

A ruler who commanded his subordinates would not need to curse them. The oath
is addressed precisely to the people who could defect and could not be
prevented from defecting — the captains and the coastal chiefs whose loyalty
was the whole apparatus of the state. Water, a stone and a supernatural penalty
are what you reach for when you have no garrison to send.

It also tells you what the ruler's authority was understood to be. Not a right
of command backed by force, but a power that could reach a man who broke his
word, anywhere, including at sea and out of sight. For a polity whose
subordinates spent most of the year beyond anybody's reach, a god who could
follow them was not a decoration on the constitution. It *was* the
constitution.

## What survives, against what it ran

Set the evidence beside the achievement.

A handful of stones near Palembang. A Chinese pilgrim's travel notes. Tribute
records kept by other people's courts, in other people's languages, for their
own purposes.

From that: six hundred years, the trade of half of Asia, and a monastic centre
that Bengali and Chinese scholars crossed oceans to study at. Srivijaya is the
clearest demonstration in the region that the quantity of surviving evidence
has almost nothing to do with the importance of what produced it.
