Srivijaya left few monuments and no chronicle. What it left is a small set of
seventh-century inscriptions in Old Malay, and they are unusually direct about
how the polity worked.

The Telaga Batu stone carries an oath. It lists the people bound by it — princes,
commanders, judges, scribes, naval captains, merchants — and specifies what
happens to them if they conspire against the ruler: an elaborate curse, invoked
over water poured across the stone and drunk by those swearing. Those who stay
loyal are promised prosperity.

That is the political technology of a thalassocracy stated in its own words. A
power based on straits cannot garrison the coastline; it has to bind the people
who could otherwise become pirates, using oath, ritual sanction and a share of
the trade. The inscriptions read as a state's honest account of its own weakness
and the arrangement built to compensate for it.

They also record Buddhist patronage, and the Chinese pilgrim Yijing stayed at
Palembang in the 670s to study Sanskrit before continuing to India, describing a
community of a thousand monks. That is the transmission counted on this page.

Everything on this page's rating panel is empty except the span. The polity is
known from a handful of stones, a Chinese pilgrim's travel notes, and tribute
records — and it organised the trade of half of Asia for six centuries.
