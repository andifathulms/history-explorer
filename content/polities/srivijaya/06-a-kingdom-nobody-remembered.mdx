In 1275 Kertanegara of Singhasari, on Java, sent an expedition across to Sumatra
— the Pamalayu — and after it the Melayu polity that had been carrying
Srivijaya's position stops appearing as an independent power.

This record's span ends there and its `ended.type` is `gradual absorption` with
an empty `by`, which is accurate and unsatisfying in the way several endings in
this corpus are. Nobody annexed anything. A position stopped being held by the
people who had held it, and eighteen years later the Javanese state that
succeeded Singhasari — this corpus's Majapahit record — was claiming the
archipelago.

## And then it was forgotten

This is the part that has no parallel anywhere else on this site.

Srivijaya was not remembered. Not in Sumatra, where later Malay court chronicles
trace their genealogies to Palembang without knowing what had been there. Not in
Java. Not in the European scholarship, which by 1900 had catalogued the temples
of Java and the inscriptions of Cambodia and had no name for a six-hundred-year
maritime power in between.

In 1918 George Cœdès, a French epigrapher, published a paper putting three sets
of evidence together: Chinese records of a kingdom called Shilifoshi that
southern trade came through, Arabic and Indian references to a place called
Zabag or Sriwijaya, and the Old Malay inscriptions from around Palembang that
nobody had connected to either.

They were the same polity. The paper is twelve pages.

## What that does to a record on this site

Everything on this page is a reconstruction less than a lifetime older than the
site it is published on, and the fields show it.

`reach_km2` is null, and the data comment says Taagepera does not tabulate a
polity whose power was over sea lanes. `succession_rule` is null.
`descendant_scripts` and `successor_claims` are null rather than zero — nobody
has coded them because there is very little to code from.

And `successor_claims` could not be anything else. Rule 1 counts a later polity
asserting an earlier one's legitimacy in its own public self-description, and no
polity between 1275 and 1918 was in a position to assert this one's, because none
of them knew the name.

## The one that got away with it

Set that against the first chapter's claim that this record is the case this
site's design notes name as the reason continuity is a section rather than a
spine.

The argument there is that a polity with no succession edge is complete and must
be treated as first-class. Srivijaya is the strongest possible version of it: a
state that ran the trade of half of Asia for six centuries, has no edge in either
direction, is coded on three of four institution fields, has no reach figure, and
was unknown to everybody including its own successors for six hundred and
forty-three years.

If this site's rules could not hold that, the rules would be wrong. They hold it,
and the page is thin in the fields and long in the chapters, which is what the
evidence supports.
