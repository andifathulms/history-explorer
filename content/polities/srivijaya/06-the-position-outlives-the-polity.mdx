Srivijaya did not fall so much as stop being the name for the thing.

The first move was internal. In 1079 the centre shifts from Palembang up the
coast to Jambi-Melayu, which had been a rival and then a dependency. Chinese
records begin naming the Melayu port where they had named the Palembang one.
That is the clearest sign of what the Chola raid had actually done: the strait
still had to be passed and somebody still had to be paid, but the somebody had
changed.

For two centuries after that there is very little. No ruler of the polity is
named in anything after the eleventh century. The ports go on trading, the
tolls go on being collected, and nobody records who is collecting them.

## 1275

Kertanegara of Singhasari, on Java, sent an expedition across to Sumatra — the
Pamalayu — and afterwards the Melayu polity that had been carrying Srivijaya's
position stops appearing as an independent power.

Nobody annexed anything. There was no Javanese administration in Sumatra, no
tribute list, no garrison; Singhasari itself was overthrown at home seventeen
years later. What ended was simply the last claim that the straits were run
from Sumatra by a Sumatran power.

Eighteen years after the Pamalayu, Majapahit was founded on Java, and for the
next three centuries the centre of gravity of the archipelago sat there. The
thing Srivijaya had been — a single authority selling safe passage through the
straits — was not reassembled in Sumatra. When it reappeared it was on the
other side of the water, at Melaka, and the man who built it came out of
Palembang.
