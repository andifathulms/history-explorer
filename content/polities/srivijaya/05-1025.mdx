Rajendra I of the Chola empire sent a fleet across the Bay of Bengal and
attacked the ports of the Melaka strait. The great inscription at Tanjavur
lists what was taken: something like fourteen places by name, Srivijaya's
capital among them, and it claims the king was captured along with a gate of
jewelled splendour.

The Cholas did not stay. There is no Chola administration in Sumatra, no
garrison, no tribute record of any length. They came, took the ports, and
sailed home.

## Why a raid mattered more than a conquest would have

Because what Srivijaya sold was not territory. It was a guarantee.

Ships paid to call because calling was safe and predictable. The crews who
could have been pirates were bound not to be. The ruler's authority was
understood as a power that reached anyone who broke his word. All three of
those things — the tolls, the oaths, the curse — depend on Srivijaya being the
arrangement that made the strait work.

A fleet that crosses an ocean, takes the capital and leaves has demonstrated in
a single season that the arrangement does not hold. Not to Srivijaya, which
recovered enough to go on sending embassies to China for another century, but
to every port that had been accepting its terms and every shipmaster deciding
where to stop.

After 1025 the Chinese records begin listing the Sumatran ports separately
rather than as dependencies of one power. That is the whole of what the raid
did, and it was sufficient.

## Why they came

Argued about. The candidates are a trade dispute, with Srivijaya obstructing
Chola merchants' direct access to China; a response to Srivijayan interference
with Chola embassies; a bid to open the strait for the Tamil merchant guilds
then operating across the Bay of Bengal; and plunder, which royal inscriptions
never give as a motive and which is usually part of one.

What is not in doubt is that relations before it had been good. Srivijaya
endowed a Buddhist temple at Nagapattinam on the Chola coast in about 1006 with
Chola royal consent, and the charter recording it survives. Nineteen years
later the same two courts were sacking each other's ports.

## Who the attackers were

The Cholas are worth knowing about, because a raid like this is otherwise hard
to credit.

They were a south Indian dynasty who held the Coromandel coast for roughly
three centuries and built the largest navy in the Indian Ocean. Their temple
architecture at Tanjavur defines a tradition; their bronze casters produced the
Shiva Nataraja figures that are among the most reproduced sculptures in the
world; their administration is documented in tens of thousands of temple
inscriptions, which is a denser record than almost anything in Southeast Asia
from the same centuries.

They were also, unusually for an Indian dynasty of the period, willing to
project force across open sea — Sri Lanka, the Maldives, and in 1025 the far
side of the Bay of Bengal. Srivijaya's misfortune was to be the richest thing
within reach of the one Indian power that had built a fleet capable of getting
there.
