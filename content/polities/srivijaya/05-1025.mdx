Rajendra I of the Chola empire sent a fleet across the Bay of Bengal and attacked
the ports of the Melaka strait. The great inscription at Tanjavur lists what was
taken: something like fourteen places by name, Srivijaya's capital among them,
and it claims the king was captured along with a gate of jewelled splendour.

The Cholas did not stay. There is no Chola administration in Sumatra, no
garrison, no tribute record of any length. They came, took the ports, and sailed
home.

## Why a raid mattered more than a conquest

Because this polity's asset was a reputation.

Every field on this record describes the same thing from a different angle.
`revenue_basis` is `trade-toll` alone: ships paid to call. `military_basis` is
`client-levy`: the crews who could have been pirates were bound not to be.
`legitimation` is `divine-sanction`: the ruler's authority was a power that
reached people who broke their word.

All three depend on Srivijaya being the arrangement that made the strait safe and
predictable. A fleet that crosses an ocean, takes the capital and leaves has
demonstrated in one season that the arrangement does not hold — not to Srivijaya,
which recovered enough to go on sending embassies to China, but to every port
that had been accepting its terms and every shipmaster choosing where to stop.

After 1025 the Chinese records begin listing the Sumatran ports separately rather
than as dependencies. That is the whole of what the raid did and it is
sufficient.

## Why they came

Argued about, and the volume this chapter is drafted from is twenty specialists
disagreeing productively.

The candidates: a trade dispute, with Srivijaya obstructing Chola merchants'
direct access to China; a response to Srivijayan interference with Chola
embassies; a bid to control the strait for the Tamil merchant guilds that were
operating across the Bay of Bengal in this period; and plunder, which royal
inscriptions never give as a motive and which is usually part of one.

What is not in doubt is that relations before it had been good. Srivijaya had
endowed a Buddhist temple at Nagapattinam on the Chola coast in about 1006 with
Chola royal consent, and the charter records it. Nineteen years later the same
kings sacked each other's ports.

## The Chola record this site does not carry

The other half of this chapter is a page that does not exist.

The Chola empire — south Indian, maritime, three centuries long, with the largest
navy in the Indian Ocean, temple architecture that defines a tradition, a bronze
sculpture school, and an administration documented in tens of thousands of temple
inscriptions — is not in this corpus. Its classical-India region carries Maurya,
Kushan, Gupta and the Indo-Greeks and stops.

So this record's most consequential turning point names a power with no page, the
way the Kilwa record's ending names Portugal and the Aksum record's ending names
nobody at all.

The difference here is that the missing polity is not outside this site's scope
by period or by continent. It is exactly the kind of thing this corpus carries,
in a region this corpus has, and it has not been written.
