Yijing, a Chinese Buddhist monk, sailed for India in 671 and stopped at
Srivijaya on the way. He stayed about six months to learn Sanskrit grammar,
went on to Nalanda for a decade, came back, and stayed at Palembang again — for
years this time — writing and translating.

His advice to Chinese monks heading for India was to stop here first and do a
year or two of preparation, because the Sanskrit was as good and the discipline
the same. He describes more than a thousand monks in residence.

## What that makes the polity

This record's `religions_carried` count is 1 and the item is Mahayana Buddhism,
and Yijing is most of why.

Coding rule 2 counts transmission rather than adherence: carrying a tradition the
polity did not originate is what the axis measures. Srivijaya did not invent
Buddhism and did not convert anybody at spearpoint. What it did was be the place
between China and India where the texts, the language and the discipline were
available, for six hundred years, on the route everybody had to take anyway.

The traffic ran both ways. Yijing came south to prepare for India. Atisha —
the eleventh-century master whose teaching reshaped Tibetan Buddhism, and whom
this corpus's Tibetan Empire record's successors were still arguing about
centuries later — is recorded as having studied for twelve years under a teacher
at Srivijaya before going to Tibet.

A Bengali monk trains in Sumatra and reforms Buddhism in the Himalayas. That is
what a polity on a trade route does that has nothing to do with trade.

## And the money paid for it

The connection between the monasteries and the tolls is not incidental and this
record's fields show it.

`revenue_basis` reads `trade-toll` and nothing else. `military_basis` reads
`client-levy` — the sea peoples of the straits, bound by oath rather than paid as
soldiers. A polity with no land tax and no army has two things to spend its
customs revenue on: keeping the crews loyal, which is the fourth chapter, and
building the reputation that made ships want to call, which is this one.

A ruler who endowed monasteries, hosted foreign scholars and funded a university
in Bengal was not being pious at the expense of policy. He was making Palembang a
place worth stopping at.

## What is not counted

`descendant_scripts` on this record is null and `successor_claims` is null, and
both are honest.

The script came from south India and was used unchanged for a new language, which
rule 3 excludes. And nobody claimed Srivijaya's legitimacy afterwards, because —
as the sixth chapter is about — nobody afterwards knew it had existed.
