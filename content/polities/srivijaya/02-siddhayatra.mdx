The Kedukan Bukit inscription, cut on a small stone near Palembang and dated to
683, records that a ruler called Dapunta Hyang set out on a *siddhayatra* — a
journey for magical power or success — with twenty thousand troops, took a
boat, and afterwards Srivijaya was prosperous.

It is the oldest dated text in Malay, in any form, anywhere.

## The language

That fact is worth stopping on before the politics.

These inscriptions are written in Old Malay, in a script derived from Pallava
south India, and Malay was not the language of Palembang's hinterland or of
most of the places Srivijaya dealt with. It was the language of the coasts and
the ports — the trade language of the straits — and a polity that chose to
legislate and to curse in it was making a decision about who it was addressing.

That decision outlasted everything else here. Malay went on being the lingua
franca of the archipelago for a thousand years after Srivijaya stopped
existing, was adopted by the Dutch administration for the same reason the
traders had adopted it, and is now the national language of Indonesia and
Malaysia under two names.

The letters themselves were borrowed, arriving from south India with Buddhism
and Sanskrit. Srivijaya invented no writing system. What it did was put a trade
vernacular into one, which is the smaller act and the more consequential.

## What the expedition was for

The inscriptions of the 680s are a set: Kedukan Bukit, Talang Tuwo, Kota Kapur,
Karang Berahi and Palas Pasemah, spread from Palembang up the coast to Bangka
island and into Jambi.

Read together they describe an expansion. The outlying ones stand at exactly
the places a polity would need if it wanted the Melaka strait and the Sunda
strait both, and they carry the loyalty oath the fourth chapter is about. Kota
Kapur, on Bangka, adds a line about an expedition against a land that would not
submit.

So the founding was not a conquest of territory. It was a set of stones placed
at river mouths, each carrying a curse, each addressed to whoever controlled
the boats there.

## The Sailendras, and Borobudur

In the ninth century Srivijaya's dynastic history entangles with Java's in a
way nobody has fully untangled.

Balaputradewa, the king under whom Srivijaya was at its height, appears in a
copper-plate charter from Nalanda in Bengal, where he endows a monastery and is
described as a Sailendra — the dynasty that built Borobudur on Java, the
largest Buddhist monument in the world.

Whether the Sailendras were a Javanese house that took over Srivijaya, a
Srivijayan house that ruled Java for a period, or two branches of one family is
argued and not settled.
What the Nalanda plate establishes is that a king at
Palembang was funding a Buddhist university in northeast India, and that an
Indian king recorded it on copper.

<Figure id="borobudur" />

A Sumatran ruler's endowment in Bengal, a Javanese monument, and a dynasty
claimed by both islands: it is the ordinary condition of the evidence here that
Srivijaya is visible in everybody's sources except its own.
