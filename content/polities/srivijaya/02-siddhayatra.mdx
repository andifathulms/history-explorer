The Kedukan Bukit inscription, cut on a small stone near Palembang and dated to
683, records that a ruler called Dapunta Hyang set out on a *siddhayatra* — a
journey for magical power or success — with twenty thousand troops, took a boat,
and afterwards Srivijaya was prosperous.

It is the oldest dated text in Malay, in any form, anywhere.

## The language

That fact is worth stopping on before the politics.

Old Malay in the Pallava-derived script is what these inscriptions are written
in, and Malay was not the language of Palembang's hinterland or of most of the
places Srivijaya dealt with. It was the language of the coasts and the ports —
the trade language of the straits — and a polity that chose to legislate and
curse in it was making a decision about who it was addressing.

That decision outlasted everything else on this page. Malay went on being the
lingua franca of the archipelago for a thousand years after Srivijaya stopped
existing, was adopted by the Dutch administration for the same reason, and is
now the national language of Indonesia and Malaysia under two names.

This record's `descendant_scripts` count is null, and the script is not the
claim. What Srivijaya did was not invent a writing system — the letters came from
south India with Buddhism and Sanskrit — but write a trade vernacular in it,
which coding rule 3 has no value for because a language is not a script.

## What the expedition was for

The inscriptions of the 680s are a set: Kedukan Bukit, Talang Tuwo, Kota Kapur,
Karang Berahi and Palas Pasemah, spread from Palembang up the coast to Bangka
island and into Jambi.

Read together they describe an expansion. The outlying ones are at exactly the
places a polity would need if it wanted the Melaka strait and the Sunda strait
both, and they carry the loyalty oath the fourth chapter is about. Kota Kapur,
on Bangka, adds a line about an expedition against a land that would not submit.

So the founding of this record is not a conquest of territory. It is a set of
stones placed at river mouths, each carrying a curse, each addressed to whoever
controlled the boats there.

## The Sailendras, and Borobudur

In the ninth century the record's dynastic history entangles with Java's in a way
nobody has fully untangled.

Balaputradewa, this record's peak ruler, appears in a copper-plate charter from
Nalanda in Bengal, where he endows a monastery and is described as a Sailendra —
the dynasty that built Borobudur on Java, the largest Buddhist monument in the
world.

Whether the Sailendras were a Javanese house that took over Srivijaya, a
Srivijayan house that ruled Java for a period, or two branches of one family is
argued and not settled. What the Nalanda plate establishes is that a king at
Palembang was funding a Buddhist university in northeast India and that an Indian
king recorded it on copper.

A Sumatran ruler's endowment in Bengal, a Javanese monument, and a dynasty
claimed by both islands is the ordinary condition of this record's evidence: the
polity is visible in everybody's sources except its own.
