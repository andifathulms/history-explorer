Srivijaya held no territory worth the name and ran the trade of half of Asia
for six hundred years.

It sat on the Melaka and Sunda straits — the only sea route between the Indian
Ocean and the South China Sea — and made its living by making that passage safe
for those who called at its ports and unsafe for those who did not.
Chinese
sources describe it, for six centuries, simply as the place the southern trade
came through.

## How you hold a strait

You cannot garrison a coastline that long, and Srivijaya never tried. What it
did instead was buy the loyalty of the people who would otherwise have robbed
the ships.

The coastal chiefs and the crews of the river mouths — the *orang laut*, the
sea peoples — were bound to the ruler by a combination of redistributed trade
wealth, ritual authority, and oaths sworn over inscribed stones that threaten
spectacular curses against the disloyal. The arrangement was that they raided
for Srivijaya rather than against it. A polity of this kind has no army in any
ordinary sense; it has a protection agreement with the pirates, renewed
continuously.

<Figure id="borobudur-ship" />

That is a genuinely different kind of state from the ones on either side of it.
A kingdom on a floodplain counts its people and taxes its harvests, and its
power ends where the fields end. This one had no land tax, no standing force
and no frontier. Its whole asset was a reputation for making a particular
stretch of water predictable, and every institution it had existed to maintain
that reputation.

## Which is why it cannot be measured the usual way

There is no figure for how large Srivijaya was, and there is not going to be
one.

Square kilometres of land under state control is the standard measure of a
premodern polity's size, and it describes this one about as well as acreage
describes a shipping line. Taagepera, whose tabulations supply extent figures
for most of the empires of the period, does not tabulate it. Drawing an area
off a map of the straits would produce a number, and the number would be a
picture of an idea rather than a measurement of anything.

So the honest answer is that Srivijaya was very large and unmeasurable, and the
things worth counting about it are the ports that paid, the monks who stayed,
and the centuries it lasted.
