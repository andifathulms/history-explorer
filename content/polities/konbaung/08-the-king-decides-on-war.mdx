Bodawpaya died in 1819 after thirty-seven years on the throne,
leaving 122 children and 208 grandchildren and, by the chronicles'
own judgment, ranking second only to Bayinnaung among Burma's
kings. He had settled the succession years in advance, and when his
chosen heir died young in 1808 he simply nominated that son's own
son in turn - giving the court a full decade to treat the matter as
settled, so that Bagyidaw succeeded at thirty-five without the
violence that had marked nearly every transition before his. Bagyidaw
was personally gentle enough, by all accounts, that no rebellion
troubled a reign that would otherwise have destroyed a harder king -
but he could not hold his attention on any subject for more than ten
minutes, and the men actually running his foreign policy included a
minor Portuguese adventurer whose main qualification was having once
travelled as far as Calcutta.

## A general who never lost

The kingdom Bagyidaw inherited had spent decades winning wars against
neighbours nobody else respected - Manipuris, Assamese, Chins,
Singpho hill tribes - and it produced, in these campaigns, the general
Maha Bandula: a commander who had once waited so long for royal
notice that he assaulted a minister out of frustration, was recognised
by Bodawpaya as having a valiant soul anyway, and went on to build a
reputation for silent, unshakeable self-regard that unsettled every
professional soldier who actually served under him without ever being
matched by results that impressed them. His victories, like
Alaungpaya's before him, came against opponents nobody else found
formidable. It did not matter. By 1823 the Burmese court, and not
just the court but popular opinion across the kingdom, had convinced
itself that a war with the English Company in Bengal would be no
different: one Burman was worth four Englishmen, the Company's own
repeated patience over decades of ignored letters and insulted envoys
proved they were afraid to fight, and Maha Bandula's genius for
stratagem would finish off an army the Burmese court did not believe
could really number two hundred thousand men, in a world too small,
in their own reckoning, to hold that many soldiers at all.

## A war the people wanted as much as the king

Harvey's own sources are explicit that it was not the king who led
Burma into war but Burma that led the king: Bagyidaw had spent years
as a moderating check on commanders eager for another round of
victories, and gave way only when princes, army and ordinary opinion
alike wanted the fight. Burmese troops crossed into British territory
in January 1824 and drew first blood before any formal declaration;
the Governor-General of India, recognising a war already begun rather
than starting one, proclaimed the state of war on 5 March 1824.
Weeks later, English transports lay alongside the stockade at
Rangoon - the opening exchange of a war the court that declared it
had convinced itself, against all the evidence a more curious
government might have gathered about the size and reach of the
Company it was fighting, would end quickly and in Burma's own favour.
