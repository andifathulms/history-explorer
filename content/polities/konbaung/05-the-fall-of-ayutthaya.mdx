Hsinbyushin moved the capital from Shwebo to Ava in 1765, a genuine
improvement in position - it put the court on the river and in direct
reach of the Kyaukse granary - though not the deeper move to the
Delta that might have let some outside air into a court that still
treated Rangoon as foreign territory. That same year he opened two
wars at once that would consume the rest of his reign.

## Fourteen months outside the walls

The Burmese claim on Siam rested on a conquest two centuries stale -
Bayinnaung had held it to tribute for twenty years in the 1560s and
nothing since - but Hsinbyushin sent two armies converging on
Ayutthaya anyway, one fighting down from Chiang Mai, the other up
from Tavoy. The campaign took three years of siege, flood, and
disease before the two commanders even joined their forces under the
city's walls, and both principal Burmese generals died of hardship
before the end came. Imperial armies from China were by then already
invading Upper Burma, and royal despatches urgently recalling every
available soldier reached the camp before Ayutthaya fell - if the city
had held out through one more dry season, the siege would likely have
been abandoned outright. It did not hold. On 28 March 1767 the
besiegers' mines breached the wall, the stormers went in, and the
slaughter that followed was indiscriminate; the Siamese king's body
was found the next day near the west gate. The city was burned,
razed, and never rebuilt as a capital - princes, monks, foreign
residents and thousands of ordinary people carried north into
captivity, so much treasure taken that an ordinary private soldier
could return home owning four slaves.

## A war on the other frontier

Even as the armies came home from Siam, a second and far more
dangerous war was already underway. Qing China invaded in strength
over four campaigns between 1765 and 1769, provoked by a tangle of
border tribute disputes and mistreated merchants that neither court
had the diplomatic machinery to settle peacefully. The fighting ran
in the hill country between Bhamo and the Myitnge valley, within
three marches of Ava at its closest. Burmese forces, helped by
captured French gunners and better war-canoes, won most of the major
engagements, but each defeated Chinese army was simply replaced by a
larger one. The Qing general Mingjui, the emperor's own son-in-law
and a veteran of campaigns in Central Asia, fought his way to within
three marches of the capital before his supply lines were cut and his
own colleagues failed to support him; retreating with his rearguard
fighting to the last, he cut off his own plaited hair as a token for
his emperor and hanged himself rather than be taken. Hsinbyushin
himself refused every suggestion that he flee the capital, declaring
he and his brothers would face the Chinese single-handed if it came
to that. The war ended without a treaty either court could openly
admit to: Burmese commanders granted honourable terms that let the
Chinese emperor withdraw from a costly war, and though his own pride
kept him from formally acknowledging it, his silence amounted to
consent - the caravan trade down from Yunnan resumed within months.
