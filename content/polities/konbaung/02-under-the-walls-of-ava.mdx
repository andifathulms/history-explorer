The Talaing government that had just burned Ava to the ground
responded to Alaungpaya's own rising with what Harvey calls
bewildering passivity - reinforcing its northern garrisons not at
all, replacing one ineffective commander with another who alienated
the local population by failing to observe basic customs, and losing
ground steadily as Alaungpaya's own campaign gathered momentum.
Villages that had sided with the Talaings were dealt with firmly but
selectively: Alaungpaya ordered quarter given to captured soldiers,
but burned the villages themselves and deported their families and
herds to Shwebo, a policy calculated to punish collaboration without
making every future surrender a fight to the death. By October 1752
the Talaings had withdrawn most of their outposts from the north.

## A capital retaken

By December 1753, barely eighteen months after Ava's own fall,
Alaungpaya was camped beneath its walls, and Talaing attempts to
dislodge him were beaten back with heavy losses. The reversal
completed a transformation Harvey treats as genuinely remarkable: a
minor hereditary headman, commanding forty-six villages' worth of
irregular levies at his own uprising's start, had become the
effective master of Upper Burma within a year and a half of a
catastrophe that had left the old royal capital in ashes and its
royal family in captivity. What followed carried that momentum
further still - a reunification of Lower Burma, the reduction of the
Talaing kingdom that had destroyed the old Toungoo dynasty, and, by
Alaungpaya's own last years, formal recognition from French and
English trading captains who knelt to receive his orders in
respectful silence, a homage paid to a man Harvey's own source
describes as tall, coarse-featured, and unmistakably regal in bearing
despite beginning life as nothing more than a village headman's heir.
