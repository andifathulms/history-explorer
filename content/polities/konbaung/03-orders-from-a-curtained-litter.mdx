Alaungpaya's last campaign overreached the timing that had served him
so well against the Talaings. He marched on the Siamese capital
Ayutthaya too late in the dry season, with the rains - and the
flooding that came with them - close behind, and dysentery already
spreading through a camp that included the king himself among the
sick. He offered Ayutthaya's own king a chance to submit to religious
reform rather than lose his throne outright; when no answer came,
Alaungpaya recognized, in Harvey's phrase, that "the play was over,"
and ordered a retreat within a week of arriving, abandoning forty
cannon in the process.

## A rearguard that would not fight further back

The retreat itself became one more proof of the loyalty Alaungpaya's
own rise had earned. He gave the honour of commanding the rearguard -
five hundred Manipuri horse and six thousand musket-armed infantry,
the pick of his army - to his own boyhood friend Minhkaungnawrahta.
Surrounded and pressed by pursuing Siamese forces, the rearguard's
own men wanted to fall back to safer ground; Minhkaungnawrahta
refused, telling them, "the safety of our Lord the King lies in our
keeping. Let us not fight further back, lest the sound of the guns
should break his sleep." They held their position, were defeated but
never broken, and withdrew in good order, gathering up Alaungpaya's
own stragglers along the way.

## A secret kept until home

Alaungpaya himself did not live to learn how the rearguard's stand
ended. Already ill, jolted along by litter on the forced march home,
he died at dawn on 11 May 1760 at Kinyua, in Thaton district. His own
son and attendants kept the death a closely guarded secret, fearing
it might break the army's own discipline or spark a succession
struggle before the crown prince could reach Shwebo and secure the
throne - so daily orders continued to issue in the dead king's name
from behind the curtains of his own litter, all the way home. Only at
Rangoon was the death finally made public, and his body carried the
rest of the way upriver by state barge to be buried, with full royal
ritual, in the very village - now a capital in fact as well as
claim - where an ordinary headman's stockade of felled toddy trees
had, eight years earlier, refused to accept that Burma's own kingship
was finished.
