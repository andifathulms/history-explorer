Alaungpaya was born in 1714 in Shwebo, then a village of three
hundred houses known as Moksobomyo, "town of the hunter chief" - the
same village a retreating Talaing officer had dismissed weeks earlier
as not worth pursuing after its own inhabitants cut down a small
detachment sent to collect their oath of allegiance. Alaungpaya
himself was of better standing than most of his neighbors, a
hereditary myothugyi whose family had governed locally for
generations and who later claimed - unprovably, Harvey notes, though
not implausibly in a district where genealogies were still kept -
descent from a fifteenth-century Ava military commandant. When Ava
itself fell to the Talaings in 1752, his own father counseled
submission: "We can do nothing. Fate is against us... We may as well
give in." Alaungpaya's own answer became the guiding principle of
everything that followed: "When fighting for your country it matters
little whether you are few or many. What does matter is that your
comrades have true hearts and strong arms."

## An ambush, then a rout

He fortified Shwebo with a stockade of felled toddy trees and a moat,
and made good on his own words almost immediately. A small Talaing
detachment sent to collect his village's submission was ambushed in
scrub jungle and destroyed. A second, larger force, sent with orders
to spare no one, was lured along a hollow road into a musket ambush
and all but wiped out. A third attempt, several thousand strong,
settled in for a siege rather than risk another ambush - until
Alaungpaya led a night sortie that turned into a rout, with Burmese
soldiers afterward swearing they had seen the spirit Rider of the
White Horse fighting at their side. The fleeing Talaings abandoned
their entire camp, handing Alaungpaya's own men muskets, horses and
plunder worth, in Harvey's own phrase, their weight in gold.

## No need to press men

Word of the victories spread faster than Alaungpaya could have
organized it himself. Harvey records that there was no need to offer
bounties or conscript anyone - men came from across Upper Burma
because they were fighting, as they saw it, for the survival of their
own people, and because there would be plunder besides. Rival centers
of resistance had already formed elsewhere in Upper Burma, led by men
of birth equal or superior to Alaungpaya's own, any one of whom could
have fractured the common cause by insisting on his own claim to
lead it. None did. Within months the territorial cadres of half of
Upper Burma had placed themselves under a single hunter-village
headman rather than compete with him for a throne none of them yet
held.
