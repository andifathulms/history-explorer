Alaungpaya's eldest son Naungdawgyi succeeded him without
opposition in 1760, but the calm did not survive the funeral. His
younger brother Hsinbyushin, who had loyally helped send word of
their father's death to Naungdawgyi, briefly struck for the throne
himself once the body was gone - a feeble attempt that collapsed and
was forgiven at their mother's intercession. A harder threat came
from Minhkaungnawrahta, one of Alaungpaya's own veteran generals, who
revolted after the new king executed two officers he distrusted
without warning. Idolised by the army and left in command of most of
its musketeers as rearguard, Minhkaungnawrahta occupied Ava itself
with twelve thousand men and refused every offer of pardon the king
sent him, declaring he would die like a soldier with the city walls
for his coffin. The siege that followed starved his garrison out; he
broke free with a handful of followers, was run down and shot in the
Shan hills above Kyaukse, and even wounded, killed the soldier who
first grappled with him before a second shot finished him. When his
head was brought before the king, Naungdawgyi mourned rather than
gloated: "Should ye have slain so great a man?"

## A Company envoy learns the new rules

While besieging his own general, Naungdawgyi received Captain Alves,
an envoy from the English East India Company, at Sagaing in September
1760 - the first real test of relations after Alaungpaya had wiped out
the Company's garrison at Negrais two years earlier. Pressed for
compensation, the king dismissed the massacre outright, comparing the
dead indiscriminately to grass burned off a field at the end of the
dry season regardless of what useful plants happened to grow among
it. He released the surviving English captives, granted free trade,
and asked only that the Company relocate from the remote, indefensible
Negrais to somewhere on the mainland he could actually control -
Rangoon, as it turned out, rather than the more distant Bassein
Alaungpaya himself had once suggested. Alves came away with no formal
treaty, only a royal letter confirming the terms discussed in
audience, and an impression - confirmed by villagers he met on his
way upriver, visibly relieved whenever an assault on Ava's besieged
rebel garrison failed - that ordinary Burmese were sick of a war that
had not stopped since Alaungpaya first raised his stockade.

## A king who died young

Naungdawgyi spent his final years on works of merit, building pagodas
near Shwebo and sending offerings to shrines around the kingdom; he
died at twenty-nine in 1763, leaving seven children, and the throne
passed to his brother Hsinbyushin - the same brother whose own earlier
bid for it, forgiven at their mother's word, had cost him nothing
worse than a rebuke.
