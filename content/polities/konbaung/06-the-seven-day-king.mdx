Hsinbyushin's son Singu ended his father's Siamese war the moment he
took the throne in 1776, simply withdrawing the armies still in the
field. His own wars, against Manipur's exiled raja operating out of
Cachar, cost twenty thousand men to disease before Singu let the
matter drop - the country by then so devastated there was nothing
left worth fighting over. Ordinary Burmese, worn down by two decades
of continuous campaigning under Alaungpaya's sons, were grateful for
a king who mostly left them alone to fish, pray, and build pagodas,
which was how Singu spent most of his own reign. What they did not
see was a court that had stopped respecting him: he was seldom sober,
executed a brother, an uncle and several ministers on suspicion,
drowned a deposed queen, and made a habit of leaving the palace
empty for weeks at a time on pilgrimage, returning at any hour with
no warning - exactly the kind of opening a conspiracy needs.

## A puppet on the throne, and a king who died right

At midnight in 1782, while Singu was away at a riverside pagoda, a
palace faction smuggled in an eighteen-year-old cousin, Maung Maung,
dressed to pass for the king in the dark, and seized the palace
before the Guard understood the trick. When word reached Singu, his
own followers scattered and he briefly thought of fleeing to Manipur;
his mother refused to let him run. He walked alone to the palace gate
at dawn, announced himself as "Singu, lawful lord of the palace," and
was let through by guards too startled to stop him - only to be cut
down inside by the father of one of the queens he had had killed. He
died, Harvey's sources note dryly, at least royally.

Maung Maung, who had spent most of his life in a monastery and never
wanted the throne he had just been handed, spent his own week in
power begging Alaungpaya's surviving sons to take it from him instead
- an offer they all refused, suspecting a trap, until they realised
there was nothing to fear from him. After seven days he was executed
by the one brother who was not a puppet: Bodawpaya, the senior of
Alaungpaya's sons still living, who took the throne their father had
originally decreed should pass between brothers in order of
seniority - the same rule Hsinbyushin had broken by naming his own
son Singu heir, and the breaking of which had cost the kingdom the
better part of two chaotic reigns before it was restored by force.
