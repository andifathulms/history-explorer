The tradition's last three kings belong to a single Etruscan-connected
family, and mark, even in the legendary account, a change of register:
these are the kings credited with the physical monuments a visitor to
Rome could later actually go and look at, rather than only with wars and
religious institutions no building preserved.

## Tarquinius Priscus

Lucius Tarquinius, the son of a Greek migrant to the Etruscan city of
Tarquinii, is said to have moved to Rome specifically because Etruscan
custom barred a man of mixed and partly foreign blood from holding office
in his own city, and to have been elected king there instead on the
strength of his wealth and ability - a story that reads suspiciously like
a later explanation for why Rome's kingship passed to an outsider family,
but one the Romans themselves preserved rather than suppressed.

He is credited with beginning the building programme later kings and the
early republic both continued: the Circus Maximus, laid out in the valley
between the Palatine and Aventine for chariot racing; the Cloaca Maxima,
a great drainage sewer that channelled the marshy ground between Rome's
hills into the Tiber and made the valley that became the Forum usable
land; and the beginning of construction on a temple to Jupiter on the
Capitoline hill that later kings and the early republic finished. Whether
any specific structure now identifiable in the ground actually dates to
his own traditional reign in the early sixth century BC is a separate
question from whether Rome underwent exactly this kind of transformation,
from a cluster of hilltop villages to a city with monumental public
works, in roughly this period - the archaeological record supports the
second claim considerably more confidently than the first.

## Servius Tullius

Tarquinius Priscus's successor, by the tradition's own telling, was not
his son but a former household slave - a detail the Romans preserved with
some evident discomfort, softened in one version of the story by making
him secretly a nobleman's freeborn son taken captive as a child, and left
plainly stated as slave birth in another. However he came to the throne,
Servius Tullius is credited with the single most consequential
institutional reform the kingship produced: a census dividing the male
citizen body into property classes, each obligated to supply a specified
number of armed men equipped to a standard the class could afford, voting
in an assembly - the comitia centuriata - organised by those same classes
rather than by simple headcount.

The effect, whether it worked exactly this way from the start or was
elaborated by later generations who then read it back into Servius's own
reign, tied military obligation directly to political voice: a man's
standing in the assembly matched what he was expected to contribute in
war. It is the same principle the early republic's own citizen levy ran on
for the next three centuries, unmodified in its essentials until the
manpower crises of the later republic forced a different approach
entirely.

He is also credited with extending Rome's own city wall to enclose all
seven of the traditional hills and with expanding the citizen body through
further conquest and absorption of neighbouring Latin communities - though
the wall that carries his name today, standing in visible sections around
modern Rome, is dated by its construction material to more than a century
after his own traditional reign, a discrepancy the next chapter takes up
directly.

## What "peak" means for a legendary sequence

Servius Tullius is named as the kingdom's peak here on institutional
grounds the later republic itself never abandoned, not because his own
reign can be independently dated or verified in the way a documented
ruler's can. The census, the property-based military classes, and the
assembly built on them outlasted the monarchy that supposedly created
them by centuries - which is either a remarkable case of one king's
reform surviving essentially intact through five hundred years of
political upheaval, or, just as plausibly, a later republican
institution's own origin story, retrojected onto the king the tradition
found easiest to credit with it.
