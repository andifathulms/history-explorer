The Roman tradition, as Livy records it four and a half centuries after the
fact and admits himself he cannot vouch for, traces the city's founding
through a royal house in the nearby city of Alba Longa, dispossessed by a
usurping brother who had the rightful heir's newborn twin sons, Romulus and
Remus, exposed on the flooding Tiber rather than killed outright. The
basket carrying them came to rest at the foot of the Palatine hill, where a
she-wolf is said to have suckled them until a shepherd found and raised
them as his own.

Livy himself offers the sceptic's reading alongside the miraculous one in
the same passage: perhaps the shepherd's own wife had simply been a woman
of easy reputation, whom Latin slang called *lupa*, a she-wolf, and the
story of an actual wolf grew from the word rather than the other way
around. He does not resolve which is true. He records both and moves on,
which is itself a fair description of how seriously the story should be
taken as documented history.

## A city, and then a killing

Grown to adulthood and told the truth of their birth, the twins restored
their grandfather to the throne of Alba Longa and then set out to found a
city of their own on the hills where they had been raised. They disagreed
about which hill, and about which of them the gods favoured to rule it,
and settled the question by augury - watching the sky for birds, the
standard Roman method of reading divine approval for centuries afterward.
Remus, on the Aventine, saw six vultures first. Romulus, on the Palatine,
saw twelve, later.

The dispute over whose omen counted more - the first sighting, or the
greater number - was never settled by argument. Romulus began marking out
a wall around the Palatine; Remus, mocking its modest height, jumped over
it; and Romulus, or one of his own men, killed him for it. Rome's own
foundational story about itself begins with a fratricide over the size of
a boundary wall, a detail later Roman writers found uncomfortable enough
that some ancient versions soften or omit it rather than let the city's
first act be its founder killing his own brother.

## The asylum and the Sabine women

Needing a population for a city built on a hill nobody else particularly
wanted, Romulus is said to have declared the ground between the Capitoline
and Palatine an asylum: any man, free or not, fleeing debt or crime or
simply looking for a fresh start, was welcome and protected. It grew Rome's
numbers quickly and gave it, in the tradition's own account, a citizen body
disproportionately made up of exactly the kind of men a respectable
neighbouring city would not want its own daughters marrying.

The solution the story gives is the rape of the Sabine women: Romulus
invited the neighbouring Sabines to a festival and had his own men seize
the unmarried women present by force. War followed, and the story's own
resolution is deliberately not a Roman military triumph: the Sabine women
themselves, by then married to the men who had taken them and unwilling to
see their Sabine fathers and Roman husbands kill each other, stepped
between the two armies and stopped the battle. Rome and the Sabine
community merged into one people under a joint kingship for a time, before
Romulus alone.

## What the story is doing

Read as history, almost none of this survives contact with the question of
how anyone could know it. Read as the account a much later Roman
aristocracy told about its own city's beginnings, the pattern is
consistent and worth noticing: violence answered not by more violence but
by a forced, uneasy integration of outsiders into the citizen body -
exiles and debtors first, a conquered neighbouring people second - is
exactly the mechanism the later republic used, again and again, to grow
its own population and its alliance network. Whether the story shaped the
practice or the practice shaped how later Romans told the story, the two
match closely enough that the founding legend reads less like a fantasy
unrelated to how Rome actually operated than like Rome's own explanation,
to itself, of why it operated that way.
