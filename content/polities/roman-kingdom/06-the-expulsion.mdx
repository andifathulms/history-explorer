The act that actually ended the kingdom is a single episode, one the state
that replaced it went on to tell and retell at far greater length than
almost anything else in its own early history: Tarquin's son raped a
Roman noblewoman named Lucretia, who
told her husband and father what had happened, made them swear an oath of
vengeance, and killed herself rather than live with the disgrace. Lucius
Junius Brutus - the same nephew whose feigned stupidity and kissed earth
the previous chapters have already followed - pulled the knife from her
wound, swore on it to drive out the whole house of Tarquin, and called on
Rome to join him.

## What happened to the office, not just the man

Tarquin and his family fled the city. What matters more than the flight
itself is what the Romans did with the vacancy it left: they did not
simply depose one bad king and choose a better one,
which every earlier crisis in the tradition - the year without a king
after Romulus, the disputed succession to Ancus Marcius - had resolved by
finding a new individual to hold the same office. In 509 BC, by the
tradition's own dating, the office of king was abolished outright and
replaced with two annually elected magistrates holding equal power, each
able to block the other - a structural answer to the specific failure mode
the previous chapter's stories had spent their own energy illustrating.

## Two hundred and forty-four years, on one reckoning

Whatever can or cannot be verified about any individual king's reign, the
Roman tradition treated the kingdom as a single, bounded unit of time with
a clear beginning and a clear end, more confidently dated by later
generations than almost anything that happened within it. That confidence
is itself worth noting rather than simply inheriting: a chronology
calculated backward centuries later from a fixed list of seven names is a
different kind of evidence than a contemporary record, and the previous
chapter's archaeology suggests the actual transition from scattered
villages to a kingless city-state was considerably messier, and probably
longer, than one dramatic year and seven tidy reigns.

What is not in serious doubt is that some transition of this general kind
happened around this period, and that whatever Rome had been governed by
before it, the state that emerged afterward defined itself, for the whole
of its subsequent history, in explicit and permanent opposition to the
word *rex*.
