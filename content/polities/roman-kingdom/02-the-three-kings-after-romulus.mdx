The tradition gives Rome three kings between Romulus and the Etruscan
dynasty that follows them, and credits each with a different half of what a
city needs beyond a founder: religion, war, and reach.

## Numa Pompilius

Romulus, by the tradition's own account, did not die naturally but
vanished during a storm, and was afterward worshipped as a god under the
name Quirinus - the first and most dramatic instance of a pattern later
Roman emperors would revive for themselves. His successor, chosen by the
Roman and Sabine communities together after a year without a king, was
Numa, a Sabine known for piety rather than military skill, and the
tradition makes the contrast deliberate: where Romulus had built the city
with the sword, Numa built its religious calendar, its priesthoods and the
observances that governed Roman public life for the rest of its history.

He is credited with fixing the ritual calendar, establishing the priestly
colleges - the pontiffs who oversaw religious law, the augurs who read
omens, the Vestal Virgins who kept Rome's sacred fire perpetually alight -
and closing the doors of the temple of Janus, left open in time of war and
shut only when Rome was at peace with every neighbour. Later Roman writers
record that the doors were closed exactly twice more in the whole of the
kingdom and republic's combined history after Numa's own reign, which is
itself a fair measure of how rarely the state that inherited his
institutions found itself entirely at peace.

## Tullus Hostilius

Numa's successor reversed the emphasis entirely. Tullus Hostilius fought a
war against Rome's own mother city, Alba Longa, and the tradition resolves
it through single combat between two sets of triplet brothers, the Horatii
for Rome and the Curiatii for Alba - a device, real armies substituted with
representative champions, that appears in more than one ancient
Mediterranean tradition and should be read as a story shape rather than a
documented battle plan. Two of the three Horatii died before the third,
wounded but faster than his exhausted opponents, killed all three
Curiatii in turn and won the war for Rome.

Alba Longa's subsequent betrayal of its alliance obligations led Tullus to
destroy the city outright and resettle its population in Rome, doubling
the city's citizen body at a stroke - the same absorption pattern the
previous chapter's asylum and Sabine material already established, now
applied to an entire conquered city rather than to individual migrants.

## Ancus Marcius

The fourth king, Numa's own grandson by the tradition's genealogy, combined
both emphases: he is credited with renewing Numa's religious observances
after Tullus's own neglect of them, while also extending Rome's territory
to the Mediterranean coast, founding the port town of Ostia at the Tiber's
mouth to give the city its own access to the sea and its salt trade, and
building the first bridge across the Tiber, the Pons Sublicius, deliberately
constructed of wood so it could be dismantled quickly if an enemy needed
to be kept off the city's own bank.

## What three kings together were credited with

None of these reigns survives as anything closer to documented history than
Romulus's own founding does - no independent record from the eighth or
seventh century BC corroborates any of it, and the neat progression from
piety to war to a balance of both reads at least as much like a later
Roman audience's idea of what a well-rounded sequence of founding kings
ought to look like as it does like an actual record of three men's separate
reigns. What the tradition establishes, whether or not any individual
detail happened as told, is a Rome that already had, in outline, two things
neither the kingdom nor the republic that followed it ever stopped resting
on: a religious apparatus nobody seriously proposed abolishing, and a habit
of absorbing whoever it conquered rather than simply destroying them.
