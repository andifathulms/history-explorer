The Roman tradition gives the kingdom a fixed shape: seven kings, a
founding year calculated backward from it by the scholar Varro in the
first century BC, and a total span of two hundred and forty-four years
averaging a suspiciously tidy thirty-five years per reign. Archaeology
does not confirm this shape, and does not straightforwardly contradict it
either. What it shows is a different kind of picture, built from the
ground rather than from a king list, and the two do not line up neatly.

## Huts before a city

Excavation on the Palatine has found the remains of small oval huts -
postholes, wall trenches and drainage gullies marking out one-room
structures with thatched roofs - dating to the ninth and eighth centuries
BC, the period the traditional founding date falls within. This is
consistent, in a general way, with the idea of a settlement beginning
around then. It is not consistent with a single founding act by one man in
one year: the broader archaeological culture these huts belong to, usually
called Latial culture, shows scattered hilltop villages across the region
for generations before and after the traditional date, gradually growing,
merging and competing with each other rather than one village suddenly
becoming a city on a fixed day.

What the material record does support is a genuine, gradual process of
exactly the kind the founding legend compresses into a single dramatic
year: separate hilltop communities on the Palatine, the Quirinal, the
Esquiline and Rome's other hills coalescing into one settlement over the
eighth and seventh centuries BC, roughly the period the tradition assigns
to Romulus and his immediate successors.

## The Etruscan question

The archaeological and epigraphic evidence for the sixth century BC - the
period the tradition assigns to the Etruscan-connected kings - is
considerably richer, and does show a city undergoing real transformation:
monumental temple construction on the Capitoline, elite tombs with grave
goods matching contemporary Etruscan and Greek imports, and Rome's
earliest substantial inscriptions, written in an alphabet derived from
Etruscan models. Etruscan cultural and political influence over Rome in
this period is not seriously disputed. Whether it took the specific form
of three named individual kings ruling in the exact sequence and for the
exact reigns Livy gives is a separate question the material evidence
cannot answer either way.

## The wall that is not old enough

One specific case is worth stating plainly because it can be checked
against physical evidence rather than argued from silence. The wall
enclosing Rome's seven hills, standing today in visible sections and
traditionally attributed to Servius Tullius in the sixth century BC, is
built from a volcanic stone quarried at Veii - a city Rome did not
control until it conquered Veii in 396 BC. The wall cannot have been built
from that stone before Rome held the quarry it came from, which places its
actual construction in the fourth century BC, most likely after the
Gallic sack of Rome around 390 gave the city an urgent, documented reason
to build proper fortifications. The wall that carries Servius Tullius's
name was built more than a century after his own traditional reign ended.

## What that means for the rest of the tradition

The wall is a useful case precisely because it is checkable in a way most
of the kingdom's own history is not: a specific structure, a specific king
credited with it, and a specific piece of physical evidence that settles
the question independently of what any literary source claims. It does not
prove the other kings or their other achievements are equally fictional.
It does establish that the Roman tradition was capable of attaching a
later achievement to an earlier, more prestigious name, whether through
honest confusion across centuries of oral transmission or through a
later builder's own decision to associate new work with an old and
respected king's memory. Every other specific claim about the kingdom's
own history - what Numa actually instituted, what Tarquinius Priscus
actually built, whether the census attributed to Servius Tullius took the
exact form later sources describe - should be read with the same
possibility in mind, and mostly cannot be checked against anything as
solid as a quarry's own geology.
