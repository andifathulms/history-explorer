The kingdom's seventh and last king is, in the tradition's own telling,
the one whose reign is meant to explain why the office was abolished
rather than reformed. Lucius Tarquinius Superbus - the surname itself
means proud, or arrogant, and was almost certainly applied retrospectively
by a tradition writing his character backward from his ending - took the
throne, by Livy's account, by having his own father-in-law Servius
Tullius murdered, and then governing without the senate's ordinary
consultation, executing or exiling opponents without trial, and using
forced labour on his own building projects.

## The omen and the feigned madness

Livy records a story meant to set up the man who would eventually end the
monarchy. A snake was said to have appeared from a crack in the palace and
thrown the household into a panic serious enough that Tarquin sent two of
his own sons, along with his nephew Lucius Junius Brutus, to consult the
oracle at Delphi. Brutus, the story says, had deliberately affected
stupidity for years to avoid being seen as a threat by an uncle who had
already killed other relatives with better claims to the throne - carrying
a golden rod hidden inside a hollow wooden staff as a private joke on the
oracle's own riddling style.

Asked who would rule Rome next, the oracle answered that it would be
whoever first kissed his own mother. Tarquin's sons drew lots to decide
which of them would race home to try it; Brutus, understanding the riddle
correctly where his cousins had not, deliberately fell and kissed the
earth - the mother of all things - on the way out of the temple. The story
exists to explain, before the killing that actually ends the kingdom
happens, why the man who leads the revolt was in a position to do it at
all: patient, apparently harmless, and already quietly certain of his own
future role.

## Gabii and the poppy heads

A separate episode, concerning the Latin town of Gabii, is preserved
mainly for the political lesson later Roman writers found useful to draw
from it, and is worth including for what it says about how the tradition
understood Tarquin's own method of rule. Unable to take Gabii by force,
Tarquin is said to have sent his son Sextus there pretending to have fled
his father's cruelty; once trusted and given command of Gabii's own army,
Sextus sent a messenger to his father asking what to do next. Tarquin,
walking in his garden, made no verbal reply at all - he simply walked
among his poppies, striking off the heads of the tallest with his stick,
and sent the messenger back with nothing but that image to report. Sextus
understood: he had the leading men of Gabii killed or exiled one by one,
and the town, deprived of anyone capable of organising resistance,
surrendered to Rome without further need for a siege.

## What the story is building toward

Both episodes exist in the tradition to characterise a specific kind of
rule - one that governs by removing anyone capable of independent
standing, whether through outright murder or through patient, deniable
manoeuvring - immediately before the same son's own act of violence
against a Roman noblewoman becomes the spark that ends the whole
arrangement. The kingdom's own final king is remembered, in other words,
less as a specific historical individual than as the tradition's own
fullest available illustration of exactly what the office of king could
become and exactly why a Roman audience five centuries later still found
the word itself worth fearing.
