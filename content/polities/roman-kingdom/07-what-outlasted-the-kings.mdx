---
title: What outlasted the kings
drafted_from: beard-2015
phase: afterlife
---

The republic that replaced the kingdom abolished the office of king so
thoroughly that the word itself became close to unusable in Roman
political life for the next five centuries. It did not abolish the
institutions the kingdom was credited with building. Almost the whole of
Rome's religious and social apparatus passed from the monarchy to the
kingless state that followed it essentially intact, run by different
people under a different constitution.

## The priesthoods kept their offices

The colleges Numa was credited with founding - the pontiffs, the augurs,
the Vestal Virgins tending Rome's sacred fire - continued exactly as
before, filled now by election or co-optation among the aristocracy
rather than by royal appointment, but performing the same rites on the
same calendar. One office was kept in a form that openly admitted what had
happened to it: the rex sacrorum, king of sacred rites, a priesthood
created specifically to perform the religious ceremonies a king had once
performed in person, held for life by a man permanently barred from
standing for any political magistracy at all - an office whose entire
design was to preserve the word rex inside Rome's religious life while
making absolutely certain the man holding it could never again wield the
political power that word had once meant.

## The census and the classes

Servius Tullius's property-based classes and the centuriate assembly built
on them did not end with the monarchy either. The republic went on taking
a census, organising its citizens into the same graduated classes for
military and voting purposes, and holding elections in the same
assembly, largely unmodified in its basic structure, for centuries -
one king's own credited reform outlasting every one of the kings who came
after him, functioning as ordinary republican machinery long after nobody
alive remembered a monarchy at all.

## The senate itself

The kingdom's tradition already includes a senate, advising the king and
in the interregnum periods between one king's death and the next king's
election reportedly running the state's affairs directly, a body of elders
whose standing rested on age, family and experience rather than on
holding an office of their own. The republic's own senate is, on the
tradition's own account, the same body under the same name, continuing to
advise magistrates who had replaced a king rather than starting again from
nothing.

## What actually changed

What the expulsion abolished was concentration, not government. A single
man holding religious, military and judicial authority together, for
life, with no formal check on any of it, became instead a scattered set of
separate offices - a religious king with no politics, elected annual
magistrates with no permanent tenure, a senate with authority but no
formal power to legislate - each one recognisable as a fragment of what
the kingship, in the tradition's own account, had held whole. The republic
did not invent Roman government. It took the monarchy's own institutions
apart and rebuilt them deliberately so that no single piece, and no single
man, could ever hold them all together again. Whether that design worked
for as long as its own builders hoped, and what eventually put every piece
back into one man's hands regardless, belongs to the republic's own much
longer story rather than to the kingdom that came before it.
