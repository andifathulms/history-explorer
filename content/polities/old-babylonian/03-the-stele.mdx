It is a block of black diorite, two and a quarter metres tall, carved late in
Hammurabi's reign. At the top the king stands before Shamash, god of justice, who
is seated and holds out a rod and a ring. Below, in beautifully cut archaising
script, run a prologue, something like two hundred and seventy-five provisions,
and an epilogue.

It was not found in Babylon. Elamites carried it off to Susa around 1155 BC as
plunder, along with a good deal else, and it was excavated there in 1901. Which
means the most famous object of the Mesopotamian world reached us because of the
polity this corpus does not carry, taking it from the polity this record is
about, and being dug up by a French expedition in Iran.

## What is in it

The provisions are conditional: if a man does X, then Y. They cover false
accusation, theft, the *ilkum* tenure, agriculture and irrigation, loans and
interest, deposits, marriage, inheritance, adoption, wet-nursing, assault, the
fees of surgeons and builders and boatmen, the hire of oxen, and the sale of
slaves. The penalties are graded by the status of both parties, and for the
highest class of victim they are retaliatory: an eye for an eye, a bone for a
bone, a tooth for a tooth, in those words, twelve hundred years before the
formulation appears in Exodus.

The prologue lists the cities whose gods have entrusted the king with their care.
The epilogue instructs the wronged man to come and have the stele read to him,
and curses at length anyone who erases the name on it.

## Whether it was law

No Old Babylonian court verdict cites it. Thousands of legal records survive from
the period, cases are decided, and none of them says "according to the stele of
the king". Some of the decisions do not match its provisions.

The scholarly options are roughly: it was a real code that judges were expected
to know without citing; it was a royal monument, a demonstration that the king
had made justice appear, whose function was ideological; or it was a scholarly
compilation of the kind Mesopotamian scribes made for omens and medicine and
grammar — a list of cases assembled to display the completeness of a system of
thought. Van De Mieroop leans towards the last two, and this record's turning
point for it is marked `contested: true` on exactly that ground: not the date,
not the details, but whether it was the hinge it is usually taken for.

## Why it does not matter, and why it does

For the empire, it did nothing. The territory Hammurabi assembled came apart
within twelve years of his death and the stele did not slow it down by a day.

For everything after, it did more than any other object in this region. Scribes
copied it as a school text for fifteen hundred years, down into the Neo-Babylonian
period this region's last Mesopotamian record covers. It fixed the form that
Mesopotamian and later Near Eastern legal writing took. Roth prints it in one
volume with the laws of Ur-Nammu, of Lipit-Ishtar, of Eshnunna, the Middle
Assyrian laws and the Hittite laws — four of the six belonging to records on this
site, and the whole tradition visibly one conversation.

And it is the reason a hundred-and-forty-year dynasty with a fifty-year empire is
a household name while the Third Dynasty of Ur, which governed more thoroughly,
lasted about as long and left a hundred thousand documents, is not. This site
measures reach in square kilometres and influence in three counts, and neither
instrument registers what a school text does over fifteen centuries.
