Mursili I of Hatti came down the Euphrates from central Anatolia, took Babylon,
ended the dynasty, and went home. He installed no governor and left no garrison.
He was murdered on his return by his brother-in-law, and Hatti spent the
following century tearing itself apart.

The Kassites, who had been present in Babylonia as a minority for generations,
took over the vacated kingship within a few decades and held it for four hundred
years — the longest single dynasty in Mesopotamian history, and not one this
corpus carries.

## The conquest that conquered nothing

It is easy to read 1595 as a change of masters, and it was not one.

The Hittites came a very long way, took the city, took the statue of Marduk, and
went home. Mursili I was assassinated shortly after his return and Hatti spent the
next century on its own troubles. Nobody in Anatolia governed Babylonia for a
day.

What actually followed in Babylonia was the Kassite dynasty, which ruled the
country for something over four hundred years — longer than any other dynasty in
Mesopotamian history — and which arrived without anybody recording how. So the
best-attested event at the end of this dynasty is a raid that led nowhere, and
the thing that really replaced it is one of the least documented successions in
the ancient world.

## What survived it

Two things did, and neither is a state.

The first is the writing. Hittite cuneiform is Old Babylonian cuneiform adapted,
probably by way of northern Syria, to write an Indo-European language — and
Hittite is the earliest attested Indo-European language in the world. It is
attested because Anatolian scribes took this dynasty's script and made it write
something it had never been designed for.

The second is a name, and the distinction matters. Every later ruler of
Mesopotamia wanted to be king of Babylon: the Kassites, the Assyrians, Cyrus,
Alexander, the Seleucids. None of them claimed to be continuing the First
Dynasty. They claimed a place.

That is the difference between a succession and a resumption, and Mesopotamia is
full of the second. There are two Babylons, two Assyrias and two Elams, and in
each pair the later one is the same ground and the same gods picking up after an
interruption — not an heir, not a conquest, and not an event with a year on it.
Nothing happened on the day Babylon resumed being Babylon, which is exactly why
it is so hard to say when it did.
