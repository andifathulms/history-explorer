Four consecutive provisions of the laws deal with drink-sellers. Every one of
them has a female subject, and three of the four are capital.

## The four

> §108. If a wine-seller do not receive grain as the price of drink, but if she
> receive money by the great stone, or make the measure for drink smaller than
> the measure for corn, they shall call that wine-seller to account, and they
> shall throw her into the water.
>
> §109. If outlaws collect in the house of a wine-seller, and she do not arrest
> these outlaws and bring them to the palace, that wine-seller shall be put to
> death.
>
> §110. If a priestess who is not living in a convent open a wine-shop or enter a
> wine-shop for a drink, they shall burn that woman.
>
> §111. If a wine-seller give sixty measures of drink on credit, at the time of
> harvest she shall receive fifty measures of grain.

Taken together they describe a trade that was normal, female, regulated, and
dangerous.

## What each one is about

The first is a weights-and-measures offence. The tavern-keeper takes payment in
grain at harvest and in silver otherwise, and the fraud available to her is to
weigh the silver on a heavy stone or to serve drink by a smaller measure than the
one she reckons the corn by. The penalty for cheating on the ratio is drowning.

The second is not about drink at all. It makes the tavern-keeper responsible for
policing her own premises: if criminals gather in her house and she does not
arrest them herself and deliver them to the palace, she dies. The state is
treating the tavern as the place where trouble organises, and deputising the
woman who runs it, on pain of death, because it has nobody else standing there.

The third is about a different woman entirely. A consecrated woman who has left
the cloister may not open a wine-shop, and may not so much as go into one for a
drink; if she does she is burned — the heaviest penalty in the collection, and
one reserved elsewhere for incest and for theft from a burning house.

The fourth is quiet and is the most informative of the four. It fixes the rate at
which drink sold on credit is repaid at harvest: sixty measures of beer against
fifty of grain. That is a law protecting the seller. Someone had to be given a
formula because the debts were real, seasonal and worth litigating.

## The picture that comes out of it

A tavern in an Old Babylonian town was run by a woman, extended credit through
the year against the coming harvest, and was where the men the palace was looking
for could be found.

She was liable for her own arithmetic and for her own customers, and could be
executed for either. She was also, by the fourth law, a creditor with an
enforceable claim on a harvest she had helped feed.

The collection is famous for kings, temples and retaliation. It is at its most
informative in the four paragraphs where it turns to a working woman with a debt
ledger and a room full of people she is legally obliged to inform on.
