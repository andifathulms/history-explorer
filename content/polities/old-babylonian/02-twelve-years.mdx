In his twenty-ninth year Hammurabi was one of five. In his thirty-eighth he held
Mesopotamia from the Gulf to the Habur. Everything this record is known for
happens in the last dozen years of a reign that had, until then, been
unremarkable.

The sequence, from the year-names and the Mari letters: in 1764 a coalition out
of Elam, Eshnunna, Assyria and elsewhere came against him and was beaten. In 1763
he took Larsa from Rim-Sin, who had ruled the south for sixty years, after a
siege of several months and by diverting the water supply. In 1761 he turned on
Mari, whose king Zimri-Lim had been his ally throughout, and took it; two years
later he had its walls demolished. In 1755 he took Eshnunna, apparently by
damming and releasing a river. By the end he had Ashur and Nineveh as well.

## Diplomacy, mostly

Van De Mieroop's argument, and the reason this record's institutions are coded
from him, is that reading Hammurabi as a conqueror gets the career backwards. The
letters show a man who spent thirty years on alliances, arbitration, marriage
negotiations, water disputes and the extradition of runaways, in a system where
every king had ten or fifteen clients and none could move without the others. The
conquests were possible only because that system had been ground down by a
generation of mutual exhaustion, and Babylon had spent the period being useful to
everybody.

The betrayal of Mari is the clearest case. Zimri-Lim had sent troops to
Hammurabi's campaigns for years and the two courts corresponded constantly; the
Mari archive contains letters from Babylon in a tone of complete cordiality
within months of the attack.

## What the archive stopping means

Mari burned in 1761 and the fire baked the tablets, which is why they survive.
The consequence for this record, and it is a large one, is that the empire
Hammurabi built is worse documented than the twenty years of manoeuvring that
preceded it.

Almost everything on the previous page comes from Mari. After 1761 there is no
Mari, and there is no comparable archive anywhere else; what remains for the rest
of this dynasty is year-names, a modest quantity of Babylonian legal and
administrative material, some letters, and the law collection. So the moment this
state became an empire is the moment the light goes out on it, and the reason is
that the empire's own greatest single act of destruction was aimed at the place
where the light was coming from.

This corpus's Mitanni record has a chapter about being an empire with no archive.
The situation here is stranger: an empire that had an archive, and destroyed it,
and is legible only up to the point where it started to matter.
