The best-known line in the laws of Hammurabi is *if a man destroy the eye of
another man, they shall destroy his eye*. It is quoted as the principle of the
whole collection. It is actually the first of three provisions, and the two that
follow it are the ones that tell you how the society worked.

## Three prices

The sequence runs:

> §196. If a man destroy the eye of another man, they shall destroy his eye.
>
> §198. If one destroy the eye of a freeman or break the bone of a freeman, he
> shall pay one mana of silver.
>
> §199. If one destroy the eye of a man's slave or break a bone of a man's slave,
> he shall pay one-half his price.

Three classes, and the same injury has three different consequences depending
entirely on who was hurt. For the first, retaliation in kind. For the second, a
fixed sum. For the third, compensation — paid not to the injured person but to
the person who owns him, and calculated as a percentage of his market value.

Harper's "freeman" translates a term for a class of dependants below the full
citizen and above the slave, and its exact standing is still argued. What the law
does not leave in doubt is where it sits: it costs a mana to blind one, and the
mana is not paid to him.

## Priced the same way going in

The structure repeats in a place where you might expect it not to — the medical
fees.

> §215. If a physician operate on a man for a severe wound with a bronze lancet
> and save the man's life, or open an abscess in the eye of a man and save that
> man's eye, he shall receive ten shekels of silver.
>
> §216. If he be a freeman, he shall receive five shekels.
>
> §217. If it be a man's slave, the owner of the slave shall give two shekels of
> silver to the physician.

The identical operation, on the identical organ, costs ten, five or two according
to the patient. And the doctor's exposure follows the same ladder: if he destroys
a full citizen's eye with the lancet, they cut off his fingers; if the patient is
a slave, he replaces him or pays half his value.

A surgeon in Babylon knew the price of what he was about to do before he knew the
diagnosis.

## What that makes the collection

Read this way, the famous retaliatory clauses are not the primitive core of the
laws with the money penalties as refinements. They are one rung of a single
schedule.

Retaliation in kind is what you get when the parties are equals; between unequals
the injury converts into silver, and the direction of the payment follows
property rather than pain. *An eye for an eye* has been quoted for three thousand
years as a rule about proportion. In its original setting it is a rule about
proportion between men of the same rank, and the collection is at its most
articulate precisely where the ranks differ.

None of this is hidden or grudging. It is set out plainly, on a public monument,
in the same beautifully cut script as the prologue about the king to whom the
gods entrusted the care of the people.
