Hammurabi died in 1750. His son Samsu-iluna reigned thirty-eight years and spent
almost all of them losing what his father had taken.

The south went first. In 1741 a man calling himself Rim-Sin — probably taking the
name of the king of Larsa whom Hammurabi had deposed — led a revolt; then a
dynasty that called itself the Sealand established itself in the far south and
was never dislodged. By 1738 the great southern cities had been lost, and what
happened to them next is one of the strangest events in Mesopotamian history.

## The cities stop

Nippur, Ur, Uruk, Larsa and Isin were substantially abandoned. Not sacked and
rebuilt: abandoned. The temple establishments moved north, taking their gods and
their personnel and their archives with them. The administrative record, which
had run without a break in some of these places for six hundred years, stops.
Sumerian, which had survived the fall of Ur as a written language of cult and
scholarship in the southern schools, largely relocates north with the scribes.

The cause is argued about — a shift in the Euphrates channel starving the
southern canals, salinisation, the political collapse itself, or all three
feeding each other. What is not argued about is the scale. The heartland of
Sumerian civilisation, the ground the previous record on this site governed from,
went quiet for roughly three hundred years.

## What was left

Samsu-iluna's successors held Babylon and its immediate region and not much else,
and did so for another hundred and forty years — four kings, father to son, in
the same line that runs through the whole dynasty. There are eleven of them from
Sumu-abum to Samsu-ditana across three hundred years, and from the second onward
the throne passes father to son without one contested accession on record.

This record's `succession_rule` field is null, and that is the reason it is worth
dwelling on rather than passing over. Ten consecutive father-to-son successions
produced no statement of a rule. The dynasty legislated the price of
hiring an ox and the compensation owed for a slapped face, and did not legislate
who inherited. The Ur III record two entries earlier in this region carries the
identical null across five generations for the identical reason.

Coding rule 7 says the field takes the rule and not the outcome, and the temptation
here is obvious: ten consecutive father-to-son successions look exactly like
primogeniture and it would be easy to write the word in. It would also be inventing a statute,
which is the same act hard rule 1 forbids in the citation field, committed in the
vocabulary instead.

Two records in one region carrying the same null for the same reason is not a
coincidence about two dynasties. It is a fact about what cuneiform law was for,
and the honest place to record it is an empty field with an argument attached.
