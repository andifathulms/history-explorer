The stele went to Susa as plunder. Around 1100 BC, five hundred years after
Hammurabi, an Elamite king carried it out of Babylonia along with a great deal
else, and it stood in his capital until his capital stopped existing.

Then it lay in the ground for three thousand years, and in December 1901 and
January 1902 a French expedition under de Morgan dug it out of the acropolis at
Susa.

## The object

It is a block of black diorite nearly eight feet high, found broken into three
pieces that were easily rejoined.

On the front, above the text, is a relief of the king receiving the laws from
the sun god. Below that are sixteen columns, of which the first four and a half
are the prologue. On the back are twenty-eight more, the last five forming the
epilogue.

There should be five further columns on the front. They are not there. The
Elamite who took it had them cut away — presumably to carve his own inscription
in the space, which he never did.

So the most famous legal document of the ancient world reaches us with a hole
in it, deliberately made, by the man who stole it, for a purpose he never
carried out.

## It was never only one stone

A second fragment turned up at Susa which does not belong to that monument at
all and carries text from what would be its forty-first column. That is the
proof of something the epilogue itself implies: the code was set up in more
than one place, and at least two copies had been carried off to the same
Elamite city.

Beyond the steles there are the tablets. Copies of the laws were being made by
scribes for more than a thousand years after Hammurabi died, and enough of
those later fragments survive that Harper could use them in 1904 to restore
passages where the stone was damaged.

A school exercise copied in Assyria eight centuries after the fact is what
allows a modern editor to repair a monument defaced in Elam.

## How it was read when it came back

The reception is the last part of the afterlife, and it is worth recording
exactly.

Harper's introduction, describing the relief of the king receiving the laws
from the god, adds immediately that the story of Moses receiving the Ten Words
from Yahweh corresponds to it. He identifies Hammurabi, as most Assyriologists
then did, with the Amraphel of Genesis 14. And he dates the reign to about 2250
BC, which is roughly five hundred and fifty years too early by any chronology
now in use.

None of that is careless. It is what the document meant to the people who
recovered it: a confirmation, in stone, that the world of the Hebrew Bible had
a verifiable background — and the first sustained encounter of European
scholarship with a legal system older than the one it had been taught to regard
as the oldest.

The First Dynasty of Babylon lasted about three hundred years and ruled an
empire for fifty of them. What it is now is a rock in the Louvre, and the
reason it is famous is that somebody stole the rock.
