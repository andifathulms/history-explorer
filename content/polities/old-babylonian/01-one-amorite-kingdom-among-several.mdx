The century after Ur fell is a period of Amorite dynasties. The Amorites are the
people the Ur III texts described as not knowing grain and not burying their
dead, and within three generations of that wall their chiefs were kings in every
significant Mesopotamian city, ruling with Akkadian names, Akkadian titles and
Sumerian scribes.

Babylon was not one of the significant cities. It had existed for a long time and
had done nothing in particular. In 1894 BC a man named Sumu-abum established a
dynasty there, and for the next hundred years his successors held a small
territory around it while the real powers were elsewhere — Isin and then Larsa in
the south, Eshnunna on the Diyala, Mari on the middle Euphrates, and Shamshi-Adad's
kingdom in the north.

## The world before the empire

This is the best-documented political landscape of the ancient world before the
Amarna letters, and it comes almost entirely from one building. The palace of
Zimri-Lim at Mari was excavated from 1933 and produced something over twenty
thousand tablets, mostly letters: reports from ambassadors, intelligence on troop
movements, complaints about allies, the correspondence of a king with his
officials and his wives and his diviners.

The single most quoted sentence in the whole corpus is a diplomat's summary of
the balance of power:

> There is no king who is strong on his own. Ten or fifteen kings follow
> Hammurabi of Babylon, the same number follow Rim-Sin of Larsa, the same number
> follow Ibal-pi-El of Eshnunna, the same number follow Amut-pi-El of Qatna, and
> twenty kings follow Yarim-Lim of Yamhad.

Hammurabi is first on the list and is one of five. The letter dates from
somewhere around 1770, roughly twenty years into a forty-three-year reign, and it
is a fair description: he had been king for two decades and had achieved
essentially nothing.

## What kind of state

The `military_basis` field on this record reads `land-grant` alone, and it is one
of very few institution fields anywhere on this site that can be read directly
off a statute rather than inferred from a narrative.

The *ilkum* was a field held from the palace by a soldier — the *redum*, the
regular, and the *ba'irum*, whose title probably means something like "fisherman"
and by this period means a category of soldier — on condition of service. The
land could not be sold. If the holder failed to go on campaign and sent a
substitute, he lost it. If he was taken prisoner and came home, he got it back;
if his son was old enough to serve, the son took it; if not, a third of it was
assigned to the mother to raise him on.

All of that is in the law collection, in sixteen consecutive paragraphs. It is
the earliest place in this corpus where a coded institution is legislation rather
than reconstruction, and it is a reasonable question, taken up two chapters
later, whether the legislation described a practice or proposed one.
