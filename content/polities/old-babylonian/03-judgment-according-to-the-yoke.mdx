Hammurabi's letters to Sin-idinnam, his governor at Larsa, survive in quantity,
and they are the least famous important documents of the ancient world. The
king who set up a stele of laws in a temple also spent his days writing to a
provincial governor about corn.

## What the king actually wrote about

The subjects, in the order King groups them, are these.

Land disputes. He instructs the governor to restore a property to its owner
whose claim has been fully proved, and to force a man to hand back mortgaged
property although the mortgage had been redeemed.

Corn. He forwards for trial the case of a man who says grain was stolen from
his granary; orders that a loan of corn be repaid with the interest on it; and
settles a long-running argument about the rent owed on a field out of the crop
that field produced.

Arrests. Three letters order men taken into custody and sent to Babylon. In one
of them the men are eight officials who, the king notes, *have not gone to
their posts* — insubordinates who had disobeyed him. In the other two he does
not say what they had done.

Sentences. In one case, sending a matter down for trial, he specifies the
punishment he wants if the man is convicted: judgment *according to the yoke* —
hard labour in the king's service.

## The revenue

The letters show a tax system in operation, and one detail in it is worth more
than the rest.

The provinces owed revenue; the governors and local authorities were
responsible for collecting it; and a collector who failed to bring in his full
dues had to make up the shortfall out of his own pocket. Hammurabi forwards
Sin-idinnam a complaint from exactly such a man — someone who had been
prevented from collecting in certain districts and from whom the palace had
nevertheless taken the whole amount.

He also has to write urgently summoning a collector to Babylon, because
collectors, understandably, preferred not to come.

## The calendar

The most revealing letter is about the calendar and is really about money.

The king informs the governor that an intercalary month is to be inserted — a
routine adjustment, since a lunar year needs correcting against the seasons.
Then he adds the point of the letter: the change is not to affect the day on
which Larsa's tribute falls due.

A ruler adding a month to the year, and specifying in the same breath that the
tax deadline does not move with it, is the whole of Old Babylonian government
in one sentence.

## The other Hammurabi

Set these letters beside the stele and the two do not contradict each other so
much as explain it.

The famous document opens by saying that the king was called to make justice
appear in the land, to destroy the wicked, and to keep the strong from
oppressing the weak. The letters show what that meant in practice for the man
who wrote it: reading a complaint from a tax collector who had been made to pay
someone else's arrears, ruling on a disputed field boundary, and telling a
governor four hundred kilometres away which of two men was telling the truth
about a granary.
