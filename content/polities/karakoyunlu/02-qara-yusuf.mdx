---
title: The man who came back
drafted_from: chi-6
phase: expansion
---

Timur took Azerbaijan in the 1380s and gave it to his son Miran Shah. Qara Yusuf,
who had inherited the Black Sheep from his brother, could not fight him and did
not try for long: he went west, to the Ottoman sultan Bayezid, and when Timur
destroyed Bayezid at Ankara in 1402 he went south to the Mamluks in Cairo, who
imprisoned him for a while at Timur's suggestion and then let him go.

This is the pattern of the period and it is worth naming, because it explains
why these confederations were so hard to finish. A tribal confederation can be
beaten out of a territory without being destroyed. The pastures remain, the
tribes remain, the leader goes somewhere else and waits, and when the power that
beat him dies — Timur died in 1405 — he comes back to a country where nothing
has been built that can stop him.

Qara Yusuf came back. In 1406 he beat a Timurid army at Nakhchivan. In 1408, at
Sardrud near Tabriz, he beat and killed Miran Shah, Timur's own son, and took
Azerbaijan. He then took Baghdad from the Jalayirids and finished them, which is
how the last of the Ilkhanate's real successors ended: not conquered by an
empire but absorbed by their own auxiliaries.

For the next thirty years the Black Sheep held Azerbaijan against the Timurids of
Herat, who came west three times under Shah Rukh and each time took Tabriz and
each time went home again, because holding Azerbaijan from Herat required a
garrison Shah Rukh was not prepared to leave. In 1438 he settled it the other
way: he recognised Jahan Shah, the son of Qara Yusuf, as his governor in
Azerbaijan. It was a diploma from a Timurid to a Turkmen, and within a decade
the Turkmen was the larger power.
