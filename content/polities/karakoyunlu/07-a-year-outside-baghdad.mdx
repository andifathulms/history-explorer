title: A year outside Baghdad

What Jahan Shah did after giving Herat back is the clearest illustration anybody
could want of why these confederations do not last three generations.

He had gone east in 1458 and taken the Timurid capital. Six months later two
things arrived at once: Abu Sa'id preparing to attack him from the Timurid side,
and news that his son Hasan, imprisoned in Azerbaijan, had escaped and was in
rebellion. He made peace with Abu Sa'id and went west, and the march is the
detail to keep — twelve parasangs a day, something like forty-five miles, sustained
long enough to cost him twenty thousand camels and ten thousand horses. That is
the transport of a very large army, destroyed by its own commander in order to
get home before his son could consolidate.

He put Hasan down. Then he moved his other son, Pir Budaq, out of the government
of Fars and gave him Baghdad instead. Pir Budaq revolted in Baghdad. So Jahan
Shah besieged his own son in his own second city for a whole year, took it,
killed him, installed a third son in his place, and went back to Azerbaijan.

At the end of that his realm ran from the Ottoman frontier to the two Iraqs,
Kerman and the Persian Gulf — nearly the whole of Iran except Khurasan and the
Caspian provinces. It is the largest thing any Turkmen confederation ever held,
and it had been assembled and held together by a man who had spent the previous
decade fighting his own children for it, and who had nothing to hand it to that
would not immediately do the same.

He did not get the chance to find out. In 1466 he attacked Uzun Hasan, meaning
to take Diyarbakr, and was surprised and killed by him while lingering behind
his own army on a hunting trip. Two of his sons were taken prisoner and most of
his senior nobles were killed with him. The chronogram composed for it makes the
whole event turn on the killer's name.

The one substantial character sketch of him comes from the Ottoman historian
Munajjim-bashi, whom Browne uses for this dynasty and who evidently disliked him
thoroughly: a dissolute, immoral, bloodthirsty tyrant, inclined to heresy and
atheism, who paid no attention to the sacred law, spent his nights until dawn in
revelry and slept like a dog through the day, for which reason he was called the
Bat. He was seventy, he had reigned thirty-two years, and he was buried at
Tabriz.

That is a hostile Sunni Ottoman writing about a Shi'i Turkmen who had tried to
take Herat, and it is not evidence about his character. It is evidence of
something else, which is that the man who held more of Iran than anybody between
Timur and Shah Isma'il left behind no account of himself that his own side
wrote. His poetry survives, under the pen-name Haqiqi, and it is the only
first-person thing there is.
