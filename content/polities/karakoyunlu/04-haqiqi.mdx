Jahan Shah wrote poetry. He wrote it in Azerbaijani Turkic and in Persian, under
the pen-name Haqiqi, and a divan of it survives; Minorsky edited a selection
from manuscript in 1954 and discussed the dialect it is in. The verse is
mystical, in the tradition that runs from Nasimi, and it is the work of a ruling
sultan rather than of a court poet writing for one.

The site's Karakhanid page had to argue that a Turkic literature existed at all
in the eleventh century, against a habit of treating Turkic-speaking dynasties
as patrons of Persian and nothing else. Jahan Shah is four centuries later and
the argument is different: not whether Turkic literature existed, but that a
Turkmen confederation usually filed as an interval between the Mongols and the
Safavids produced a sovereign who was a serious poet in it.

He also built. The Blue Mosque at Tabriz, finished in 1465, is one of the great
buildings of Iranian architecture — its tilework is the reason it has the name —
and it survives in fragments after an earthquake and two centuries of neglect.
The northern iwan of the Darb-i Imam at Isfahan was built under him in 1453 and
carries tile decoration that has been the subject of a substantial modern
literature about the geometry it uses.

Political weight and cultural output come apart everywhere, and usually they
belong to different people — the patron and the poet, the sultan and the
painter. Jahan Shah is the harder version, because he is both. The man who held
Azerbaijan, Iraq, Fars and Kerman is the same man who wrote the poems, and the
reason nobody reads them is not that they are bad. It is that his confederation
lost, and losing confederations do not get anthologised.
