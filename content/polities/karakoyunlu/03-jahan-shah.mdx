Jahan Shah ruled from 1438 to 1467 and was, for most of that time, the strongest
Muslim ruler between the Ottomans and Herat. He is not much read about, and the
reason is structural rather than historiographical: he lost at the end, to a man
whose confederation lasted only thirty years longer, and everything both of them
built was taken over by a dynasty that was better at explaining itself.

He began as Shah Rukh's appointee in Azerbaijan and stopped being one the moment
Shah Rukh died in 1447.

<Figure id="jahan-shah-coin" /> He took Sultaniyya and Qazvin, then Iraq — Baghdad in
1446 — then Fars and Kerman, then most of central Iran. In 1458 he took Herat
itself, the Timurid capital, which is as far as a Kara Koyunlu army ever
reached.

He could not keep it. His son rebelled in Azerbaijan behind him, and he had to
turn round and go home, and the Timurid Abu Sa'id came back into Khurasan. That
is the ceiling of this kind of polity, stated as plainly as the record allows: a
confederation that can put a very large cavalry army anywhere in Iran cannot
occupy two ends of it at once, because what holds the army together is the
leader's presence, and there is one of him.

The territory at its widest ran from eastern Anatolia through Azerbaijan and
Iraq to Fars and Kerman. Nobody has published a figure for it, which is the
ordinary condition of a state that held its greatest extent for under a
decade.

What he did not build was a succession. The confederation was held the way the
Seljuks and the Mongols and the Ghurids had held theirs — as a family
possession, divided at each death and fought over afterwards — and he spent a
great deal of his reign fighting his own sons for it. In 1467 he took an army
west against the White Sheep and was defeated and killed at Chapakchur.
