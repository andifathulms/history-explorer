In 1458 Jahan Shah invaded Khurasan and occupied Herat, which is the furthest a
Kara Koyunlu army ever went. He then handed it back.

## Why he gave it up

Browne records the sequence without explanation, and the explanation is
structural. Jahan Shah took Herat from the Timurids and afterwards relinquished
it to Abu Sa'id, who had spent the previous decade assembling what was left of
the Timurid inheritance and was not going to accept the loss of Khurasan.

A confederation can win a campaign a long way from its pastures. It cannot
garrison one. Holding Herat would have meant leaving Turkmen tribes in eastern
Iran indefinitely, away from the grazing that was the reason they followed him,
in a province with a Timurid claimant in the field. The arithmetic of the thing
does not work, and Jahan Shah appears to have known it.

So he came back west with the prestige and without the city, having demonstrated
that he could reach Herat and could not keep it.

## Ten years of standing still

The decade after that is the contraction, and almost nothing happens in it that
a chronicler thought worth recording, which is itself the fact.

Jahan Shah held Azerbaijan, the two Iraqs, Fars and Kerman. He built at Tabriz.
He wrote. His son Hasan Ali quarrelled with him and was imprisoned. And on the
western flank, in the country between Diyarbakr and Lake Van, Uzun Hasan of the
White Sheep was doing what Jahan Shah's own father had done fifty years earlier
— assembling tribes, marrying into neighbours, and waiting.

The Black Sheep did not deal with him and did not destroy him. There was no
particular moment at which that decision was taken; it is simply what a large
confederation does about a small one, until the small one is not small.

## Caught hunting

On 30 October 1467 Uzun Hasan attacked, and the manner of it is the whole
lesson.

He took Jahan Shah off his guard while the ruler was away from his army on a
hunting expedition. The sources disagree about where: Browne, following
Munajjim-bashi, puts the engagement near Khoy in Azerbaijan; the more usual
location is Chapakchur, in the hills between Mush and Bingöl, three hundred
kilometres west. Nobody has reconciled them.

What is not disputed is that the ruler of a state stretching from eastern
Anatolia to Kerman was killed because he had gone hunting without his army, and
that the state did not survive him by a year.
