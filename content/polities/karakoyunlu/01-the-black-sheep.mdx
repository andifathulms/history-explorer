This site's Ilkhanate page ends in 1335 with a fragmentation that has no
conqueror to name, and its Timurid page begins with a man who could not be
khan. Between and around those two facts, eastern Anatolia, Azerbaijan and Iraq
were held for most of the fifteenth century by two Turkmen confederations named
after sheep.

The Kara Koyunlu — the Black Sheep, from the animal on their standards — were
Oghuz Turkmen of the Yiwa, and the ruling house came from the Baharlu. Their
country was the pasture north of Lake Van and towards Mosul, and their first
appearance in the political record is as somebody's clients: in the 1370s they
were vassals of the Jalayirids, who had taken Iraq and Azerbaijan out of the
Ilkhanate's collapse and could not hold them without Turkmen cavalry.

Bayram Khwaja and then Qara Muhammad turned that position into an independent
one, in the way such groups do, by being the largest coherent armed body in a
country where the state had stopped. Roemer's account of the century in the
Cambridge History is careful about what this means: these were not migrations
or invasions. The tribes were already there, had been for two centuries, and
what changed in the fourteenth century was that the apparatus which had been
taxing them and hiring them stopped functioning, and the confederations became
the government by default.

Then Timur arrived, and being the largest armed body in Azerbaijan turned out
to be the wrong thing to be.
