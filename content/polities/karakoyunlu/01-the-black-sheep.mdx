This site's Ilkhanate page ends in 1335 with a fragmentation that has no
conqueror to name, and its Timurid page begins with a man who could not be
khan. Between and around those two facts, eastern Anatolia, Azerbaijan and
Iraq were held for most of the fifteenth century by two Turkmen confederations
named after sheep.

The Kara Koyunlu — the Black Sheep — were Oghuz pastoralists in the country
around Lake Van, and they emerged as a power in the 1370s the way such groups
do: by being the largest coherent armed body in a place where the state had
stopped. They took Tabriz, lost it to Timur, got it back after he died, and
under Qara Yusuf and then Jahan Shah assembled something that was more than a
confederation and never quite a state.

Jahan Shah is the one worth the page. He ruled from 1438 to 1467 and held
Azerbaijan, Iraq, Fars and much of central Iran; he was the strongest ruler
between the Ottomans and the Timurids of Herat; he built the Blue Mosque at
Tabriz, which is one of the great buildings of Iranian architecture and is
still standing in fragments; and he wrote Turkic poetry under a pen-name, which
is worth registering in a corpus where this site's Karakhanid page had to argue
that Turkic literature existed at all.

What he did not build was a succession or an administration that could survive
him, and the record here codes appanage for the reason it does on the Seljuk,
Mongol and Ghurid pages: the confederation was held as a family possession and
divided at each death, and the divisions were fought over. In 1467 he took an
army against the rival confederation to his west and was defeated and killed at
Chapakchur. Within a year the Black Sheep had lost everything to the White, and
this page ends.
