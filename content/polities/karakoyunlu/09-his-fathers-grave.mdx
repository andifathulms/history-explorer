Uzun Hasan killed Jahan Shah, destroyed his confederation, took his provinces
and his capital — and then buried him in his father's tomb.

## The burial

Browne records it in a subordinate clause, the way the chroniclers did. Having
put the captured Timurid Abu Sa'id to death, Uzun Hasan suffered Jahan Shah's
body to be buried in the grave of his father Qara Yusuf.

It is worth stopping on, because nothing else about the Kara Koyunlu was
preserved with that much care.

Qara Yusuf had spent his life being chased around western Asia by Timur, had
sheltered with the Jalayirid Sultan Ahmad and then killed him, and had built the
confederation his son enlarged. Putting the son into the father's grave is a
statement about lineage made by the man who had just extinguished it — the
courtesy one Turkmen chief extends to another, and an assertion that this was a
family with a tomb worth being in, which Uzun Hasan's own family was in the
process of becoming.

## What did not survive

Everything else went.

There was no restoration and no claimant. Hasan Ali could not hold Tabriz for a
year. The tribes reappear inside the Ak Koyunlu and then, thirty years later,
inside the Qizilbash, which is where the human material of both confederations
ended up — the same men, or their sons, following a Sufi order instead of a
pasture agreement. Nobody afterwards thought the Black Sheep name was worth
taking.

Ninety-four years, a state that reached Herat, and no successor willing to claim
it.

## And the poems

The one thing that came through is the least likely.

Jahan Shah wrote under the pen-name Haqiqi, in Turkish and in Persian, and the
divan was not lost — it survived in manuscript, unread, until a twentieth-century
scholar went looking and published it. A ruler who is a footnote in every
political history of the fifteenth century turns out to have left a body of
lyric verse by his own hand.

Set that beside what happened to his neighbour. Uzun Hasan, who beat him, has no
comparable self-portrait in any eastern source at all; what survives of him is a
Venetian ambassador's account of his table manners. Jahan Shah left several
thousand lines in his own voice and is not read, because his side lost and
losing confederations do not get anthologised.

The tomb was the courtesy. The poems are the accident.
