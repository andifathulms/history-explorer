Everything anybody knows about how the Lombards got to Italy was written down
two centuries later by a monk descended from one of them, and he was honest
enough to say when he was repeating a story rather than a record.

Paul the Deacon puts the crossing in 568. Alboin had just destroyed the Gepid
kingdom on the middle Danube, killing its king Cunimund in the battle and — Paul
says this flatly, in the middle of an otherwise dry campaign narrative — making
a drinking cup out of his skull. He carried off Cunimund's daughter Rosemund
and married her, *to his own injury, as afterwards appeared*. Four years later
she had him killed.

There was almost nothing in Italy to stop the army that followed him. Justinian's
war against the Ostrogoths had finished fifteen years earlier and had taken
eighteen years to finish; what it left behind was a peninsula with no field army
in it. The Po valley went quickly. Pavia held out for three years and then
became the capital.

## The dukes

Alboin's successor Cleph was killed by a servant of his own household after a
year and a half.

And then the Lombards did not replace him. Paul says they had no king for ten
years and were under dukes — Zaban at Pavia, Wallari at Bergamo, Alichis at
Brescia, Euin at Trent, Gisulf at Cividale, *and there were thirty other dukes
besides these in their own cities*. Three other chronicles say twelve years
rather than ten, and there is no way to settle it.

What ended it was an invasion. The emperor Maurice paid the Frankish king fifty
thousand solidi to come into Italy and drive the Lombards out, and the dukes,
facing a Frankish army and a Byzantine subsidy at the same time, agreed by common
consent that Cleph's son Authari should be king. To pay for the restored crown
they gave up half their own possessions.

That is the founding transaction, and it fixed the shape of the kingdom for two
hundred years. The crown existed because the dukes had decided it was cheaper
than the alternative, and they had shown they could do without one. Spoleto and
Benevento in the south stayed effectively independent for the whole period and
occasionally fought the king. No Lombard king ever held all of Italy.

## The golden age that was not

Paul's account of the years after the restoration is worth quoting because of
what it is doing.

> There was no violence, no ambuscades were laid, no one constrained another
> unjustly, no one took spoils, there were no thefts, no robberies, every one
> proceeded whither he pleased, safe and without fear.

The footnote printed under that sentence, crediting a German commentator,
disposes of it in one line: *this description of the golden age is not borne out
by the facts*. It is not: the same book has Roman
landowners killed or driven out under Cleph, the subject population parcelled
out among Lombard "guests", and a war with the Franks running through the
decade in question.

The passage is a Lombard who had spent years at the court of the king who ended
the Lombard kingdom, describing the moment his people first made themselves a
state.
It is not evidence about the 580s. It is evidence about the 780s, and about what
a conquered people's historian wanted his conquerors to read.
