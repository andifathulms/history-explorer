The kingdom's memorial was written by a man who went to the court of the king
who destroyed it, to beg for his brother's life.

Paul was born in Friuli somewhere around 720, into a family that had come into
Italy with Alboin. He was educated at a Lombard court, learned Greek, and by the
760s was attached to Arichis, duke of Benevento, and his wife Adelperga, who was
a daughter of Desiderius — the king Charlemagne would depose. Paul wrote
Adelperga an acrostic poem whose initial letters spell *Adelperga Pia*. When she
complained that the Roman history he had given her to read was too short, he
wrote her a longer one. For most of his working life he was a client of the last
Lombard royal house.

## Benevento

In 774 the kingdom ended and the southern duchy did not.

Benevento had been effectively its own country for two centuries and it was
never taken. In the same year the northern kingdom fell, Arichis stopped calling
himself duke and started calling himself prince. That is the whole of the
Lombard state's continuation: one southern principality, the title upgraded in
the year of the disaster, going on issuing Lombard law and calling itself Lombard
long after there was no king in Pavia.

## The letter from the Moselle

Paul's brother Arichis — named for the grandfather, not the duke — was taken
prisoner after a Lombard rising in Friuli against the Franks, and his property
was confiscated and his family reduced to beggary. Paul went north to ask
Charlemagne for him.

He was kept there. The letters he sent back to Monte Cassino are the letters of
a man who cannot leave:

> I am kept here for a while only by a feeling of pity, only by the injunctions
> of love, only by the demand of the soul, and what is still more than all this,
> by the quiet power of our lord, the king.

He asks the abbot to write with news, and for the names of the brothers who have
died, *for I hear that many of them have died*. He signs off with three lines of
verse noting the date and that the letter was sent from the shore of the glassy
Moselle.

The prisoners were eventually released. And then something odd happened, which
is that Paul and the king began exchanging joke poems. Charlemagne, or a court
poet writing as him, teases Paul with three questions: will he wear heavy chains,
or lie in a dungeon, or go and convert the king of the Northmen? Paul answers
that the Northmen have no Latin and will seem to him like shaggy goats and he to
them like a dumb beast, but he has no fear, because if they know he comes under
Charlemagne's name they will not dare lift a finger — and if their king refuses
baptism, Paul will drag him to the foot of the throne with his hands tied behind
his back, and Thor and Woden will not help him.

That is a Lombard whose people had just been conquered, whose brother had just
been a hostage, writing comic verse for the conqueror. It is also what a change
of dynasty rather than a destruction actually looks like from underneath: the
kingdom continued, and its educated men went to work for the new management.

## The ash tree

Paul went home to Monte Cassino and wrote the history of his people, and he put
his own family into Book IV.

His great-grandfather Lopichis was one of five brothers carried off by the Avars
in a raid on Friuli. Four stayed in captivity. Lopichis, grown, escaped, and set
out west through the mountains with no idea of the way. A wolf appeared and went
in front of him, looking back, and he took it for a guide from heaven. His bread
ran out; starving, he tried to shoot the wolf to eat it and it slipped away; he
lay down on the ground expecting to die, and dreamed of a man telling him to
walk in the direction opposite to the way his feet were pointing, *for there is
Italy which you are seeking*. He woke and walked that way. An old Slav woman
took him in and fed him a little at a time, so that feeding him properly would
not kill him.

He reached the house he had been born in. It had no roof and was full of brambles
and thorns. When he had cut them down he found a large ash tree growing inside
the walls, and hung his quiver on it. Relatives helped him rebuild. He never got
back any of his father's property, because the people holding it had held it too
long.

That is what a Lombard historian chose to put on the record about his own
descent: not a hero, but a returned prisoner reclaiming a ruin and losing the
lawsuit.

## What did not get written

Paul died before the book was finished. It stops in 744, at Liutprand's death,
thirty years short of the year it was written in the shadow of. He never wrote
the fall.

Two other things of his outlasted it. He compiled the collection of homilies
Charlemagne had asked the empire's churches to use. And a hymn to John the
Baptist — the protecting saint of the Lombards — was attributed to him, on
authority his editors call doubtful; each of its lines begins a step higher than
the last, and a monk at Arezzo three centuries later took the first syllable of
each line and made it the name of a note: *ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la*.

Whether or not the hymn is his, the kingdom that had no letters worth the name
in Rothari's day produced the man who was plausibly credited with it, and the
history of the Lombards exists because he sat down and wrote it after there were
no more Lombard kings to write it for.
