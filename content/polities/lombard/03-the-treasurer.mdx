Almost everything known about the Lombard conquest was written by Lombards, two
centuries after the fact. One substantial archive survives from the other side of
it, written while it was happening, and it belongs to the man who had to live
with it: the bishop of Rome from 590 to 604 left something over eight hundred
letters, and a great many of them are about Lombards standing outside his walls.

They are not history. They are correspondence — Gregory writing to generals,
exarchs, bishops and the emperor about grain, hostages, ransoms, unpaid soldiers
and the terms of a truce, in the ordinary irritable voice of a man doing a job he
did not expect to be doing.

## Running a war

The duke of Spoleto at this period was Ariulf, and the letters show Gregory
handling him as a head of government would.

To two commanders of imperial troops, on the news that Ariulf was close: we gave
thanks to God for your safety, we were afraid the soldiers we sent might fall
into his hands, our own commander here has prepared himself against him, and if
the enemy advances this way, do what you can in his rear.

To the same two, a little later: Ariulf has sent us this letter, which we
forward to you. Read it, find out whether the people of Suana have kept the
loyalty they promised, take adequate hostages from them, bind them again by
oaths, restore the pledge you took from them, and bring them to a right mind by
your discourses. But if you establish that they have already treated with Ariulf
about surrendering, or given him hostages, then — after wholesome deliberation,
lest your souls or mine be burdened with respect to our oaths — do whatever you
judge to be to the advantage of the republic.

That is a bishop authorising his correspondents to break an oath if the military
situation requires it, and worrying in writing about whose soul carries the cost.

To the bishop of Ravenna, explaining why he had not written: when Ariulf came to
the Roman city and killed some and mutilated others, I was affected with such
great sadness as to fall into a colic sickness. Then the complaint underneath it.
The imperial commander in Italy both evades fighting the enemy and forbids us to
make peace with him; the soldiery has been removed from the city of Rome; the
troops who remain have not been paid and are with difficulty induced to guard the
walls. *How shall the city subsist, left destitute as it is by all, if it has not
peace?*

He made the peace himself, on his own responsibility, and was blamed for it.

## The letter to the emperor

The reprimand came back from Constantinople in the form of a compliment. The
emperor's orders had described Gregory as *simple*, and he understood exactly
what was meant.

> The Piety of my Lords in their most serene commands, while set on refuting me
> on certain matters, in sparing me has by no means spared me. For by the use
> therein of the term simplicity they politely call me silly.

He works through the scriptural senses of simplicity for a paragraph, doves and
serpents, and then concedes the charge in the only way that lets him make his
point:

> I therefore, who am denounced in the most serene commands of my Lords as
> simple without the addition of prudence, as having been deceived by the
> cunning of Ariulph, am plainly and undoubtedly called silly; which I also
> myself acknowledge to be the case. For, though your Piety were silent, the
> facts cry out. For, if I had not been silly, I should by no means have come to
> endure what I suffer in this place among the swords of the Lombards.

Then the substance. He is also accused of lying about Ariulf's willingness to
come to terms; he has long been aware that the emperor believes Nordulf before
him and Leo before him. He would pass over contempt and ridicule of himself in
silence if the captivity of his land were not increasing daily — but from his
being charged with falsehood it follows that Italy is led captive under the
Lombard yoke, his representations are not believed, and the strength of the enemy
grows hugely. Believe facts rather than words, he tells the emperor, and think
anything bad you like about me.

## What the Church was paying

The line that explains the next two centuries of Italian history is in another
letter, dropped in among complaints about a bishop's title and an irregular
ordination in Dalmatia.

It is now seven years, Gregory writes, that we have been living in this city
among the swords of the Lombards. How much is expended on them daily by this
Church, so that we may be able to live among them, is not to be told. Then the
comparison, which he clearly thought was the clinching one: as in the region of
Ravenna your Piety maintains a treasurer to meet the daily expenses of the first
army of Italy, so I also in this city am your treasurer for such purposes. And
this Church, which already spends unceasingly on clergy, monasteries, the poor
and the people, is spending on the Lombards as well.

A Roman bishop is telling a Roman emperor that the see of Peter has become the
paymaster of central Italy — negotiating with the enemy, garrisoning the walls,
ransoming captives and buying the invaders off out of church revenue — because
the empire has stopped sending anything but reprimands.

Elsewhere he describes what he could see from those walls while the corn was
running short: Romans led away with ropes round their necks, like dogs, to be
sold in France. He had been delivering a course of homilies on Ezekiel when the
siege began and abandoned it, and told his congregation why. Let no one blame me
if I cease my speaking, since, as you all see, our tribulations have increased;
we are surrounded on all sides by swords.

## The thing that was made

The Lombard kingdom did not intend to create the papal state. It intended to take
Italy, and it never quite managed either.

What it did was destroy the alternative. By the time Gregory died there was no
imperial administration in central Italy capable of defending Rome, and the only
institution left that could raise money, hire soldiers, treat with a Lombard duke
and be obeyed was the bishopric — which had acquired all of those functions in
fourteen years of letters, complaining the whole way that they were not its job.
A hundred and fifty years later a pope with exactly those capabilities, and by
then the habit of using them, would send across the Alps for a Frankish army.
