On 22 November 643 King Rothari published three hundred and eighty-eight
chapters of law. Paul the Deacon's sentence about it is one line: the king
collected in a series of writings the laws of the Lombards, which they had been
keeping in memory only and by custom, and directed the code to be called the
Edict.

A later chronicle is blunter about what changed. By Rothari, it says, law and
justice began among the Lombards, and the judges first went through them in
writing; before that, lawsuits were decided by custom, discretion and usage.

## The tariff

Most of the Edict is a price list for injuries.

Every tooth is named and costs its own figure. So are the fingers and the toes.
Bodily injuries are catalogued down that level of detail, with a separate scale
for what is owed when the injured party is an *aldius* — half-free, and generally
understood to be where the conquered Roman population sat — or a household
slave, in which case the money is owed to the owner. Above all of it stands the
wergild, the price of a man's life, graded by rank.

There is a category for simply getting in someone's way. To place yourself in
the path of a free man, without doing him any bodily harm, costs twenty solidi.
To do the same to a free woman or a girl costs nine hundred.

The purpose of a schedule that exhaustive is to make the feud unnecessary by
putting a price on every injury that could start one. It is a document written
by people who expected the alternative to be killing.

## Whose law

The Edict binds Lombards. It mentions Romans as such exactly once, in a clause
fixing what is owed for an offence against another man's bondwoman, where a
Roman woman is priced lower than a slave of Germanic origin.

Elsewhere it gives itself away by implication. One law speaks of *any free woman
living according to the law of the Lombards*, which means there were women who
were not; foreigners settling in the country were to live by Lombard law unless
the king granted them leave to live by some other. Roman law was not abolished
in Lombard Italy and was not officially recognised either. It went on
underneath, as custom, governing the private affairs of the people who had been
there first — and the fact that nobody recorded its disappearance is one of the
better arguments that it never disappeared.

Law here follows the person and not the ground. Two men standing in the same
street in Pavia could be under different law, and which one applied to a child
depended on the father.

## The spear

Property was given away in front of witnesses, at a *thing* — an assembly of at
least a few free men, the survivor of the old Germanic folk-moot. The verb for
it in the Latin of the Edict is *thingare*, to thing something away, and the act
itself is the *gairethinx*, the spear-donation. Rothari's law requires that a man
who wants to thing away his property do it not secretly but before free men.

The word is the whole institution in miniature: a Germanic assembly, a spear, a
Latin verb ending, and a king in a former Roman province writing it into a
statute.

## The seventy-seventh year

Rothari dated the Edict, and the way he dated it is the most deliberate thing
in the book.

Not by the emperor's regnal year, which is how a document produced anywhere in
the Roman world would have been dated. Not by the indiction. He counted from
the year the Lombards came into Italy, and made it the seventy-seventh.

That is a people declaring in a legal formula that its own arrival is the event
from which time is measured. Paul, writing in the eighth century, uses the
prologue to that Edict as his evidence for what happened in the sixth — and
tells a reader who doubts one of his stories to go and look it up there. The
code was the Lombards' account of themselves, and the history of the people was
written out of it.
