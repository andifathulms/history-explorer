In 643 King Rothari issued three hundred and eighty-eight chapters of Lombard
law, in Latin, with the consent of the army assembly. It is the earliest
surviving Lombard legislation and the earliest written law of the Lombards in any
form; the prologue says so, and says that what preceded it was custom held in
memory.

Most of it is a tariff. So much for a tooth, more for a front tooth, so much for
each finger by name, so much for a scar on the face, and a scale of wergild — the
price of a man's life, payable to his kin — graded by status. The purpose of a
compensation schedule that detailed is to make feud unnecessary by pricing every
injury it could arise from, and Rothari says as much: the aim is that the
composition should be accepted and the feud laid aside.

## Two codes, eleven years apart

This corpus's Visigothic record carries the *Liber Iudiciorum* of 654, and the
pair is the reason both records are worth having.

The Visigothic code is territorial: five hundred laws applying to every
inhabitant of the kingdom regardless of descent, built on Roman legal categories,
abolishing the distinction between Gothic and Roman law.

Rothari's Edict is personal: it binds Lombards and leaves Romans to Roman law,
and it is built on wergild rather than on Roman jurisprudence.

Two Germanic minorities ruling Roman majorities, legislating in Latin a decade
apart, arriving at opposite answers to whether law follows the person or the
ground. The Visigothic answer is the one that had a future — the territorial
principle is how every state in this corpus's modern half works — and the Lombard
one lasted longer in fact: Lombard law was still being pleaded in southern Italy
in the twelfth century, four hundred years after the kingdom ended.

## A state without a tax

This record's `revenue_basis` reads `land-tax, trade-toll` and the data comment
says the first value is doing a job the vocabulary has no word for.

There is very little evidence that the Roman land tax survived in Lombard Italy.
What the kings had were the *curtes regiae*, the royal estates, plus tolls at
river crossings and city gates and the judicial fines that a code like Rothari's
generates in quantity. The largest single item was the king's own property.

This site's Carolingian record names that gap first and its Holy Roman record
names it second. This is the third, which is worth stating because a pattern is
what eventually changes a vocabulary here — nine records asking the same question
produced the `investiture` value — and three is not nine, but it is no longer a
one-off. All three are in the Latin West, which is the point: the disappearance
of Roman taxation is the defining institutional fact about post-Roman western
Europe, and a corpus that codes revenue in seven values cannot currently say it.

The Visigothic and Ostrogothic records, which did keep a Roman land tax running,
are the control group.
