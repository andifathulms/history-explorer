Authari took a second name when the dukes made him king. He was called Flavius,
and Paul the Deacon says that all the kings of the Lombards afterwards used it.

It was not a Lombard name. It was the family name of Vespasian and Titus, worn
by a long line of emperors after them and by Odoacer and by the Visigothic kings
of Spain, and taking it was a claim: a barbarian king raised by an assembly of
armed men in a Roman province, announcing in one word that he stood where the
emperors had stood. The Lombards had been in Italy less than twenty years and
had spent ten of them without a king at all.

## Lumello

Authari died in 590 after a reign of about six years. He left a Bavarian widow,
Theudelinda, and no settled successor, and what the Lombards did next is the
strangest constitutional act on the record.

They kept the queen. Paul says she pleased them greatly, so they allowed her to
remain in her royal dignity and advised her to choose for herself whomsoever she
might wish from all the Lombards — *such a one, namely, as could profitably
manage the kingdom*. She took counsel with the prudent and chose Agilulf, duke
of Turin.

Paul tells the meeting as a scene. She sent for him and went out to meet him at
Lumello; she had wine brought, drank first, and handed him the cup; he took it
and kissed her hand; and the queen said, smiling and blushing, that he should
not kiss the hand of a woman whose lips he ought to be kissing, and raised him
up, and then explained about the marriage and the crown.

The footnote is merciless. All of this has to have happened within about two
months of her first husband's death, which as the commentators observe detracts
considerably from the charm of an otherwise delightful saga. The likeliest
reconstruction is that Agilulf seized the throne and married Theudelinda
afterwards, to attach a legitimate descent to a title he had taken by force.

Both versions describe the same underlying fact, which is why the story was
worth inventing. Theudelinda's grandfather had been a Lombard king; her
descendants reigned for five generations; and for a decade the person through
whom the kingship passed was a woman. The saga made that dignified. It did not
make it up.

## Gregory's correspondent

Agilulf campaigned hard — Paul has him taking Perugia and marching on Rome, and
Pope Gregory the Great so alarmed at his approach that he broke off his
commentary on Ezekiel and said so in a homily. From the walls Gregory watched
Roman captives being driven out of the countryside roped together at the neck.

What ended that war was not a battle. Gregory had been writing to Theudelinda
for years, and she pressed her husband, and in 598 or 599 the king made a firm
peace with the pope. Gregory's letter of thanks survives:

> We have learned from the report of our son, the abbot Probus, that your
> Excellency has devoted yourself, as you are wont, zealously and benevolently,
> to making peace.

The Lombards had come into Italy Arian, and the correspondence is the beginning
of their leaving it. Paul notes without comment that Pavia had two bishops in
these years, an Arian one with his own baptistery at the church of St Eusebius
and a Catholic one in the Catholic church, in the same city — and that the Arian
one eventually converted and governed the Catholic church there.

## The wall at Monza

Theudelinda built a church of St John the Baptist at Monza, twelve miles above
Milan, on a site where Theoderic the Ostrogoth had put a summer palace because
it was cool and near the Alps. Beside it she built a palace of her own, and had
it painted with the deeds of the Lombards.

Paul saw it, and the paragraph in which he describes it is the reason anyone
knows what these people looked like:

> They shaved the neck, and left it bare up to the back of the head, having
> their hair hanging down on the face as far as the mouth and parting it on
> either side by a part in the forehead.

Loose garments, mostly linen, with broad woven borders in several colours; shoes
open nearly to the tip of the big toe and fastened with interlacing latchets;
trousers and shaggy leggings only later. A queen commissioned a wall painting to
tell her subjects who they were, and two centuries afterwards a monk stood in
front of it and wrote down the haircut.
