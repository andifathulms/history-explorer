Einhard declines to describe the crossing.

> At this point I should describe Charles's difficult passage over the Alps
> into Italy, and the hardships that the Franks endured in climbing the
> trackless mountain-ridges, the heaven-aspiring cliffs and ragged peaks, if it
> were not my purpose in this work to record the manner of his life rather than
> the incidents of the wars that he waged.

Having refused to describe it, he has described it. It is the single most
Einhardian sentence in the biography, and it is the last thing the Lombard
kingdom gets before the summary of its destruction.

## The siege

The first war, Pepin's, had lasted a few days. This one did not. Einhard's
account is that Charles did not cease, after declaring war, until he had
exhausted King Desiderius by a long siege and forced him to surrender at
discretion; drove his son Adalgis — *the last hope of the Lombards* — not only
from his kingdom but from all Italy; restored to the Romans everything they had
lost; put down the duke of Friuli, who was plotting revolution; reduced all
Italy to his power, and set his own son Pepin as king over it.

Desiderius was taken to Francia and banished for life. Adalgis went to
Constantinople and spent the rest of his life trying to get an imperial army to
put him back, and never did.

## A change of family

The kingdom was not dissolved, and the distinction is not pedantic.

Charlemagne became king of the Lombards and added the title to his own. The
*regnum Langobardorum* went on being an administrative entity with its own law
and its own officers. The duchies went on. Lombard counts and gastalds went on
doing what they had been doing. What changed in 774 was which family held the
crown — and the proof is in the settlement itself, since a conqueror who intended
to abolish a kingdom does not install his son as its king.

Einhard's own framing gives the point away without meaning to. The Lombard war
appears in his sixth chapter, between Aquitaine and Saxony, and it takes a page.
The Saxons get thirty-two years and the longest chapter in the book, because the
Saxons had to be destroyed as a people before they could be governed. Italy had
a working state in it, and Charlemagne took the state.

There is one more thing Einhard supplies without dwelling on it, and it is the
real reason the armies met. When Charlemagne's brother Carloman died in 771, his
widow took her sons to Italy and put herself under the protection of Desiderius
— *without reason*, says Einhard, *despite her husband's brother*. Charlemagne
had by then married a daughter of Desiderius, at his mother's insistence, and
repudiated her at the end of a year for a reason Einhard says nobody knows.

So the kingdom fell partly over a marriage that lasted twelve months and a
sister-in-law who chose the wrong protector. Einhard is writing to praise a
dynasty, and he puts both facts on the page anyway, in the section about the
family rather than the section about the war, where a reader is least likely to
put them together.
