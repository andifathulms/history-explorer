Liutprand reigned thirty-one years and seven months and died in his bed, which
no Lombard king before him had managed on that scale. Paul the Deacon, who was
born a few years before it ended and grew up in the country it governed, gives
him the longest character sketch in the book:

> a man of much wisdom, very religious and a lover of peace, shrewd in counsel,
> powerful in war, merciful to offenders, chaste, modest, prayerful in the
> night-watches, generous in charities, ignorant of letters indeed, yet worthy
> to be likened to philosophers, a supporter of his people, an increaser of the
> law.

The last phrase is the one that matters, and it is not a compliment about
character.

## The first of March

For fifteen of his thirty-one years, on the first day of March, Liutprand issued
laws.

They were framed on the advice of his judges. They were no longer put to the
assembly of free men that had given Rothari's Edict its authority a century
earlier, and the king's justification for issuing them without it was that his
own heart was in the hand of God. The Latin they are written in is bad enough
to be difficult to read.

What is in them describes a different country from the one Rothari legislated
for. The hunting and falconry provisions are gone. In their place are rules for
enforcing contracts and foreclosing mortgages. The composition for murder — the
tariff the Edict was built on — is replaced by outright confiscation of the
killer's property, and where the property does not cover it, the killer himself
is handed over to the dead man's heirs as a slave. There are laws against the
king's own agents oppressing people, and heavy penalties for judges who sit on a
case.

There is also a law about scribes, and it is the quiet one. Anyone drawing up a
deed, *whether according to the laws of the Lombards or those of the Romans*,
must not draw it contrary to these statutes. The kingdom's own legislation now
takes for granted that a substantial part of the population is living under
another law, and legislates over the top of it anyway.

## What he could not change

The most revealing sentence Liutprand left is an admission of defeat.

Trial by combat was in the Edict and he restricted it, and he explained why he
was not abolishing it:

> We are uncertain about the judgment of God, and we have heard of many persons
> unjustly losing their cause by wager of battle, but on account of the custom
> of our nation of the Lombards we cannot change the law itself.

A king who says in a statute that a procedure of his own legal system produces
unjust results, and that he lacks the power to remove it, is telling you exactly
how far a Lombard king's authority ran. It ran over mortgages and it stopped at
custom.

## The last act

Liutprand spent his reign pushing at the two things that stood between the
kingdom and the whole peninsula: the semi-independent southern duchies, and the
Byzantine exarchate at Ravenna. He brought Spoleto and Benevento to heel. He
took territory around Ravenna. He came to Rome.

And there, at the end, he stopped. He met the pope, was entreated to desist, and
agreed to give back the country districts around Ravenna and two-thirds of the
territory of Cesena, and to hold a truce while his envoys went to Constantinople
to settle a final treaty. It was one of his last public acts, and it undid the
work of the reign.

Nobody knows why. He was old and near death; his successor was not the man to
finish what he had started; the pope was persuasive. What is on the record is
that the strongest king the Lombards ever had reached the point where Italy was
within his grasp, and handed part of it back.

His successors did not make the same choice, and it was the choice that killed
the kingdom.
