Almost nothing survives about how Demak was actually governed. Neither the
Javanese chronicles nor the Portuguese reports describe the officials or the
palace with any care, and what can be said comes from scattered notices about
individuals and from what later Javanese courts inherited.

What they inherited turns out to be the answer.

## The furniture of a Hindu kingdom

Javanese writers of the seventeenth century and after took it for granted that
the Muslim kingdom of Demak was the continuation of Hindu-Buddhist Majapahit,
and pushed the point as far as making the legendary Raden Patah a son of the
last Majapahit emperor. The writers who said this were Muslims, and many of
them were religious scholars. The difference of religion mattered less to them
than the continuity of the machinery of state.

They had a case. The Muslim lords of the small north-coast ports had spent
years as vassals of a Hindu overlord before Demak felt able to call itself an
independent Muslim kingdom, and the annual journey to court at which a vassal
renewed his loyalty kept them in working contact with Majapahit's officers,
above all with the *patih*. When the ruler of Demak became a sultan in his own
right, the arrangements he knew how to run were those.

The evidence is in the paperwork. A collection of laws and rules of procedure
is ascribed to the Demak king Senapati Jimbun, and it carries an Old Javanese
title, *Salokantara*. The old manuals on government and royal officials, the
*Wadu Aji*, went on being copied in central Javanese versions. Majapahit's
supreme court had been staffed by consecrated Hindu and Buddhist judges called
*dharmadhyaksa*; the judges of Demak are *jeksa*, which is the same word with
the consecration removed, and the *pradata* court they sat in was still the
high court of the central Javanese principalities at the end of the nineteenth
century.

## Where the new religion actually sat

Islamic law had to be given a place, and the place it was given was a narrow
one: marriage and what attaches to marriage, and questions of worship in the
strict sense. Beside it stood customary law and a court law of Hindu descent,
and neither gave way. This is not a Javanese peculiarity — the theoretical
supremacy of *fiqh* has been rare everywhere — but it is worth being exact
about in a kingdom whose whole claim was to be Islam's protector on Java.

The man who administered that law was a scholar from among the *ulama*, and at
Demak the office of jurist and the office of leading prayer in the mosque were
already close together. The title the Demak imams used was *pangulu*, head, and
they held authority in law as well as in the mosque.

## The armed mosque

The soldiery looks equally familiar. As at Majapahit, the king's strength
rested largely on volunteers from the landholding families and the free
peasantry, and the corps a young man joined to make a career — the Tamtama —
had existed at Majapahit and was still a named regiment in the palaces of
Surakarta and Yogyakarta in the twentieth century. The legend of Jaka Tingkir,
who enlisted in the Tamtama at the capital and ended as sultan of Pajang, is a
story about that ladder.

What was genuinely new was the mosque quarter. The zeal of recent converts and
the martial tradition of Islam pushed the artisans and traders around the
capital's mosque and the village mosques into armed bands, formed to defend the
faith and extend its territory — the "armed *penghulu*" of the *Serat Kandha*
and the babad, who are remembered as having joined the holy war that finished
the Hindu capital for good under the lord of Kudus, himself descended from a
line of penghulu.

Those half-military, half-religious men are probably the ancestors of the
Suranata, the corps that stood closest to the king in the central Javanese
palaces from the seventeenth century onward. A century after Demak's greatness
the district was being governed by a *tumenggung* of the Suranata, and the
Dutch who met him in 1678 took him for a crown prince.
