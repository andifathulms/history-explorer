The Javanese chronicles say nothing at all about the reign of Sunan Prawata,
who took the throne when his father was killed in 1546. What survives instead
is one Portuguese letter.

Manuel Pinto wrote it from Melaka on 7 December 1548, to the archbishop at Goa.
He had stopped in Java on his way back west from South Sulawesi and had talked
with the king, who by that date is very probably Prawata.

The king told him he was working to bring the whole island of Java to Islam,
and that if he succeeded he would be *segundo turco* — a second Turk, the equal
of Süleyman, who in 1547 had just settled his position in Hungary by treaty
with Charles V and was the great champion of Islam of the age. He said he
thought he could bring Melaka down without fighting for it, by cutting off the
rice that Java sent there. And he said he was considering an expedition to
South Sulawesi, to conquer it and convert it.

Pinto argued him out of the last one, on the ground that a Javanese army would
be inconvenient for a priest who was trying to introduce Christianity there at
the same moment.

## Where a Javanese king learned about Süleyman

Someone at Demak was following European politics closely enough to know who
Süleyman was and what he had just achieved.

The likeliest channel is a man in the palace: a Portuguese renegade from the
Algarve, known as Coje Geinal, who cast cannon for the king in the middle of
the century and is probably the same gunfounder who made the piece Trenggana
sent to Banten. A deserter with a trade brought both the artillery and the
news.

## The scale of the claim, and the size of the reality

Set the letter beside what was actually happening and it is a remarkable
document. Prawata seems to have understood himself as the rightful lord of the
Muslim kingdom and the protector of the faith on Java — while the real power
was already draining to rulers who were nominally under him.

That is the middle of the sixteenth century in one sentence. Cirebon and Banten
in the west, Surabaya and Gresik-Giri in the east, all becoming
half-independent Muslim states in exactly these years; Prawata could not stop
any of it. He was murdered with his wife in 1549, on the orders of a nephew, in
a feud over an earlier killing of his own.

Java would not be brought to Islam by a sultan of Demak. It would be brought
there anyway, over the next two centuries, largely by the men whose ports were
slipping out of his hands while he was explaining to a passing Portuguese what
he intended to become.
