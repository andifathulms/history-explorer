Raden Patah's successor reigned three years and this record's rulers list does
not name him — `founder` is Raden Patah, `peak` is Trenggana, and Pati Unus falls
in the gap between them, as the middle ruler of a three-generation sultanate.

He is on this record's second chapter as the commander of the fleets that
attacked Portuguese Melaka in 1512 and 1513 and failed. Javanese tradition calls
him Pangeran Sabrang Lor, the prince who crossed to the north.

## The ship

Portuguese accounts of the 1513 attack describe the Javanese flagship as a
*jong* of extraordinary size — bigger than anything they had, with multiple
layers of hull planking that their guns could not penetrate, carrying a very
large crew.

The numbers in those accounts are the numbers of men who were frightened and are
not entered anywhere on this site: hard rule 2 forbids producing a figure, and
`peak_population` and `reach_km2` on this record are both null. What the accounts
establish, without a number, is that in 1513 a Javanese sultanate could build
ships that a European fleet found alarming, and that the reason the attack failed
was not the ships.

It was that Melaka is eight hundred miles away, the fleet had to be supplied
across that distance, and an assault on a fortified position from the sea is the
hardest operation there is.

## The comparison that makes the point

This corpus's Ternate record besieged a Portuguese fort for five years, from
1570, and took its surrender. That is the only successful expulsion of a European
power anywhere in the archipelago and it was conducted against a garrison on Ternate's own
island, which the sultanate could blockade with kora-kora and starve.

Demak had the larger polity, the larger fleet and the larger ships, and lost —
because it was doing the harder thing.

This record's `military_basis` reads `conscript` and `client-levy`, the second
value being the coastal chiefs whose fleets made this possible. It is the same
value Ternate carries for its kora-kora. Two records, one field value, and the
difference between success and failure was distance.
