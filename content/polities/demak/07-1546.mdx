Trenggana took the throne in 1521 and held it for twenty-five years. Under him
Demak took the north coast in both directions, took Banten and the Sunda ports
in the west, absorbed what was left of Majapahit, and campaigned east into the
last Hindu-Buddhist holdouts of Java's eastern salient.

He was killed on one of those campaigns, at Panarukan, in 1546.

His son Sunan Prawata was murdered in 1549. The kinsman who arranged it, Arya
Penangsang, held the throne until 1554 and was killed in turn, and the man who
won the war that followed was Jaka Tingkir, who moved the court inland to
Pajang.

Eight years, three rulers, two murders. That is a short distance between a
kingdom's greatest ruler and its last one.

## No rule for what came next

Eight years, three rulers, two murders, and nothing in the sources describes a
rule that was supposed to prevent it. There is no designated heir installed in
a father's lifetime, no council with a recognised right to choose, no practice
of any kind standing between the death of a sultan and the ambitions of his
relatives. A sultanate that had taken Java in fifty years did not survive its
own founder's grandson, and what it lacked was not soldiers.

## What replaced it

Jaka Tingkir moved the court inland to Pajang, and argued his right to it from
two directions at once: descent from Trenggana, whose dynasty he had just
finished off, and descent from Brawijaya, the last king of Majapahit, whose
kingdom Trenggana's dynasty had absorbed thirty years earlier.

Which is exactly the pair of claims Demak itself had made — Raden Patah's line
traced to Majapahit's royal house, and the right to rule Java that came with
it. Two consecutive Javanese sultanates justified themselves by descent from
the thing their predecessor had destroyed, and each was destroyed in turn by
somebody making the same argument about it.

The centre of Javanese power went inland with him in 1568 and never came back
to the sea.
