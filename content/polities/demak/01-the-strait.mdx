Demak stood on a strait. The Muria highlands, which are now a massif rising out
of the north Java plain, were then separated from Java by a channel that could
be sailed, and a trader coming from Semarang could cut through it to Rembang
instead of going round. The district lay on the southern shore of that
shortcut, on quiet water, with rice land on both banks.

That is the whole explanation of the place. Two things a sixteenth-century
Javanese state needed were a harvest it could tax and a passage it could
control, and Demak had them in the same few miles of coast.

## Juwana

The collecting point for the rice of those shores, around 1500, was not Demak
but Juwana, further east.

Tome Pires reports that by about 1513 Juwana had been destroyed and emptied by
the Gusti Patih, the great commander of the still-Hindu Majapahit. It was one
of the last exertions of the old kingdom, and its effect was the opposite of
what was intended: with Juwana gone, Demak held the strait south of the Muria
outright.

Jepara lay on the other side of the highlands, with a sheltered harbour
originally screened by three small islands, and it suited the larger ships
running along the north coast to Maluku and back. The two towns worked as a
pair, and the pairing outlasted the channel that made it: by the seventeenth
century the shortcut had silted to the point where big boats could no longer
use it at all, and Jepara simply became Demak's port.

## The road inland

The link to the interior was the Serang, which now reaches the Java Sea between
Demak and Jepara and which small trading boats could still ascend to Godong in
the eighteenth century. Its tributaries rise in the central limestone hills.
Beyond those hills lie the old inland districts of central Java — Pengging and
Pajang — and from there cart roads crossed the low watershed between the Serang
and Lusi valleys to the Bengawan Solo, which is the road between southern
central Java and the east.

The rice of the Demak plain was already good and the ground took irrigation
well, but the surplus a ruler could actually raise, for his own table and for
sale, went up sharply if he held that road into Pengging and Pajang.

So the interior was not a later ambition. It was in the arithmetic of the place
from the beginning, and Demak's kings spent their reigns acting on it. The
kingdom that eventually took the paramountcy from them sat at the far end of
the same road.
