In 1512 and again in 1513 Demak sent a fleet against the Portuguese at Melaka,
under Pati Unus, and both attempts failed.

It is worth marking because there are so few of them. The ordinary shape of
these encounters is a European fleet arriving somewhere and a local polity
dealing with the consequences: Melaka taken in 1511, Kilwa sacked in 1505,
Ternate's alliance of 1512 ending with its sultan stabbed inside a Portuguese
fort, Aceh ground down over thirty years from 1873.

Demak crossed eight hundred miles of sea to attack a fortified city held by the
power that had just taken it. That the attempt failed is less surprising than
that it was made.

The one that worked is Ternate's: five years of siege against a fort on its own
island, ending in 1575 with the garrison leaving. The difference between the
two is the difference between besieging something you live next to and mounting
an amphibious expedition across an ocean, which is a distinction about
logistics rather than about resolve.

## The rice again

Demak sat on the north coast with the Javanese granary behind it, and what made
it rich was not growing rice but exporting it — to the ports of the archipelago
that could not feed themselves, including, until 1511, Melaka.

Melaka had no farmland at all and bought most of its food from Java. The two
cities were the two halves of one arrangement. When Melaka fell to the
Portuguese, the polity that had been selling it rice sent ships to try to take
it back — which is a commercial motive as much as a religious one, and the
sources give both.
