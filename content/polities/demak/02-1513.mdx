In 1512 and again in 1513 Demak sent a fleet against the Portuguese at Melaka,
under Pati Unus, and both attempts failed.

It is worth marking because there are so few of them. This site now carries
twenty-two records across the archipelago, and the ordinary shape of their
contact with European
power is a European fleet arriving somewhere and a local polity dealing with the
consequences: Melaka taken in 1511, Kilwa's coast one region over sacked in 1505,
Ternate's alliance of 1512 ending with a sultan stabbed in a fort, Aceh ground
down from 1873.

Demak crossed eight hundred miles of sea to attack a fortified city held by the
power that had just taken it. That the attempt failed is less surprising than
that it was made.

The one that worked is Ternate's: five years of siege against a fort on its own
island, ending in 1575 with the garrison leaving. The difference between the two
is the difference between besieging something you live next to and mounting an
amphibious expedition across an ocean, which is a distinction about logistics
rather than about resolve.

## The rice again

This record's `revenue_basis` is `land-tax` and `trade-toll`, and the second
value is why a fleet existed at all.

Demak sat on the north coast with the Javanese granary behind it. What made it
rich was not growing rice but exporting it — to the ports of the archipelago
that could not feed themselves, including, until 1511, Melaka.

This corpus's Melaka record's third chapter is about that from the buying end: a
city with no farmland at all, coded `trade-toll` alone, importing its food
largely from Java. The two records are the two halves of one arrangement, and
they are on opposite sides of it. When Melaka fell, the polity that had been
selling it rice sent ships to try to take it back.
