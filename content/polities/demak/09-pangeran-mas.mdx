The babad tell the end simply. Jaka Tingkir of Pajang beat Arya Panangsang of
Jipang, who had murdered the last king of Demak; the coastal lords and the
princes of central and eastern Java acknowledged the victor as paramount; the
old capital became a district town under a lord who answered to Pajang.

It is too simple, and the chroniclers who wrote it had reasons. Seventeenth-
and eighteenth-century writers in the inland courts consistently magnified
Pajang and Mataram at the expense of the coast they had displaced. In fact the
Ratu of Kalinyamat at Jepara was the real power along the western north coast,
as far as Banten, well into the second half of the sixteenth century.

## An emperor at Demak in 1564

There is also a Portuguese notice that will not fit the tidy version.

De Couto reports that in 1564 the sultan of Aceh, preparing one of his
expeditions against Portuguese Melaka, sent envoys to the *Rey de Dama,
Imperador do Java* to ask for help. The ruler of Demak refused, on the ground
that an Aceh victorious over the Portuguese would become too strong and would
draw Java into its orbit. The envoys were killed at Demak, which ended the
question. De Couto tells the story at length because to the Portuguese it
looked like divine intervention.

Fifteen years after the last king's death, then, somebody at Demak was still
worth an embassy from the most formidable ruler in the western archipelago. He
is not identifiable in the Javanese chronicles. The likeliest reconstruction is
that he was a religious figure of the old royal house, whose weight came from
being protector of the faith and *panatagama*, orderer of religion, rather than
from soldiers — probably the prince known as Pangeran Kediri or Pangiri, a
nephew of the murdered king and of the Ratu of Jepara.

## Carried home with his hands tied in silk

When the sultan of Pajang died in 1587 the succession was open, and the ruler
of Demak came to Pajang with troops of his own — among them slaves who were
Balinese, Bugis and Makassarese — to press his claim to the paramountcy.

Senapati of Mataram, backing another candidate, scattered them at once. Then he
treated the old man with extravagant courtesy: an escorted litter home to
Demak, and his hands bound with nothing but a silk sash. If the story is true,
the courtesy is the measure of how little was left. A rival who has to be
killed is a rival; a rival who can be sent home in a litter is a relic.

What the episode also shows is that in the interior, among people whose fathers
or grandfathers had become Muslims only fifty or seventy-five years earlier,
the Demak line still carried religious authority when it had no political power
at all.

## Melaka, Banten, and a son with a knife

Pangeran Kediri's son was called Pangeran Mas, or Pangeran Juru. A Javanese
chronology used by Raffles has it that in 1588, after the collapse, the great
men of Demak and their people took ship. In 1587 Paulo de Lima had stormed
Johor for the Portuguese, a victory that rang through the western archipelago,
and a Javanese embassy appeared at Melaka that same year; a landless claimant
looking for help against Mataram had reason to look there. No Portuguese
document records a meeting.

By 1596 he was at Banten, where the Dutch of the first voyage met him. They
were told his father had once ruled all Java and had been deposed by his own
vassals, and that the son, impoverished by war, had gone to Melaka and then
here, where the king of Banten — his kinsman — received him with the honours of
his birth and lodged him outside the town. The same voyage brings back a phrase
about his homeland: *Dauma, waer den Keyser noch voor Coning ghekent wordt*,
Demak, where the Emperor is still acknowledged as king.

Between 1602 and 1604 Demak rose against Mataram. It is very likely that
Pangeran Mas meant to sail from Banten and put himself at the head of it. The
English factor Scott heard in 1604 that on a voyage from Banten to somewhere
else on the north coast he had been killed by one of his own sons. He was
buried in the Banten cemetery at Pangkalan Tangka, and it is by that place-name
that the last claimant of Demak is remembered — Sultan Pangkalan Tangka, named
for where a man ends rather than for anything he held.

Why the son did it is not recorded.
