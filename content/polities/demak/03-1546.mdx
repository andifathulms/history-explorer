Trenggana took the throne in 1521 and held it for twenty-five years. Under him
Demak took the north coast in both directions, took Banten and the Sunda ports in
the west, absorbed what was left of Majapahit, and campaigned east into the last
Hindu-Buddhist holdouts of Java's eastern salient.

He was killed on one of those campaigns, at Panarukan, in 1546.

His son Sunan Prawata was murdered in 1549. The kinsman who arranged it, Arya
Penangsang, held the throne until 1554 and was killed in turn, and the man who
won the war that followed was Jaka Tingkir, who moved the court inland to Pajang.

Eight years, three rulers, two murders. It is the shortest interval on this site
between a polity's `peak` ruler and its `last` one.

## The null

`succession_rule` on this record is null, and it joins a hundred and thirteen
others. The reason is the commonest one on this site: what the sources describe
is a sequence of outcomes, not a rule, and coding rule 7 asks for the rule.

The comparison worth making is one region over. This corpus's Dai Viet record
carries `nomination` — the only non-null succession field in Mainland Southeast
Asia — for the Tran practice of abdicating in favour of a designated heir and
then reigning beside him, so that the succession was executed rather than
announced. That record's fourth chapter observes that the practice was abandoned
at the end of the dynasty and that the throne was usurped within two reigns.

Demak is what the absence of such an arrangement looks like on a compressed
timescale: a sultanate that had taken Java in fifty years and could not survive
its own founder's grandson.

## `by` is not empty any more

`ended.type` is `dynastic replacement` and `ended.by` reads `[pajang]`.

It was empty when this chapter was written, and the paragraph here argued that
Pajang was the smallest identifiable hole left in Maritime Asia: eighteen years,
one significant ruler, one page, one closed field. That page exists now, and it
carries the double claim this paragraph predicted — Jaka Tingkir asserted descent
from Trenggana and from Majapahit's last king at once, which is the same pair of
claims this record makes, made against this record.

Fourteen of this region's twenty-two records still have an empty `by`, and none
of them is the kind this record's was — a Javanese successor with a known name
that nobody had written yet. Most are empty because Portugal, the Netherlands, a
chartered company, a Sulawesi kingdom, a Siamese dynasty, the United States or
an English adventurer has no page here and mostly is not going to get one.
Johor's and Banten's are empty because the people who ended them were the
ruler's own family; Medang's because the attacker is a place-name in one
inscription; Gelgel's because it broke into nine.

What that page also did was finish a run. Java's sequence on this site is now
unbroken from Kediri in 1042 to the division of Mataram in 1755: seven records,
each one named in the `ended.by` of the record before it, and the longest
continuous chain anywhere in this corpus.
