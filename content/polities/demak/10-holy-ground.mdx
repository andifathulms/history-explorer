Once Senapati held southern central Java, around 1588, the lords of Pati, Demak
and Grobogan counted in the Mataram chronicles as *sampun kareh* — already
subdued. In 1589 they were ordered out on his campaign against the princes of
east Java, which failed; in 1591 they were ordered out again, to Kediri, under
one of his sons who needed a reputation. That is what Demak had become: a
contingent.

After that the office changed hands the way offices do. Ki Mas Sari of Wirasari
was made adipati in 1595, apparently because his predecessor could not be
trusted. In 1601 the new king of Mataram appointed his own elder half-brother,
Pangeran Puger, who unsurprisingly took up arms from Demak against him, was
beaten at Tambak Uwos, and was sent to live at Kudus with his family and
without his followers. His replacement was a spearman promoted for good
service, who took the name Tumenggung Endranata; in 1627 he was found to be
entangled in the war between Pati and Sultan Agung and was stabbed with a kris
as a traitor on the sultan's orders.

## What was left to be

As a seaport, Demak meant nothing by the end of the sixteenth century. The
channel that made it was closing, and the ships had moved to Jepara.

As farmland it went on mattering for a long time. Rice and the other produce of
the Demak plain kept a real place in the economy of the Mataram kings, which is
the same fact the first Demak lords built on, now owned by somebody else.

And then there is the third thing, which is why the name is still in use. Into
the nineteenth century, believers all over Java kept up their reverence for the
Great Mosque and for the graves at Kadilangu, where Sunan Kalijaga lies. The
town counted as holy ground.

A polity that lasted about seventy-five years as an independent kingdom, and
whose royal line died out in exile at Banten, is remembered in Javanese history
beside Majapahit itself. It is not the strait or the fleet or the law book that
did that. It is a mosque people kept walking to.
