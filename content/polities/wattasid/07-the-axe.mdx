The last Wattasid did not go quietly, and what he left behind killed the man
who beat him.

## Abu Hassun in Algiers

Driven out of Fez by Muhammad al-Shaykh of the Saadis, Abu Hassun escaped to
Algiers to save his life and ask for help. Al-Ifrani gives his pitch to the
Turks almost as direct speech:

> He has taken my kingdom from me, the kingdom of my fathers; he has stripped
> me of the inheritance of my forefathers. Come with me, let us go and fight
> him, and it is likely that God will help us and give us success and victory,
> without your losing any part of the treasure and plunder you have gathered.

He worked on them constantly with brilliant descriptions of the Maghreb, and he
promised them a large sum, and they went. The Turkish pasha Salih led them.
They entered Fez, and after hard fighting Muhammad al-Shaykh had to abandon the
city and run.

Abu Hassun made his entry on 9 January 1554. The people received him with great
transports of joy. He dismounted, embraced everyone, great and small, noble and
common, and burst into tears at the memory of what the sharifs had done to him
and his family.

Then the complaints started. The Turks were seizing women and committing every
sort of excess. Abu Hassun hurried to pay them what he had promised and got
them out of Fez, and very few of them stayed.

*Very few of them stayed.* That clause is the whole afterlife.

## Choose it for yourself

Muhammad al-Shaykh went to Marrakesh, rallied the tribes, rebuilt his army and
came back. Abu Hassun came out to meet him with the archers of the city and
whatever forces of the Maghreb had joined him, was beaten in the field, and
withdrew behind the walls of Fez to stand a siege. The dynasty ends there.

On entering the city the second time, the sultan ordered the execution of the
chief qadi of Fez, Abu Muhammad Abd al-Wahhab ibn al-Zaqqaq, whom he suspected
of having favoured Abu Hassun. When the man was brought before him he said:
*choose the manner of your execution.*

The qadi answered: choose it for yourself, for a man perishes in the way he
makes others perish.

Let his head be cut off with an axe, said the sultan.

## Three years

The Turks who had come with Abu Hassun and had not gone home were still in Fez.
Muhammad al-Shaykh took them into his own army, made them a special division,
and when he moved to Marrakesh he kept them constantly around him and showed
them the greatest confidence.

He had also, by this time, told the Ottoman sultan's ambassadors to inform
their master that he intended to conquer his states and march against him — and
he was in the habit of referring to Suleiman as *the sultan of the fishermen*,
because the Turks were always at sea. Suleiman sent word to Algiers for his
head.

The Algerians picked one of their bravest, Salih al-Kahya, who took a small
troop of Turks, pretended to have deserted the Ottoman service, presented
himself to the Moroccan sultan and asked to be taken into his army and
protected from pursuit. The sultan was delighted and said so publicly. The
Turks already in his guard were delighted too and made much of the newcomers —
every foreigner in a country, al-Ifrani observes, finds himself somehow related
to the foreigners he meets there.

They waited for their moment through a campaign in the mountains, and got it at
a place called Guelaguel. The guard was careless; they went into the prince's
tent; one of them struck him with an axe and took his head off the body at a
single blow. They carried it away in a nosebag and fled in the dark towards
Sijilmasa, telling anyone who asked that they were men being sent to Tlemcen.
They were caught on the road and a number of them were killed, but some got
away with the head, and sent it to Constantinople, where it hung on a wall of
the city until it rotted.

## What a defeated dynasty leaves

Nothing that looks like an inheritance. No claim, because it never had one; no
territory, because it lost the coast and then the interior; no line, because
the family was taken in chains to Marrakesh and, it is said, put to death after
first being pardoned.

What it left was a division of Turkish soldiers in the Saadi army, a prophecy
made by a judge under sentence, and an axe.
