Leo Africanus served the Wattasid sultan and went out with his army, and his
account of how Morocco lost its coast is the account of a man who was there and
did not enjoy it.

## How Safi was taken

Safi had killed its own king in a tumult and was being fought over by two
governors, and the Portuguese merchants who frequented the town in great
numbers wrote to their king asking him to send soldiers, because they were in
good hope the city could be had cheaply. The king would not move until they
confirmed it.

Meanwhile the merchants persuaded the townsmen that during the disorder
everybody's lives and goods were at risk, and that what was needed was a strong
castle on the sea shore where merchandise might safely be kept. The townsmen
agreed. Then, in among their vessels of oil and their other wares, the
merchants cunningly conveyed guns and every other kind of warlike instrument
into it, while the town, knowing nothing of this, exacted nothing from them but
the ordinary customs on their goods.

Having armed the castle, they looked for a quarrel. A Portuguese servant buying
meat in the city provoked a butcher; they came to blows; the servant, feeling
himself hurt, ran the butcher through and fled to the castle. The people rose
and came at it in one body to pull it down and cut the throats of everyone
inside, and the guns and crossbows made such havoc among them that a hundred
and fifty citizens were killed at the first encounter. They kept assaulting it
daily until the king of Portugal sent five thousand foot, two hundred horse and
a great number of guns; at which the citizens fled to the mountain, nobody
daring to stay in the town except the man who had built the castle.

That is how a Moroccan port changed hands: on a permit, a stack of oil jars and
a butcher's quarrel.

## Arzila, from the inside

Leo also gives the other side, and puts himself in it.

Muhammad the Portuguese, who had been a hostage in Lisbon as a boy, attempted
repeatedly to recover Arzila. Once he came at it suddenly, beat down a great
part of the wall, entered the breach and set all the Muslim captives free. The
Christians retired into the castle and promised to yield within two days —
until Pedro Navarro arrived with a great fleet and, with continual discharging
of ordnance, compelled the sultan not merely to leave the city but to take his
whole army away. Afterwards the Portuguese fortified it on all sides, and every
later attempt was thrown back.

> I my selfe seruing the king in the foresaid expedition could find but fiue
> hundred of our companie slaine. But the warre against Arzilla continued from
> the yeere of the Hegeira 914. to the yeere 921.

Seven years of campaigning to recover one town, and five hundred dead in a
single expedition, counted by a man walking the field afterwards.

## Where the money came from

The other half of the picture is what the war cost at home, and Leo records his
own part in that too, without softening it. A great deal of gold was being
raised in ransoms and levies. The king of Fez exacted fifty thousand ducats
from the Jews of Fez, on the grounds that they were said to favour his enemies.

> I my selfe bare him companie, that went in the Kings name to receiue the sayd
> summe of the lewes.

He is not confessing and not excusing. He is a young official noting where the
money for the Arzila war came from, and the sentence sits in his book beside
the ones about the beauty of the colleges.

## A government losing on both sides of the ledger

Portuguese fortresses now ran down the Atlantic from Ceuta to Agadir and the
Spanish were doing the same on the Mediterranean side. Every one of them was a
customs house that no longer paid Fez. Every campaign to remove one cost more
than the port had been worth. And a dynasty whose only claim was that it held
the country was demonstrating annually that it did not hold the edges of it.

Somewhere south of the Atlas, a family that could claim descent from the
Prophet was organising the same war and winning it.
