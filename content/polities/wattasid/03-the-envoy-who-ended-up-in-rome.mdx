title: The envoy who ended up in Rome

Al-Hasan ibn Muhammad al-Wazzan was born in Granada around 1494, into a family
of the Nasrid administrative class, and was carried to Fez as a small child in
the exodus the previous chapter describes. He was educated there, entered
Wattasid service, and travelled for the sultan.

The travelling is the point. As a young man he went with his uncle, an
ambassador, across the Sahara to the Songhai empire — this site's Songhai page,
a lifetime before the Saadi army destroyed it. He went again on his own account.
He saw Timbuktu and Gao, the Hausa cities, the Atlas, Tunis, Cairo, Constantinople
and Arabia. He was, on the evidence of what he later wrote, an observant and
sceptical traveller with a diplomat's interest in how places were actually
governed.

In 1518, returning by sea from Egypt, he was captured by Christian corsairs off
the Tunisian coast. Because he was obviously a man of learning he was not sold
as an ordinary captive but presented to the pope. Leo X had him instructed,
baptised him with his own names — Johannes Leo de Medicis — and kept him in
Rome, where he taught Arabic, worked on an Arabic-Hebrew-Latin dictionary, and
in 1526 finished a book in Italian describing Africa.

The *Descrittione dell'Africa* was printed in 1550 and translated into French,
Latin and English within a generation, and it was Europe's principal source of
information about the interior of Africa for something close to three hundred
years. Almost everything a European reader knew about Timbuktu before the
nineteenth century came from it, including the exaggerations.

Three things make this the right chapter for this page.

The man was a Wattasid official, and the knowledge he sold to Rome was
knowledge the Wattasid state had sent him to acquire. Whatever else the dynasty
failed at, its diplomatic service reached the Niger.

He is the exact inverse of the movement in the last chapter. This corpus has
recorded refugees going one way across the strait for eight hundred years, and
Andalusi learning arriving in the Maghreb. Here a man of that same diaspora
carried the Maghreb's knowledge into Europe, involuntarily, as a captive.

And it is the same transaction this site keeps recording in different
directions: the Abbasid page has Greek science translated into Arabic at
Baghdad, the Abbadid page has it translated out again at Toledo, the Ghaznavid
page has al-Biruni writing about India for a court that had conquered it. Here
a Granadan exile in a Roman palace writes Africa for readers who had never
been there, and Europe reads him until it can go and look.

What none of it registers on is this page's rating panel, which gives the
Wattasids a null reach, a null population and three empty influence counts.
