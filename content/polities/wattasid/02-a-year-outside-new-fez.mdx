The Wattasids took power twice, and the second time was harder than the first,
because the first had not been a conquest at all.

They had held the regency since 1420 and run the government while a succession
of Marinid children occupied the throne. Then in 1465 a citizen of Fez killed
the last Marinid and the city made him ruler — a sharif, a man with the best
credential in Morocco, put up by popular acclamation over the heads of the
family that had actually been governing.

Leo Africanus tells what happened next in a few sentences, and they are the
whole founding of the dynasty.

## Beaten, and then not

The Wattasid — Leo writes his name as Saic Abra — was *pricked forward with
ambition*, and went about to conquer the city of Fez and make himself king. The
sharif took the advice of a counsellor of his, who happened to be the
Wattasid's own cousin, and vanquished him and put him to flight to his great
disgrace.

Then the sharif sent that counsellor away to Temesna to pacify a province that
was about to rebel.

The Wattasid came back. He besieged New Fez — the walled administrative city
the Marinids had built beside the old one — for a whole year with eight
thousand men, and at length took it by the treason of the townsmen, and
compelled the sharif and all his family to flee to the kingdom of Tunis.

## What that founding is made of

Not a doctrine, not a genealogy, not a battle in the field. A siege won by
waiting until the other man's best adviser was out of town, and then by paying
somebody inside.

It is the same method the Marinids had used to take Sijilmasa two centuries
earlier, and it worked for the same reason: in a country of walled cities and
mobile tribes, the decisive question is usually who inside the walls can be
bought. The difference is that the Marinids went on to hold Morocco for two
hundred years and the Wattasids were about to spend eighty of theirs watching
the coast go.

## The bill arrived in the same season

Leo puts it in the next sentence, and the sequence is not a coincidence — a
Moroccan civil war was exactly the opportunity Lisbon had been waiting for:

> The same time therefore that Saic besieged Fez, the king of Portugall
> sending a fleete into Africa, tooke Arzilla.

Among the captives shipped to Lisbon was a boy of seven and his sister,
children of the man who would rule Morocco next. He spent seven years there and
learned the language exactly; his father eventually ransomed him for a great
sum; and when he came to the throne people called him Muhammad the Portuguese.

A dynasty that had just won a year-long siege of its own capital acquired, in
the same months, a coastal port in Portuguese hands and an heir who spoke
Portuguese. Both facts stayed with it until the end.
