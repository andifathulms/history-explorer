In 1010 Qirwash ibn al-Muqallad, Uqaylid emir of Mosul, had the Friday sermon
in his territories — Mosul, Kufa, al-Mada'in, the towns of the middle Euphrates
— read in the name of the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim in Cairo instead of the
Abbasid caliph al-Qadir, whose city was a few days' ride downriver.

It is one of the most audacious single gestures in the eleventh century and it
lasted a few months. The Abbasid caliph and the Buyid amir who controlled him
responded with an army and with money, and Qirwash restored the Abbasid khutba,
having established what he needed to establish.

<Figure id="uq-gharib" />

What he had established is the shape of the polity. The Uqaylids were too
strong to remove and not strong enough to be independent, and the currency in
which that position was traded was the name read on a Friday. Cairo would pay
for it. Baghdad would pay more not to lose it. The Mirdasids at Aleppo ran the
same business against the same two customers, with Byzantium as a third. Both
emirates would have said, correctly, that they ruled by caliphal investiture.
The formula conceals the whole point, which is that in this corner of the
Islamic world the caliph was a variable.

The point is not that a caliph was named. It is that after 1010 both sides knew
what the naming cost, and the Uqaylids spent eighty years being paid to remain
exactly where they were.
