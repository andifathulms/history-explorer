---
title: The Friday the caliph changed
drafted_from: kennedy-2004

In 1010 Qirwash ibn al-Muqallad, Uqaylid emir of Mosul, had the Friday sermon in
his territories — Mosul, Kufa, al-Mada'in, the towns of the middle Euphrates —
read in the name of the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim in Cairo instead of the Abbasid
caliph al-Qadir, whose city was a few days' ride downriver.

It is one of the most audacious single gestures in the eleventh century and it
lasted a few months. The Abbasid caliph and the Buyid amir who controlled him
responded with an army and with money, and Qirwash restored the Abbasid khutba,
having established what he needed to establish.

What he had established is the shape of the polity. The Uqaylids were too strong
to remove and not strong enough to be independent, and the currency in which
that position was traded was the name read on a Friday. Cairo would pay for it.
Baghdad would pay more not to lose it. The Mirdasids at Aleppo ran the same
business against the same two customers, with Byzantium as a third, and this
site's institutions field records both emirates as legitimated by caliphal
investiture — which is true, and which conceals the whole point, because in this
corner of the corpus the caliph was a variable.

The turning point this site records is not that a caliph was named. It is that
after 1010 both sides knew what the naming cost, and the Uqaylids spent eighty
years being paid to remain exactly where they were.
