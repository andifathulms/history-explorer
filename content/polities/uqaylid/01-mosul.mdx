title: The buffer that took the city

The Banu Uqayl were an Arab tribe of the Jazira, and their entry into the record
as a polity is a piece of Buyid frontier management that went wrong in the
ordinary way. Around 990 the Buyids, ruling Iraq from Baghdad with the caliph in
their custody, settled the Uqayl in Diyar Bakr as a counterweight to Badh ibn
Dustak and the Kurds who were then taking the upper Tigris. Muhammad ibn
al-Musayyab fought Badh, as commissioned. He then kept going.

By 996 the Uqayl held Mosul. The Hamdanids — who had ruled it for a century and
who are on this site as the great Arab dynasty of the tenth-century Jazira —
ended there, in a takeover by another Arab tribe that had been hired to guard
the road. Their Aleppo branch outlived the Mosul one by eight years and then
passed to a freedman of its own, which is the other way these houses finished.

Kennedy's account of the tenth and eleventh centuries treats this as the period's
characteristic movement rather than as an accident. The caliphate's fiscal
machinery had been failing outward from Baghdad for a hundred years; what
replaced it in the Jazira and Syria was not chaos and not a state in the Abbasid
sense, but tribal confederations that could raise cavalry, hold a city, collect
its taxes and negotiate with empires. They were poor at building institutions
and very good at not being defeated, and between about 990 and 1090 they were
what the central lands had.

Mosul was a real prize and not a frontier town. It sat on the Tigris at the point
where the roads from Syria, Azerbaijan and Iraq met, it had a bridge and a
customs post, and its hinterland grew grain. It was the largest revenue in the
region outside Baghdad and Aleppo, and the Uqayl had it for a hundred years.
