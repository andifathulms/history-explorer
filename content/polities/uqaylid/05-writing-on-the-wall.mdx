title: Writing on the wall

A man called Abu'l-Hayja was travelling the road between Sinjar and Nisibin, in
the Jazira, in the company of the Uqaylid emir Qirwash. They stopped for the
night, and some time after dismounting the emir sent for him. He was lodged in
a castle nearby — a fine building looking out over gardens and watercourses,
named after a soldier called al-Abbas ibn Amr al-Ghanawi, who had been dead
about a century.

He found Qirwash standing and staring at something written on the wall.

## The first inscription

The traveller read it. It ran:

> *Castle of al-Abbas ibn Amr! Why did your Ibn Amr leave you? Long did you defy
> the strokes of fortune; how then did fortune take you by surprise? Alas! Where
> is your magnificence? Or rather, where is your hospitality? Or rather, where is
> your renown? Or rather, where is your glory?*

Underneath was written: *Written by Ali, the son of Abd Allah, the son of
Hamdan, with his own hand, in the year 331.*

That is Sayf al-Dawla, the Hamdanid, in 942 or 943 — a year or two before he
took Aleppo, at the very start of the career that made him the most celebrated
prince of his century.

## The second inscription

Beside it was a second poem:

> *O castle! What has become of the noble ones who dwelt here in former times?
> You were their contemporary and, in long duration, have outlived them all. The
> lines traced upon your walls renew my grief for the son of al-Musayyab. Know
> that I shall soon join him and that I am hastening on in his footsteps.*

Subscribed: *Written by Qirwash, the son of al-Mukallad ibn al-Musayyab, with
his own hand, in the year 401* — 1010 or 1011. The son of al-Musayyab he is
grieving for is his own father.

So the Uqaylid emir had stood in this room before, read the Hamdanid's lament
for a dead soldier, and answered it with a lament for his own dead father and
for himself.

## What he said next

The traveller was astonished and asked whether Qirwash had really written it.
He said he had, and then added: *I am thinking of having this castle thrown
down, for it is an unlucky place and has sent many to their graves.*

His companion wished him long life. Three days later they rode on, and — Ibn
Khallikan finishes the anecdote in a single clause — the castle has not yet
been demolished.

## Three hands on one wall

A tenth-century commander mourning an earlier one; an eleventh-century emir
mourning him and himself; a thirteenth-century judge in Cairo writing all of it
down because somebody told him about it; and a building that outlived every one
of them and was still standing when the last of them wrote.

The Uqaylids ran Mosul and the Jazira for a century by manoeuvring between
Baghdad and Cairo and being paid by both. Qirwash was the one who made that pay
best. What he wrote on the wall of somebody else's ruined castle, in his own
hand, is that he knew exactly how long it lasts.
