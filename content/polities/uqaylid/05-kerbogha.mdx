After 1085 the Uqaylid emirate was a Mosul-sized thing with a Jazira-sized
memory. Muslim's brothers and sons held pieces of it, quarrelled, and
acknowledged the Seljuks, and Malikshah's officers took the Jaziran towns one by
one. When Malikshah died in 1092 and the sultanate went into its own succession
war, Mosul survived a few years longer in the confusion.

In late 1096 a Turkish officer named Kerbogha took the city and made himself its
amir, and Uqaylid rule at Mosul ended. There is no battle in the sources worth
naming and no last emir this site will name, because the works consulted do not
supply one — the dynasty at Mosul stops being mentioned rather than being
defeated.

The timing is the thing worth noticing. Kerbogha took Mosul in 1096, which is
the year the First Crusade set out, and he is remembered in European writing for
one thing: the great relief army he brought to Antioch in 1098 and lost outside
its walls to a starving Frankish garrison. The atabeg who ends this polity is a
footnote in somebody else's founding story.

A branch of the family held Qal'at Ja'bar, a fortress on the Euphrates, until
1169, when Nur al-Din took it. That is seventy years of an Arab tribal emirate
surviving as a rock with a garrison, alongside the Artuqids doing the same thing
at Mardin for three centuries, and it is the ordinary fate of these houses: not
extinction, but a long reduction to whatever could not be conveniently besieged.
