Qirwash ibn al-Muqallad held Mosul for about half a century and is the reason
there is an Uqaylid state to write about. Ibn Khallikan gives him a life, and it
opens the way most emirs' lives in this region open — with the family.

## Two uncles

His father died while Qirwash was away, and he came back to a principality he had
to fight two of his own uncles for.

Ibn Khallikan names them both and then disposes of them in a sentence: one died
in 1001-2, the other in 1006-7, after which Qirwash *remained sole master of the
principality and was delivered from the anxiety which they had given him*.

What he was then master of is worth listing because it is larger than the name
Mosul suggests: Mosul itself, Kufa, al-Mada'in, and the country along the
Euphrates. An Arab tribal dynasty of the Banu Uqayl held the middle of Iraq and
the northern approaches to Baghdad, at a moment when the caliph in Baghdad was a
Buyid pensioner.

## The Ghuzz

Then something arrived that nobody in Mosul had a category for.

The Ghuzz — Turkmen, moving west ahead of the Seljuk state that had not yet
happened — got into Mosul, plundered Qirwash's palace, and carried off *more than
two hundred thousand dinars*.

He did not face them alone. He called in Dubais ibn Sadaqa, the Mazyadid chief of
Hilla, and their combined forces attacked the Ghuzz, killed a great many of them
and won.

An Arab poet of Baghdad wrote a *qasida* about it, and the terms he reached for
are the interesting part:

> You spared your native soil the disgrace of entombing their corpses; the only
> tomb they got was the vulture's maw. Yet they had overrun the land and obtained
> all that was possible of worldly wealth. They had broken open the gate of the
> barrier which kept Gog confined, but they found, in your prowess, the might of
> Alexander.

Gog and Magog, penned behind Alexander's wall until the end of the world. That is
the register in which the first Turkmen incursion into Iraq was described by the
people it happened to — not as a raid or a migration but as an eschatological
event, an apocalyptic barrier giving way.

The wall was breached and this time it held. Two Arab tribal chiefs, acting
together, beat the Ghuzz off. Within forty years the Seljuks would take Baghdad
and neither the Uqaylids nor the Mazyadids would be able to do anything about it.

## The man himself

Ibn Khallikan is a biographer rather than a chronicler, and what he wants recorded
about Qirwash is that he was a good scholar and an elegant poet whose pieces got
into general circulation.

One of them:

> Praised be adversity! It tarnishes the vile but gives polish to the noble. I was
> a mere mass of iron when adversity forged me into a sword, and the vicissitudes
> of fortune gave to my edge a free career.

A man who had taken his inheritance off two uncles and had his palace emptied by
strangers out of the steppe wrote a couplet arguing that being hammered is what
makes a blade. It is a boast, and it is also an accurate description of the
career.

He bore the honorific Majd al-Din, glory of religion. His mother was sister to
the lord of Irbil. And in 1010 he had the Friday prayer said throughout his
territories for the Fatimid caliph in Cairo — and then, Ibn Khallikan notes
without elaboration, *discontinued the practice*.
