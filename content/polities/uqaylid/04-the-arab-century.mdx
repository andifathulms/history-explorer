---
title: What the eleventh century was
drafted_from: kennedy-2004
phase: aside
---

This page and the three next to it — Mirdasid, Marwanid, Burid — exist because
the region's record had a hundred-year hole in it, between the Hamdanids ending
in 1004 and the Artuqids arriving in 1102. It is worth saying what was in the
hole, because it is the one stretch of this region's fifteen hundred years that
does not look like the rest of it.

Read the military basis field down this region's pages. Tulunid, Ikhshidid,
Zangid, Ayyubid, Mamluk all carry `slave-soldier`, across six centuries, and so
does the Hamdanid page alongside its tribal levy. Turkish and Circassian boys
bought on a frontier, trained in barracks, attached to a household, running
Egypt and Syria. It is the central lands' single most durable political fact and
this site returns to it on every page.

The eleventh century is the exception. Mirdasid, Uqaylid and Marwanid are all
coded `tribal-levy` — Arab and Kurdish confederations raising horsemen through
kinship, holding cities they mostly lived outside, financing themselves by being
strategically inconvenient to two empires at once. None of them bought a
regiment. None of them built an institution that outlived a strong emir. And all
three were finished inside fifteen years of each other by the arrival of Turkmen
who fought for a sultan.

The fourth page, the Burids at Damascus, is where the exception closes.
Toghtekin was a purchased Turkish soldier, his army was a purchased Turkish
askar, and after 1104 no polity ruling this region from Damascus or Cairo ever
raised its troops another way. The qualifier is doing real work: the Artuqids,
arriving in the Jazira two years earlier, are coded `tribal-levy` and held their
rocks on it until 1409. Tribal cavalry did not stop working. It stopped being
enough to hold a capital.

That makes the century a control group rather than an interlude, and it answers a
question the corpus otherwise cannot ask. The slave-soldier system is easy to
read as a pathology — the recurring failure this site has recorded from the
Samanids to the Mamluks is an army that eventually makes a ruler it prefers. The
eleventh century shows the alternative operating in the same country with the
same revenue, and the alternative could not raise a force that stayed in the
field past harvest, could not hold a city against a state, and did not last.

Neither arrangement is being recommended here. But a page that records only the
slave-soldier polities makes their world look inevitable, and it was not: for a
hundred years the central lands were governed by tribes, and the reason they
stopped being is on this page.
