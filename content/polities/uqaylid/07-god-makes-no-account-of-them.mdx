Qirwash governed the Uqaylid state for fifty years — Mosul, Kufa, Mada'in and
the Euphrates bank — and Ibn Khallikan's portrait of him is the sharpest thing
written about any ruler of the Jazira in that century.

He was, the dictionary says, very generous, a great plunderer and a free giver,
following in this the custom of the desert Arabs. He was a good scholar and an
elegant poet, and some of his pieces were in general circulation.

## Two remarks

Two of his sayings were remembered, and both are the sort of thing a man says
when he is certain nothing will happen to him.

He was reproached for being married to two sisters at the same time, which is
plainly forbidden. He answered: *tell me what thing we ever did which was
permitted by law.*

And on his own conscience, he said: *I have nothing on my conscience except the
death of five or six inhabitants of the desert whom I slew; as for the
townspeople, God makes no account of them.*

That is the whole political theory of an Arab tribal dynasty holding cities it
did not belong to, delivered in one sentence by its most successful ruler. The
Uqaylids drew Mosul's revenue for a century and never stopped regarding the
people who produced it as a different order of being from the tribe. Killing a
bedouin was a matter for the conscience. Killing a townsman was not an event.

## What it cost him

In 1049 or 1050, after fifty years in power, Qirwash quarrelled with his
brother Baraka, who was living outside the city. He fell into his brother's
hands, was put in chains and thrown into prison.

Baraka took the supreme command, gave himself a title meaning guardian of the
empire, ruled two years and died in 1052. His nephew Quraysh succeeded, and the
first thing Quraysh did was hold a public audience and have his uncle Qirwash
put to death in front of him.

He was buried at Tell Tawba, east of Mosul.

## The pattern

A man who had written verses on a ruined castle wall about the transience of
princes, and who had said in so many words that the lives of townsmen did not
count, spent his last three years chained up by his brother and was executed at
an audience by his nephew.

The dynasty had thirty-four years left. What killed it in the end was Turkish
armies out of the east, and they walked into a state that had spent two
generations demonstrating that its own family could not hold a succession, in a
city whose inhabitants had been told plainly where they stood.
