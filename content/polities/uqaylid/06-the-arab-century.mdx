For six hundred years the lands between the Nile and the Tigris were governed
by slave soldiers. Turkish and Circassian boys bought on a frontier, trained in
barracks, attached to a household — Tulunid, Ikhshidid, Zangid, Ayyubid,
Mamluk, and the Hamdanids alongside their tribal cavalry. It is the single most
durable political fact about the region.

The eleventh century is the exception, and it is the reason this hundred years
is worth separating out.

## Governed by tribes

Between the Hamdanids ending in 1004 and the Turkmen arriving a century later,
the Jazira and northern Syria were held by Arab and Kurdish confederations: the
Uqaylids at Mosul, the Mirdasids at Aleppo, the Marwanids at Diyarbakr.

They raised horsemen through kinship. They held cities they largely lived
outside. They financed themselves by being strategically inconvenient to two
empires at once — Fatimid Cairo on one side, Byzantium and then the Seljuks on
the other — and by being cheaper to subsidise than to remove.

None of them bought a regiment. None of them built an institution that outlived
a strong emir. And all three were finished within about fifteen years of each
other by the arrival of Turkmen who fought for a sultan.

## Why it closed

Damascus is where it closes. Toghtekin was a purchased Turkish soldier, his
army was a purchased Turkish *askar*, and after 1104 no polity ruling from
Damascus or Cairo ever raised its troops another way.

The qualifier matters, though. Tribal cavalry did not stop working: the
Artuqids arrived in the Jazira two years earlier, raised their men exactly the
same way as the Uqaylids had, and held their rocks on that basis until 1409.
What tribal cavalry stopped being was enough to hold a capital.

That makes the eleventh century something better than an interlude. It is the
closest thing available to a control: the same country, the same revenues, the
same neighbours, governed for a hundred years by the alternative system.

The slave-soldier arrangement is easy to read as a pathology, and the failure
mode is real and recurs from the Samanids to the Mamluks — an army that can
make a ruler eventually makes one it prefers. But the alternative could not
raise a force that stayed in the field past harvest, could not hold a city
against a state, and did not last. Neither arrangement is being recommended.
The point is that the world of the slave regiments looks inevitable only if you
never look at the century when it was not there.
