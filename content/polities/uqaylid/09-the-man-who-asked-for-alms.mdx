Mosul passed out of Arab hands in 1096 and never came back into them.

What it got instead was a series of Turkish amirs appointed from the east, and
the best account of what that looked like is Ibn al-Qalanisi's description of
one of them dying in Damascus seventeen years later.

## The procession

On the last Friday of Second Rabi' 507 — 2 October 1113 — Mawdud, lord of
Mosul, came in from his camp outside the Iron Gate to the great mosque of
Damascus, as he did every week, with the atabeg of the city walking in front of
him as a mark of respect.

Around the two of them were Daylamites, Turks, Khurasanis, armed men and
armour-bearers with weapons of every kind — tempered blades, thrusting-swords,
rapiers, drawn poniards — so that, Qalanisi writes, they walked as if through a
tangled thicket of intertwined spikes, while the people stood around to see
their pomp and the magnificence of their state.

They entered the courtyard. A man came out of the crowd, unnoticed by anybody,
approached Mawdud as though to bless him and beg alms, took hold of his cloak
belt and struck him twice below the navel.

Swords fell on the assassin from every side. His head was cut off so that he
could be identified, and he could not be, so a fire was lit and he was thrown
onto it.

Mawdud walked on a few paces. The crowd, seeing him upright and moving, calmed
down and assumed he had survived. He collapsed near the north gate, was carried
to the atabeg's residence with the atabeg walking beside him, and died a few
hours later after a surgeon had sewn up part of the wounds.

## The province he had left

Qalanisi appends a judgement on his government at Mosul that is worth having.

His conduct there had at first been tyrannical, and his treatment of the
citizens was such that a great many people fled the province to get away from
him. Then he learned that the sultan's attitude towards him had changed, and he
abandoned that conduct entirely; his actions became upright and his fairness
was manifest, and he started again on exactly the opposite lines.

A governor's justice, in other words, was a function of whether his master was
pleased with him.

## What the Uqaylids had been

Set that beside what Mosul had had before. The Uqaylids were an Arab tribal
dynasty of the country itself, who could be bad rulers and often were, but who
were nobody's appointment and had nowhere else to be. Their emirs were buried
outside Mosul.

Mawdud's followers, when he died, packed his baggage, his jewels and his
treasure and went home to Mosul and elsewhere, which is the sentence that
describes the new arrangement exactly: the province was somewhere its rulers
came from and returned to, and their property travelled with them.

Fourteen years after Mawdud's death the sultan sent another officer to Mosul,
and that one was Zangi. The Jazira never again produced a state of its own.
