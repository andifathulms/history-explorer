Muslim ibn Quraysh, titled Sharaf al-Dawla, ruled from 1061 and made the
Uqaylids briefly into the largest Arab power between Egypt and Iran. He held
Mosul and the Jazira; he took Harran and Raqqa; he acknowledged the Seljuk
sultan Malikshah and used the acknowledgement as cover. In 1080 the people of
Aleppo, unable to get protection from their own Mirdasid emirs against Turkmen
raiding, invited him in, and he took the city without a siege.

That is the high point on the map and it lasted five years. The problem was the
one every Arab power in this period eventually hit: the Turkmen bands moving
into the Jazira and northern Syria were not a state that could be treated with
and not a raid that could be waited out. Muslim used them, hired them, fought
them, and was in the end destroyed by one of their commanders. In 1085, near
Antioch, he was killed fighting Sulayman ibn Qutulmish — the Seljuk prince who
had taken Anatolia after Manzikert, and who founded the line this site records
as the Sultanate of Rum.

Wilson's reading of the decade is that the Syrian and Jaziran elites were not
overwhelmed by an invasion so much as outcompeted inside their own political
system: they used Turkmen troops the way they had always used auxiliaries, and
the auxiliaries turned out to have a sultan behind them, a claim on the land they
grazed, and no interest in going home. The Uqaylids, the Mirdasids and the
Marwanids all made the same calculation and all lost within fifteen years of each
other.

Everything Muslim had assembled came apart in a season. Aleppo went to the
Seljuks; the Jazira was divided among his relatives; Mosul held out, smaller
each decade.
