The system that had produced a sukkalmah powerful enough to be
addressed as an elder by the kings of Babylon and Mari did not stay
that strong. Toward the later part of the sukkalmah period the sources
grow thin, and the claims made in what does survive grow
correspondingly grand. A number of later rulers style themselves
descendants of the great sukkalmah Shilhaha, but the scribes who
copied their inscriptions, working centuries afterward, knew one such
king only as "a son of Lanku" - an otherwise unknown figure whose
actual place in the succession is impossible to establish.

## Reaching for a name when the specifics run out

Reaching for a famous ancestor's name while the documented specifics
grow vaguer is, historically, often exactly what a weakening state
does - a government that had once produced enough correspondence to
survive independently in a foreign archive at Mari was, within a
generation or two of Siwepalarhuppak's own reign, no longer generating
the kind of record that lets a modern history narrate individual
kings at all. The triarchic system itself may well have persisted in
some form; what did not persist was the documentary richness that had
made the earlier sukkalmahs legible as individuals rather than as
names in a genealogical claim nobody could any longer verify.
