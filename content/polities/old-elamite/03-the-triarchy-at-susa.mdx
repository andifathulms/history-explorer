title: The triarchy at Susa
drafted_from: cameron-1936

Power in Elam eventually reorganized itself into a system unlike
anything the Awan or early Shimashki kings had used: a triarchy of
sorts, with a Grand Regent, or sukkalmah, ruling from Susa alongside
two subordinate viceroys - one over Elam and Shimashki jointly, one
over Susa itself. It was a durable answer to a problem Kindattu's own
generation had never had to solve, since Kindattu had simply held
Elam and his Mesopotamian conquests together in one hand; the
sukkalmah system was built instead for a state that expected to govern
itself as three linked offices indefinitely, not as one man's personal
holding.

## A succession that ran sideways before it ran down

Succession among the three offices did not run simply from father to
son. The pattern that emerges from the sources is collateral, passing
between brothers before descending, eventually, to a sister's son - an
arrangement that kept the three highest offices in Elam within one
extended family across generations without ever depending on any
single ruler producing a son who outlived him. Whatever its origins,
the system produced real continuity: a line of sukkalmahs governed
Elam through this triarchic arrangement for roughly two centuries, a
span of stable, documented government longer than either the Awan or
the Shimashki line had managed to sustain on its own.

## A government legible enough to be named individually

The system's durability is itself the reason a sukkalmah's own reign
can be narrated in more than outline at all: unlike the Awan king list
that reduces eleven rulers to little more than names, the sukkalmah
period produced enough correspondence and enough inscriptions that at
least one grand regent, Siwepalarhuppak, is well enough attested to be
treated as an individual figure rather than a line in a succession
list - a change in the kind of evidence Elam's own government was
generating, not merely in how long it lasted.
