Eleven kings of Awan had already ruled at Susa, most of them barely
more than names on a king list, before Puzur-Inshushinak, the twelfth
and last of that line, gave the dynasty a real place in recorded
history. He served, for a time, as a nominal subject of the Akkadian
kings to the west - the same empire that ruled Sumer and Akkad from
Agade - but he waited. Only after his overlord Naram-Sin died and gave
way to a weaker successor, Sharkalisharri, did Puzur-Inshushinak make
his move.

## An invasion that reached the enemy's own heartland

Allied with the neighboring land of Zahara, Puzur-Inshushinak invaded
Babylonia early in the new king's reign, and his troops were driven
back only after penetrating as far as the territory of Opis, deep in
the Akkadian heartland. For an Elamite army to reach that far and
return home intact was itself remarkable - a genuine military
achievement, not a raid that happened to go unpunished. On the
strength of it, he was crowned king of Awan and began styling himself,
on his own monuments, "King of the Four World-Regions" - a title of
universal kingship no earlier Elamite ruler had claimed.

## A revival of language alongside a revival of power

Puzur-Inshushinak's own inscriptions show him working on two fronts at
once. Some of his dedications are in Akkadian, the diplomatic language
of the age, but others carry a parallel text in a still-undeciphered
script scholars call proto-Elamite - a deliberate attempt, it appears,
to revive a specifically Elamite written tradition alongside Elamite
political independence. He rebuilt the temple of the god Inshushinak
at Susa with real ceremony, sacrificing daily and stationing musicians
at its gate. It was the record of a ruler building a state's identity
in more than one register simultaneously - the last king of a fading
line who briefly made himself the most powerful sovereign Elam had
yet produced.
