Siwepalarhuppak did not merely hold the office of sukkalmah - for a
stretch of years in the middle of his reign, he was the most powerful
individual ruler in the whole of the Near East, and the evidence for
it does not come from Elam's own archive at all. It survives in the
diplomatic correspondence of a Mesopotamian kingdom four hundred miles
to the west: the royal archive of Mari, on the Euphrates, whose kings
maintained a running correspondence with every significant power of
their day and, in doing so, incidentally preserved a record of
Siwepalarhuppak's own standing that no Elamite source could match.

## A kinship vocabulary that was really a hierarchy

Mesopotamian diplomatic correspondence of the period used family terms
to encode rank: a king addressed an equal as "brother," a weaker
counterpart as "son," and a superior as "father." In the letters that
survive from Mari, both Zimri-Lim of Mari and Hammurabi of Babylon
address Siwepalarhuppak as father - the position ordinarily reserved
for an acknowledged overlord. It is the clearest evidence available
that an Elamite sukkalmah, for a time, outranked in practice the two
kings who would otherwise be remembered as the leading powers of their
own generation in Mesopotamia.

## The coalition Siwepalarhuppak built, and the one he lost

Siwepalarhuppak used that standing directly: he brought Mari and
Babylon together in an alliance against Eshnunna, a rival Mesopotamian
power, and the coalition succeeded - Eshnunna fell within Elam's
sphere of influence not long afterward. But an overlord's position
built on other kings' cooperation rather than on direct conquest does
not survive a shift in what those kings judge to be in their own
interest. Hammurabi, who spent decades absorbing his Mesopotamian
rivals one at a time including the old kingdom of Larsa itself, did
not remain a junior partner for long: within a few years of the
Eshnunna campaign he turned against the Elamite coalition that had
helped install him as the region's rising power, and Siwepalarhuppak's
own moment as the acknowledged senior figure of the whole Mesopotamian
and Elamite world passed with it.

## What the correspondence actually proves

None of this is visible in Elam's own inscriptions, which is itself
worth noting: the clearest evidence of a sukkalmah's real power comes
from a foreign archive that happened to survive, in exactly the same
way a Kassite king's own reputation survives only through his son's
letters to Egypt. Siwepalarhuppak's reign is also the clearest point
of contact between Elam's own triarchic government and the
Isin-Larsa-Babylon rivalry playing out at the same time in southern
Mesopotamia - a reminder that the sukkalmahs governing Elam through
these two centuries were not a power operating in isolation, but
active participants in exactly the same contest for the region that
Gungunum's own secession from Isin had opened generations earlier, and
that a chief with an Elamite name would go on to found the dynasty
that eventually gave Larsa its own last king.
