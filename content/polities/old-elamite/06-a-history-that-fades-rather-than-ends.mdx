There is no recorded battle that ends this long Elamite sequence, no
named conqueror, no captured king. The sources themselves simply stop
providing usable information at a certain point - by around 1750 BC,
in the words of the record consulted here, Elam "fades almost entirely
from our view."

## What the silence does and does not mean

The fade is not evidence that Elamite kingship itself stopped, only
that the documentation of it did. Nothing in the record describes a
foreign conquest, an internal collapse, or even a disputed succession
of the kind that ends other Elamite dynasties in this account; the
triarchic system built around Susa, Elam-and-Shimashki, and a
collateral sukkalmah succession may well have continued to function
for some time after the last inscription this source can date. What
changed was the world outside Elam's own borders rather than anything
demonstrably internal to it: the Babylonian correspondents who had
once addressed a sukkalmah as an elder stopped writing letters that
survived, and without that outside archive to supplement Elam's own
thin epigraphic record, the individual kings of the kingdom's final
decades simply stopped being visible to history at all.

## A gap, not an ending

What filled the two and a half centuries between that silence and the
next clearly attested Elamite kingdom is not recoverable from this
source; only that the identity persisted long enough, in some form,
for a later dynasty to pick up the old traditions of Susa and Anshan
as though they had never fully stopped.
