Power in Elam eventually reorganized itself into a system unlike
anything the Awan or early Shimashki kings had used: a triarchy of
sorts, with a Grand Regent, or sukkalmah, ruling from Susa alongside
two subordinate viceroys - one over Elam and Shimashki jointly, one
over Susa itself. Succession among these offices did not run simply
from father to son; the pattern that emerges from the sources is
collateral, passing between brothers before descending, eventually, to
a sister's son. The system produced real continuity for a time - a
line of sukkalmahs, among them the well-attested Siwepalarhuppak,
governed Elam through this arrangement for roughly two centuries.

## Claims to greatness from rulers the record barely knows

Toward the end of the period covered here, the sources grow thin and
the claims made in them grow correspondingly grand. A number of later
rulers style themselves descendants of the great sukkalmah Shilhaha,
but the scribes who copied their inscriptions, working centuries
afterward, knew one such king only as "a son of Lanku" - an
otherwise unknown figure whose actual place in the succession is
impossible to establish. Reaching for a famous ancestor's name while
the documented specifics grow vaguer is, historically, often exactly
what a weakening state does.

## Not a conquest, but a silence

There is no recorded battle that ends this long Elamite sequence, no
named conqueror, no captured king. The sources themselves simply stop
providing usable information at a certain point - by around 1750 BC,
in the words of the record consulted here, Elam "fades almost entirely
from our view." What filled the two and a half centuries between that
silence and the next clearly attested Elamite kingdom is not
recoverable from this source; only that the identity persisted long
enough, in some form, for a later dynasty to pick up the old
traditions of Susa and Anshan as though they had never fully stopped.
