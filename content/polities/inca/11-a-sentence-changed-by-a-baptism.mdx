Atahualpa spent months in captivity after Cajamarca, filling a room
with gold and silver as ransom for a freedom Pizarro never actually
granted him. Rather than release their prisoner once the treasure
arrived, the Spanish put him on trial before a tribunal of their own
devising, on charges - idolatry, the killing of Huascar, plotting
against the Spanish themselves - that many of Pizarro's own men
considered a legal fiction dressed up to justify a decision already
made. Atahualpa, when he understood his death was certain, pleaded for
his own life in the most piteous terms his captors had yet heard from
him, offering any guarantee the Spanish might want and double the
ransom already paid if only he were given time to raise it. By one
eyewitness's own account, Pizarro was visibly moved as he turned away
from the appeal - and proceeded with the execution regardless.

## A stake exchanged for a garrote

The sentence was proclaimed by trumpet in the great square of Cajamarca,
and two hours after sunset on the twenty-ninth of August, 1533, Spanish
soldiers gathered by torchlight to watch it carried out. Atahualpa was
led out in chains, Father Valverde at his side making one final attempt
to win a genuine conversion rather than merely a formality. Bound to the
stake with the firewood meant to burn him already laid around his feet,
Atahualpa was offered a different death: convert and accept baptism,
Valverde told him, and the sentence would be commuted from burning to
the comparatively merciful garrote. Atahualpa asked whether this was
truly so, and on Pizarro's own confirmation, agreed. Valverde baptized
him on the spot under the name Juan de Atahuallpa, and the Inca who had
once thrown a Bible to the ground died by strangulation rather than by
fire, still in the same square where his own empire's fate had already
been decided nine months earlier.
