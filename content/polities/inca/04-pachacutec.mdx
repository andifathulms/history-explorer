Inca Yupanqui prepared Cuzco's defence with whatever time the city had
left. He promised land and privileges to anyone from the wider province
willing to come and fight as a citizen of Cuzco, drawing reinforcements
from beyond the city's own walls, and appeared in the great square
wearing a lion's skin over his head as a sign that he meant to be as
strong as the animal it came from. When the Chanka army pitched camp on
a hill overlooking the city, the people of Cuzco dug concealed pits
filled with sharpened stakes along the approaches, and the women and
children, seeing the enemy for the first time, filled the streets with
their own frightened cries.

## A parley that ended in blows

Inca Yupanqui first tried to avoid a general slaughter, sending word to
the Chanka commander Hastu Huaraca proposing a negotiated settlement.
Hastu Huaraca thought little of the offer and wanted the matter settled
by battle alone, though his own relatives eventually persuaded him to
meet the Inca in person. The meeting achieved nothing: both men had
already decided on war, and their own heated words turned quickly to
open fighting between their armies at the city's very approaches.

## A victory that unseated a brother as well as an enemy

The battle raged for a full day and into the night before Inca Yupanqui
broke the Chanka assault decisively; by most accounts, barely five
hundred of the invading army escaped alive, Hastu Huaraca himself among
them. Inca Yupanqui returned to Cuzco in triumph and put the question he
had deferred squarely to the city's own nobles: he had defended them
while his father and brother had done nothing. The Orejones agreed, and
by common consent stripped Inca Urco of the succession and gave the
royal fringe to Inca Yupanqui instead, who took the name by which
history would remember him - Pachacutec, "he who remakes the world." A
regional lordship that had nearly fallen to a rival power instead had, in
the same crisis, found the king who would turn it into an empire.
