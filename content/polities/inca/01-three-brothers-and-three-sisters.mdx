When Pedro de Cieza de León visited Cuzco around 1550, he sought out
surviving descendants of Huayna Capac and members of the Orejones, the
Inca nobility most respected for their own knowledge of the past, and
asked them directly where their people had come from. What they told
him, he suspected, had been exaggerated by generations of retelling -
but it was the fullest account he could get, and he wrote it down as
given.

## A cave called the House of Production

In a time when the peoples of the region lived in disorder, killing
each other and sunk in vice, three men and three women appeared at a
place near Cuzco called Pacarec Tampu - "the House of Production." The
Orejones gave the men's names as Ayar Ucho, Ayar Cachi Asauca and Ayar
Manco, and the women's as Mama Huaco, Mama Cora and Mama Bahua. Other
traditions Cieza recorded gave entirely different names for the same
six figures - Balboa's version calls the founding brother Manco Capac
rather than Ayar Manco, and Garcilaso de la Vega's account agrees on
Manco Capac but names only one sister, Mama Ocllo, as his wife. No two
chroniclers who wrote down this story told it quite the same way.

## A doubt the storyteller did not hide

Cieza himself was frank about his own scepticism. What he believed
actually happened, he wrote, was that three valiant and powerful
brothers - whatever their real names - conquered and acquired the
lordship they came to hold, and that later generations dressed up an
ordinary conquest as a divine origin to justify calling their rulers
children of the Sun. He allowed, without much conviction, that what the
Orejones told him of magical stone-throwing and earth-splitting might
be the work of sorcerers rather than embellishment - but concluded,
either way, that no better explanation was available to him.
