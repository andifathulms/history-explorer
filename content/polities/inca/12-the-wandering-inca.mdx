---
title: The wandering Inca
drafted_from: markham-1910

Atahualpa's execution did not end the Inca throne, only Atahualpa's
own hold on it. Pizarro needed a native ruler he could govern through,
and the councillors of Cuzco, judging Spanish power irresistible,
brought out a young prince named Manco in the royal litter to meet
the conquerors at the Apurimac bridge. He was escorted to Cuzco and
crowned with the full ceremony of his ancestors - a puppet installed
with every outward mark of legitimacy, and treated, at first, with
real cordiality by Pizarro himself.

## A yoke that became unbearable

It did not last. As Manco grew into manhood he found himself ruling
in name only, while his own people suffered cruelty and injustice
under the Spaniards occupying his capital. He escaped Cuzco, raised
an army of Orejones - the Inca nobility - and laid siege to the city
his own ancestors had built, occupying the fortress of Sacsahuaman
above it. The siege very nearly succeeded; only the fortress's fall,
and the approach of a relieving force under Almagro, forced Manco to
withdraw. He fell back down the Urubamba valley to the stronghold of
Ollantaytambo, where he won the last outright Peruvian victory of the
war, repulsing an assault led by Hernando Pizarro in person - before
being driven out of that position too and retreating deeper still,
into the remote mountain country between the Apurimac and Urubamba
rivers known as Vilcapampa. There, thirty miles wide and walled in by
its own rivers and tropical forest, Inca sovereignty survived the fall
of Cuzco by another thirty years.

## A wife shot to death, and reprisals that followed

Manco rebuilt a court in exile, with temples and a palace raised to
replace what he had lost, and watched every approach to his own
territory. Pizarro tried repeatedly to negotiate a surrender; Manco,
who trusted no Spanish promise, refused every overture. Pizarro's
response was to take one of Manco's own wives prisoner, strip and
flog her, and finally shoot her to death with arrows - an atrocity
that drove Manco to answer with reprisals of his own against Spaniards
caught on the roads to Cuzco. When the younger Almagro's own rebellion
was crushed and its survivors scattered, a handful of his defeated
followers fled into Vilcapampa itself, where Manco, his hostility
aimed at Pizarro's faction rather than at Spaniards as such, received
them with hospitality and armed them from his own stores.

## Killed over a game of bowls

That hospitality killed him. One of the refugees, a violent man named
Gomez Perez, was playing bowls with the Inca when his own insolence
provoked Manco to push him back with a rebuke. Perez, in a sudden
rage, struck Manco with the wooden ball hard enough to kill him
outright. The Inca's own household cut down all eight of the
Spaniards responsible, burning them out of the house where they took
refuge and shooting the survivors as they fled the flames. Manco had
reigned in Vilcapampa for a decade, watching events, still hopeful to
the end that a new viceroy and new laws from Spain might yet improve
his people's condition. He left three sons - Sayri Tupac, Titu Cusi
Yupanqui and Tupac Amaru - to carry on the resistance he had built in
the mountains his ancestors had never ruled from before him.
