Sayri Tupac inherited his father's throne in Vilcapampa still a
minor, governed by regents until he came of age - and, unlike Manco,
he eventually chose to leave. The Spanish viceroy, the Marquis of
Cañete, spent years pressing for exactly this outcome, and in 1557,
after careful negotiation and rich gifts carried in by trusted
intermediaries, Sayri Tupac agreed to come out of the mountains and
live among the Spaniards. He was received cordially at Lima, granted
a pension and an estate in the Urubamba valley, married a granddaughter
of the murdered Inca Huascar, and was baptized alongside her at Cuzco.
He died in 1560, the first of Manco's sons to choose accommodation
over resistance - and the last Inca to die a natural death.

## A brother who would not be moved

Titu Cusi Yupanqui, who succeeded him in Vilcapampa, proved a harder
case. Spanish envoys arrived at his own court seeking to persuade him,
as his brother had been persuaded, to submit; Titu Cusi received them
courteously, listened to every argument, and simply temporized until
the embassy gave up and went home. He did allow one friar, Diego
Ortiz, to remain behind as a missionary, and eventually accepted
baptism himself under the name Felipe. When Titu Cusi then fell
fatally ill, the same Spaniards who had promised his people miraculous
cures found themselves blamed for his death: Ortiz and the interpreter
who had accompanied him were killed by the Inca's own household, and
a second Spanish embassy, arriving before news of the death had even
reached Cuzco, met the same fate at the border of Vilcapampa.

## A reluctant king, and an invasion with its pretext ready-made

Titu Cusi's own younger brother, Tupac Amaru, had been kept in
seclusion in the House of the Sun on the grounds of his inexperience,
and played no part in the killings that followed his brother's death.
It made no difference. Once the crisis passed, Vilcapampa's own chiefs
brought him out of seclusion and crowned him regardless - the full
Inca regalia, the fringe, the mantle, the ceremonial litter, placed on
a man who had wanted none of it. For the Spanish viceroy, Francisco
de Toledo, the deaths of the two Spanish envoys were exactly the
pretext an invasion of Vilcapampa had been waiting for. He sent a
column under Martin Hernando de Arbieto that fought its way down the
Urubamba valley and through the mountain passes; Tupac Amaru, fleeing
toward friendly tribes in the lowland forest with canoes already
waiting on a river to carry him to safety, was overtaken and captured
on 4 October 1571.

## A public execution, and a cry the Spaniards themselves could not silence

Tupac Amaru was marched back to Cuzco in his own imperial robes, a
rope around his neck, and given a mock trial that condemned him to
beheading. Church leaders across the city - the Bishop of Popayán,
the heads of every religious order, the Jesuit rector - went on their
knees before Toledo begging that the young Inca's life be spared and
his case sent to the king in Spain instead. Toledo refused. Tupac
Amaru was baptized in his cell, led out through streets packed with
weeping Indians, and beheaded on a public scaffold before Cuzco's own
cathedral - a crowd's grief loud enough, one witness wrote, that "it
seemed as if the day of judgment had come." Even some of the
Spaniards present were said to be horrified, knowing the young man
they had just killed had committed no crime of his own. His head was
displayed on a pole; his body was carried to his mother's house.
Not forty years after Pizarro first met Atahualpa at Cajamarca, the
line of Inca sovereigns that had ruled from Cuzco, then survived in
exile among the mountains of Vilcapampa, ended on a scaffold in the
same city its own founders had built.
