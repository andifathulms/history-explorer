War between Huascar and Atahualpa became certain once each brother's
own supporters realized neither would yield the succession without a
fight. Huascar commanded the loyalty of Cuzco itself and its
surrounding heartland; Atahualpa held the army and the native lords of
the northern provinces around Quito, along with the captains who had
served under their shared father in his own northern campaigns.
Huascar's general Atoc marched north to confront him, exhorting his own
troops to defend their rightful Inca and sending Orejón envoys ahead to
urge Atahualpa to submit rather than plunge the empire into civil war.

## A laugh, then an execution

Atahualpa's answer to the peace embassy was to laugh at it, then, after
a round of threats, to have the messengers killed outright. He handed
command of his own army to his general Chalcuchima and continued his
own journey in a rich litter carried by favoured attendants while the
armies did the fighting in his name. The two forces met near Ambato,
where Chalcuchima's own reserve of five thousand chosen men struck
Atoc's exhausted troops at the decisive moment, breaking them
completely. Atoc himself was captured, tied to a post, and killed with
deliberate cruelty; Chalcuchima had his skull made into a drinking cup
rimmed with gold. Between both sides, as many as fifteen or sixteen
thousand men died in the battle - a toll later observers, judging by
the scattered bones still visible at the site, suspected was an
understatement.

## A victory too far from Cuzco to be made official

Atahualpa's own renown spread through the kingdom on the strength of the
win, and his supporters began hailing him as Inca in his own right. He
declared he would take the royal fringe at Tumebamba rather than travel
to Cuzco for it - though by the empire's own custom, a coronation
performed anywhere but the capital was considered invalid. Atahualpa
proceeded anyway, treated as a king by his own followers in everything
but the one ceremony that would have made the title beyond dispute.
