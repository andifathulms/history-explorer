title: "The clouds have an unfavourable flight"

Nearly a decade of war between the three rival chiefs of Hawaii ended
not on a battlefield but at the dedication of a temple. With a great
new heiau built at Puukohola for his war god nearing completion,
Kamehameha sent two of his most trusted counsellors, Keaweaheulu and
Kamanawa, to Keoua's own court in Kau with an offer: come to Kona and
be reconciled, and rule the island together as brothers. Keoua's own
chief adviser urged him to kill the two envoys instead and seize the
advantage their deaths would hand him. Keoua refused - "they are the
brothers of my father, and they shall not die" - and agreed to go.

## A chief who prepared for either outcome

Whatever mixture of trust, fatalism, and weariness with endless war
moved him, Keoua traveled toward Kawaihae with a divided mind. On the
very morning of his arrival he bathed and dressed himself for a
chief's death as carefully as he had accepted the promise of peace,
selecting from his own company those who would die with him if it
came to that, and sending the rest ahead under the charge of a young
relative he judged Kamehameha would spare. As his canoe rounded Puako
and the bay of Kawaihae came into view - the great new temple, a
semicircle of war canoes mounted with guns, crowds of armed men on the
beach - Keoua read the signs plainly. "It looks bad ashore," he told
Keaweaheulu, "the clouds are flying unfavourably." Keaweaheulu asked
what evil could come on so pleasant a day. Keoua only repeated
himself.

## Struck down while stepping ashore

As Keoua's canoe reached the landing, Keeaumoku's men surrounded it
and cut it off from the envoys who had brought him. Kamehameha called
out from the beach for Keoua to come ashore so they might know each
other. As Keoua leapt down, Keeaumoku struck him with a spear; Keoua
seized the shaft and struggled to wrench it free, but fell and died
where he landed. Nearly everyone in his own canoe and escort was
killed with him, save a priest who hid himself under the mats of a
tabu house and one man who had quietly slipped away earlier at Puako.
With Keoua's death and his body's consecration at the newly finished
temple, the last rival claim to Hawaii Island's own throne was gone,
and for the first time since Mokuohai, Kamehameha ruled the whole
island alone.
