title: I yielded to avoid bloodshed
drafted_from: liliuokalani-1898

Liliuokalani wrote her own account of the surrender within five years
of it, and was plain about why she chose not to resist an armed force
she had no realistic hope of defeating. "I yielded my authority to
the forces of the United States," she wrote, "in order to avoid
bloodshed, and because I recognized the futility of a contest with so
formidable a power." She protested the same day to the men who had
just deposed her, and again, later, to the government of the United
States directly, on the understanding - never honored - that her case
would be referred to Washington for arbitration rather than settled
by the men who had benefited from her overthrow.

When a treaty of annexation was signed at Washington without her
consent or her people's, she sent her own secretaries to the State
Department to lodge a formal protest in her own name, declaring the
treaty "an act of wrong toward the native and part-native people of
Hawaii" and "the perpetuation of the fraud whereby the constitutional
government was overthrown." The protest changed nothing. But it stands,
in her own words rather than an official's, as the clearest statement
of how the kingdom's own last monarch understood what had been done to
her - not a peaceful transfer of power freely made, but a surrender
extracted under the shadow of a warship she had no means to answer.
