The provisional government that replaced the monarchy in 1893 gave
way, within a year, to a formally declared Republic of Hawaii under
President Sanford B. Dole - a government built from the start to
negotiate its own absorption into the United States rather than to
govern indefinitely on its own account. An 1893 annexation treaty was withdrawn by the incoming President
Cleveland, whose own investigator, Commissioner Blount, reported that
the revolution had been brought about with the aid of the American
minister to Hawaii - a finding that led Cleveland to try, unsuccessfully,
to have the queen restored before the matter passed out of his hands
entirely. The 1897 treaty signed
under his successor met the same fate in the Senate, unable to command
the two-thirds vote the Constitution required.

What a treaty could not do, a joint resolution did. Passing the House
209 to 91 and the Senate 42 to 21, the resolution for annexation was
signed by President McKinley on the 7th of July, 1898. The news
reached Honolulu on the 13th to what the record calls great
enthusiasm among the annexationist residents who had engineered the
whole process from the Committee of Safety onward. On the 12th of
August, 1898, the formal transfer of sovereignty took place, and the
flag of the United States was raised over the executive building - the
same building the Committee of Safety had occupied five years earlier
- with ceremonies the source calls appropriate and impressive.

## From kingdom to territory

The republic's own officers continued to govern, now subject to the
President of the United States rather than to any constitution of
their own making, until Congress passed an organic act in April 1900
establishing the Territory of Hawaii outright, with its own elected
legislature and a governor appointed from Washington. Sanford Dole,
who had presided over the Committee of Safety's provisional government
in 1893, was appointed the Territory's first governor - continuity of
personnel across every stage of a kingdom's dismantling, from the
palace steps in January 1893 to the executive building five and a half
years later, under a flag that was never Hawaii's own.
