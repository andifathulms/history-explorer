Kauai alone still stood outside Kamehameha's rule after Nu'uanu, and
its own king, Kaumuali'i, knew exactly how thin his chances were
against it. He had a schooner built in secret, a last resort if he
ever needed to flee the islands entirely, and for years he traded
messages and gifts with Kamehameha rather than risk a meeting in
person - remembering, as Kamehameha's own court well knew he would,
the fate of Keoua at Puukohola. It took a sandalwood trader's word as
hostage, and Kamehameha's own pledge of safety, to finally bring
Kaumuali'i aboard ship to Honolulu in 1810.

The meeting went better than the fears that preceded it. Kaumuali'i
offered Kamehameha his islands outright; Kamehameha told him to keep
them in fief for his own lifetime, on the single condition that his
son Liholiho would inherit them after him. Some of the chiefs urged
Kamehameha to have the Kauai king killed once he was in reach - and
when that failed, a plot to poison him at a feast very nearly
succeeded, foiled only by a warning from Kamehameha's own retainer
Isaac Davis, who paid for the warning with his life not long after.
Kaumuali'i sailed home unharmed. For the first time, every inhabited
island in the chain answered to a single government.

## Eighty-two years, and the faith of his ancestors

Kamehameha lived nine years more, settled at Kailua on Hawai'i
Island, presiding over a kingdom no longer at war with itself. He
died there on the 8th of May, 1819, at eighty-two, in the religion he
had been raised in. When his priests proposed sacrificing a number of
men to prolong his life, he refused them with a single sentence: "The
men are sacred to the king" - meaning Liholiho, his son and heir, for
whom he meant to leave a kingdom rather than a debt of blood. His work,
as his own people understood it, was done: the islands united,
the wars ended, and a son ready to take a throne no rival remained to
contest.
