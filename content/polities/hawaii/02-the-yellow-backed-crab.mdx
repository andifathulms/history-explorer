Kalaniopuu died three years after Cook, of old age, leaving the
kingship of Hawaii to his son Kiwalao and the guardianship of the war
god Kukailimoku - the office that carried real religious authority -
to his nephew Kamehameha. It was an arrangement built for conflict.
When Kiwalao's own redistribution of lands after his father's death
favored his mother's Kau relatives at the expense of the Kona chiefs
who had backed Kamehameha, several of those chiefs rose in open
revolt, urging Kamehameha to join them.

## A spear that decided a kingdom

The armies met at Keei in 1782, in what became known as the battle of
Mokuohai. Early success favored Kiwalao's own forces, until
Kamehameha's arrival on the field restored the balance. In the
fighting, the rebel chief Keeaumoku fell, entangled by his own spear
in the rocky ground; Kiwalao, seeing his enemy down, called out to
his men to mind the ivory ornament round Keeaumoku's neck "lest it be
spoiled with the blood" - callousness that told Keeaumoku no mercy
would follow if he were taken. A relief party reached him first. One
of them felled Kiwalao himself with a sling-stone, and Keeaumoku
crawled to the stunned king and cut his throat with a shark-tooth
blade, while another of his own men mocked the fallen king's own
surname with the cry, "the spear has pierced the yellow-backed crab."

## One island, three kings

Kiwalao's death broke his army, and with it Hawaii's own unity.
Fornander is careful to note that the chroniclers who came after
treated Mokuohai too easily as a straightforward usurpation; in fact
it split the island into three real, rival claims. Kamehameha held
Kona, Kohala and part of Hamakua. Keawemauhili, the highest-ranked
survivor of the old royal house, held Hilo and the rest of Hamakua and
Puna. Keoua Kuahuula, Kiwalao's own half-brother, held Kau and the
remainder of Puna, and considered Kamehameha nothing more than a rebel
who had murdered his king. None of the three would yield, and Hawaii
remained divided between them for the better part of a decade.
