Kamehameha's death left the old religious order in the hands of a son
who had never fully believed in it and a regent, the dowager
Ka'ahumanu, who had even less patience for it. At her urging, Liholiho
- now Kamehameha II - returned to Kailua and, before an assembled
company of chiefs of both sexes, sat down and ate openly with the
women of his own court: a direct, public violation of the kapu that
had governed Hawaiian eating for as long as anyone could remember.

The common people who watched expected the gods to strike him down.
When nothing happened, they raised a shout that carried the news
faster than any messenger could have: "The tabus are at an end, and
the gods are a lie!" The effect matched the shout. Idol worship and
the kapu system collapsed within days across the whole island chain;
the high priest himself set fire to the temples and images in his own
keeping, and messengers carried word of the abolition as far as Kauai,
where Kaumuali'i accepted it without objection.

## A defender of the faith

Not everyone accepted it so readily. Kekuaokalani, a popular young
chief, retreated to Kaawaloa in open protest, and priests loyal to the
old order flocked to him, quoting an old proverb back at him: a
religious chief would possess a kingdom, but irreligious chiefs would
always be poor. Armed followers rallied to his cause, and when negotiation failed,
Kekuaokalani marched on Kailua hoping to catch the royal party by
surprise. Ka'ahumanu's general Kalanimoku met him instead at Kuamo'o
with muskets Kamehameha had stockpiled in his own final years and a
mounted gun carried by canoe. Outnumbered and short of powder,
Kekuaokalani fought on after he was wounded, reloading from a seated
position until a second shot killed him outright; his wife Manono
fought at his side to the end and did not long outlive him. The
old religion that had sanctioned every one of Kamehameha's own
conquests did not survive its founder by even a single year.
