Master of Hawaii Island alone, Kamehameha turned his fleet toward
Maui, Molokai and finally Oahu, held by Kalanikupule with the backing
of his father Kahekili's old dominion. During the crossing from
Molokai, a high chief named Kaiana - who had joined Kamehameha's own
ranks but kept close family ties to the Maui royal line - grew
convinced, from overheard conversations and evasive answers, that
Kamehameha's own council had marked him for secret execution. Rather
than wait to find out, Kaiana broke off with his brother and their
own followers during the crossing, landed separately on Oahu, and
joined Kalanikupule instead - telling his own wife, who chose to stay
with Kamehameha's fleet, that he would rather die in battle facing
the enemy than be killed quietly among supposed allies.

## Cannon on both sides, and a rout down one valley

Kamehameha landed his army between Waialae and Waikiki and marched up
the Nuuanu valley, where Kalanikupule had drawn up his own defense
across a line of ridges. The two forces met at Puiwa, and the fighting
was hotly contested until the superior number and practice of
Kamehameha's own artillery began to tell. What began as a defeat
became a rout: some of the beaten army scrambled up the valley's steep
sides, but a large number were driven over the pali of Nuuanu itself,
a cliff several hundred feet high, and died in the fall. Kaiana and
his brother were killed early in the fighting. Kalanikupule escaped
into the mountains, living as a fugitive for months before he was
finally captured, killed, and - like Keoua before him - sacrificed to
Kamehameha's own war god.

## The closing scene of the old history

Fornander dates the battle to April 1795, and treats it as more than
a military victory: it made Kamehameha master of Hawaii, Maui,
Kahoolawe, Lanai, Molokai and Oahu outright, and though the
submission of Kauai was still years away, he judges that the conquest
of Oahu is where the unification of the whole Hawaiian group under one
government properly dates from - "the closing scene," in his own
words, "in the ancient history of the Hawaiian Islands." Whatever of
the old order survived the battle, he writes, was already living on
borrowed time in the glare of the new one it had just made possible.
