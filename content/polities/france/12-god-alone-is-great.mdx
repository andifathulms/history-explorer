Louis XIV's own dying words to his five-year-old great-grandson and
heir carried an admission the old king had never made in public
during his fifty-four years of personal rule: "I have been too fond
of war," he told the child who would become Louis XV, "do not
imitate me in that, nor in my too great expenditure." He died on
1 September 1715, and the deathbed itself bore out how thoroughly
the splendour of his reign had curdled into something more like
isolation. Madame de Maintenon, his own companion for decades,
withdrew to a convent rather than sit with him in his final hours,
returning only reluctantly when he woke and asked for her before
slipping away again; his confessor and one of the sons he had done
the most for stayed largely absent from the bedside as well. Louis
met the end with the same dignified bearing that had marked his whole
public life, telling the Duke of Orléans only that he left the
kingdom's affairs in good order - "here you have one king in the
tomb, and another in the cradle."

## A sermon that named what came next

At his funeral in the Sainte-Chapelle, the preacher Massillon opened
with four words that rang through the church as something closer to
a warning than a eulogy: "Dieu seul est grand, mes frères" - God
alone is great, my brothers. Aimed at a King said in his own lifetime
to have gone about "as if he were worthy to be Emperor of all the
earth," the line struck a note of leveling equality that historians
would later read as an early expression of the very sentiment that,
within the century, would sweep away the throne Louis had spent his
reign exalting. Kitchin's own account of the reign that followed
opens under the heading "The Decadence of the Monarchy" - a
seventy-eight-year drift, under Louis XV and then Louis XVI, from the
absolute personal rule Louis XIV had built toward a crisis his own
successors would prove unable to master.
