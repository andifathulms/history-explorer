Confined in practice to the Tuileries after the events of 1789, Louis
XVI grew increasingly restless under a captivity that never called
itself one. When an attempt to leave for a day's hunting at Saint-
Cloud in April 1791 drew a Parisian crowd that cut his carriage traces
and forced him back, the King understood plainly what he had become.
The count of Mirabeau, who had struck up an unlikely alliance with
the crown and dreamed of steering France toward an English-style
constitutional monarchy with himself as its architect, pressed a plan
to get the royal family safely into the protection of loyalist troops
under the Marquis de Bouillé at Metz. Mirabeau died before it could
be carried out, but the plan went ahead without him.

## Eight leagues short

On the night of 20 June 1791 the royal family slipped out of Paris.
The early stages went smoothly, and they came within eight leagues -
one more stage - of Bouillé's waiting troops at Stenay before they
were recognised and stopped at the small town of Varennes. Bouillé's
own force arrived barely an hour after the King had already been
turned back toward Paris. The journey home ran through a gauntlet of
open hostility at every village along the way; no voice was raised in
the King's defence anywhere on the road. Republican sentiment, until
then confined to a minority, became an openly declared political
position in Paris for the first time once news of the flight and
capture spread.

## The tenth of August

Louis's own conduct over the following year - refusing offers of
rescue in the belief that allied armies mobilizing against
revolutionary France would soon arrive to restore him, while a
Prussian duke marched into eastern France proclaiming exactly that
intention - fused his name in the public mind with the kingdom's
foreign enemies. On 10 August 1792 Paris rose against the monarchy
outright. Louis refused to fight; the royal family took refuge with
the Legislative Assembly while insurgents led by Robespierre and
Danton overran the Tuileries and cut down the King's Swiss Guard. A
deputation from the city's revolutionary government appeared before
the Assembly and, in Kitchin's own words, "the municipality of Paris
had overthrown the Monarchy." The royal family was arrested and moved
first to the Feuillants and then to the Temple prison. Six weeks
later, on 21 September 1792, the newly elected National Convention
formally decreed that royalty was abolished and proclaimed the
Republic - ending, eight hundred and five years after Hugh Capet's
election at Noyon, the kingdom his line had built.
