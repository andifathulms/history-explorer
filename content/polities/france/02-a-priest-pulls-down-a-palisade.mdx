The kingdom Louis VI inherited in 1108 was, in practice, barely
larger than the royal demesne itself - a scatter of towns around
Paris and Orleans, with only a nominal claim on the great duchies and
counties that ringed it. From high ground near Paris, the castles of
his own rebellious vassals were visible on the skyline; the lord of
Montmorency disputed the plain of Saint-Denis at the capital's own
gates, and the fortress of Montlhéri, held by a hostile baron, cut the
royal domain physically in two along the road to Orleans.

## A king with a nickname for his own vigor

Louis, known to contemporaries as le Gros - the Fat - and, earlier
in life, as "the Wide-awake" and "the Fighter," made his whole reign
the project of forcing the barons within reach of Paris to actually
obey the king they nominally served. He had little beyond his own
energy and a household guard of young noblemen eager for glory to do
it with, and the account of his reign - drawn largely from the
biography his own friend and advisor, Abbot Suger of Saint-Denis,
wrote of him - reads less like the reign of a great conqueror than a
long campaign of local sieges against men who had simply stopped
answering to any authority above their own castle walls.

## A robber baron's stronghold broken by his own tenants

The siege of Le Puiset in 1111 became the reign's defining episode.
Hugh the Fair, lord of Le Puiset, had spent years plundering the
lands of the Church around his own fortress, and when Louis finally
moved against him, the King's army was joined by an unlikely militia:
the peasants of the church lands Hugh had been robbing, armed and led
into battle by their own parish priests. Suger himself recorded how
one village curate, at the head of his rustic company, walked alone
and unarmed up to the fortress's palisade, began pulling the stakes
loose with his hands, and, finding no one moved to stop him, waved
his men forward to break through the gap he had opened while the
King's own troops pressed the assault from another side. It was, in
Suger's own telling, a peasant rising blessed by royal authority
rather than a conventional siege - proof that the crown's claim to be
the defender of the weak against the barons was, for once, something
more than a formula in a coronation oath.

## An abbot who built a kingdom out of a demesne

Suger's own role went well beyond biography. Entrusted with the
priory of Toury near Le Puiset, he helped supply the intelligence and
local support that let Louis's campaigns against the robber barons
succeed one after another, and he became, over two reigns, the crown's
most trusted administrator - the churchman later historians credited
as one of the true architects of the French monarchy's recovery.
Louis never controlled more than a fraction of the kingdom his
successors would rule, but the years spent pulling down castle after
castle within a day's ride of Paris built the first working precedent
for something the Capetian dynasty had lacked since Hugh Capet's own
election: a king whose word actually carried force inside his own
borders.
