The throne did not stay empty. Within weeks of Napoleon's first
abdication in 1814 the victorious powers restored it to Louis XVIII,
the executed king's brother, and what he offered France was not the
old monarchy back unaltered but a bargain: a Charter, granted by the
crown but binding on it, that kept the Empire's centralised
administration and the Revolution's principle of equality before the
law intact. Men who did not believe in the divine right of kings could
still serve this monarchy, because their allegiance rested on the pact
rather than on the legitimist creed behind it.

The pact held for less than a year. Napoleon returned from Elba in
March 1815, Louis XVIII fled to Ghent, and the Hundred Days ended only
at Waterloo. The king who came back a second time came back to a
country the ultra-royalists around his own brother, the Comte
d'Artois, wanted to punish rather than govern, and the decade that
followed was a running argument over how much of 1789 the restored
monarchy could actually undo.

## Reaction

Louis XVIII spent his first four years holding the ultras at arm's
length. The assassination of his nephew the Duke of Berry in 1820
broke that restraint: a wave of monarchist reaction swept the ultras
back into government, an electoral law was rewritten to hand half the
Chamber's seats to the twelve thousand most heavily taxed landowners
in France, and a "priest-party" organised out of a Jesuit-run
congregation in Paris began working to restore the Church's authority
over marriage, education and the provinces.

Charles X, who succeeded his brother in 1824, was the movement's
believer rather than its manager. He had himself consecrated at Reims
in the old style, compensated the nobility for estates confiscated in
the Revolution, and by 1830 was governing through Jules de Polignac, a
minister who answered to no parliamentary majority and meant to prove
it. When the Chamber of Deputies sent him an address of protest in
March 1830, Charles dissolved it; the country re-elected the same
deputies in July with fifty-three seats added to their number; and on
25 July, citing a clause of his own Charter that let the crown act by
ordinance in an emergency, Charles issued three ordinances dissolving
the newly elected Chamber before it met, rewriting the electoral law
again, and muzzling the opposition press.

## The three days

Paris rose on 26 July. Printers and journalists who stood to be ruined
by the press ordinance called the city to arms; by 28 July the royal
troops had lost the Hôtel de Ville; by 29 July the insurgents had
taken the Louvre and the Tuileries and forced the royal garrison out
of the capital altogether. Charles X, who had been on his way to a
hunting party when the ordinances were published, abandoned Polignac
and offered to negotiate only once negotiation could no longer save
him. He abdicated, and the deputies and peers who had spent the
Restoration governing beside the Bourbons now turned the crown over to
a cousin, Louis-Philippe, Duke of Orléans, instead.

Sixteen years after a returning king had offered France a charter as a
concession from the throne, a Paris crowd took the same document and
made it a constitution the throne no longer had the choice of
granting.

## What the claim did next

The senior Bourbon line did not accept that its history had ended. In
1832 Charles X's daughter-in-law, the Duchess of Berry, landed
secretly in the south and tried to raise the Vendée for her son, the
nine-year-old Duke of Bordeaux, whom the party that had lost in 1830
now called Henri V. The rising never spread past a few hundred men; the
duchess was arrested at Nantes in November and held until an
inconvenient pregnancy, the result of a secret second marriage, made
her an embarrassment rather than a martyr and the July Monarchy let
her go.

Nothing else followed. The eloquent legitimist lawyer Berryer kept
arguing the senior line's case in the courts and the Chamber for
another two decades, and the Bordeaux/Chambord claim itself survived
its own hopeless defeats far longer than the 1832 rising did. But the
verdict the Restoration's own historians reached about 1830 reached
further than any one rising could reverse: legitimism, as a working
claim on the government of France, had succumbed with Charles X on the
road to Rambouillet, and no later Bourbon ever sat on the throne again.
