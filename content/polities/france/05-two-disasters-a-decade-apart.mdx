Edward III of England renewed his own family's claim to the French
throne in the 1330s, and the war that followed opened with two
defeats so complete that French chivalry itself never fully recovered
its old confidence in the decades between them.

## Crécy: bowstrings kept dry

Philip VI rode out from Abbeville on 26 August 1346 with an army that
advanced, in Froissart's own account, "without order, no man waiting
for his neighbour" - a chaotic column that outran its own scouts and
ignored the King's order to halt and regroup, each contingent pushing
forward rather than yield the honor of the front rank to another. When
they came in sight of the English position on the ridge at Crécy,
drawn up in quiet, disciplined order under Edward III and his
sixteen-year-old son, Philip's own hot temper got the better of him
and he ordered his Genoese crossbowmen forward at once, exhausted from
the march and with their bowstrings slackened by a sudden summer
storm. The English archers, who had kept their own strings dry under
their coats, cut the Genoese down as they advanced; the Count of
Alençon, enraged at what he took for cowardice, ordered his own
knights to ride the retreating crossbowmen down rather than let them
fall back through the French lines. From there the battle dissolved
into successive futile charges. The blind King of Bohemia had his
knights tie their horses' reins to his own and rode into the melee to
die alongside them rather than watch from safety; by nightfall Philip
himself, with only four barons left at his side, fled the field for
Amiens, leaving Edward to come down from the windmill where he had
watched the entire battle without committing his own reserve and
embrace the son who had won it.

## Poitiers: a king captured by his own cousin's enemy

A decade later, in September 1356, King John II cornered the Black
Prince's much smaller army near Poitiers with a French force
outnumbering the English roughly seven to one - and lost anyway.
Having learned nothing from his own father's defeat at Crécy, John
ordered his vanguard to charge the English position along a narrow,
hedge-lined road where numbers counted for little; the charge broke
against English archers and an ambush hidden on the flank, and the
King's own eldest son, the Dauphin Charles, fled the field with eight
hundred lances that might have turned the battle. John dismounted his
remaining troops and fought on foot, defending himself with a
battle-axe while his youngest son, Philip, still a boy, stood at his
side warding off blows and earning the name "the Bold" that would
follow him the rest of his life. When the King was finally
surrounded, a knight of Saint-Omer named Denis of Morbecque pushed
through the crowd and asked him, in French, to surrender; John asked
for his "cousin, the Prince of Wales" by name and yielded on the
promise of being brought to him safely. English and Gascon soldiers
nearly tore him apart fighting over the ransom before two barons
rescued him and delivered him, bruised but alive, to the Black Prince
- who received the captured King of France with a deep bow and served
him wine with his own hands.

## What eleven thousand dead cost the crown

Poitiers left some eleven thousand Frenchmen dead on the field,
nearly twenty-five hundred of them nobly born, and put the King of
France himself in English captivity for the ransom negotiations that
would drag on for years and drain the kingdom's treasury even further.
Between Crécy's slaughter of the old feudal cavalry and Poitiers's
capture of the reigning king, French chivalry's own confidence in its
numerical superiority - the assumption that had sent both armies
charging headlong at smaller, better-disciplined English positions -
never fully recovered for the rest of the war.
