By June 1789, the Estates-General Louis XVI had summoned to address
the crown's financial crisis had already slipped from his control.
When the King tried to keep the three orders - clergy, nobility, and
the commons - meeting separately, the Third Estate's own spokesman
told the royal master of ceremonies sent to disperse them that they
sat "by the will of the people, and will retire only at the point of
the bayonet." Within days Louis backed down and ordered the
remaining nobles and clergy to join the commons in a single National
Assembly; the crown's authority had been defeated, as Kitchin puts
it, "almost without the striking of a blow."

## An arsenal, then a prison

A war party at court, unconvinced the crown was really beaten, pushed
Louis to dismiss his popular finance minister Necker and prepare
force. Instead the army sided with the crowd. Insurgents seized the
Hôtel des Invalides and its stores of muskets and cannon, and on 14
July turned toward the Bastille - not primarily for the handful of
prisoners it held, but as the physical symbol of the old royal order.
Its garrison of a little over a hundred men, thirty of them Swiss
regulars, held out only briefly before the aged commander de Launay
negotiated surrender terms guaranteeing his men's safety. The crowd,
inflamed past any such promise, killed him and several of his
soldiers once the gates were opened. Searched afterward, the fortress
yielded just seven prisoners, none of them political - a measure of
how far Louis XVI's own comparatively mild rule had already let the
old machinery of arbitrary imprisonment fall into disuse. The
building was demolished stone by stone in the weeks that followed.

## A reconciliation that did not hold

Louis's response was conciliatory: he withdrew troops from Paris and
Versailles, and rode into the capital himself to be received at the
Hôtel de Ville, where the mayor presented him the city's keys with a
pointed comparison to Henry IV - "I offer to your Majesty the very
keys which were presented to Henry IV. He entered Paris as its
conqueror; now the people have conquered their sovereign." Louis
appeared at a window wearing the new tricolour cockade to loud
acclaim, and for a moment reconciliation between crown and people
seemed possible. It did not last. Marie Antoinette's own circle
treated the King's visit to Paris as a capitulation rather than a
gesture of goodwill, and abandoned the royal cause that same day,
opening the wave of noble emigration that would leave Louis
increasingly isolated in the years still to come.
