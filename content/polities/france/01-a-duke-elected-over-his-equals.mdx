The last Carolingian king in the west, Louis V, died in 987 without
a son, and the great men of the western Frankish kingdom did not
look to the dead king's own uncle to succeed him. Instead they
turned to Hugh, duke of the Franks - a Neustrian baron whose own
lands were centred on Paris, and whose own line already descended
from Robert the Strong, an adventurer risen to defend the land
between the Seine and the Loire whose son Odo the nobles had elected
king a century earlier, in an assembly that likewise passed over the
Carolingian line without managing to end it for good. Under the
influence of
Gerbert of Aurillac, the era's most learned churchman and later Pope
Sylvester II, and with Archbishop Adalberon of Reims presiding, the
assembled nobles proclaimed Hugh king at Noyon and had him crowned
and anointed at Reims.

## A king no stronger than his neighbours

What the election actually created was modest. Neither "France" nor
Paris carried anything like their later weight: Paris was the chief
town of a small dukedom, and France itself named only a narrow
district around it, overshadowed by the greater lordships of
Normandy, Aquitaine and Burgundy that in theory owed the new king
their homage. Hugh's own reign was a constant struggle against lay
and clerical neighbours who did not consider him their superior in
any practical sense, and at his death in 996 he was far from the
strongest man in his own kingdom - both the duke of Normandy and the
duke of Aquitaine commanded more real power than the king who was
supposed to be their lord.

## Why 987 mattered anyway

Hugh's own weakness is exactly what makes his election a genuine
turning point rather than a footnote. Every king who followed him
down to Philip IV inherited a title that no assembly of nobles ever
again put to a vote among rival candidates - father succeeded son
without a break for more than three centuries, an unbroken run later
generations treated as something close to providential. A dynasty
that began as one baron chosen by his equals ended by claiming an
authority none of those equals had ever imagined conceding.
