Charles VI came to the throne as a boy in 1380 and, by 1392, had
begun suffering recurring fits of madness that never fully lifted for
the rest of his life. In his lucid intervals he governed with visible
effort and tried honestly to end the papal schism his own kingdom had
helped prolong; in his relapses, by his own account, he begged those
around him to kill him rather than let the fits return. The kingdom
he nominally ruled split into warring factions around him - Burgundian
and Armagnac princes contesting control of a king who could not
reliably govern himself, let alone his own court.

## Azincourt

Henry V of England renewed his family's old claim to the French
throne in 1415 and landed with an army that French forces, gathering
in far greater numbers, moved to block near Azincourt. The ground was
against them: French knights, advancing on horseback over mud churned
by rain, were slowed and broke against a barrage of English arrows
before ever reaching the archers behind their palisades. The wounded
horses turned back into the ranks behind them, and the English closed
in on knights too exhausted and mired to fight effectively. The
Constable of France, three dukes, six counts and roughly a thousand
men-at-arms died; many more, including the Duke of Orléans, were
taken prisoner. It was among the heaviest single defeats French
arms had suffered, and it left the divided court no army capable of
stopping Henry's next advance.

## The murder at Montereau and the Treaty of Troyes

Five years later the war's political course turned on an
assassination rather than a battle. The Dauphin's own followers,
inviting John the Fearless, Duke of Burgundy, to a parley on the
bridge at Montereau, murdered him as he knelt before the Dauphin.
His son and successor, Philip, threw Burgundy's full weight behind
Henry V rather than negotiate with the family responsible. The
Treaty of Troyes that followed in 1420 disinherited the Dauphin
outright, married Henry to Charles VI's daughter Catherine, and
named Henry regent of France and heir to its throne in the mad
king's own place - a settlement that, on paper, ended the Capetian
succession three hundred and thirty years after Hugh Capet's own
election.
