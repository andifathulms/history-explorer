Louis XIV had already sat on the throne for eighteen years, nominal
king since early childhood, when Cardinal Mazarin, Richelieu's own
successor as chief minister, died in 1661. For all that time Mazarin
had governed in fact while Louis deferred to him almost as a
stepfather, obeying his political guidance and absorbing his maxims
even in matters as personal as whom to marry. Once Mazarin was gone,
Louis chose not to appoint a new chief minister at all, dividing
administration instead among subordinate secretaries answerable
directly to himself - Lionne for foreign affairs, Le Tellier for war,
Fouquet for finance - men meant, in the King's own phrase for their
role, "not to govern, but to serve." It was the beginning of a reign
that would run for fifty-four more years and would leave its mark as
the fullest expression of the personal, centralized monarchy that
Richelieu's own destruction of places like La Rochelle had cleared
the ground for.

## A court built to overawe

Louis governed through display as much as through decree. Versailles,
raised at enormous cost alongside the Louvre and Saint-Germain,
became the physical expression of his rule - grand and sumptuous, a
palace one contemporary called a "favourite without merit," where
nature itself was subordinated to formal design in the way the
kingdom's older local liberties had already been subordinated to the
crown. Louis was acutely sensitive to how his actions would be
judged, a vanity that pushed him to prefer a brilliant policy over a
cautious one throughout his reign, and courtiers understood early
that flattery, especially of his own conduct in war, was the surest
route to his favour and to real influence over the state.

## The revocation

The Edict of Nantes had already been reconfirmed once, by Mazarin
himself in 1653, after Huguenot towns proved loyal to the crown
during the noble revolt known as the Fronde. But Louis had spent
years pressuring French Protestants toward conversion, and by the
1680s he judged - wrongly, and against the advice of English
observers who found Huguenot refugees a useful and industrious
addition to their own country - that so few genuine Huguenots
remained that the Edict no longer served any purpose. On 18 October
1685, with the sympathetic Catholic James II newly on the English
throne and no longer a reason for caution, Louis signed the edict
revoking Nantes outright. Protestant worship was forbidden entirely;
pastors were ordered out of the realm within a fortnight on pain of
the galleys; children of Huguenot families were required to be
raised Catholic; and any Protestant caught trying to flee the kingdom
faced severe penalties. Louis's own courtiers celebrated the
revocation as the crowning glory of his reign, and Louis himself
believed he had reached the summit of his own glory in signing it.
Kept largely ignorant by his advisers of the revocation's real human
cost, and surrounded by counsel that flattered rather than informed
him, Louis had turned what he took for his greatest triumph into what
later judgment would call the gravest and most self-defeating
blunder of his reign.
