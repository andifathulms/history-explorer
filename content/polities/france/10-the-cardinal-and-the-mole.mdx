The regency that followed Henry IV's murder drifted through favourites
and factions for over a decade before the obscure bishop his own
assassination had left waiting in the wings finally entered the royal
council in April 1624. Armand Jean du Plessis, Cardinal Richelieu,
found a kingdom whose Huguenot minority still held two fortified
towns of refuge under the Edict of Nantes - La Rochelle and Montauban
- with the right to keep their own garrisons and shut their gates
even against the king. To Richelieu this was not religious toleration
but a state within the state, and by 1627 he had resolved to end it.

## A siege as an act of faith

Richelieu made the siege of La Rochelle his own personal project,
acting as general, chief engineer and paymaster together, gathering
troops at Marennes and directing the construction of forts that
ringed the town by land. The harder problem was the sea: La
Rochelle's harbour stayed open to English relief until Richelieu
ordered a mole built directly across the harbour mouth, a project of
sunken ships and stonework driven out from both shores that closed
all but a narrow, deep channel no ordinary blockade could have shut.
Twice English fleets tried to break through and resupply the town,
once with a crude floating powder-charge that failed to ignite; both
times they were turned back, and by late 1628 they abandoned the
effort and sailed for home. The Huguenot defenders, reduced to
sending their starving women and children out to the royal lines only
to be driven back to the walls, held out for fourteen months of siege
before hunger left barely a hundred men able to bear arms.

## What survived the surrender, and what did not

La Rochelle capitulated on 30 October 1628; fifteen thousand of its
people had died of famine in the siege. Richelieu's terms were not
vindictive - Huguenots kept the right to worship, in recognition of
the town's refusal to sell itself to England - but its walls were
razed, its privileges stripped, and its independence as a fortified
Protestant stronghold ended for good. No French city rose against the
crown again until Paris itself swept the monarchy away in 1789. The
victory secured Richelieu's own position at court and let him turn,
with only months to spare, to the wider European war already
consuming Germany - but it also marked, as much as any single event
could, the point at which France chose to crush the institutions
that might have grown into a check on the crown's own power, rather
than build on them.
