The French Protestant movement that grew up in the decades after
Castillon took its name from an unlikely source: Genevan Swiss
reformers who bound themselves by oath into an *Eidgenossenschaft*
against their neighbours' hostility gave French Protestants, allied
to the same Genevan cause that the Picard reformer John Calvin had
built into a civic religious order after fleeing Francis I's
persecutions in the 1530s, the nickname "Huguenot" - a corruption of
the German *Eidgenossen*, "oath-comrades." By the 1570s the
Huguenots counted powerful noble houses among their number, chief of
them the Admiral Gaspard de Coligny, and stood locked in on-and-off
civil war against the crown and the Catholic Guise family.

## A wedding, a truce, a bell in the night

By August 1572 an uneasy peace held: Coligny had gained real
influence over the young king, Charles IX, unsettling the Queen
Mother, Catherine de' Medici, and the court's Catholic faction.
Huguenot nobles gathered in Paris trusted the goodwill that peace
seemed to promise, even as ordinary Parisians grew visibly restless
around them. Between one and two o'clock on the morning of 24
August, a bell began ringing from the tower of Saint-Germain-
l'Auxerrois; within moments the bells of a hundred churches answered
it, and armed men wearing white crosses filled the streets. The Duke
of Guise's men killed Coligny first. What the plot's authors had
meant as the murder of a handful of leaders became something the
mob would not be held to: by daybreak Paris lay littered with the
dead, corpses thrown from windows and dragged through the streets to
the Seine, while every private grudge and debt in the city found an
excuse to settle itself in blood.

## A hundred churches, a continent watching

The two senior Huguenot princes present, Henry of Navarre and the
young Prince of Condé, were given a stark choice between the Mass
and death; both yielded, at least for the moment. Estimates of the
dead vary wildly by source - a low figure puts it near two thousand
in Paris and twenty thousand across the provinces, while more
partisan tallies range as high as seventy or a hundred thousand - but
the scale was never really the point of the outrage. Rome had a
commemorative medal struck and held services of thanksgiving; Philip
II of Spain wrote his brother monarch warm congratulations, believing
his own Protestant enemies broken at a stroke. Charles IX, in the
end, formally claimed the massacre as his own command rather than
let it pass for mob violence - and within days was said to wake
screaming in the night, haunted by cries only he could hear, broken
in body and mind for what remained of his short reign.
