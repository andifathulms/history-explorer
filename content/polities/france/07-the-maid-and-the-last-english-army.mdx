By 1429 the Treaty of Troyes's settlement had left the disinherited
Dauphin, Charles, holding little more than territory south of the
Loire, while an English army besieged Orléans, the last major
stronghold guarding his remaining lands. Into this collapse came
Jeanne Darc, a seventeen-year-old peasant from Domrémy who reported
visions of St Michael, St Catherine and St Marguerite instructing her
to deliver Charles and see him crowned. Turned away at first by the
local commander at Vaucouleurs, she persisted until he relented and
sent her on to Chinon, where she talked her way into the Dauphin's
own presence and, against the doubts of his corrupt favourites, was
given a small force.

## Orléans relieved, Reims reached

Her arrival broke the siege within days - contemporaries on the
English side, including the Duke of Bedford himself, later admitted
in writing that fear of her had done as much as any French weapon.
With the siege lifted, Jeanne pressed the reluctant Dauphin to march
on Reims for his coronation rather than consolidate first, overriding
courtiers who preferred caution. Towns opened their gates along the
way; Troyes itself, held by the English, surrendered without a fight
when Jeanne moved to storm it. Charles was crowned and anointed
Charles VII at Reims in July 1429 with Jeanne standing beside him
holding the royal standard - restoring, in a single ceremony, the
legitimacy Troyes had tried to strip from the Capetian line.

## Betrayed, burned, and outlived by her victory

Jeanne's influence made her as many enemies at Charles's own court as
on the battlefield; favourites who resented her jealously blocked
further advances, and a failed assault on Paris that autumn was, by
her own supporters' account, undermined by the king's own reluctance.
The following year Burgundian forces captured her near Compiègne and
sold her to the English for ten thousand gold francs. Tried at Rouen
on charges of heresy and sorcery by a court that included only one
Englishman among her judges but answered to English purposes
throughout, she was burned at the stake in May 1431, not yet twenty.
Charles VII, whom she had crowned, made no recorded effort to save
her.

The war she had turned did not stop with her death. French forces
retook Normandy through the 1440s, crushing an English relief force
at Formigny in 1450, and by August 1451 had cleared English garrisons
from Guienne in the south as well. England's last attempt to hold the
southwest ended at Castillon in July 1453: the aged commander Talbot,
sent to relieve the city, attacked a fortified French artillery camp
without waiting for support, was thrown from his killed horse, and
died fighting alongside his own sons when the French sallied out
against his disordered line. No organized English army remained on
French soil outside Calais - the end, a hundred and sixteen years
after it began, of the war Jeanne Darc had once seemed powerless to
affect.
