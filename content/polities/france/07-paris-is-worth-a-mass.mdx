Henry of Navarre, the Huguenot leader forced to convert at knifepoint
on the night of the Bartholomew massacre, spent the following two
decades fighting his way back to the throne he inherited by strict
succession in 1589 - a Bourbon cousin's claim that most of Catholic
France, and above all Catholic Paris, refused for years to accept
from a Protestant king. He weighed converting to Catholicism as early
as 1589, when hardline Catholic nobles urged it, and judged the
moment wrong: faction still ran too deep for a conversion to settle
anything. By 1593, with the whole country worn out by war and even
Paris ready for peace, the calculation had changed, and he made his
formal abjuration. Whether or not he ever actually said the words
history remembers him for - "Paris is worth a Mass" - the phrase
caught something real: Henry treated the question with something
close to scorn, a working politician's indifference to a theological
line most of his contemporaries thought worth dying over. The effect
was immediate. City after city that had held out against him laid
its keys at his feet once the religious objection was gone.

## A settlement built to last

Securing Paris did not end the Huguenots' grievances, and by 1598
Henry judged that only a real settlement, not another truce liable to
collapse into fresh fighting, would hold. The Edict of Nantes, signed
that April, broke from every earlier agreement by giving Huguenots
rights of worship across most of the kingdom, access to judicial and
financial office, and a Protestant chamber seated within the
Parlement of Paris itself alongside mixed chambers in the provincial
courts. Parisian preachers who had lived through 1572 raised protest,
and the Parlement resisted registering it until Henry faced the body
down in person; once in force, the Edict gave the Huguenot minority a
durable legal footing no earlier peace had managed, even though it
left France's underlying constitutional weaknesses untouched. Three
weeks later the Peace of Vervins closed out the parallel war with
Spain on the simplest of terms - Spanish withdrawal from every place
still held on French soil - ending three decades in which a French
civil war had doubled as a proxy front in Spain's wider European
wars.

## The knife in the street

Henry's reign gave France over a decade to recover, but old hatreds
outlived the peace he had made. On 14 May 1610, two days before he
was due to leave Paris for his queen's coronation, Henry had himself
driven with a small escort to visit his ailing minister Sully. His
carriage stopped in traffic on a narrow street; a man named
Ravaillac stepped up onto a wheel and stabbed him through the heart.
Whether Ravaillac acted alone was never established with certainty,
though he insisted to the end that he had. The king who had ended
France's religious wars and negotiated Spain out of them was gone in
an instant, leaving the kingdom's affairs in the hands of a regency
under his widow, Marie de' Medici - and, waiting in the wings, an
obscure provincial bishop who would in time take up Henry's
unfinished work under the name history remembers him by: Richelieu.
