In 88 BC Mithridates VI sent letters to the cities of Roman Asia instructing
them that on a named day, thirty days after the letters were written, they were
to kill every Roman and Italian in their territory — men, women, children and
freedmen — leave the bodies unburied, and take their property, half of which
would go to the killers and half to the king. Debtors who killed their creditors
were released from the debt. Anyone who hid a Roman was to be punished.

The cities did it. The figures the sources give are eighty thousand from one and
a hundred and fifty thousand from another and neither is checkable. Appian names
the cities and describes what happened at Ephesus, Pergamon, Adramyttium, Caunus
and Tralles: people dragged from the temples they had taken refuge in, killed at
the altars, drowned, thrown from the walls.

## Why they did it

It is filed here as a catastrophe rather than as a victory, and not because of
the morality. It acted on the kingdom the way a disaster does: after it, there
were no options left.

Why the cities complied is the part worth dwelling on. Rome had held Asia as a
province for forty-five years, since the Attalid king left it by will, and had
farmed its taxes
to companies of publicani who bid for the contract at Rome and recovered the bid
with interest from the province. Cities borrowed to pay, at rates that
compounded, from the same men. Communities were selling public buildings, temple
property and their own citizens into slavery to service the debt.

<Figure id="athens-tetradrachm" />

Mithridates arrived as a liberator with a Persian and a Macedonian lineage, and
what he offered was the cancellation of the debt by the removal of the creditor.
The cities were not misled about what he was; they wanted the thing he was
offering.

<Figure id="pergamon-stater" />

## What it foreclosed

It made the war unendable by negotiation, which McGing takes to be at least
partly the point. A city that has massacred every Roman in it cannot make a
separate peace, and a king who has organised it cannot be reconciled with. Sulla
made a settlement at Dardanos in 85 anyway, because he had a civil war waiting
in Italy and needed his army there, and it left Mithridates his kingdom and his
fleet and settled nothing at all.

The Roman punishment of the cities fell mostly on the cities. Sulla fined the
province twenty thousand talents, quartered troops on it, and required it to pay
the arrears of five years of tribute; the debt problem that produced the
massacre was made several times worse by the response to it, and was still
producing crises when Lucullus tried to restructure it a decade later.

Two further wars followed. Everything after 88 is a consequence of an act
designed to make retreat impossible for everybody involved, including the
man who arranged it.
