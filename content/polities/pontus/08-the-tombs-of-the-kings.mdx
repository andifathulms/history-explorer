Cornered in the Crimea by his own son's revolt, Mithridates took out the poison
he carried next to his sword and mixed it.

Two of his daughters were with him, Mithridatis and Nyssa, girls growing up
together, betrothed to the kings of Egypt and of Cyprus. They asked for some
first, insisted, and would not let him drink until they had swallowed theirs. It
worked on them at once.

It did not work on him. He had spent his life taking small doses of everything
as a defence against poisoners — the compounds are still called Mithridatic — and
now he walked about rapidly trying to make it act, and it would not.

So he turned to a Gallic officer named Bituitus who was standing there, and
asked him to do it. Appian gives the speech. I have profited much from your right
arm against my enemies; I shall profit most of all if you kill me now, and save
from a Roman triumph a man who has been an autocrat so many years and ruler of
so great a kingdom, and who cannot now die by poison because, like a fool, he
fortified himself against the poison of others. *Although I have kept watch and
ward against all the poisons that one takes with his food, I have not provided
against that domestic poison, always the most dangerous to kings, the treachery
of army, children, and friends.*

Bituitus did as he was asked.

## What Rome did with the body

The news reached Rome and the city held a festival, being rid of a troublesome
enemy.

Pharnaces — the son whose revolt had cornered him — sent his father's corpse to
Pompey at Sinope in a trireme, along with the men who had captured a Roman
officer and a quantity of Greek and barbarian hostages, and asked to be allowed
to rule either his father's kingdom or the Bosporus alone.

Pompey paid for the funeral. He instructed the king's own servants to give the
remains a royal interment and to place them in the tombs of the kings at Sinope,
*because he admired his great achievements and considered him the first of the
kings of his time*.

Pharnaces was inscribed a friend and ally of the Roman people, for having
delivered Italy from much trouble, and was given the Bosporus — except
Phanagoria, whose people were made free and independent because they had been
the first to resist Mithridates when he was rebuilding, and had led the others
into the revolt that finished him.

## The triumph

Two years later Pompey brought the war home.

The procession carried images of the men who were not there to be led in it:
Tigranes and Mithridates, shown fighting, shown beaten, shown fleeing. The siege
was represented, and the silent flight by night. Finally the death was shown,
with the daughters who died beside him, and figures of the sons and daughters who
had died before him, and the barbarian gods in the dress of their own countries.

Pompey rode in a chariot studded with gems, wearing — it was said, and Appian
adds that the reader may believe it if he can — a cloak of Alexander the Great,
supposed to have been found among Mithridates' possessions, which the people of
Cos had received from a Ptolemaic queen.

At the Capitol he broke with custom and killed none of the prisoners, sending
them home at public expense.

## What the kingdom became

Pontus was divided. The western part went into a province with Bithynia; the
interior was parcelled out among client dynasts and cities. The Bosporus went to
the son.

Pharnaces waited fifteen years and then, while Rome was busy with its own civil
war, came back over the sea to take his father's kingdom. Caesar beat him, and
he fled to Sinope with a thousand cavalry and surrendered the city on terms.

So the dynasty's afterlife is three things: a body buried with royal honours at
Rome's expense by the general who destroyed it; a painted image of a man's own
death carried through a foreign city; and a son who reproduced the whole
enterprise in miniature and was disposed of in an afternoon.
