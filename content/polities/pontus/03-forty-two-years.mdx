The best summary of what Mithridates VI built is the list Rome read out in the
street when it was over.

Pompey's triumph in 61 BC carried a tablet with an inscription recording what
the eastern campaign had produced, and it is a strange document: eight hundred
ships with bronze beaks captured; eight cities founded in Cappadocia, twenty in
Cilicia and Coele-Syria, one in Palestine; and six kings conquered by name —
Tigranes of Armenia, Artoces of Iberia, Oroeses of Albania, Darius the Mede,
Aretas the Nabataean, Antiochus of Commagene.

Not one of those six is Mithridates, because he was not conquered. He was the
reason all of them were.

## What forty-two years of war implies

Appian's summing-up is the sentence that gives the scale: the Romans conquered
King Mithridates *at the end of forty-two years*, and in doing so reduced
Bithynia, Cappadocia and the other peoples living round the Black Sea; and in
the same war took the part of Cilicia not yet theirs, together with Syria,
Phoenicia, Coele-Syria, Palestine and everything between them and the Euphrates.

Read that backwards and it describes the Pontic achievement. A kingdom on the
northern edge of Anatolia obliged the Roman Republic to spend four decades and
three of its best commanders in the East — and the map Rome drew when it finally
finished, from the Caucasus to the Egyptian border, is the map of everything it
had to take in order to get at one king.

That is expansion measured in the size of the response.

## What he actually held

The kingdom itself grew round the Black Sea rather than into Anatolia at first.
Colchis and the Crimean Bosporus came in early — Greek cities on the northern
shore that wanted protection from the steppe peoples and got a Pontic garrison
with it — and gave him grain, tribute and a recruiting ground outside anybody
else's reach. Then Cappadocia and Bithynia by intervention in their succession
quarrels, which is what finally brought Rome.

By 88 he had the resources to put a fleet at sea, an army into Greece, and a
letter into every city of Roman Asia at once. What he did with that is the next
chapter, and it is the reason the forty-two years were forty-two rather than
five.
