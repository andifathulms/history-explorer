Lucullus broke the Pontic army at Cyzicus and Cabira in the 70s and Mithridates
fled to his son-in-law Tigranes of Armenia — the Greater Armenia
carries the same episode from the other end, and says that sheltering him is what
gave Lucullus a reason to destroy Tigranocerta in 69. Then Lucullus's own troops
mutinied, and Mithridates came back and reoccupied his kingdom.

Pompey replaced him in 66, destroyed the last army in a night attack at
Nicopolis in the upper Euphrates country, and Mithridates went the other way:
north-east across the Caucasus to the Crimea, through territory held by people
who had no particular reason to let him pass, more than a thousand kilometres,
in his mid-sixties.

<Figure id="caucasus" />

He spent three years there raising another army and planning to march on Italy by
land around the Black Sea and through the Danube country. His son Pharnaces
revolted, the army went over, and Mithridates went up into a tower and tried to
poison himself and his daughters. The daughters died. He did not, having spent a
lifetime taking prophylactic doses against exactly this, and had to ask a Gallic
officer of his guard to do it with a sword.

## What Rome did with the kingdom

Not what it had done to Macedon and not what it had done to Pergamon, which is
the argument for entering these three records together.

The Antigonid record ends with a monarchy abolished outright and the country cut
into four republics forbidden to trade with each other. The Attalid record ends
with a will accepted and a province organised. Here Pompey took the coast and the
western part as the province of Bithynia et Pontus, gave the interior and the
mountain country to a set of client rulers and priest-dynasts, and left Pharnaces
the Bosporan kingdom in the Crimea because it was too far away to be worth
holding.

<Figure id="chersonesos" />

Three kingdoms wound up by the same republic within a century, and three
different instruments. Rome had no policy about kings; it had a series of
decisions, each taken on what was cheapest at the time.

## The dynasty afterwards

Pharnaces II tried to take his father's kingdom back while Caesar was occupied
with Pompey, and was beaten at Zela in 47. The Bosporan kingdom in the Crimea,
ruled by his descendants and their successors, lasted another four hundred years
as a Roman client on the edge of the steppe — longer than the Pontic kingdom
itself, and almost entirely unremarked.

What the dynasty transmitted was one claim, and it was a claim about somebody
else. The Mithridatids asserted descent from Darius, publicly and on the
coinage, which puts a Black Sea kingdom that fought Rome for forty years in the
same company as the Parthians and the Sasanians — the two empires that actually
ruled Iran — on the strength of having said the same thing they said.

Rule 1 counts whose legitimacy was worth claiming and does not filter on
recognition, and that is the rule producing exactly the result it was written to
produce.
