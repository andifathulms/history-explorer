Appian stops at the end of the Mithridatic Wars to write an obituary, and it is
the fullest ancient account of what an enemy of Rome looked like from the inside
of the Roman tradition.

The last words come first. Mithridates, having decided to die, found that he
could not: he had spent his life dosing himself against assassination and the
poison would not work. Appian gives him the line. *Although I have kept watch and
ward against all the poisons that one takes with his food, I have not provided
against that domestic poison, always the most dangerous to kings, the treachery
of army, children, and friends.* A Gaulish officer named Bituitus then did what
was asked.

<Figure id="mithridatum-jar" />

Then the catalogue. He was sixteenth in descent from Darius son of Hystaspes, and
eighth from the Mithridates who left the Macedonians and took Pontus — a figure
Appian gives as sixth elsewhere in the same book, which his translators note has
caused some controversy in the learned world. He lived sixty-eight or sixty-nine
years and reigned fifty-seven of them, having come to the throne an orphan. He
fought Rome for forty years. He was beaten by Sulla, Lucullus and Pompey, and
beat each of them at times; he captured three Roman commanders and carried them
about with him, killing one because the war had been his doing; he defeated five
more by name. He made alliances with the Samnites and the Gauls and sent envoys
to Sertorius in Spain, and left no avenue of attack against Rome untried.

<Figure id="theriac-jars" />

Then the body, itemised with an antiquarian's interest in physical evidence. He
had a large frame, as the armour he sent to Nemea and Delphi shows. He was strong
enough to ride and throw the javelin to the end of his life. He could cover a
thousand stades in a day, changing horses. He drove a chariot with sixteen horses
at once.

<Figure id="delphi" />

Then the mind. He cultivated Greek learning and through it the religious
observances of Greece, and was fond of music. He was abstemious and patient of
hard work for the most part, and gave way only to women.

And then, without a change of tone, the rest of it. He was bloodthirsty and cruel
to everyone — the slayer of his mother, his brother, three sons and three
daughters.

Appian puts that sentence between the horses and the Greek learning, which is
the reason to read the passage as a whole rather than to quote the good parts. It
is not a defence and it is not a denunciation; it is a list of the things a king
was supposed to be able to do, compiled by a man who thought the answer was
genuinely impressive, with the murders entered in the same column as the chariot
teams.

The other sentence worth keeping is the one about the end. None of the
conspiracies against him ever escaped his detection, Appian says — not even the
last one; he simply chose to overlook it, and died of it. *So ungrateful is the
wickedness that has been once pardoned.*
