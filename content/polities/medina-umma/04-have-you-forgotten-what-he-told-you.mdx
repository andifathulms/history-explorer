Badr had been won by a raiding party that stumbled into a fight it did not
plan for. Uhud, the following year, was a battle Medina chose, and the
choosing is where the authority the Constitution had built first came under
real pressure — before a single blow was struck.

## The argument over how to fight

Muhammad's own preference, when word came that Mecca was marching on Medina
with three thousand men, was to stay inside the town and let its houses and
lanes do the fighting. Men who had missed Badr and wanted their own chance at
the enemy argued him out of it, and he gave way, put on his armour, and led
about a thousand men out to open ground below Mount Uhud.

He had not finished losing men before the battle even began. Abdullah ibn
Ubayy, the most powerful chief in Medina and no friend of the arrangement he
had been outmanoeuvred into accepting, pulled roughly a third of the force
back to the town, unconvinced by the plan he had been overruled on. A
community that had agreed nobody could go to war without Muhammad's
permission had just demonstrated that permission and participation were not
the same thing: a chief could still take his own men home.

## The order, and what happened to it

Muhammad posted fifty archers on a rise covering the army's exposed flank and
gave a direct order, plainly enough that no one afterwards claimed to have
misunderstood it: hold the position, whatever happened in front of you, and
do not come down.

The battle opened well. The Meccan line broke and its camp lay open, and the
archers watched their own side start taking the plunder below. Most of them
left the hill for it. *The booty! O people, the booty! Your companions have
won — what are you waiting for now?* Their own commander, Abdullah ibn Jubayr,
tried to hold the rest to the order he had been given and was asked, in
effect, why it still mattered. *Have you forgotten what the Messenger of God
told you?* He had not forgotten. It did not matter. Enough of them went down
the hill that the flank Muhammad had told them to cover was open, and Khalid
ibn al-Walid, commanding the Meccan cavalry, rode round it.

## The reversal

What had been a Muslim victory became a rout inside the same afternoon.
Khalid's horsemen came in from the rear the archers had been posted to deny
them, the Meccan line that had broken reformed, and the Muslim force that had
been advancing found itself fighting in a circle with no clear front.

Muhammad went down in the middle of it. A blow drove two rings from his
helmet into his cheek; a thrown stone split his lip; he lost a tooth and fell
into one of the pits an apostate named Abu Amir had dug against exactly this
kind of scramble. Ali pulled him out and Talha took his weight while Abu
Ubaydah worked the iron rings loose from his face. And into that confusion
went the report that mattered more than any wound: that he was dead.

## What the rumour actually threatened

It is worth being exact about why that report was dangerous, because it was
not simply bad news.

The whole apparatus the Constitution had built — the referral of disputes,
the monopoly on the decision to fight, the *ummah* itself — ran through one
man. There was no
office to inherit it, no procedure for a successor, nothing but the
arrangement he personally held together. A false report of his death was not
a rumour about a battle. For as long as it was believed, it was a test of
whether the thing built in the Constitution could survive for even an
afternoon without the one person the whole document was written around. Umar
ibn al-Khattab, hearing it and refusing to accept it, is recorded rallying
men on exactly that logic before anyone had confirmed anything either way.

Muhammad was alive, badly hurt, and got back up the mountain with the
survivors before the Meccan cavalry — unable or unwilling to press the
advantage into the high ground — turned for home. Around seventy Muslims were
dead, among them his uncle Hamza, killed by a thrown javelin and mutilated
afterward. Meccan losses were a third of that.

## What survived it

Nothing about the authority collapsed, which is the fact worth sitting with
longer than the battle itself.

The disobedience on the hill was not punished by exile or execution; it was
named, in the revelation that followed, as exactly what it was — men who
wanted the plunder more than the order — and left there. Abdullah ibn Jubayr
kept his place among the community afterward. The archers who ran for the
camp were not expelled the way Qaynuqa already had been, the year before, for
a market brawl. What Uhud tested was not obedience in the abstract but
whether an authority built on consent rather than force could take an open,
undisguised act of disobedience, in the middle of a battlefield reversal that
nearly cost the arbitrator his life, and still be standing the following week
to arbitrate the next dispute.

It was. The clause about permission to fight had never been tested by
victory. It was tested by a rout, by a false report of the founder's death,
and by fifty men choosing loot over an order given to their faces — and the
community that had agreed to obey him went on agreeing to obey him. That is a
stronger proof of what the Constitution had actually built than Badr ever
supplied.
