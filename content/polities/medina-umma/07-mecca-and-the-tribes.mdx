Mecca submitted in 630 without a battle, and Muhammad required nothing of its
people. No profession of faith was demanded of the city that had driven him out.
Many Meccans fought alongside him at Hunayn weeks later while still not Muslims.

That is worth sitting with, because it cuts against the shape the story is
usually given. What was being extended over Arabia in these years was not, in
the first instance, a religion. It was a peace.

## The Pax Islamica

Watt's name for it is the *Pax Islamica*, and his argument is that it grew out of
the problem the oasis had hired Muhammad to solve. Medina's clans had been
feuding; the founding agreement stopped them. Feuding was not a Medinan
peculiarity but the ordinary condition of Arabia, and once it became visible that
somebody had a working method for suspending it, other tribes wanted in.

So the system spread partly by being useful. In the early Medinan years
Muhammad made alliances with neighbouring tribes on what Watt calls a purely
secular basis — no religious condition at all. Only gradually, as the zone of
peace widened and his position strengthened, did he begin requiring belief in
God and recognition of himself as prophet as a condition of alliance. The
tightening came after Hudaybiyya, when nomads he had counted on failed to turn
out; from then, accepting Islam also meant accepting that the prophet could give
orders.

And even then it was not uniform. He went on making purely secular alliances
with groups from whom no religious demand was made at all, and this was the
normal arrangement with distant and powerful tribes, right up to his death.
There were two kinds of membership, and the sources describe both.

## The Arabs, as an idea

The unification is easier to see now than it was then.

The word *Arab* is barely present in pre-Islamic poetry. The adjective *Arabic*
is first attested in the Qur'an, where it is used to contrast clear speech with
the chewed or indistinct speech of the foreigner — the *ajami* — and otherwise as
a description of the revelation itself. There is no noun for *the Arabs*. What
the word *al-arab* means in the later passages is the nomadic tribes as opposed
to townsmen.

So the category was still forming. An *Arabic Qur'an* addressed to the
clear-speakers implies a body of people set against Abyssinians, Byzantines and
Persians, and Watt thinks a good deal of the appeal of the new religion over
Christianity or Judaism was exactly that: it was not foreign.

Muhammad had begun by understanding himself as sent to his own tribe. The
widening happened in steps that the sources do not mark clearly, and by the end
he was the head of a system covering most of the peninsula. Whether he set out
to unify the Arabs is doubtful. He set out to stop people killing each other,
and unification is what that turned out to require.
