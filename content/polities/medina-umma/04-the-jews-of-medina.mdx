title: The opportunity that was lost

The agreement that founded the community named the Jewish clans of the oasis as
members of it, on their own religion and their own property. Within five years
two of those clans had been expelled and the third destroyed. What happened in
between is the hardest part of the Medinan decade to write about, and it cannot
be left out, because the founding document does not make sense without it.

## What happened

The first was Qaynuqa, in 624, shortly after Badr. The occasion was squalid: in
the Qaynuqa market someone pinned an Arab woman's skirt so that she was exposed
when she stood, a Muslim who saw the joke killed the man responsible, and the
man's people killed him back. The clan withdrew into its strongholds, Muhammad
treated the killing as grounds for war, and after a fifteen-day siege they
surrendered and were expelled. They were allowed to take their families and
three days to collect debts owed them, and had to leave their arms behind.

The politics beneath it are visible in one detail. Qaynuqa were confederates of
Abdullah ibn Ubayy, the most powerful man in Medina, and expected him to protect
them. He spoke for them, and it achieved nothing; when he tried to force his way
in to Muhammad, a guard shoved him into a wall hard enough to bloody his face,
and he could neither avenge it nor claim compensation. Watt's point is that
Qaynuqa accepted exile only when they understood what that told them. The
expulsion measured how much the balance in the oasis had shifted since the
document was written.

Nadir followed, and their date-lands went not to the Muslims at large but to the
emigrants, along with two of the poorest of the Ansar — the first landed property
the community disposed of. Then in 627, after the siege of Medina,
came Qurayza, and it did not end in exile.

## Watt's reading

Watt is explicit that the fundamental cause was not the money.

He does not deny the money existed. The wealth of the Jewish clans, he writes,
was of great benefit to Muhammad and considerably eased his financial position,
and the prospect of it may well have influenced the *timing* of the attacks. But
making it the whole explanation he calls unduly materialistic. The quarrel, on
his reading, was theological on both sides and inescapable once joined: the Jews
of Medina held that God had chosen them and revealed Himself through them, and
Muhammad's claim to receive messages could not be accommodated to that; while
Muhammad had come to see his prophethood as the only possible basis for uniting
the Arabs, which made public criticism of it a threat to the entire political
experiment rather than a difference of opinion.

He is unusually blunt about the rejection being reasonable. It was altogether in
keeping with the traditional outlook of Jewry that the Jews of Medina should
reject Muhammad, he writes, and even leaders more far-sighted than the ones they
actually had would have done the same.

And then he writes the sentence the chapter exists for:

> In the early months at Medina the seeds were sown of a great tragedy; a great
> opportunity was lost.

His counterfactual is that at several points terms were available that would
have given the Jewish clans religious autonomy inside the community, and that
had they been taken, the Jews might have become partners in the Arab empire and
Islam a sect of Jewry.

That is one scholar writing in 1956, and it is speculation, which he says
himself. It is included here because it is the judgement of the standard study
of this polity, and because the alternative — telling the story of the founding
agreement without saying what became of many of the parties to it — would be
worse than partisan. It would be incomplete.
