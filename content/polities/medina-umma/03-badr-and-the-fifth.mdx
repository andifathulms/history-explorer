The emigrants had a problem nobody records anyone solving: they had to eat.

Seventy or so Meccan Muslims had moved to an oasis whose land was already owned
and already worked. They did not take up farming. They could not live
indefinitely on Medinan hospitality, though for the first two years they largely
did — one of the complaints levelled at Muhammad by men trying to prove they were
not Muslims was that he and his followers were a burden on the town. Some of
them traded, and a few were very good at it: Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf turned down
the offer of half a Medinan's property, asked to be shown the market instead, and
came back the same day with butter and cheese he had made a profit on. Umar
missed an instruction from Muhammad because he was in the middle of a
transaction. Ali gathered rushes to sell.

But the Medinan market already had a trading clan working it, and the obvious
alternative was the one that led straight to war: the Meccan caravans passing
within reach on the Syrian road.

## Badr

In 624 a raiding party went out after a caravan and met the force Mecca had sent
to protect it. The Muslims won.

What matters here is the argument afterwards. There was a dispute over dividing
the spoil, and it was settled by a ruling that Muhammad could dispose of the
whole as he chose. Out of that came the arrangement that held for the rest of
his life and long after: a fifth — the *khums* — went to Muhammad for public
purposes, and the rest was divided equally among the men who had fought, a
horseman drawing two extra shares for his horse.

That fifth is the beginning of the state's finances. Until then the community
had money in the sense that individuals in it had money. Now there was a fund
held by the head of the community, spent on its behalf, with a collecting agent
appointed to handle it — a man named Mahmiya ibn Jaz, of the Zubayd. A treasury
with a treasurer is a different kind of institution from a preaching.

## An old shape, filled differently

The proportion was not invented. An Arabian tribal chief customarily took a
quarter of what his tribe seized, and Muhammad took a fifth; he also took the
*safi*, the chief's traditional right to pick one item out before the division
began, and at Badr he picked a sword.

So the office he was building was recognisable. He drew what a chief drew, a
little less of it, in the manner chiefs drew it. What was different was where it
went. A chief's quarter was his; the fifth was for public purposes. The same custom, pointed at a different object, is most of
how the institutions of this community were made — and when he later dealt with
nomad tribes far from Medina, what he required of them was this same fifth, his
share and his pick, in terms every one of them already understood.
