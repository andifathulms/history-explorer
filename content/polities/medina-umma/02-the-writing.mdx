The document usually called the Constitution of Medina survives because Ibn
Ishaq copied it. He says nothing about where he got it or when it came into
force, and its position near the start of his Medinan narrative is probably just
where logic put it rather than where evidence did. Watt treats it as an ancient
document nonetheless, and the internal reason is strong: it gives Muhammad so
little that it is hard to see a later generation composing it.

It opens by naming what it is making:

> This is a writing of Muhammad the prophet between the believers and Muslims of
> Quraysh and Yathrib and those who follow them and are attached to them and who
> crusade along with them. They are a single community distinct from other
> people.

The last verb is *jahadu*, which Watt renders as crusade and which later
vocabulary would handle differently; here it simply marks out those who campaign
with the community from those who merely live alongside it.

*Ummah*, the word translated community, is doing the founding work. The clause
asserts that a body of people drawn from two different towns and a dozen
unrelated clans is now one thing.

## What it actually regulates

Most of the clauses are about killing, and that is not a curiosity — it is the
whole design.

Nine near-identical articles set out that each clan continues to pay blood-money
and ransom its captives *according to their former condition*, meaning by the
arrangements already in force. Nothing is centralised. The emigrants from Mecca
are simply added to the list as a tenth unit with the same obligations, which is
how a body of refugees with no land became a clan for legal purposes.

What is new sits on top of that. Believers do not abandon a debtor among them.
No believer kills a believer over an unbeliever. God's protection is one, and
protection granted by the least of them binds all of them. Where a man is killed
wrongfully the whole community is against the killer, *nothing is permissible to
them except to oppose him* — which takes the most dangerous decision in a feuding
society, whether to avenge or accept payment, and puts it beyond a single clan's
discretion.

The old order is left standing and a second order is built across it. That is
why the document works: nobody had to give anything up.

## The Jews are inside it

The clauses that surprise modern readers are the ones about the Jewish clans,
and they are unambiguous:

> The Jews of Banu Awf are a community along with the believers. To the Jews
> their religion and to the Muslims their religion.

The formula is then repeated for Najjar, Harith, Saida, Jusham, Aws, Thalaba and
their clients. They bear their own expenses and the Muslims bear theirs; between
them there is help against anyone who attacks the people of the document, and
*sincere friendship, and honourable dealing, not treachery*. The valley of
Yathrib is declared sacred to all of them. Whoever attacks the oasis is to be
resisted by all of them.

This is a defensive league of religiously distinct parties, not a confessional
state. What happened to that arrangement over the following five years is a
separate and much darker story, and it is told later.

## The two clauses that mattered

Buried in the list are the provisions that turned an arbitrator into a
government.

> Whenever among the people of this document there occurs any incident or
> quarrel from which disaster is to be feared, it is to be referred to God and
> to Muhammad, the Messenger of God.

And: no member of the community may go out to war without Muhammad's permission.

One clause makes him the final court. The other gives him a monopoly on the
decision to fight, which in a society where any clan could start a war by
raiding was the single most consequential thing to control. Neither reads like
much. Between them they are the whole apparatus of the state he had, and for
several years they were enough.
