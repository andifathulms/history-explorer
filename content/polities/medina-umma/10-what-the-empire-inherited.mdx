Within twenty years of Muhammad's death Arab armies held Syria, Egypt and the
Iranian plateau. The institutions they governed those places with were not
designed for them. They were worked out in an oasis for a population of a few
thousand, and then applied to two former empires without much alteration,
because they were what existed.

## The Khaybar arrangement

The clearest case is a settlement made in 628 at an oasis north of Medina.

When Khaybar fell, its Jewish cultivators were not expelled and the land was not
distributed to the conquerors. The previous owners stayed on it and worked it,
handing over a share of what it produced. Watt notes that this was a new
arrangement, and that it became the system followed in the conquests after
Muhammad's death.

It is difficult to overstate what that decision did. The alternative — settling
the victorious army on the land it had taken — was the normal outcome of
conquest, and it would have turned the conquerors into a scattered landholding class
inside a far larger population, to be absorbed by it in a few generations. What happened instead was that the conquerors stayed together in
garrisons and drew the surplus of somebody else's agriculture. The Sawad of Iraq
and the Nile valley were run on the principle first applied to a date plantation
in the Hijaz.

## The fifth, and the stipend

The other inheritance is fiscal.

The *khums* had been instituted after Badr, when a quarrel over dividing spoils
was settled by giving Muhammad a fifth for public purposes. He required the same
fifth of every tribe that submitted, along with his share and his pick, and it
was collected by an appointed agent. When the conquests began, the fifth simply
scaled: the same fraction of vastly larger sums, taken by the same right.

And on the other side of the ledger, membership was already tied to payment.
Watt observes that once Umar fixed stipends in 636, every full member of the
community drew one — and that some such arrangement was presumably in force
before that, though the details are lost. Combined with the rule that nobody
belonged as an individual, this produced the register that governed the caliphate
for a century: to be in the community was to be in a group that had treated with
Muhammad, and to be in that group was to be on the roll.

## What it means for the conquests

The caliphate did not improvise its way through the seventh century. It applied a
small number of arrangements, all of them worked out under Muhammad and most of
them worked out for reasons that had nothing to do with governing conquered
provinces, to a situation none of them had anticipated.

Some of them fitted remarkably well. The garrison-and-stipend model held an
enormous conquest together with very few men. Others did not survive contact.
The assumption that plunder would keep arriving was written into the stipend roll
and became untenable the moment the frontiers stopped moving, which is the fiscal
crisis the caliphate's first civil war was conducted over.

The oasis outlived itself by a very long way.
