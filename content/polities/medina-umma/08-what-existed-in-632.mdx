Muhammad died at Medina in June 632, having named nobody to follow him and
having built nothing that would produce a successor on its own.

It is worth asking precisely what it was that he left, because the answer is
stranger than either "a religion" or "a state" suggests.

## A tribe that was not one

Arabian political thinking ran on the tribe. A tribe was a blood group; it was
the whole of a man's social security, and in exchange it took his highest
loyalty. Tribes could confederate, usually for a limited purpose such as fighting
another confederation, and outsiders could be attached as confederates, protected
neighbours or clients — but none of that displaced kinship as the organising
idea. The main tribes were sovereign. Arabs knew the Byzantine, Abyssinian and
Persian empires existed, had a notion of kingship and disliked it, and understood
foreign rulers by analogy to tribal chiefs anyway: in the story of Muhammad's
letter to Heraclius, the emperor behaves like the chief of an Arab tribe.

The *ummah* took that shape and changed one thing. It was based on religion
rather than on kinship. Watt notes this is nowhere stated as a theory and
everywhere assumed in practice.

Everything else follows from it. If Muhammad is a messenger there is a message,
and a message means God is issuing directions about practical affairs; so on
matters of principle Muhammad does not decide, he announces. God is the head of
the community. The founding document says the security members enjoy is the
*dhimmah* of God — His guarantee — and in the treaties Muhammad's name is coupled
with God's: groups are given the protection of God and of Muhammad together.

## Not a collection of individuals

The other structural fact is that nobody belonged to this community on their own.

You were a member because your group was. A man was in because he belonged to a
clan named in the founding document, or to a tribe that had since made a treaty,
or because he had left his tribe and come to Medina and been classed among the
emigrants. Watt draws the consequence: this is why later generations were so
eager to establish that their tribe had sent a delegation to Muhammad, and why
non-Arabs who became Muslims had to be enrolled as clients of an Arab tribe.
There was no procedure for simply joining.

By the end there was not even a settled name for the thing. *Ummah* drops out of
use once Mecca is in and the tribes are confederated; the treaties occasionally
say *jamaa* or *hizb Allah*. Watt's explanation is that no official term was
needed, because diplomacy was conducted in the name of God and Muhammad and
that was sufficient designation.

## The gap

Put those together and the problem of June 632 is visible.

The community's head was God; its intermediary was a man who announced what God
commanded; and its membership was a bundle of treaties made with that man
personally. He then died, and the one function nobody could inherit was the one
the whole arrangement rested on.

The treaties were the immediate crisis, because a great many tribes took the
reasonable view that an agreement made with Muhammad had ended with Muhammad.
Whether they were right was decided by force over the following two years.
