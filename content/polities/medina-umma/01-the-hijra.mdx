In September 622 a preacher who had failed in his own city moved two hundred
miles north to an oasis that had sent for him.

That is the least dramatic way to state it and it is the most useful, because
what Yathrib wanted was specific. The oasis was two confederations of Arab
clans, the Aws and the Khazraj, with several Jewish clans among them, and it had
been feuding for years without any of its chiefs being able to stop it. What it
lacked was an arbitrator nobody's cousin — someone from outside with standing
enough to be obeyed and no blood in the local quarrels. Muhammad had been
preaching in Mecca for a decade against increasing hostility, and had the
standing.

## How little he was given

The most striking thing about the arrangement is how weak the position was.

Watt's reading of the founding agreement is that Muhammad's formal powers in 622
were so slight that they cannot have been much less at the very start. The
document gives him exactly one operative function: disputes are referred to him.
He is named in the preamble as *Muhammad the prophet*. His own following, the
emigrants from Mecca, appears in the list as one group alongside the eight
Medinan clans, which puts him level with the clan chiefs — first among them in
order of mention, and perhaps in honour, but level. He is not the ruler of
Medina. He is one important man among a number of important men, and for the
first year several of them were probably more influential than he was.

Nor was the right of arbitration much of a power in itself. A man to whom
disputes are referred has authority only if his settlements are accepted, and
they are accepted only if he finds answers people can live with. The office was
an opportunity to acquire influence, not a grant of it.

## What that looked like in practice

Two episodes show the constraint working.

When Abdullah ibn Ubayy, the most powerful man in Medina, spread the slander
against Aisha, Muhammad could not simply act against him. He had to convene the
Ansar and ask permission of the men who might feel bound to avenge any injury
done to Ibn Ubayy. He got his way, but he had to ask.

Later, when the fate of the Jewish clan of Qurayza was decided after the siege,
Muhammad did not pronounce the judgement himself. If he had ordered blood shed,
honour might have obliged Qurayza's confederates — Muslims by then — to avenge
it. The decision was handed to the chief of the clan they had been allied to.

Both are cases of a man routing around a limit rather than overriding it. The
clan chiefs of Medina kept their power for years, and it bounded what he could
do; his authority grew inside those limits rather than by abolishing them.

Ten years later the same man's writ ran over most of Arabia. That distance,
travelled without a bureaucracy or a standing army, is the subject of everything
that follows.
