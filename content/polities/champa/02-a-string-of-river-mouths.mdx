Champa never became one kingdom and the reason is the coastline. Between the
Annamite range and the sea there is no plain — only a series of short river
valleys, each opening onto its own bay, separated from the next by spurs of
mountain running down to the water.

Each valley is a polity. What the sources call Champa is the set of them, in
varying arrangements of alliance and overlordship, for about thirteen hundred
years.

## The five

The Cham territories are conventionally counted as five, north to south:
Indrapura around modern Da Nang, Amaravati, Vijaya inland from Qui Nhon,
Kauthara at Nha Trang, and Panduranga in the far south around Phan Rang.

Each has its own dynastic inscriptions, its own temple groups, and its own
periods of prominence. The capital moves between them — north to Indrapura in
the ninth century, south to Vijaya after the Vietnamese pressure begins,
eventually to Panduranga alone.

That mobility is usually read as decline. For most of the period it is simply
how the thing worked: whichever valley had the strongest ruler held the primacy,
and the others acknowledged it while running their own affairs.

## What the expansion was made of

Not territory. There was nowhere to expand to: mountains behind, the Vietnamese
in front, the Khmer beyond the range, and the sea.

What the Chams expanded was their share of the traffic. The coast sits directly
on the route between the Malacca Strait and Canton, and a ship working north on
the summer monsoon has to pass it. The Cham ports supplied water, provisions and
shelter, and exported what the mountains behind them produced: eaglewood and
calambac — the aromatic resinous heartwood that was the single most valuable
forest product in the China trade — along with ivory, rhinoceros horn, beeswax
and slaves.

The uplands were the other half of the system. The Cham lowland courts were tied
to the highland peoples of the Annamite cordillera by trade and by ritual
kinship, and the goods that made the ports rich came down from them. That
relationship is invisible in the inscriptions and is the whole economic basis of
the state.

## Indrapura, and the temples

The ninth and tenth centuries are the high point of building.

Dong Duong, founded in 875, is the great Mahayana Buddhist monastery complex of
Champa, and it is unlike anything in the Hindu Khmer or Javanese tradition —
its sculpture is heavy, frontal, and immediately recognisable.

My Son, in a valley inland from Da Nang, was the dynastic sanctuary for
something over eight hundred years — brick towers built and rebuilt from the
fourth century to the thirteenth, with the inscriptions that supply almost all
of Cham dynastic history.

## And the raiding

The other Cham export was violence, and the Chinese and Vietnamese sources are
consistent about it.

Cham fleets raided the Vietnamese coast repeatedly; Cham ships appear in the
records of the Gulf of Tonkin as pirates as often as traders. In 1177 a Cham
fleet went up the Mekong and sacked Angkor itself.

That is not a contradiction of the trading economy. It is the same economy: a
polity that lives on controlling a stretch of sea route will tax the traffic
when it can and take it when it cannot, and the distinction between the two was
never as sharp as the word "kingdom" makes it sound.
