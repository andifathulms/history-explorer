Le Thanh Tong took Vijaya in 1471. The Cham territory north of Cape Varella
became Vietnamese, and what remained of Champa was a small polity in the far
south that lasted, in diminishing form, until 1832.

<Figure id="cp-canhtien" />

Vietnamese historiography calls the long process this belongs to the *Nam
tiến*, the march to the south: five centuries in which the Red River delta's
state expanded down a narrow coast, absorbing Cham principalities one at a time
and eventually reaching the Mekong. 1471 is its decisive act.

## Who did it

The Le dynasty, and the king was Le Thanh Tong.

He is the most consequential Vietnamese ruler between the Mongol wars and the
nineteenth century, and 1471 is only part of what he did. He rebuilt the
administration on a Ming model with six ministries and a censorate, promulgated
a law code that stayed in force for three hundred years and is unusually
protective of women's property rights for its period, ordered the compilation of
the standard dynastic history, and commissioned a cadastral survey of the whole
country.

The Cham campaign belongs to that programme rather than standing apart from it.
A state that has just given itself a working land register and a functioning
army goes looking for land to put in the register.

He annexed the northern Cham territories outright, organised them as Vietnamese
provinces, and settled soldiers and their families on them. That is the
mechanism the whole march south ran on, applied at the largest scale it ever
reached.
