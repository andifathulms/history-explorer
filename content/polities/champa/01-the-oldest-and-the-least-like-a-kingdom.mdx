Champa is the oldest Indic polity in Southeast Asia and the hardest object in
this region to fit into a record.

The Vo Canh inscription, in Sanskrit, from somewhere around the third century,
is the earliest Sanskrit inscription found anywhere in the region — earlier than
anything at Angkor by three hundred years. The Đông Yên Châu stone, from around
the fourth, is the oldest known text in any Austronesian language, which puts it
a thousand years ahead of the earliest Malay. Both are Cham.

<Figure id="cp-vocanh" />

And Champa was not one kingdom for thirteen centuries. It was a series of
principalities at river mouths along six hundred miles of coast — Indrapura,
Vijaya, Kauthara, Panduranga and others — each with its own hinterland running
back into the mountains, sometimes under a single authority and more often not.
Chinese records treat it as a state because Chinese records treat everything as
a state. The archaeology and the inscriptions describe something looser and more
durable: a set of ports sharing a language, a script, a religion and a coast.

<Figure id="cp-map" />

Both conventional dates for Champa are soft in the same way. 192 is the date
given for Lin-yi, the polity Chinese sources place here and which later Cham
history is continuous with. 1471 is the Vietnamese capture of Vijaya, after
which Cham polities survived in the far south for another three and a half
centuries without anything the sources treat as a state on the earlier scale.

Neither date describes a single kingdom starting or stopping so much as it
marks where the sources change what they can see: a polity Chinese records
first noticed in 192, and a coast that stopped mattering to anyone outside it
after 1471.
