Champa lost to Dai Viet over four hundred years, valley by valley, and the
mechanism was demographic before it was military.

## What was pushing

The Red River delta is one of the most productive rice landscapes on earth and
it is small. A Vietnamese population growing inside it had two directions to go:
into the sea, which was already being reclaimed, or south along the coast, into
the next river valley.

That process — the *nam tien*, the march to the south — ran for eight hundred
years and it is the central fact of Vietnamese history. It was not a policy
announced by a court. It was families moving into the next valley behind
soldiers, building dykes, planting rice, and being taxed by a government that
then had a reason to defend them.

Champa was in the way of all of it.

## The sequence

The dates read like a ratchet, and each one is a defeat that cost provinces
permanently.

In 1069 a Ly emperor took the Cham king prisoner and released him in exchange
for three northern provinces. In 1306 two more provinces were transferred as a
marriage settlement — a Cham king married a Vietnamese princess and paid for her
with territory, and the Vietnamese court took the land and then took her back
after he died rather than let her be burned on his pyre.

Che Bong Nga in the second half of the fourteenth century reversed all of it for
a generation: he sacked Thang Long, the Vietnamese capital, three times between
1371 and 1383, and came close to ending the Tran dynasty. He was killed in 1390
by a cannon shot from a Vietnamese ship, after a defector pointed out which
vessel he was on.

That is the last time Champa was the stronger party.

## Why the recoveries never held

Because the two states were built for different things.

Dai Viet could raise infantry from a dense taxed peasantry in a single delta,
lose an army and raise another. Champa's power was ships, upland trade goods and
whichever valley currently held the primacy, and its coalitions had to be
reassembled after every reverse.

And the ground itself favoured one side. Every valley the Vietnamese took was
settled, diked and put under rice, and became a base for the next one. Every
valley Champa retook was a coastline it had to hold against people who now lived
on the plain behind it.

## The thing that made it worse

The Cham court, from the twelfth century, was fighting the Khmer as well.

The 1177 sack of Angkor brought a Khmer reprisal that put Champa under direct
Angkorian occupation for about twenty years from 1203 — a Cham prince ruling as
a Khmer appointee, on the Khmer side of a war Champa had started.

A polity on a two-hundred-mile coastal strip that manages to be at war with the
two largest states in mainland Southeast Asia simultaneously is not going to
finish the century in the same shape.

By the middle of the fifteenth century what was left was Vijaya and the two
southern valleys. In 1471 a Vietnamese army of, by the chronicle's account, two
hundred thousand men took Vijaya, and the northern Cham lands were annexed
outright.
