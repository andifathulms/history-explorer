The date given for the end of Champa is 1471, and a Cham polity went on
existing for another three hundred and sixty-one years.

## What survived the fall, and what finally ended it

The Vietnamese annexed the north after Vijaya fell and left the far south
alone. Panduranga continued as a Cham principality for another two hundred
and twenty-two years, until 1693 reduced its own kingship to a Vietnamese
province and, after that, to a treaty-bound jurisdiction under an "aboriginal
king" answerable to Phu Xuan. Even that arrangement outlasted every ruler who
signed it: not until 1832 did the Nguyen emperor Minh Mang abolish the last
Cham kingship outright, absorb the territory fully, and suppress the rising
that followed — seventeen years after the Napoleonic wars ended, and eleven
hundred kilometres of coast from where the whole business began.

The reason the ending is dated to 1471 rather than 1832 is that after Vijaya
there was no Cham state that anybody outside the region had to take account of.
The distinction is real. It is also, from Panduranga's side, the difference
between a country and a footnote.

## What happened to the people

They are still there, which is the part most accounts of the "vanished kingdom
of Champa" leave out.

Something over a hundred and fifty thousand Cham live in Vietnam and perhaps
twice that in Cambodia, with communities in Thailand, Malaysia and — since the
1970s — the United States and France.

They split religiously in a way that has no parallel anywhere. The Cham of
Cambodia and much of southern Vietnam are Sunni Muslim, converted through the
maritime trade from the fifteenth century onward. The Cham Bani of Ninh Thuan
and Binh Thuan practise a form of Islam that developed in isolation for
centuries — a lunar calendar, three daily prayers, a Ramadan observed on behalf
of the community by the priesthood, and a body of ritual that orthodox Muslims
elsewhere do not recognise. And the Cham Balamon are Hindu, still — the only
indigenous Hindu community in Southeast Asia outside Bali, still worshipping at
Cham towers built a thousand years ago.

Po Nagar at Nha Trang, the great shrine of the Cham mother goddess, is now a
Vietnamese Buddhist temple and a Cham pilgrimage site simultaneously, and both
communities use it.

## The language, and the script

Cham is an Austronesian language — the same family as Malay, Javanese and
Tagalog, and one of the very few Austronesian languages spoken on the Asian
mainland.

That is the deep fact about Champa and it explains a great deal. Its people came
by sea, they were part of the island world rather than the mainland one, and
their ports faced outward while their Vietnamese and Khmer neighbours faced
their own river plains.

The Cham script, derived from a south Indian original, is still used for
religious texts by the Balamon and Bani communities. The earliest inscription in
it — the Dong Yen Chau stone, from about the fourth century — is the oldest
surviving text in any Austronesian language anywhere, which means the first
written Austronesian words come from the mainland rather than from the islands
where almost all the speakers live.

## The towers

Between Da Nang and Phan Rang there are something like twenty surviving groups
of Cham brick towers, standing on hills above the coastal plain.

They were built without mortar, of bricks fired and then rubbed together and
bonded with a vegetable resin, in a technique that has not been reproduced. They
have survived monsoons, the sixteenth-century wars, the French period and the
American bombing of the 1960s and 1970s, which flattened My Son — the dynastic
sanctuary of eight hundred years — in a week in 1969.

What is left of the largest tower group in Southeast Asia after Angkor is
therefore a set of foundations, a pile of numbered bricks, and the photographs
and drawings the French made before the war.
