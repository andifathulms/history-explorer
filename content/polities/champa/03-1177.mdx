In 1177 a Cham fleet went up the Mekong, crossed the Tonle Sap, and sacked
Angkor.

It is the event that most disturbs the ordinary picture of mainland Southeast
Asia, in which the great inland rice kingdoms are the powers and the coast is a
margin between them. The largest polity in the region, on an inland plain with a
population no other settlement here approached, was taken by a string of ports
with a fraction of its resources — by water, from the sea, by a route nobody
appears to have thought needed watching.

Angkor's answer is on that record: Jayavarman VII drove them out, took the
throne in 1181, campaigned into Cham territory and held parts of it for two
decades, and then built the Bayon, whose outer gallery shows the naval battles
in detail from the winning side. The most famous reliefs in Cambodia are a
record of a war that started with this.

## Where the money came from

Champa is the one state in mainland Southeast Asia that did not live on rice.
Its neighbours were agrarian: a delta or a floodplain, a taxed peasantry, and a
court on top. Champa had no plain to speak of, and lived on tolls and on
plunder.

The Cham ports sat on the sea route between the straits and China at the point
where ships had to come close inshore, and they supplied it: eaglewood,
calambac, sandalwood, ivory and the other aromatics of their own mountains, which
were among the most valuable cargoes in the eastern trade. When supplying was
not enough they took, and the same fleets and the same crews did both.

Tolls and plunder are one activity seen in two directions, and the same was
true across the straits, where Srivijaya and later Melaka had the *orang laut* —
sea peoples who raided for the polity rather than against it — on the payroll.

The difference is that we know who was in the boats for Srivijaya, and for
Champa we do not. The Chinese and Vietnamese sources describe Cham fleets and
Cham campaigns constantly, and never once say who crewed them.
