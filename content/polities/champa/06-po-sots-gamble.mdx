Vijaya fell in 1471, but a Cham court went on ruling the dry country around
Phan Rang and Phan Ri for another two centuries, shrinking with every
generation and surviving on the same terms that had governed weaker states on
this coast for a thousand years: acknowledge whichever power controlled the
Vietnamese court, pay what was demanded, and be left alone.

## A death in Hue, and a gamble that failed

Po Sot had governed the remaining Cham lands from Phan Ri since 1659, careful
enough in his dealings with the Nguyen lords at Phu Xuan to keep what
independence he had. In 1691 the Nguyen lord Nguyen Phuc Tran died with the
Mekong frontier in disarray and his own commanders discredited, and his
successor Nguyen Phuc Chu came to power at only sixteen. Po Sot read the
transition as an opening and seized the Phan Rang region outright, defying
Phu Xuan's authority for the first time in a generation.

It was the wrong moment to test an untested ruler. Nguyen Phuc Chu sent
Nguyen Huu Canh south, and in 1693 Po Sot was captured. What remained of the
Cham kingdom was reorganised as a Vietnamese province, with Vietnamese
officials placed at Phan Rang, Phan Ri and Phan Thiet; several thousand Cham
Muslims left for Cambodia rather than live under the new administration, and
many of those who stayed moved into the uplands among the Churu, Ede and
Jarai. Po Sot himself died in captivity soon afterward.

## A king kept on, in diminished form

Vietnamese rule did not extend to abolishing Cham kingship outright — not yet.
Po Sot's younger brother, Po Saktiray Depatih, was installed as an "aboriginal
king" answerable to Phu Xuan, ruling what remained of the Cham population
under Vietnamese oversight. An uprising in the winter of 1693-94, led by a
Cham noble and a Chinese adventurer who claimed magical powers, besieged Phan
Rang twice before being driven off into Cambodia — the last armed attempt to
reverse 1693 rather than live with it.

Po Saktiray Depatih chose the other course. For more than three decades, until
his death in 1727, he kept the peace, sent the tribute Phu Xuan demanded —
elephants, cattle, tusks, rhinoceros horn, cloth, honey, dried fish, salt and
timber, itemised down to the bamboo mat — and used what standing he still had
to shield ordinary Chams from Vietnamese administrators reaching further into
their affairs than the arrangement allowed.

## The treaty that outlasted everyone who signed it

In 1712 he secured a five-point agreement with the Vietnamese court that, at
least on paper, remained the legal basis of Cham self-government for the next
hundred and twenty years: Cham officials retained sole jurisdiction over
disputes between Chams, disputes between Chams and Vietnamese were to be
judged jointly, and Vietnamese authorities were barred from oppressing the
people the treaty covered. It was not independence. It was a kingdom reduced
to a jurisdiction, kept alive on paper for longer than most kingdoms of any
kind last outright — until Minh Mang cancelled the whole arrangement in 1832
and there was no aboriginal kingship left to defend it.
