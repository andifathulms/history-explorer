Chinggis Khan gave his eldest son the land furthest west — as far, the formula
went, as Mongol hooves had reached — which at the time was not very far, and
was therefore an inheritance that had to be gone out and taken.

## The son whose paternity was in doubt

Jochi's mother Börte had been carried off by the Merkit early in Chinggis's
career and recovered some months later, and Jochi was born soon after. The
question of whose son he was hung over him all his life; the *Secret History*
records his brother Chagatai throwing it at him in front of their father.

Chinggis never repudiated him. But the inheritance he was given was the far
edge, and he died in 1227, a few months before his father, without ever
governing much of it.

The ulus passed to his sons — Batu in the west, Orda in the east — and it was
Batu who turned a claim on empty steppe into a state.

## The great western campaign

The assembly of 1235 resolved on a campaign to the west, and it was resourced
as an imperial undertaking rather than a family one: princes from all four
branches went, with Subedei, the best soldier the empire produced, in effective
command.

What followed in seven years is the most successful sustained campaign in
military history and is worth listing plainly. The Volga Bulghars in 1236. The
Rus principalities one at a time through 1237 and 1238 — Ryazan, Vladimir,
Suzdal — in a winter campaign, because the frozen rivers were roads and the
Russian forests were not passable otherwise. Kiev in December 1240. Then a
simultaneous advance into Poland and Hungary in the spring of 1241, in which two
separate armies destroyed a German-Polish force at Legnica and the Hungarian
royal army at Mohi within two days of each other, four hundred kilometres apart.

Two days apart, four hundred kilometres apart, coordinated in advance. That is
the thing about the Mongol armies that is hardest to convey and hardest to
match: not the cavalry, which many peoples had, but the staff work.

## Then they went home

In December 1241 Ögödei died in Mongolia. The princes broke off and rode east
for the succession assembly, and Hungary and Poland were never attacked in that
way again.

Whether the death is the whole explanation is argued — the Hungarian plain may
simply not have carried enough grass for the horse herds, and the armies had
been in the field a long way from home for six years. What is not in doubt is
that the withdrawal was not forced.

## What Batu kept

He did not go back to Mongolia to live. He settled on the lower Volga, built a
capital at Sarai, and governed the western steppe and the Rus principalities
from it.

He also never took the title of Great Khan, though he was the senior prince of
the family and could probably have had it. What he did instead was make khans:
his support decided the succession of 1251 in favour of the Toluid line, and for
a decade he was the most powerful individual in the empire without holding its
highest office.

The state he left behind is usually called the Golden Horde. It called itself
the ulus of Jochi, and for two hundred and fifty years the question of who
governed Russia was answered on the Volga.
