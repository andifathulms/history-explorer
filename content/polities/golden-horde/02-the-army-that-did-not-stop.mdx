Batu's campaign of 1236 to 1242 is the most successful winter offensive in
military history and one of the least examined, because the people it fell on were
in no position to write about it afterwards.

## Why winter

Every previous steppe raid on Rus' had come in summer, when horses could graze and
armies could move. Sübedei, who had ridden this ground in 1223 and remembered it,
brought the army in at the end of the year — and the Novgorod chronicler's dates
show why.

Ryazan was invested on 16 December 1237 and fell on the 21st. Vladimir fell in
February. The grand prince died on the river Sit in March. Torzhok was invested on
the first Sunday in Lent.

The country between the Rus' towns is forest and marsh, impassable to a mounted
army for most of the year. In January it is a road. The rivers that defend the
towns in summer are highways in winter; the Oka, the Volga, the Kliazma and the
Msta all freeze hard enough to carry siege equipment, and every principality is
connected to every other by ice.

Nobody had ever attacked Rus' that way because nobody had ever had an army that
could live in the open at those temperatures. The Mongols could.

## Town by town

The chronicler records a sequence rather than a battle, and the sequence is the
argument.

The Ryazan princes went out to meet the envoys rather than let them into the towns,
and refused the demand for a tenth of everything. Ryazan was taken in five days and
its people killed by fire and by the sword — the prince, the princess, men, women,
children, monks, nuns and priests.

At Vladimir the prince fled the city and his son shut himself in it. When the
battering rams were set up and the walls were clearly going, the young prince, his
mother, his wife, his sister and the bishop were all shorn into the monastic order
in the cathedral, and when the city burned they shut themselves into the sacristy.
The Mongols broke down the doors, piled wood against the church and set fire to it.

Then Moscow, Pereyaslavl, Yuriev, Dmitrov, Volok and Tver, and then Torzhok, fenced
round in the same way as the others.

There is no defeat in the field anywhere in the account, because there was no army
in the field. Each town was taken by itself, in order, at a rate of a few days
each, by an enemy that never had to fight more than one of them at a time.

## The help that did not come

The grand prince of Vladimir was asked for help by Ryazan and *neither went himself
nor listened to the request*, wishing to make war separately.

The chronicler explains this theologically, as God taking away their strength and
putting perplexity into them for their sins. The political explanation is in his own
text a few lines earlier: Rus' was a dozen principalities whose princes had spent
two generations fighting each other for Kiev, and every one of them calculated that
the storm would fall on a rival first.

They were all correct, in turn.

## Why it stopped

The army went on into Poland and Hungary in 1241, destroyed a Polish and German
force at Legnica and the Hungarian army at Mohi within two days of each other, and
reached the Adriatic. Then in 1242 it turned round and went home.

Ögödei had died in Mongolia, and the princes of the blood who were with the army —
including the future great khans Güyük and Möngke — had to go back for the
assembly. That is the entire reason, and it is the clearest illustration in this
history of what a Mongol army was: not a migration and not a horde looking for
land, but an expeditionary force under orders, which could be recalled.

Europe was not saved by a battle. It was saved by a funeral.
