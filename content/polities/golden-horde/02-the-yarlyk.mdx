The Horde governed the Rus principalities by issuing each prince a *yarlyk*, a
patent to rule, and requiring tribute. Which prince received which patent was the
Horde's decision, and the princes competed for it — travelling to Sarai,
petitioning, denouncing rivals, and occasionally being executed there.

Moscow won that competition. Its princes obtained the right to collect the
tribute for the whole of north-eastern Rus, which meant they handled other
principalities' money, could enforce payment, and could keep a margin. They also
obtained the grand-princely title and, in 1325, persuaded the metropolitan of the
Russian church to move his seat there. A minor town on a minor river became the
centre of the region inside a century, using authority delegated by an overlord.

Halperin's argument is that Russian historiography has systematically understated
this, preferring a narrative of unbroken resistance to a foreign yoke, when the
formative period of the Muscovite state was spent as the Horde's most effective
collaborator, learning administration, taxation and the census from it.

The pattern is familiar from elsewhere on this site. The Samanids, Tahirids and
Ghaznavids all accumulated the substance of independence while holding a formal
grant from a distant overlord, and none of them declared independence either.
What differs here is only that the overlord was not Muslim and the eventual
successor state wrote a history in which the arrangement was purely an
oppression.
