For three decades a Jochid general who was never khan decided who else
would be. Nogai commanded armies under Berke, campaigned into the Caucasus
and Poland, married a Byzantine emperor's own natural daughter, and built,
west of the Dnieper, a personal following large enough that Russian
princes travelled to his camp rather than to Sarai when they needed a
patron. He had put down one khan already before the crisis that made his
name a byword for kingmaking.

## Tulabugha's fall

When Tuda Mangu's short, troubled reign ended around 1287, the succession
went to two joint khans, Tulabugha and Konchek. Nogai, who had helped
install them, went on campaigning at their side for a few years - but the
partnership soured. Tulabugha grew suspicious of the general who had made
him, tried to draw the sons of Möngke Temür into a plot against him, and
in the autumn of 1290 marched an army toward Nogai's own camp meaning to
settle the quarrel by force.

Nogai did not wait to be attacked. He invited Tulabugha to a conference,
had him seized, and had him put to death along with his fellow-khans.
Howorth calls it the first violent succession in the Kipchak khanate's
whole history. In their place Nogai raised Toktu, a son of Möngke Temür,
to the throne - his third royal installation in a row, and the one that
would finally turn on him.

## The patron who would not be summoned

Toktu ruled Sarai for several years as Nogai's grateful client. Then the
relationship curdled the way it had with Tulabugha. Toktu began ignoring
repeated summonses to Nogai's court, and by the mid-1290s outright war had
broken out between the khan on the Volga and the general who had put him
there - the state's two real centres of power, one holding the throne and
the mints, the other holding the largest single army in the west.

The accounts that survive disagree on exactly when it ended, but not on
how: Nogai was defeated and killed, and his family's power was broken with
him. Toktu distributed his dead patron's appanage among his own brothers
and sons rather than let it stand as a rival household, and for the first
time since Batu's own generation, one man held Kipchak without a general
of Nogai's rank able to unmake him.

## The son who tried to finish it

Nogai's surviving son Turai was not finished. In 1301 he persuaded Toktu's
own brother Seraibugha to rebel and recover what their house had lost.
Seraibugha tried to draw a second brother, Buzluk, into the plot; Buzluk
informed Toktu instead, and the conspirators were seized and executed
before they raised a single soldier. It was the last attempt by Nogai's
line to reverse what the war had settled.

## What Toktu left behind

Toktu reigned another decade after that, corresponding with the Ilkhans of
Persia over the long-disputed provinces of Arran and Azerbaijan, marrying
into the Byzantine imperial family as Nogai had before him, and issuing
coin from mints at Sarai, Bolghar, Khwarezm, Crimea and half a dozen other
cities - the clearest surviving evidence of how completely Kipchak now
answered to one court again. He died in 1313, by one account drowned in
the Volga.

The throne did not go to any of Toktu's own three sons. His elder
brother's son Uzbeg, sent as a boy to live among the Circassians so he
could never contend for the succession, was recalled by Toktu himself on
his deathbed in an act of late repentance. Toktu's son Tukel moved to kill
him first; Uzbeg's own retainers struck first instead, and Nogai's old
role - deciding who sat on the Kipchak throne by force rather than
descent - passed, for one last succession, to men loyal not to a general
but to the returning exile himself.
