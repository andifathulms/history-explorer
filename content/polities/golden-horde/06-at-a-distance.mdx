Batu's campaign destroyed the Rus principalities between 1237 and 1240 and took
Kiev. What followed was not occupation. The Mongols withdrew to the steppe, kept
their capital at Sarai on the lower Volga, and governed the Russian lands through
the existing princes — who travelled to Sarai for a *yarlyk*, a patent
confirming their right to rule, and who collected and remitted the tribute
themselves.

It is the same arrangement, arrived at independently, that the Abbasids ended up
with in the east: authority exercised through local rulers who kept their
positions by acknowledging a distant overlord. The difference is that the Horde
could enforce it, and did, with punitive expeditions for two centuries.

The system had a beneficiary the Mongols did not intend. Moscow was an
insignificant town in 1240; its princes made themselves the Horde's preferred
tax collectors for the whole of north-eastern Rus, used the position to absorb
their rivals, and eventually stopped remitting. That the state which became
Russia was assembled inside a Mongol administrative arrangement is Halperin's
central point, and it complicates the national historiography considerably.

## The census

The instrument that made it work was counting people, and the Rus' had never been
counted before.

Mongol officials — the *chislenniki*, the numberers — went through the principalities
household by household in the 1250s, registering everyone liable. It was the same
apparatus applied in China and in Iran, run by the same central administration, and
it produced a tax that was assessed on heads rather than negotiated with princes.
Novgorod resisted it and was made to submit. The count established who owed what,
and it did not have to be repeated often.

Collection was first farmed to Muslim merchants out of Bukhara and Khwarazm, backed
by *baskaks* — resident agents with troops. That arrangement produced the risings of
1262, in which several towns killed the tax farmers, and the Horde drew the correct
conclusion: it was cheaper to let the princes collect.

## What that bought and what it cost

For the Horde, everything in the short run. No garrisons in the forest, no
administration to pay for, no need to keep an army in a country where an army could
not feed itself, and a tribute that arrived on time because the man collecting it
had to answer for the shortfall in person at Sarai.

For the princes, a machine. A prince with the collection rights held other
principalities' money, could compel payment with the Horde's authority behind him,
and could keep the margin. He also acquired the census, the assessment lists, the
idea of a tax that was not negotiated, and the habit of an administration that
worked without consulting the people it worked on.

Every one of those things was still in use in Moscow four centuries later.

## Why the Horde stayed on the steppe

Not from lack of ambition. Because the grass stops.

The Volga and the Don steppe feeds horses. North of the forest line it does not,
and an army that lives on its herds cannot be quartered in Suzdalia through a
winter. The Mongols understood the limit exactly: they took the whole Kipchak
steppe as far as the Danube and settled in it, and treated the forest zone as a
tributary they visited.

The same logic governed everywhere the Mongols went. Where there was pasture they
stayed and ruled directly. Where there was not, they ruled through whoever was
already there. It is the reason the Horde outlasted the Ilkhanate by a century and
a half, and it is also the reason it lost.

Hülegü's people went into Iran, took the cities and the revenue, became Persian and
Muslim, and were gone in eighty years. Jochi's people stayed in the grass, kept the
army and the language and the mobility, and stayed nomads — and a state that never
took over the administration of its richest subject province gave that province a
hundred and fifty years to learn how to run one.

## Uzbeg

The Horde adopted Islam formally under Uzbeg in the 1310s, and became one of the
great Muslim powers — which changed its diplomacy, its script and its law, and did
not change its relationship to Rus' at all. Uzbeg went on issuing patents, and the
metropolitan of the Russian church went on being exempted from tribute by them.

The Horde broke up in the fifteenth century into the khanates of Kazan, Astrakhan
and the Crimea, which the state its own tax system had assembled then took one at a
time.
