What the Mongol advance looked like from the far end of Europe is preserved by a
monk at St Albans who never went further from home than London, and it is a study
in how information and panic travel.

## The herring

Matthew Paris's first Mongol entry is under 1238, and its most striking detail is
economic.

The fishermen of Gotland and Frisia, dreading the Tartars' attacks, did not come to
Yarmouth that year at the herring season, where their ships usually loaded. In
consequence herrings were considered of no value in England on account of their
abundance, and forty or fifty of them, though very good, sold for a single piece of
silver — *even in places at a great distance from the sea*.

An army on the Volga moved the price of fish in the English midlands. It is the
earliest clearly recorded case of that kind of transmission, and it is in a chronicle
written by a man who had no idea that was what he was recording.

## The embassy

The other half of the entry is stranger. Special ambassadors came to the king of
France on behalf of the Saracens, and chiefly on behalf of the Old Man of the
Mountain — the head of the Nizari Isma'ilis, the Assassins — to say that a monstrous
and inhuman race had burst out of the northern mountains and taken the rich lands
of the East, and to ask for help.

A second messenger from the same party came on to the king of England, with the
argument stated plainly: if we cannot withstand them, nothing remains to prevent
their devastating the countries of the West. The chronicler supplies the tag:

> For when your neighbour's house doth burn, the fire will seize on yours in turn.

The chief of the Mongol embassies, the envoys reported, had declared that he was the
messenger of God on high, sent to subdue the nations who rebelled against him.

## The answer

The bishop of Winchester was present, wearing the crusader's cross, and interrupted
the speech to reply — *jocosely*, the chronicler notes:

> Let us leave these dogs to devour one another, that they may all be consumed, and
> perish; and we, when we proceed against the enemies of Christ who remain, will
> slay them, and cleanse the face of the earth, so that all the world will be
> subject to the one Catholic church, and there will be one shepherd and one fold.

Peter des Roches died a few months later, which spared him finding out how the
proposal worked in practice. Within four years the Mongols had destroyed the armies
of Poland and Hungary and reached the Adriatic, and it was the death of a khan in
Mongolia rather than any Christian army that turned them round.

## What Europe thought it was fighting

Paris's 1240 entry shows how little anyone knew and how hard they worked at it.

They burst out of mountain-bound regions, he says, *like demons loosed from
Tartarus, so that they are well called Tartars, as it were inhabitants of
Tartarus* — a false etymology from a real fear, and the reason a Mongol is called a
Tatar in most European languages to this day. Elsewhere he offers a second guess:
that they are named from a river called Tartar that runs through their mountains.

Then the description, which mixes observation with nightmare in a way that is worth
keeping intact. Short and thickset, compact and very strong; invincible in battle
and indefatigable in labour; wearing armour on the front of the body and none on
the back — a detail he repeats twice, and explains as being so that they may not
take to flight. Excellent archers. Swords with one edge. Boats made of ox-hides,
ten or twelve men to one, in which they cross the largest rivers without delay or
trouble. Horses so short in the leg that they are mounted in three steps instead of
with stirrups, and which eat leaves and even the trees themselves. They bring their
herds and their wives, who are brought up to war the same as the men.

And then: that they drink blood from their flocks and consider it a delicacy; that
they eat dogs and human beings; that they know no other language than their own and
no other nation knows it.

The last point is the true one and explains the rest.

> For never till this time has there been any mode of access to them, nor have they
> themselves come forth, so as to allow any knowledge of their customs or persons to
> be gained through common intercourse with other men.

Europe had no information at all. Within eight years it sent a friar to find out,
and he came back with the Great Khan's seal and the demand that the Pope come in
person and submit.
