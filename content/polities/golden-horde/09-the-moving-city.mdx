In 1332 Ibn Battuta reached the camp of Uzbeg Khan on the steppe and found that
it was a city and that it was travelling.

> Here we witnessed a moving city, with its streets, mosques, and cooking-houses,
> the smoke of which ascended as they moved along. When, however, they halted,
> all these became stationary.

He is describing the *ordu* — the khan's household in motion, with the whole
apparatus of a capital carried on wagons: the tents of the wives, the markets,
the mosques, the kitchens, the administration. That is what the Golden Horde
was. Sarai on the Volga was a real city with a mint and quarters for foreign
merchants, but the government was wherever the khan happened to be, and the khan
moved with the seasons.

## Where the khan sat

Ibn Battuta's account of the Friday audience is the fullest description anyone
left of a Chinggisid court in the fourteenth century, and it turns on something
that astonished him.

Uzbeg sat after the Friday prayer under an ornamented pavilion he calls the
golden alcove, on a throne plated with gilded silver and set with jewels. And
his four wives sat on the throne with him.

> Whenever one of these wives enters, he arises, and taking her by the hand,
> puts her into her place upon the throne. Thus they are exposed to the sight of
> all, without so much as a veil.

Beneath the throne stood his two sons, one on each side; in front of him sat his
daughter. Then the great emirs on chairs to right and left, the princes of the
house standing before the king, the sons of the emirs by the door, and the army
officers behind them; and everyone else entering by rank, saluting, and sitting
down at a distance.

For a Maghribi jurist who had spent his life in courts where royal women were
not seen, a khan rising to take his wife by the hand and seat her beside him in
public was the single most remarkable thing in the Kipchak steppe, and he says
so repeatedly. *The wives of this King are highly honoured*, he writes, and each
has her own establishment, and when the sultan wishes to visit one of them he
sends word first and preparation is made.

## One of the seven

Ibn Battuta places Uzbeg in a list, and the list is worth having because it is
how a well-travelled Muslim of the 1330s ranked the world.

The seven great kings: the sultan of the West, the sultan of Egypt and Syria,
the sultan of the two Iraqs, Uzbeg of the Turks, the sultan of Turkestan and
Transoxiana, the sultan of India, and the sultan of China.

Four of those seven were Chinggisid or ruled a Chinggisid successor state. The
Golden Horde is in the list on its own account, not as a remnant of something.

## What the state ran on

Three things, and Ibn Battuta touches all of them.

Islam: Uzbeg's reign is when the khanate becomes definitively Muslim, and the
traveller is received precisely because he is a Maliki scholar and had already
paid his respects to each of the khan's wives.

Trade: the Genoese and Venetian colonies on the Black Sea coast — he passes
through Kaffa, and notes that most of its inhabitants are Christians under the
khan's authority — bought slaves, grain, furs and wax out of the steppe and the
northern forests and shipped them to the Mediterranean. A great many of the
Mamluks who ruled Egypt came out of this trade.

And the Rus. He does not go there and the omission is itself informative: the
Russian principalities were a source of revenue administered at arm's length,
and there was no reason for a khan's guest to visit them.

## The journey he was refused and then allowed

One of Uzbeg's wives was a daughter of the emperor of Constantinople, and in
1334 she was pregnant and asked leave to visit her father, intending to leave
the child there and return.

Ibn Battuta asked to go with her. He was refused — the khan had some fears
respecting him — and got the permission by flattery, promising he would never
appear before her except as the khan's servant and guest.

So a Moroccan jurist travelled from a Mongol camp on the Volga to Constantinople
in the train of a Byzantine princess who was one of a Muslim khan's four wives,
was presented to her father, and came back.

That single journey is the Golden Horde at its height: a state that could put a
Greek princess, a Berber scholar and a Turkic khan in the same sentence without
anybody finding the combination remarkable.
