The Golden Horde was founded on a campaign that took two winters. The fullest
Russian account of the first of them was kept in the one large city that was not
taken, and it opens with a demand.

## The terms

In the winter of 1237 the chronicle of Novgorod records that foreigners *called
Tartars came in countless numbers, like locusts*, into the land of Ryazan,
halted at a river, and sent their emissaries to the princes: a sorceress and two
men with her.

> demanding from them one-tenth of everything: of men and Knyazes and horses — of
> everything one-tenth.

The princes of Ryazan, Murom and Pronsk would not let the envoys into their
towns. They came out to meet them, and answered:

> Only when none of us remain then all will be yours.

That is the whole negotiation. A tithe of the population, the horses and the
ruling family, demanded in advance of any fighting, by three people, of whom the
one the chronicler names first is a woman he calls a sorceress.

## What the Russians did about it

The chronicle is as hard on its own side as on the invaders.

Ryazan sent to Yuri of Vladimir for help, or for him to come himself. *But Yuri
neither went himself nor listened to the request of the Knyazes of Ryazan, but he
himself wished to make war separately.* When a relief force did reach the
Ryazanians at Kolomna it was surrounded and driven against its own ramparts, and
the commanders were killed; and of the contingent from a town that was not yet
important, the chronicler records only that *the men of Moscow ran away having
seen nothing*.

The method is described the same way at every town: the Tartars came, *fenced it
all round with a fence*, and brought up battering rams. Ryazan was invested on 16
December and taken on the 21st. The bishop escaped because he had left at the
moment the troops closed on the town; everybody else, the chronicle says — prince,
princess, men, women, children, monks, nuns and priests — was killed by fire or
by the sword.

At Vladimir the prince's son, his mother, the bishop and the whole province shut
themselves in. When they saw in the morning that the town must fall, the bishop
tonsured them all — the prince, the princess, their daughter and
daughter-in-law, and the good men and women — into the monastic order inside the
cathedral. Then the rams came up, the town was fired, and the family took refuge
in the sacristy; the Mongols broke the doors, piled wood against the church and
burned it with everyone inside.

Torzhok, ninety miles from Novgorod, was fenced round in the same way and held
out under the rams for two weeks. *And the people in the town were exhausted and
from Novgorod there was no help for them.* It fell on 5 March and everyone in it
was killed.

## Why the chronicle exists to say so

Novgorod was not taken. The Mongols turned back short of it — the traditional
explanation is the spring thaw and the marshes — and the city subsequently
submitted, paid, and was counted in the census of 1257 like everywhere else.

So the chronicle that gives the best surviving Russian account of the conquest was
written in the one place that experienced it as a tribute assessment rather than
as a massacre, by clergy whose framework is that this was punishment for sin:
*thus also did God before these men take from us our strength and put into us
perplexity and thunder and dread and trembling for our sins.*

That framework is worth taking seriously rather than deducting. A chronicler who
believes the invasion is a judgement has no motive to conceal how badly his own
princes behaved, and this one does not. The refusal of Vladimir to help Ryazan,
the flight of the Moscow men, the absence of relief for Torzhok — the failures
are all on the Russian side of the page, recorded by a Russian, because in his
scheme they are the evidence of what the judgement was for.

## What the demand established

The tithe was not a war aim invented for Ryazan. It was the standard Mongol
requirement of submission everywhere, and refusing it is what made a town an
enemy rather than a subject.

Two decades later the Horde got its tenth anyway, by census, from a Rus that had
stopped fighting — counted household by household by officials who came north
with an escort, and collected through princes who found it easier to gather the
money themselves than to have the tax farmers do it. The princes who did that
best were the ones in the town whose men had run away having seen nothing.
