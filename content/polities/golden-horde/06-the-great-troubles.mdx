The Russian chronicles have a name for what happened to the Golden Horde after
1359: the *velikaya zamyatnya*, the great troubles. In twenty years there were
something over twenty khans.

## What broke

Uzbeg's son Janibek was murdered by his own son in 1357, and that son was
murdered two years later. After that the throne was decided by whichever
non-Chinggisid strongman could produce a Chinggisid to put on it, and the
strongest of them was Mamai — a commander who was not of the line and therefore
could not take the title himself.

The Black Death had gone through the steppe and the cities in the late 1340s,
arriving in the west, by the best-known account, from the Genoese colony at
Kaffa. Sarai's population and the trade that fed it were both cut.

And the Rus principalities, which had spent a century learning that the khan's
authority was real and unarguable, watched twenty khans in twenty years.

## Kulikovo

In 1380 Dmitry of Moscow met Mamai's army on the Kulikovo field near the Don
and beat it.

Russian tradition has treated that battle as the beginning of liberation ever
since, and it is worth being careful about what it actually settled. Dmitry beat
Mamai, a usurper's general who had no right to the throne. Two years later
Tokhtamysh — a genuine Chinggisid, backed by Timur — took the Horde, marched on
Moscow, took the city by a trick, burned it and massacred the population, and
Dmitry resumed paying tribute.

So Kulikovo did not end the tribute and was not meant to. What it demonstrated
was narrower and, in the long run, more corrosive: that a Russian army could
beat a Horde army in the field.

## Timur

Tokhtamysh then made the mistake of attacking the man who had put him there.

Timur invaded in 1391 and again in 1395, beat him twice, and on the second
occasion did what no Rus prince had ever attempted: he went up the Volga and
destroyed the Horde's own cities. Sarai, Astrakhan, Tana and the towns of the
lower Volga were sacked, and the trade route that ran from the Black Sea through
the steppe to Central Asia was broken.

The khanate never recovered its economy. A state whose revenue came from
transit and tribute lost the transit permanently, in two campaigns, at the hands
of a fellow Muslim who was not even trying to conquer it.

## The pieces

What was left came apart over the next sixty years into successor khanates, each
under its own Jochid line: Kazan on the middle Volga in the 1430s, the Crimea in
the 1440s, Astrakhan at the mouth of the river, the Kazakhs and the Uzbeks in
the east, and the Great Horde in the middle claiming to be the original.

In 1480 Ivan III of Moscow and Akhmat of the Great Horde brought their armies to
opposite banks of the Ugra river, faced each other for several weeks, and both
withdrew without a battle. Russian historiography calls it the end of the yoke.

In 1502 the Crimean khan destroyed the Great Horde, and the state that had
governed Russia from the Volga stopped existing — extinguished by another
Chinggisid, not by the people it had governed.
