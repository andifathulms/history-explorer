The Golden Horde's most significant heir is the state that eventually conquered
its successors, and the inheritance is uncomfortable enough that Russian writing
spent four centuries denying it.

## The yoke

The phrase "the Tatar yoke" is not medieval. It appears in Polish and then
Russian writing much later, and it carries an argument: that the Mongol period
was an interruption, an external oppression suffered by a Russia that was
otherwise continuous with Kiev and with Europe.

There is a competing case, and Halperin's is the sharpest version of it. Moscow
rose because the Horde raised it: the grand-princely patent, the *yarlyk*, was
the khan's to give, and from the 1320s he mostly gave it to Moscow, which then
collected the tribute of the other principalities on his behalf and kept a cut.
A century of doing that made a minor town into the fiscal centre of Russia.

The methods came with the job. The census and the tax registers were Mongol
practice; the postal relay system the Muscovite state ran was the *yam*; a good
deal of the vocabulary of money, customs and horses in Russian is Turkic. And
the political style — an autocracy in which the ruler is not one lord among
others but a sovereign of a different kind — is a great deal closer to Sarai
than to anything in Kievan Rus.

## Why it was denied

Because of what a national history is for. A church-led tradition that had
survived the period by making terms with a pagan and then Muslim overlord had
every reason to describe the whole thing afterwards as a punishment for sin and
a foreign imposition, and no reason at all to describe it as an education.

That framing then hardened into something else. By the nineteenth century the
Mongol period was being used to explain everything about Russia that European
observers found backward, and by Russians to explain why they had been kept out
of the Renaissance while shielding Europe from the steppe.

Both versions are arguments about identity in the writers' own present. The
thing being argued over is a two-hundred-and-fifty-year administrative
relationship that nobody involved at the time described in either set of terms.

## The last khanates

The practical afterlife runs the other way and takes three hundred years.

Ivan IV took Kazan in 1552 and Astrakhan in 1556, which opened the whole Volga
to Moscow and made Russia a multi-confessional empire with a large Muslim
population — the condition it has been in ever since.

The Crimean khanate lasted much longer, under an Ottoman protectorate, raiding
into Russian and Polish territory for slaves on a scale that depopulated the
southern frontier; a Crimean army burned Moscow in 1571. Russia went on making
payments to the Crimea into the eighteenth century, which is to say that
something recognisably descended from the tribute was still being paid four
hundred years after Batu.

Catherine II annexed the Crimea in 1783.

That is the last Chinggisid state anywhere. Five hundred and seventy-seven years
after the assembly on the Onon, a descendant of Jochi lost his throne to a
German-born empress of Russia, and the arrangement that had begun with a winter
campaign across the frozen rivers ended in a treaty.
