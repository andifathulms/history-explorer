A confederation that left almost nothing behind left one thing, and it is the
item it had least control over: a word other people used for it.

The Arabic geographers of the ninth and tenth centuries knew the Haytal. They
knew, correctly, who they had been — in the fifth century AD the chief enemies
of the Sasanian monarchy, the people the Byzantine writers called Ephthalites
and everyone else called the White Huns. That much was still common knowledge
three hundred years after the confederation had been partitioned out of
existence.

But the word had drifted. By the tenth century Haytal had stopped meaning a
particular people and had come to mean, loosely, everything beyond the Oxus: all
the Turanian peoples and all their lands. Muqaddasi uses it that way. The
formal Arabic name for the region was Ma wara al-nahr, That Which Is Beyond the
River, and Haytal was the alternative — an ethnic name turned into a direction.

Le Strange adds a further suggestion in a footnote, and it is worth having as a
suggestion rather than a fact. On the upper Oxus, in the mountains, there was a
district the geographers write variously as Khuttal, Khutlan and Khuttalan.
Timur's historian calls it Khutlan when describing the campaigns there. It
appears, Le Strange says, to be the same word as Haytal.

If he is right, then a people known to us only in six other languages' versions
of their name ended up with the name itself doing two separate jobs: a vague
word for the far side of a river, and a specific mountain district that kept it
for another eight hundred years.

## The country they held

Tokharistan, their centre, went on being a place with an identity long after
they stopped.

It lay east of Balkh, along the south bank of the Oxus as far as Badakhshan,
bounded southward by the ranges north of Bamiyan, and it was divided into an
Upper and a Lower. Muqaddasi and the others name a great many of its towns and
say almost nothing about most of them, so that the majority can no longer be
placed on a map at all: Khulm two days east of Balkh, a small city with a good
climate among large villages; Siminjan, larger, with a Friday mosque and good
fruit, lying in a maze of valleys that had been settled by Arabs of the Tamim;
Baghlan beyond it; and Andarab, with fine markets, in valleys under the
Panjshir range whose recesses held silver mines.

That is what a Chinese envoy's thirty subjugated kingdoms had turned into by the
tenth century: a list of market towns in an Islamic geography, most of which are
now unidentifiable.

## What that adds up to

The Hephthalites had no capital, no script, no chronicle and no king-list that
survives intact. They beat the greatest power in western Asia, took its money,
chose one of its kings, and broke an empire in India, and it took an alliance of
two states to end them.

And what came through is a place-name, a district on the upper Oxus that may or
may not be their name, and a vague Arabic word for everything on the wrong side
of a river.
