title: What a Chinese embassy saw

In 518 the dowager empress of the Northern Wei sent a mission west to collect
Buddhist texts. Song Yun and the monk Huisheng travelled through Tokharistan and
Gandhara and were back in 522, and their account survives in a later compilation.
It is the only description of the Hephthalites by somebody who was there.

What they report is worth setting out because it is the whole of the
ethnography. The king had no walled city and moved with the seasons, living in a
large felt tent — they give its dimensions — with carpets on the floor. He sat on
a golden couch or throne carried on four gold phoenixes. His principal wife wore
a horn-shaped headdress several feet long, covered with pearls, and the other
wives wore smaller ones by rank. The court did not observe Buddhism, and Song
Yun's attempt to interest it in Buddhist doctrine went nowhere; the mission was
received with formal courtesy and no particular deference to the Wei.

They also record that the Hephthalites had subjugated more than thirty
kingdoms, took tribute from all of them, and had no writing.

That last item is the whole difficulty in five words, and it is worth spelling
out what follows from it.

There is no capital, because the king moved with the seasons and no source names
a seat. There is no reliable king-list: the names have to be assembled from
coins, Chinese notices and Sasanian mentions, and they do not match each other.
Nobody can say how one ruler followed another, or how much country the
confederation held, or how many people were in it.

What can be said is how it was paid for and how it fought — tribute, tolls on
what crossed, and levies from the tribes in it — and what it did to two of its
neighbours. It is a power recorded almost entirely through its effects on other
people.

Compare the alternatives. Saba' in South Arabia is known from tens of thousands
of its own inscriptions and no narrative; Greek Bactria from its money; the
Parthians from what their successors decided to leave in. The Hephthalites are
the fourth case and the hardest: described only by the people they frightened,
in six languages, none of which was theirs.
