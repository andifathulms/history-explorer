In 518 the dowager empress of the Northern Wei sent a mission west to collect
Buddhist texts. Song Yun and the monk Huisheng travelled through Tokharistan and
Gandhara and were back in 522, and their account survives in a later compilation.
It is the only description of the Hephthalites by somebody who was there.

What they report is worth setting out because it is the whole of the
ethnography. The king had no walled city and moved with the seasons, living in a
large felt tent — they give its dimensions — with carpets on the floor. He sat on
a golden couch or throne carried on four gold phoenixes. His principal wife wore
a horn-shaped headdress several feet long, covered with pearls, and the other
wives wore smaller ones by rank. The court did not observe Buddhism, and Song
Yun's attempt to interest it in Buddhist doctrine went nowhere; the mission was
received with formal courtesy and no particular deference to the Wei.

They also record that the Hephthalites had subjugated more than thirty
kingdoms, took tribute from all of them, and had no writing.

That last item is the difficulty this whole page runs on and it is worth naming
against what this corpus can do. Almost every field on this record is null. No
capital, because the king moved and the sources do not name a seat this site
would print. No founder and no last ruler, because the king-list is reconstructed
from coins and Chinese notices and Sasanian mentions and the names do not
reliably match between them. No succession rule, no extent, no population, no
influence counts.

What is not null is `tribute`, `trade-toll` and `tribal-levy` — how it was
financed and how it fought — and two turning points about what it did to
somebody else. That is a polity recorded almost entirely through its effects.

The corpus has been here before from the opposite direction. Its Saba' record is
a kingdom known from tens of thousands of its own inscriptions and no narrative;
its Greco-Bactrian record is a kingdom known from its coins; its Parthian record
is one erased by its successors. This is the fourth kind: a power that was
described only by the people it frightened, in six languages, none of which was
its own.
