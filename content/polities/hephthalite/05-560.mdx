title: Two empires at once

In the 550s a new confederation came out of the eastern steppe. The Göktürk
khaganate — this site's Inner Asia record — destroyed the Rouran, took the
Mongolian plateau, and kept moving west along the northern edge of the
Hephthalite territory.

Khusrau I, who had spent his reign rebuilding the Sasanian state on the survey
and the fixed assessment, saw the arrangement available and took it. A Sasanian
alliance with the Türks was agreed, sealed by a marriage, and between roughly
557 and 561 the two powers attacked from opposite sides: the Türks from the
north across the Oxus, the Sasanians from the south-west.

The confederation broke. This record's `ended` field names both conquerors,
because both did it and because neither took the whole: the territory was
partitioned along the Oxus, with Sogdiana and the north going to the Türks and
Tokharistan and the south to the Sasanians. The Hephthalite principalities did
not all disappear — several continued as vassals of one side or the other for
decades — but the tribute stopped and the confederation as a power did not
recover.

This is the only entry in this corpus where two polities jointly destroy a third
and both are in the fields, and it is worth noticing what it took. A steppe
confederation that had killed a Sasanian king, taken his successors' money for a
generation and installed one of them on the throne was ended by a coalition of
the empire it had been extorting and a larger confederation from further east.

The partition did not hold either. Within forty years the Türks and the
Sasanians were fighting each other over the same ground; within eighty the
Sasanian empire was gone and this site's Rashidun record was on the Oxus. What
followed in Tokharistan is a set of local dynasties — the Turk Shahis among them
— governing the Hephthalite territory under whichever larger power was nearest,
until the Arabs and then the Samanids on this corpus's Iranian Intermezzo pages
absorbed it.

What the confederation left is on the previous page and is nearly nothing:
overstruck coins, a handful of Bactrian documents, six versions of a name, and
the reforms a Sasanian king had to make because it beat him.
