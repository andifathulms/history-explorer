title: Two empires at once

In the 550s a new confederation came out of the eastern steppe. The Göktürk
khaganate destroyed the Rouran, took the Mongolian plateau, and kept moving west
along the northern edge of Hephthalite territory.

Khusrau I, who had spent his reign rebuilding the Sasanian state on the survey
and the fixed assessment, saw the arrangement available and took it. A Sasanian
alliance with the Türks was agreed, sealed by a marriage, and between roughly
557 and 561 the two powers attacked from opposite sides: the Türks from the
north across the Oxus, the Sasanians from the south-west.

The confederation broke, and neither of the two who broke it took the whole of
it. The territory was partitioned along the Oxus: Sogdiana and the north to the
Türks, Tokharistan and the south to the Sasanians. The Hephthalite principalities did
not all disappear — several continued as vassals of one side or the other for
decades — but the tribute stopped and the confederation as a power did not
recover.

It is worth noticing what it took to do it. A confederation with no cities, no
writing and no bureaucracy, which had killed a Sasanian king, taken his
successors' money for a generation and installed one of them on his own throne,
was brought down by a coalition of the empire it had been extorting and a larger
confederation from further east. One of them was not enough.

The partition did not hold either. Within forty years the Türks and the
Sasanians were fighting each other over the same ground; within eighty the
Sasanian empire was gone and Arab armies were on the Oxus. What followed in
Tokharistan was a set of local dynasties — the Turk Shahis among them —
governing the old Hephthalite country under whichever larger power was nearest,
until the caliphate and then the Samanids absorbed it.

The confederation itself left almost nothing: overstruck coins, a handful of
Bactrian documents, six versions of a name, and the reforms a Sasanian king had
to carry out because it had beaten him.
