---
title: A name in six languages
drafted_from: litvinsky-1996

The Greeks called them Ephthalitai, the Persians Haytal, the Arabs Hayatila, the
Indians Sveta Huna — white Huns — the Chinese Yeda, and their own coins carry a
Bactrian form usually read as Ebodalo. Every one of those is somebody else's
word, and the question of who they actually were has been argued for a century
and a half without resolution.

The positions are roughly these. They were Huns, part of the same movement out
of the Altai and the Central Asian steppe that produced the European Huns and
the Kidarites before them. They were Iranian-speaking, and the Chinese sources
that describe their language as unlike the Rouran and the Turks are describing an
Iranian one. They were a Turkic group. Or — which is the direction most recent
work goes — the question is badly posed, because a steppe confederation is a
political thing rather than an ethnic one and absorbed whoever joined it.

What is not disputed: from the 440s or thereabouts a confederation with its
centre in Bactria and Tokharistan was the dominant power between the Sasanian
frontier and the Tarim basin, and for about a century it was strong enough to
kill Persian kings and break Indian empires.

It is worth being blunt about why they are usually left out. The history of
Sasanian Iran is normally written as six centuries of war with Rome, because
that is the frontier both sides wrote books about. The eastern frontier produced
no literature at all and produced most of the consequences: it killed a king of
kings, extracted tribute from his successors for a generation, and forced the
fiscal reforms that remade the empire.

What makes them hard to write about is that they left no writing of their own. Coins,
overstruck on Sasanian silver; a few Bactrian documents; seals. Everything else
is Chinese, Byzantine, Syriac, Armenian, Sasanian and Indian — six traditions
writing about an enemy or a curiosity, in six languages, with six sets of
assumptions and six versions of the name.
