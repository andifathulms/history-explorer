While the Hephthalites were taking tribute from Iran they were also going south,
and the second campaign is the one with the larger long-term consequence.

Huna forces crossed into Gandhara and then into the Gangetic plain from the
later fifth century. This corpus's Gupta record ends in 550, and the standard
account has the Huna invasions as a principal cause: Skandagupta beat them off
in the 450s at considerable cost, they came back, and by the early sixth century
Toramana and then his son Mihirakula were ruling from Sakala in the Punjab and
raiding far into the interior. Yashodharman of Malwa defeated Mihirakula around
528 and left an inscription boasting about it, which is most of what is known.

Mihirakula has a bad reputation in two traditions. Indian sources and the Chinese
Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang, writing a century later, describe him as a destroyer
of monasteries and a persecutor of Buddhism on a large scale. Whether that is
what happened, or a memory of one episode generalised, or the standard treatment
a hostile tradition gives an invader, is not settled — and the coinage shows
Huna rulers patronising Shaivite and Buddhist establishments as well.

A caution belongs here that the older literature does not make. Modern
scholarship increasingly separates the group in India — the Alchon, who struck
their own coins and used their own tamghas — from the Hephthalites proper in
Bactria, and treats them as related but distinct successors of the same
movement rather than as one empire operating on two fronts. This page carries
the Indian campaigns because the sources the corpus is drafting from treat them
together, and states the distinction rather than assuming it away.

What is secure enough to record either way is the shape. The same chain of
displacements out of the steppe that this site's Greco-Bactrian record describes
ending Greek Bactria in the second century BC was still running six hundred years
later, and in one century it killed a Sasanian king, put another on his throne,
and broke the last classical empire of northern India.
