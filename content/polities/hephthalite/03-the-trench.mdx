Procopius describes the Hephthalites at some length before he describes what they
did to the Persian king, and the description is worth having first, because it is
a Roman writer taking apart the category he has just put them in.

## Not what the name says

They are Huns in fact as well as in name, he says — and then spends a paragraph
explaining that almost nothing else about them fits.

They do not mingle with the Huns anyone in the Mediterranean knows, because their
land is nowhere near. Their territory lies immediately north of Persia and their
city, Gorgo, sits on the Persian frontier, which is why the boundary is
perpetually disputed. They have never raided Roman territory except alongside a
Persian army.

And then:

> They are not nomads like the other Hunnic peoples, but for a long period have
> been established in a goodly land... they are ruled by one king, and since they
> possess a lawful constitution, they observe right and justice in their dealings
> both with one another and with their neighbours, in no degree less than the
> Romans and the Persians.

*In no degree less than the Romans and the Persians.* A sixth-century
Constantinopolitan is saying that a steppe-descended people on the Oxus kept law
as well as his own empire did. He also notes that they are the only Huns who are
not, to his eye, ugly — which is the kind of remark that tells you what the
default assumption was.

## The companions

The one custom he reports at length is not a legal one.

Wealthy Hephthalites attach to themselves friends — twenty or more — who become
permanent banquet-companions and share in all their property, holding a kind of
common right in it.

> Then, when the man who has gathered such a company together comes to die, it is
> the custom that all these men be borne alive into the tomb with him.

Procopius sets that down two sentences after praising their justice, without
appearing to notice any tension. Both things were true, and he reports both.

## The prostration

Peroz of Persia went to war with them over the frontier and was drawn into a
defile shut in by precipitous mountains and screened by forest, with the
Hephthalites closing the entrance behind him. The Persians saw clearly what
straits they were in and thought the situation hopeless.

The Hephthalite king sent messengers. He upbraided Peroz at length for the
senseless foolhardiness by which he had destroyed himself and his people — and
then offered to let the whole army go, on two conditions: that Peroz prostrate
himself before him as the proven master, and that he swear never to campaign
against the Hephthalites again.

Peroz consulted the Magi with him. Their advice was a piece of theological
engineering: as to the oath, settle it as you please — but Persians prostrate
themselves before the rising sun every day, so meet the Hephthalite king at dawn,
face east, and make the obeisance then.

He did exactly that, gave his pledges, and withdrew *with the whole Median army
intact, gladly*.

## What the ditch was for

Not long afterwards, disregarding the oath, he came back.

He brought every Persian and allied force he could raise, and of his roughly
thirty sons he took all but one — leaving behind only Kavadh, just past boyhood.

The Hephthalites reproached their own king for having let Peroz go. He asked them,
laughing, what of theirs he had abandoned — their land, their arms, anything they
owned? They answered that he had abandoned nothing except the one opportunity on
which everything else depended.

Then he told them to wait, on the grounds that the Persians were still inside
their own borders. And in the plain where the invasion would come, he marked out
a very large tract and dug a deep, wide trench across it — leaving one narrow
strip of ground intact in the middle.

Peroz died on that field with most of his army and most of his sons. The son left
at home became king, and the tribute the Hephthalites then levied on Iran ran
long enough to help produce the fiscal crisis, the Mazdakite convulsion, and the
reforms that rebuilt the Persian state.
