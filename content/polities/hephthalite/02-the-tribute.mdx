---
title: What killing a king of kings was worth
drafted_from: litvinsky-1996
phase: peak
---

Peroz I fought the Hephthalites three times. The first campaign, around 469,
ended with the king himself captured; he was ransomed, left his son Kavad as a
hostage, and undertook not to cross the frontier again. The second ended in a
humiliating settlement. The third, in 484, ended with Peroz dead on the field
with most of his army, in what the sources describe as a trap involving a
concealed ditch.

The consequences on the Sasanian side are on that record: a generation of
tribute, a fiscal crisis, the Mazdakite convulsions, and eventually the reforms
of Kavad and Khusrau I that rebuilt the state. What it did on this side is the
subject of this page's first turning point.

A steppe confederation with no cities acquired a fixed annual income from the
richest state in western Asia. That is an unusual thing to be able to say about
a polity of this kind, and it is why this record's `revenue_basis` field reads
`tribute` — the vocabulary's term for revenue received from another polity, used
here in its most literal application anywhere in this corpus.

It also acquired standing. In 498 Kavad — the same man who had been a hostage —
was deposed by his own nobles and priests, escaped, went to the Hephthalites,
married a woman of their ruling house, and returned with their troops to take
his throne back. He kept it for another thirty-three years and paid for the
loan.

A confederation without a bureaucracy, without a capital and without a written
language was, for about twenty years around the turn of the sixth century,
deciding who ruled Ctesiphon. That is the peak on this record and there is no
better measure of it available, because the fields this corpus uses for extent
and population are both null and no consulted source supplies either.

The coins are the other measure and they say the same thing in metal. Hephthalite
issues are Sasanian drachms overstruck — the defeated empire's own silver, with
the fire altar and the attendants left on the reverse and a new portrait and a
tamgha stamped over the front. A conqueror reissuing his victim's money with his
own head on it is making a public claim, and this record's `legitimation` field
has no value for it.
