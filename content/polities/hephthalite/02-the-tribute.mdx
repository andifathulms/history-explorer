Peroz I fought the Hephthalites three times. The first campaign, around 469,
ended with the king himself captured; he was ransomed, left his son Kavad as a
hostage, and undertook not to cross the frontier again. The second ended in a
humiliating settlement. The third, in 484, ended with Peroz dead on the field
with most of his army, in what the sources describe as a trap involving a
concealed ditch.

The consequences inside Iran were a generation of tribute payments, a fiscal
crisis, the Mazdakite convulsions and eventually the reforms of Kavad and
Khusrau I that rebuilt the state. The consequence on the other side is the one
worth dwelling on.

A steppe confederation with no cities had acquired a fixed annual income from
the richest state in western Asia. Empires normally extract from confederations.
This one paid.

It also acquired standing. In 498 Kavad — the same man who had been a hostage —
was deposed by his own nobles and priests, escaped, went to the Hephthalites,
married a woman of their ruling house, and returned with their troops to take
his throne back. He kept it for another thirty-three years and paid for the
loan.

A confederation without a bureaucracy, without a capital and without a written
language was, for about twenty years around the turn of the sixth century,
deciding who ruled Ctesiphon. That is the height of Hephthalite power, and there
is no better measure of it available: nobody publishes a figure for how much territory the
confederation held or how many people lived in it.

The coins say the same thing in metal. Hephthalite issues are Sasanian drachms
overstruck — the defeated empire's own silver, with the fire altar and its
attendants left standing on the reverse, and a new portrait and a tamgha
hammered over the front. Reissuing your victim's money with your own head on it
is a public statement, and it is not one that any of the usual vocabularies of
royal legitimacy has a word for.
