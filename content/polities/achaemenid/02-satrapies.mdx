title: Satrapies

Cyrus took Media in 549, Lydia in 546 and Babylon in 539, and his successors
added Egypt and the Indus. Within thirty years a minor people of the south-west
Iranian plateau held everything from the Aegean to the Punjab — the first time
one authority had governed the whole Near East.

What made it hold was administrative rather than military. The empire was
divided into satrapies under governors who kept substantial autonomy, connected
by a road system and a relay post, taxed on a fixed assessment, and audited by
travelling inspectors. Local law, local religion and local elites were left in
place: Cyrus is remembered in the Hebrew Bible for returning the Judaeans from
Babylon, and in a Babylonian inscription for restoring Marduk's cult. Aramaic
did the administrative work across the whole empire regardless of what anyone
spoke.

Briant's insistence is worth carrying forward. The Achaemenid empire is
overwhelmingly described in Greek sources written by its enemies, and the
picture of oriental despotism and decadent decline they produced is a literary
construction that the empire's own documentation does not support. It was
conquered by Alexander in three battles, which is a fact about Macedonian
military capacity rather than evidence that it was rotten.

Every later Iranian dynasty claimed it. The Parthians and the Sasanians both
asserted descent or succession from the Achaemenids, and neither was in any
position to be contradicted.
