Darius was not Cyrus's son, and the empire he inherited fell apart the moment he
took it. The account of how he put it back together is his own, cut into a cliff
where nobody could reach it, and its organising idea is not conquest or descent.
It is truthfulness.

## What he says happened

The narrative on the rock begins with a killing inside the royal family, told
flatly:

> Cambyses had a brother, Smerdis by name, of the same mother and the same father
> as he; and afterwards Cambyses slew Smerdis. When Cambyses slew Smerdis, the
> people knew not that Smerdis was slain. Thereupon Cambyses went into Egypt.

Then the consequence, in the sentence that supplies the inscription's whole
vocabulary:

> Then the people became hostile, and the Lie multiplied in the lands, even in
> Persia, as in Media, and in the other provinces.

A magus named Gaumata, Darius says, rose claiming to be the murdered brother, and
was believed — because the murder had been secret, and there was nobody to say
otherwise. Cambyses died. The imposter held the empire. Darius killed him, and
then had to fight everyone else.

## Nineteen battles

The scale of the collapse is the part usually skipped, and Darius states it as a
total:

> After I became king, I fought nineteen battles.

Nineteen battles and nine captured kings, in something close to a single year,
across Persia, Media, Elam, Babylonia, Armenia, Parthia, Margiana and Sagartia.
Every province of the empire Cyrus and Cambyses had assembled either rose or
produced a claimant.

This is not the record of a usurper covering his tracks. It is a man admitting
that the empire he claims to have inherited had, in fact, comprehensively refused
him — and then arguing that putting it down again is what makes the claim good.

## Why it is all about lying

The word that does the work throughout is *drauga*, the Lie, and it is doing
theology rather than ethics.

Every rebel is a liar. Gaumata lied when he said he was Smerdis; each pretender
lied when he said he was of the old royal line; the provinces went over because
the Lie multiplied among them. Against that, Darius's qualification is stated as
a negative:

> because I was not wicked, nor was I a liar, nor was I a tyrant

And the instruction to whoever comes after is the same one:

> Thou who shalt be king hereafter, beware of lies; the man who is a liar, destroy
> him.

In Zoroastrian terms this is not a figure of speech. Truth and the Lie are the
two principles the world is built out of, and a king who is on the side of the
first is doing cosmology by putting down rebels. It converts nineteen battles
against his own subjects into a religious duty, and it does so without ever
claiming that Darius was the eldest son of anybody.

## What the argument was for

Cyrus took the empire and did not have to explain himself. Darius took it from
the inside, at speed, against nine other claimants, and needed a doctrine.

What he produced is the ideological apparatus every later Achaemenid inscription
runs on: a king made by Ahuramazda rather than by birth, whose legitimacy is
demonstrated by success and whose enemies are liars by definition. It outlasted
him by two centuries.

And he had it cut a hundred metres up a cliff on the road from Babylon to
Ecbatana, in three languages, above a road everyone used — and then had the ledge
below it cut away, so that nobody could get close enough to read it, or to damage
it.
