Persia in 560 BC was a kingdom in the south-west of the Iranian plateau,
subordinate to the Medes, and of no consequence to anybody writing anything
down. Within thirty years it held the Near East.

Cyrus II took Media around 550 — the Median king's own army is said to have
handed him over, which is either propaganda or the ordinary way a subordinate
dynasty replaces its overlord. He took Lydia and the Anatolian coast around
546, defeating Croesus. In 539 he took Babylon, and the manner of it is the
thing worth stopping on.

Babylon was the greatest city in the world and it did not fight. Cyrus entered
after a battle at Opis, kept the priesthoods and the temple administration
intact, restored cults the previous king had neglected, and had himself
proclaimed in Babylonian terms as the man Marduk had chosen to restore order.
The Cyrus Cylinder, a foundation deposit in Akkadian recording this, is a
document of exactly that kind: a conqueror speaking to a conquered elite in its
own formulas.

<Figure id="cyrus-cylinder" />

It is also, in modern usage, routinely described as the first charter of human
rights, and it is worth saying plainly that it is not. It is a building
inscription in a long Mesopotamian genre, and every claim in it — restoring
temples, returning displaced gods, releasing captives — appears in earlier
Assyrian and Babylonian examples of the same genre. What is true, and more
interesting, is the policy it describes: the Achaemenids governed by leaving
local institutions in place and speaking to each population in its own
language, and they did it consistently for two centuries.

The Hebrew Bible's account of Cyrus authorising the return from the Babylonian
exile and the rebuilding of the temple is part of the same policy and,
unusually for this empire, is a Persian action recorded by beneficiaries rather
than enemies. It is most of the reason a Persian king has a positive reputation
in the Western tradition at all, and the next chapter is about why nothing else
does.
