For roughly two thousand three hundred years the Achaemenid empire had no voice
of its own. Everything anybody could read about it had been written by Greeks.
The empire's own account of itself was cut into the face of a near-vertical
rock a hundred metres above the plain, on the high road from Babylon to
Ecbatana, in plain sight of everyone who used the road, and nobody alive could
read a word of it. Travellers sketched the sculptures in the seventeenth and
eighteenth centuries and guessed; one of them decided the figures were Jews,
because the writing on their garments reminded him of phylacteries.

Darius had it carved shortly after 520 BC: the relief of the king with his foot
on a fallen pretender and nine rebels roped at the neck, and beneath and beside
it the text of the year in which he took the empire back, in Old Persian, in
Elamite and in Babylonian. Three versions of one statement, which is the
condition of a bilingual key. It had been waiting on the rock for the whole of
the interval, and travellers had been walking under it and guessing.

## Getting it down

The man who took it down was Henry Rawlinson, a lieutenant in the Indian
service who had been sent to Persia in 1833 to help train the Shah's army and
was posted to Kermanshah, twenty miles from the cliff, in 1835. He had already
worked out the values of many Old Persian signs from shorter inscriptions on
Mount Elwend, near Hamadan. Behistun gave him a long text to test them on.

The difficulty was not scholarly. The Persian columns sit on a foot-ledge about
eighteen inches wide. He described the working position himself: a ladder too
short to be given any slope, so that the upper lines could only be copied
standing on the topmost step, steadying his body against the rock with his left
arm, the notebook in his left hand and the pencil in his right. He adds that the
interest of the occupation entirely did away with any sense of danger, which is
the sort of thing men wrote in the 1840s and is also, on the evidence of what he
did next, true.

The Elamite version is in a recess set further back, and reaching it meant
bridging a gap over a sheer drop. His first attempt was made with a ladder laid
on its side; the bars, which Persian ladder-makers fitted into their sockets
without clenching them outside, worked loose under his weight, and the lower
half of the ladder went over the precipice while he hung from the upper half
until his friends pulled him in.

The Babylonian text — the one that mattered most, because Babylonian was the
language of the entire Mesopotamian written record — was on an overhanging mass
of rock that the local cragsmen, who tracked goats across the whole face of the
mountain, said could not be reached at all. In 1847 a Kurdish boy who had come
from a distance volunteered. He worked himself up a cleft to the left of the
projection, drove in a peg, tied a rope, failed to swing across, and then
crossed twenty feet of smooth perpendicular rock on his fingers and toes.
From the far cleft he could swing out over the overhang, and there he rigged a
short ladder into a hanging seat like a painter's cradle and, sitting in it,
took the paper squeeze of the Babylonian version under Rawlinson's direction
from below.

Rawlinson's own note on that squeeze is the reason to tell the story. He thought
the block it came from was doomed: water trickling from above had almost
separated the overhanging mass from the rest of the rock, and its weight
threatened shortly to bring it down into the plain in a thousand pieces.

## What it opened

Rawlinson published his memoir on the Persian text in 1847, the first correct
translation of a long Old Persian inscription; the conclusions he reached then
are still accepted with only small modifications. Edwin Norris took the Elamite.
Rawlinson took the Babylonian himself, and Babylonian is the door to Assyria,
to Babylon, to Sumer — to the entire cuneiform world.

So the reading of the ancient Near East was unlocked by a Persian king's
account of how he had put down nine rebellions, which he had carved on a
mountain because he wanted it to last.

It nearly did not. The paper squeezes were exhibited in London, handled by
scholars for decades, and crumbled; the inscription on the rock was perishing at
the same time. In 1904 the British Museum sent L. W. King and R. C. Thompson to
Behistun to collate the whole thing again on the cliff itself, and their edition
is why the text can be quoted with confidence now.

Two collations, sixty years apart, both made by men on ledges. The empire that
the Greeks described as an oriental despotism ruling slaves had left an account
of itself in its own voice all along. It took until 1847 for anyone to hear it,
and the sentence standing at the head of every section is the same one: *thus
saith Darius the king*.
