Almost everything commonly known about this empire comes from people it was at
war with. Herodotus, Xenophon, Ctesias and the Alexander historians supply the
narrative; the empire's own archives survive as administrative tablets from
Persepolis, royal inscriptions, and scattered documents in Aramaic and
Babylonian, none of which tells a story.

<Figure id="ac-duel" />

The consequences run deep enough to be worth naming. The Greek tradition
produced a picture of oriental despotism — a king ruling slaves rather than
subjects, a court of eunuchs and harem intrigue, an empire in long decline from
Darius onward, ripe for Alexander. Briant's reconstruction from the
administrative record gives something else: a functioning tax and audit system,
governors who were checked and rotated, garrisons paid on time, and a
Babylonian and Egyptian documentary trail that shows no collapse before the
conquest.

The decline narrative is the most durable of these inheritances, and it is
circular. The empire fell, therefore it must have been failing. But it lost
three battles to an exceptional army and a commander who took every risk
available, and its administrative machinery was intact enough that Alexander
and then the Seleucids simply carried on using it, satrapy by satrapy.

Even the modern measurements inherit the problem. The standard figures for the
empire's extent are computed from maps that descend, ultimately, from the same
Greek geography. They are properly sourced and they are still a Greek estimate
of a Persian empire.
