Everybody knows that Xerxes had the sea whipped. It is the standard image of
oriental despotism, and it comes from Herodotus, who is hostile. What is less
often noticed is that the same passage, a few lines later, contains one of the
best engineering descriptions to survive from antiquity — and that it is about
the same project.

## The first attempt

Two bridges were begun from Abydos across to a headland on the European side,
seven furlongs of water. The Phoenicians built one with cables of white flax; the
Egyptians built the other with papyrus rope. Two crews, two materials, working in
competition.

A storm came on and smashed both.

## What Xerxes did about it

Herodotus's account is precise about the punishment, and reports it at one remove
in places, which is his way of flagging that he is passing on what he was told.

The Hellespont was to receive three hundred strokes of the lash, and a pair of
fetters was to be let down into the sea. He had also heard, he says, that branders
were sent to brand it. And the men doing the beating were to say this:

> Thou bitter water, thy master lays upon thee this penalty, because thou didst
> wrong him not having suffered any wrong from him: and Xerxes the king will pass
> over thee whether thou be willing or no; but with right, as it seems, no man
> doeth sacrifice to thee, seeing that thou art a treacherous and briny stream.

Then he had the heads cut off the men who had been in charge of the bridging.

Herodotus calls the words barbarian and presumptuous, and he is writing for
Athenians who had recently been invaded. Whatever happened at the water's edge,
the executions are the part that reads as institutionally true: a project failed,
and the responsible officers were killed for it.

## The second attempt

And then new chief-constructors were appointed, and Herodotus describes exactly
what they did, in the tone of a man who has taken the trouble to find out.

They assembled penteconters and triremes as pontoons — three hundred and sixty
under the bridge on the Black Sea side, three hundred and fourteen under the
other. The hulls were laid in the direction of the Hellespont's current, so that
they took the tension of the cables rather than fighting the flow. Very large
anchors went down on both sides: on the Pontus side against the winds that blow
outward from within, and on the western side against the south-east and south
winds. And they left an opening, so that shipping could still pass.

Six hundred and seventy-four vessels, requisitioned, positioned, moored against
two different prevailing wind systems, and detailed to hold cable tension — with
a navigation gap engineered into it.

## The two halves of the story

Both halves are Herodotus and both are in the same chapter, and it is worth
resisting the urge to pick one.

An empire that can whip the sea in a fit of temper and behead its engineers is
also an empire that can, immediately afterwards, put a second and better bridge
across a strait using six hundred and seventy-four ships from its own fleet, on
schedule, in a way that accounts for the local winds.

The whipping is what the Greeks remembered because it explained the Persians to
them. The anchors are what the Persian empire actually was — and the reason the
invasion reached Greece at all was the second thing, not the first.
