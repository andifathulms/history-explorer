---
title: Not in decline
drafted_from: briant-2002
phase: contraction
---

The standard account of the fourth century BC has the Achaemenid empire in long
decay — a succession of weak kings, palace intrigue, satraps in revolt, and a
structure so hollow that Alexander pushed it over. Briant's book is, among other
things, six hundred pages of argument against that, and this site's page follows
him because the alternative is to record a Greek literary convention as a fact.

The evidence usually offered is real and reads differently in sequence.

Egypt revolted successfully in 404 and stayed independent for sixty years. That
is a serious loss — the richest province in the empire — and it is also a loss
the empire kept trying to reverse and did reverse, in 343, at the fourth attempt.
An empire that recovers its wealthiest province after six decades of trying is
not decaying; it is slow.

The satrapal revolts of the 360s were serious and were also, on examination,
less coordinated than the Greek accounts suggest and were put down. Artaxerxes
III, who is generally the villain of the decline narrative, reconquered Egypt,
reduced Phoenicia and Cyprus, and left the empire larger than he found it.

The dynastic violence was real and is not evidence of institutional failure.
This corpus records violent successions in almost every polity it carries; the
Ottomans institutionalised fratricide and lasted six hundred years.

And the documentary record does not show collapse. The Babylonian business
archives run continuously through the fourth century; the Egyptian and Bactrian
material shows administration functioning; there is no fiscal crisis visible
anywhere.

What defeated the empire was an army. Macedonian infantry with a long pike in
depth, supported by heavy cavalry used as a shock arm and commanded by a man who
took risks that ought to have killed him, was the best military instrument in the
world for about twenty years, and the Achaemenids met it three times. The
argument that they lost because they were rotten is circular — they fell,
therefore they must have been falling — and this site's aside on the sources is
about how that reasoning got into the record.
