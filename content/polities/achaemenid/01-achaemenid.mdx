Cyrus took Media in 549, Lydia in 546 and Babylon in 539, and his successors
added Egypt and the Indus. Within thirty years a minor people of the south-west
Iranian plateau held everything from the Aegean to the Punjab — the first time
one authority had governed the whole Near East.

What made it hold was administrative rather than military. The empire was
divided into satrapies under governors who kept substantial autonomy, connected
by a road system and a relay post, taxed on a fixed assessment, and audited by
travelling inspectors. Local law, local religion and local elites were left in
place: Cyrus is remembered in the Hebrew Bible for returning the Judaeans from
Babylon, and in a Babylonian inscription for restoring Marduk's cult. Aramaic did
the administrative work across the whole empire regardless of what anyone spoke.

Briant's insistence is worth carrying into the rest of this site: the Achaemenid
empire is overwhelmingly described in Greek sources written by its enemies, and
the picture of oriental despotism and decadent decline they produced is a
literary construction that the empire's own documentation does not support. It
was conquered by Alexander in three battles, which is a fact about Macedonian
military capacity rather than evidence that it was rotten.

Every later Iranian dynasty on this site claims it. The Parthians and Sasanians
both asserted descent or succession from the Achaemenids, and those are the two
successor claims counted on this page.
