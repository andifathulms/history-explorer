Between 1933 and 1934 an excavation at Persepolis found tens of thousands of
clay tablets in the fortification wall of the terrace, written in Elamite, and
a smaller archive in the treasury. They are the empire's own paperwork, and
they are the reason a modern account of it can be written at all.

<Figure id="fortification-tablets" />

They are not a narrative. They are travel authorisations, ration issues,
livestock accounts, receipts for grain and wine and beer and flour, records of
workers paid in kind, and orders for the supply of parties moving on the royal
roads. There is no history in them and no ideology. What there is instead is
what a functioning imperial administration leaves behind when nobody is
composing anything.

What they establish is worth listing, because each item contradicts something
in the Greek tradition. A road system with authorised stages at which named
officials issued fixed rations against sealed documents. Workers of many
nationalities — Egyptians, Ionians, Lycians, Bactrians — paid in commodities at
graded rates, women among them, some receiving supplements for childbirth. A
treasury converting rations to silver at fixed rates from the 490s onward,
which is a monetising economy rather than a static tribute-collecting one.
Royal women, Artystone among them, running estates and issuing orders in their
own names on their own seals.

None of that is compatible with an empire of slaves ruled by whim, and it is
also not the sort of evidence that produces a story. This is the general
problem with the Achaemenids: the documentation and the narrative come from
opposite directions and do not meet. The tablets tell you how the empire worked
and nothing about what happened. The Greeks tell you what happened and are
wrong about how it worked.

The tablets are also the reason anything can be said about how the empire was
actually financed and manned — a land tax and tolls on movement, a professional
core with levies behind it, and a succession the king designated. Every one of
those comes out of the administrative record rather than out of Herodotus,
which is the only reason to trust them.
