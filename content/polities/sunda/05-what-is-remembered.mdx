Sunda ended in 1579 and the Sundanese did not.

They are the second largest ethnic group in Indonesia, something like forty
million people, with their own language, their own literature, their own
gamelan tuning and their own account of what happened to them. That account is
organised around two things on this record, and neither of them is the fall of
the capital.

## Bubat

The incident of 1357 — a Sundanese royal party killed at Bubat after Majapahit's
chief minister received them as tribute-bearers rather than as guests — is
recorded on this record as a turning point and on this corpus's Majapahit record
as one too, from the other side, where it is noted that the memory of it kept
Sunda and Java apart.

It kept them apart for a long time. There is no street named for Gajah Mada in
Bandung. There is a tradition, variously observed and variously denied, against
Sundanese women marrying Javanese men. Sundanese and Javanese accounts of Bubat
differ about who was at fault in ways that map exactly onto which side is telling
it.

A fourteenth-century diplomatic humiliation is a live subject. This site has no
measure for that and this corpus's Kediri and Sunda records have both now had to
say so about something.

## Siliwangi

The other is the legend on this record's third chapter: the king who is said not
to have died but to have withdrawn, with his people, into the forest, and to be
there still.

A kingdom whose end nobody can date precisely, whose last ruler this site cannot
name, and whose people were converted by the polity that destroyed it produced a
story in which the king refused to convert and refused to die, and left.

That is not evidence about 1579. It is evidence about what being conquered by
your neighbours does to what you tell your children, and it is the kind of thing
a site organised around spans, capitals, institutions and influence counts will
record in a `turning_point` typed `siege` and nowhere else.
