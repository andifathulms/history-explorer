The Indic kingdoms of Java went down within a few decades of each other.
Majapahit was absorbed by Demak by 1527; the Hindu-Buddhist courts of the
eastern salient fell to Trenggana's campaigns in the 1540s.

Sunda lasted until 1579.

It held the western third of the island — the Sundanese highlands and the coast
on both the Java Sea and the Sunda strait — through the whole Islamisation of
the rest of it. And it stood outside the dynastic chain that runs down the
Brantas: Kediri, Singhasari, Majapahit, Demak, Pajang, Mataram, seven hundred
years of kingdoms each replacing the last, all of them east of the Citarum
river.

Sunda is the other Java.

## Where it starts, and how firmly

669 is the date the Sundanese chronicle tradition gives for the transition from
Tarumanagara, and it is worth saying plainly what that is worth.

It is a traditional date, not a documented one. Inscriptional attestation of
Sunda's kings does not begin until the eleventh century, and the capital the
kingdom is best known by is fifteenth-century. Anybody insisting on firm
attestation would begin the kingdom around 1030 and lose three and a half
centuries that the sources describe less firmly but do describe.

Both numbers mislead in different directions. 669 implies a continuity nobody
can demonstrate; 1030 implies an absence nobody has evidence for. What the
tradition here has going for it — and this is unusual in the archipelago — is
that the Sundanese wrote their own past down, which the next chapter is about.
