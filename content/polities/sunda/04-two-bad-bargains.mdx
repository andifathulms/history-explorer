Sunda's two most consequential decisions were both attempts to deal with a
stronger neighbour, and both made things worse.

## Bubat, 1357

A Sundanese king brought his daughter to Java for a marriage to Hayam Wuruk of
Majapahit. Gajah Mada, Majapahit's chief minister, received the party as
tribute-bearers rather than as guests. The Sundanese refused the terms and were
killed on the field at Bubat, the princess with them.

The memory of it kept Sunda and Java apart for centuries, and it is still live
in West Java. The tradition of not naming a street after Gajah Mada, and of not
marrying Sundanese women to Javanese men, is something people will tell you
about in connection with a fourteenth-century diplomatic incident.

## The padrão, 1522

Two centuries later, with the Muslim ports of the north coast expanding and
Demak taking everything east of it, Sunda signed a treaty with the Portuguese
at Melaka: a fort at Sunda Kelapa, in exchange for protection. A stone pillar
went up to mark it.

It is the only case in the archipelago of a polity inviting the Portuguese in
as protection against its neighbours, and the calculation was not stupid. What
it produced was the opposite of what it was for: a Demak expedition took the
port in 1527 before the Portuguese could occupy it, and renamed it Jayakarta.

That name is the one it still has. The Dutch company took the place in 1619 and
called it Batavia for three hundred years, and when the name went back it went
back to the one given by the force that arrived in 1527 to stop this kingdom
selling the harbour.

## 1579

Banten — the sultanate that grew out of that same 1527 expedition — destroyed
Pakuan Pajajaran, the inland capital.

Fifty-two years after Majapahit and at the other end of the island, the last
Indic kingdom on Java ended, and the Sundanese highlands were Muslim within a
generation.
