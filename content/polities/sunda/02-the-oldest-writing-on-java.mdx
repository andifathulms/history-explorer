The earliest inscriptions on Java are not Javanese. They are from western Java,
in Sanskrit, from the polity called Tarumanagara, some time around the fifth
century — stone slabs by rivers recording a king named Purnawarman, one of them
with a pair of footprints carved into it and an inscription declaring them the
feet of the ruler, likened to Vishnu's.

Tarumanagara is not a record on this site. Sunda is what came after it on the
same ground, and this record's traditional start date of 669 is roughly where the
Sundanese chronicles put the transition.

## The Sundanese written record

What Sunda produced afterwards is unusual for the archipelago in one specific way: a
body of writing in the vernacular, on palm leaf, about the kingdom's own past and
its own geography.

The *Carita Parahyangan* is a dynastic chronicle. *Bujangga Manik* is the account
of a Sundanese ascetic's journeys across Java, and it names hundreds of places,
roads and boundaries with enough precision that it functions as a
fifteenth-century itinerary of the island. The *Sanghyang Siksakandang
Karesian*, from 1518, is a manual of what an educated person ought to know.

None of it is coded anywhere on this record. `descendant_scripts` reads zero,
because the Old Sundanese script is a Kawi derivative and Kawi's own parent is a
South Indian Brahmi with no page here — a count that goes two steps up a chain
and falls off the end of it.

This corpus's Kediri record's second chapter is about exactly this problem from
the Javanese side: a kingdom whose bequest was a literature, three influence
counts reading zero, and the argument that the counts were chosen so they could
be applied uniformly to two hundred records and that literature cannot be counted
that way without becoming an opinion wearing a number.

Two records, on two halves of one island, in the same position for the same
reason.

## What it lets this record do that others cannot

One thing, and it is worth having.

Almost every polity in the archipelago is described from outside — by Chinese
notices, Portuguese and Dutch reports, Arab geographers, or by a court chronicle
written after the fact by the successor. This corpus's Melaka record is drafted
from a chronicle composed at Johor a century after the city fell; its Ternate and
Tidore records are built largely on Iberian and Dutch documents.

Sunda has a fifteenth-century description of Java written by a Sundanese for
Sundanese readers, in which Java is a place with roads rather than a coastline
with ports. That is a rare thing and it is the reason this record's span is
allowed to start at a traditional date: the tradition here has a written record
of its own behind it.
