The earliest inscriptions on Java are not Javanese. They are from western Java,
in Sanskrit, from the polity called Tarumanagara, some time around the fifth
century — stone slabs by rivers recording a king named Purnawarman, one of them
with a pair of footprints carved into it and an inscription declaring them the
feet of the ruler, likened to Vishnu's.

Sunda is what came after Tarumanagara on the same ground, and 669 is roughly
where the Sundanese chronicles put the transition.

## The Sundanese written record

What Sunda produced afterwards is unusual for the archipelago in one specific
way: a body of writing in the vernacular, on palm leaf, about the kingdom's own
past and its own geography.

The *Carita Parahyangan* is a dynastic chronicle. *Bujangga Manik* is the
account of a Sundanese ascetic's journeys across Java, and it names hundreds of
places, roads and boundaries with enough precision that it functions as a
fifteenth-century itinerary of the island. The *Sanghyang Siksakandang
Karesian*, from 1518, is a manual of what an educated person ought to know.

The Old Sundanese script these were written in is a Kawi derivative, and Kawi
descends in turn from a South Indian Brahmi. Sunda invented nothing; it
inherited a writing system two removes down a chain that began in India, and
then used it for something the rest of the archipelago mostly did not — writing
about itself, in its own language, for its own readers.

Kediri, on the other half of the island, is in exactly the same position: a
kingdom whose real bequest was a body of literature, and no way to weigh that
against a conquest or a coastline.

## What that makes possible here

One thing, and it is worth having.

Almost every polity in the archipelago is described from outside — by Chinese
notices, Portuguese and Dutch reports, Arab geographers, or by a court
chronicle written after the fact by whoever won. Melaka is known through a
chronicle composed at Johor a century after the city fell. Ternate and Tidore
are known largely through Iberian and Dutch paperwork.

Sunda has a fifteenth-century description of Java written by a Sundanese for
Sundanese readers, in which Java is a place with roads rather than a coastline
with ports. That is rare enough to be worth the whole chapter, and it is why
the traditional dating deserves more patience here than it would elsewhere: the
tradition has a written record of its own behind it.
