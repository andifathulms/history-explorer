By the 1320s a distinct religious body calling itself the Bosnian Church
had emerged from the communities whose priors had renounced their
errors at Bilino Polje a century earlier, governed by its own bishop, the
djed - literally "grandfather" - rather than by Rome or Constantinople.
What kind of church it actually was became, and long remained, one of
the most contested questions in Balkan religious history.

## Manichees, in the pope's own words

The dominant scholarly view for most of the twentieth century traced
directly back to Pope Pius II, writing in the 1450s and 1460s, who
described the Bosnian Church's members as Manichees - dualists holding
that the material world and the human body were the creation of an evil
principle, in the same broad family as the Bogomils of Bulgaria and the
Cathars of southern France. Pius's account was copied into Renaissance
chronicles and, from there, into most of the standard histories written
over the following four centuries. It rests almost entirely on papal and
inquisitorial documents - sources with an obvious institutional interest
in describing the Bosnian Church as heretical rather than merely
schismatic.

## Orthodox in all but name

A rival view, argued most forcefully by Serbian scholars, holds that the
Bosnian Church was never dualist at all - that it differed from
mainstream Serbian Orthodoxy only in having its own separate hierarchy,
and that its members held entirely conventional Christian beliefs. This
reading draws on a different body of evidence: Bosnian domestic charters
and Ragusan diplomatic correspondence, in which members of the
Bosnian Church appear observing standard Christian practices with no
trace of dualist doctrine. Set against this is the inconvenient fact that
Serbian sources themselves - Church synod records and royal biographies
- also brand the Bosnian Church heretical, which is difficult to square
with a reading that makes it simply an unauthorized branch of Orthodoxy.

## A will and a list of articles that cannot both be telling the same story

The starkest illustration of the impasse comes from two documents
written within five years of each other in the 1460s. Cardinal
Torquemada drew up fifty specific articles of dualist belief for three
Bosnian "Manichees" to renounce in 1461. In 1466, the Bosnian Church
leader Gost Radin wrote a will that contradicts several of those very
articles and shows no trace of a dualist worldview at all. Both documents
are genuine. Both describe, or claim to describe, the same religious body.
Reconciling the standard dualist and Orthodox-adjacent readings requires
discarding one or the other set of sources on no better grounds than that
it is inconvenient - and no one has yet managed to justify doing so.
What both extremes obscure is a body that held its own religious life
apart from both great churches without necessarily matching either
side's polemical description of it, shaped as much by Bosnia's own
mountainous isolation and its rulers' own political convenience as by any
settled theology at all.
