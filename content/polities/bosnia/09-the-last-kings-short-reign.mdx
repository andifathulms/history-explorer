King Stefan Tomaš died in July 1461, possibly of an illness he had
already sought Ragusan medical help for that same June, though a story
soon circulated - and found its way into several Franciscan chronicles -
that he had been murdered on the orders of his own brother with his
son's knowledge. Whatever the truth, the son, Stefan Tomašević,
succeeded him and immediately sought what his father, out of fear of
provoking the Ottomans, had once refused: a royal crown from Rome.

## A king eager to prove his own faith

Tomašević's own embassy to Pope Pius II in 1461 stressed, according to
the pope's own later account, that the new king had been baptized as a
boy, had learned Latin letters, and firmly grasped the Catholic faith - a
pointed contrast with his father, described as a recent convert who had
not yet expelled the "Manichees" from the kingdom. A papal legate
crowned Tomašević at Jajce on 17 November 1461, a coronation that
marked, in its own way, the furthest point Catholicism ever reached in a
kingdom whose religious loyalties had been contested for two and a half
centuries.

## An invasion meant to finish the question for good

The crown bought Tomašević less than two years. In 1462 Vladislav,
son of the rival magnate Herceg Stefan, revolted again against his own
father and, unable to exploit an existing war as he had a decade earlier,
turned to the Ottomans for help. This time the sultan came in his own
interest, not as anyone's mercenary ally, and the invasion of 1463 aimed
at ending Bosnian independence outright rather than merely raiding it.
Bosnian fortresses fell in rapid succession. Tomašević fled toward
Croatia with an Ottoman company in close pursuit, was cornered at the
fortress of Ključ on the Sana river, and surrendered it only after being
promised safe passage - a promise broken as soon as he was in Ottoman
hands. He was brought back to Jajce, beheaded, and buried on a nearby
hill from which his own castle could not be seen.

## Scapegoats for a fall nobody could otherwise explain

The speed of the collapse - most of the kingdom fell within weeks -
stunned contemporaries enough that stories of betrayal sprang up almost
immediately, several of them blaming the Bosnian Church specifically.
The most famous names a supposed Patarin, "Radak Manichee," who is
said to have betrayed the fortress of Bobovac to the Ottomans; the story
appears in Pope Pius's own memoirs and was repeated in chronicles for
centuries afterward, surviving even into local Bobovac folklore. Sources
closer to the actual fighting, however - the Ottoman chronicler
Dursun Beg and the soldier-memoirist Konstantin Mihailović among
them - describe Bobovac falling only after real resistance, and no other
source corroborates Radak's existence at all. The betrayal legends say
less about what actually happened at Bobovac than about how urgently a
shocked kingdom needed someone to blame for a collapse that poor noble
cooperation and low morale explain considerably better than treachery
does.
