Stjepan Kotromanić, ban from around 1318 to 1353, left Bosnia stronger
and better connected to its neighbors than he found it, but it was his
successor Tvrtko who turned a banate into something considerably larger.
Tvrtko's own claim rested on a genealogical accident: the extinction of
Serbia's ruling Nemanjić dynasty, combined with his own descent, several
generations back, from the Nemanjić king Stefan Dragutin through a
daughter's marriage into the Kotromanić line.

## Crowned by a church he did not himself belong to

In 1377, at the monastery of Mileševo, Tvrtko had himself crowned King
of Raška and Bosnia - by an Orthodox metropolitan, despite Tvrtko's own
Catholic leanings and active relations with the Roman church throughout
his reign. It was a coronation of striking religious pragmatism: a
Catholic-aligned ruler accepting the crown of a defunct Orthodox
kingdom from an Orthodox cleric, apparently without anyone involved
regarding the arrangement as contradictory. Three years earlier, Tvrtko
had married Dorothea, an Orthodox princess from Vidin, in a Catholic
ceremony performed by his own longstanding rival, the Catholic Bishop
of Bosnia - whom Tvrtko rewarded afterward with a substantial land
grant, evidently bearing no grudge for their earlier disputes.

## A kingdom that reached the Adriatic

Tvrtko held the title King of Raška and Bosnia from 1377 until his death
in 1391, and he used the years of Hungarian civil war that followed to
expand well beyond his own inherited territory. Taking the side opposed
to Sigismund of Luxemburg in the contest for the Hungarian throne, he
extended his own influence along most of the Dalmatian coast, becoming
overlord of every major town there except Zadar. In 1390 the Archbishop
of Split himself traveled to Tvrtko's court at Sutjeska to receive from his
new overlord a promise to respect the property of the Church of Split -
a striking reversal for a ban's line that, two generations earlier, had
been a minor power answering to bigger neighbors on every side.

## An expansion built on other people's loyalty

Tvrtko's reach depended heavily on regional magnates who held their own
territories under his suzerainty rather than through direct royal
administration. The Hrvatinić family, based in the Donji kraji to the
northwest, had backed Tvrtko in an earlier war with Hungary in 1363, and
Vukac Hrvatinić's son Hrvoje Vukčić was rewarded in 1380 with the title
Great Vojvoda of Bosnia, going on to accumulate enormous influence of
his own in the decades that followed. Bosnia's peak, in other words, was
built as much on the loyalty of great regional lords as on the crown's
own direct strength - an arrangement that worked as long as a king
strong enough to command that loyalty sat on the throne, and one that
would prove far less durable once he did not.
