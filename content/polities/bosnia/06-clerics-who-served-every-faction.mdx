Bosnia's own kings were never members of the Bosnian Church. Stjepan
Kotromanić was Orthodox until converting to Catholicism in the 1340s;
Tvrtko I was Catholic throughout his entire reign. Both rulers kept
cordial relations with the Bosnian Church's clergy - Kotromanić even had
Patarin monks witness two of his own charters - but nothing in the
surviving record suggests the church held any real influence at court
during the fourteenth century, or that it functioned as a religious arm of
any particular faction.

## Diplomats chosen for practical reasons, not doctrine

That changed, in a narrow and specific way, from 1403 onward, when King
Ostoja began using a Patarin monk named Vlatko Tumarlić as an envoy to
Dubrovnik - the first clear instance of the Bosnian Church's clergy
playing a genuine political role. Over the following decades, individual
Patarin diplomats - Tumarlić, Starac Dmitar, Radašin, and above all Gost
Radin - served Bosnia's kings and its great regional lords alike on
missions to Dubrovnik and elsewhere. The advantages were practical
rather than ideological: a cleric could pass through contested and
warring territory more safely than a layman, and Patarin monks, mostly
drawn from outside the great noble families, had no competing dynastic
interests of their own to advance - qualities that made them useful
regardless of who was doing the choosing.

## A church without a party

What the pattern does not support is any picture of the Bosnian Church
as the nobility's own instrument against the crown, or as a body with
consistent political convictions of its own. Patarin diplomats served
kings, and they served the same kings' noble rivals, apparently with
equal loyalty to whichever lord currently employed them. When
persecution of the Bosnian Church began in earnest under King Stefan
Tomaš in the 1440s, the nobility showed little sign of rallying to defend
it - further evidence that most of Bosnia's ruling class regarded the
church's clergy as useful functionaries rather than as allies bound to
them by shared belief or shared interest. A church whose own doctrine
remained a genuine mystery to outsiders turned out to be, in practical
terms, thoroughly unremarkable: it served whoever needed literate,
mobile envoys, and asked for nothing in return beyond being left alone.
