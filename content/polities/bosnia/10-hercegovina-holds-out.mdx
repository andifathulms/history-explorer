The kingdom's fall in 1463 was not the end of every Bosnian throne. In
the south, the magnate Stefan Vukčić - styled Herceg Stefan, "Duke
Stefan," the title that gave the region of Hercegovina its own later name -
had carved out an effectively independent duchy for himself, and it
survived the kingdom that had once claimed to rule it by eighteen years.
Patarins expelled from Bosnia proper in 1459-60 took refuge in his
territory, and the Bosnian Church's last real community of believers
lived out its final years there rather than in Bosnia itself.

## A duke who kept faith with everyone

Herceg Stefan died in 1466, and his own will - drawn up that year and
witnessed jointly by the Orthodox Metropolitan of Mileševo and Gost
Radin, the Bosnian Church's own leader - shows a ruler who, to the end,
kept working relationships with every confession active in his lands
rather than committing decisively to one. The following year he
deposited a large sum of money in Dubrovnik for safekeeping against the
day the Ottomans might take his own territory too, a transaction again
witnessed by an Orthodox metropolitan alongside two Patarin monks -
hardly the behavior of a ruler who saw the various faiths around him as
rivals rather than as a set of relationships worth maintaining
simultaneously.

## A slow fade rather than a second sudden collapse

Herceg Stefan's sons, Vlatko and Vladislav, inherited a duchy already
narrowing under Ottoman pressure. A 1481 Ragusan document still refers
to Vlatko as a Patarin, suggesting the Bosnian Church's last adherents
persisted in his territory almost to the very end - not through any
special resilience, but simply because no one had yet moved to suppress
them and the general Ottoman conquest, when it came, would have made
the question moot regardless. Hercegovina's last territory fell to the
Ottomans in late 1481. Vlatko retired to Venice, the power he had leaned
on for support through his final years in charge of a state that no longer
existed.

## Two brothers, two entirely different afterlives

A third of Herceg Stefan's sons, also named Stefan, took a different path
entirely. Left holy relics and an icon in his father's will, he traveled to
Istanbul in 1473 or 1474, converted to Islam, and took the name Ahmed.
As Ahmed Hercegović he rose rapidly through the Ottoman administration
- a nobleman's son who ended up serving the same empire that had
conquered both his father's duchy and the kingdom it had once answered
to, his own new name a small, permanent record of exactly how total
that conquest had been.
