The kingdom Tvrtko I left in 1391 did not have a second ruler anywhere
near his own stature, and within a decade the throne had become
something regional magnates competed to control rather than something
a king controlled magnates from. By 1403 King Ostoja was at war with
Ragusa, and the small Adriatic commune - unable to fight Bosnia
outright - turned instead to diplomacy aimed at removing him. Its
ambassadors were dispatched north to meet Duke Hrvoje Vukčić, by then,
in Filipović's own words, "the most influential baron between the
Adriatic Sea and Buda," with instructions to flatter him into taking
the crown for himself, or failing that, to back a Kotromanić candidate
more pliable than Ostoja. Ragusa had one already in mind: a certain
Pavle Radišić, who had lived in the commune for two years and whom the
ambassadors were told to describe as "a good, discreet and devoted
person."

## A king nobody outside Ragusa wanted

In January 1404 Hrvoje duly signed a pact agreeing to back Radišić's
claim with his own army, in exchange for Ragusan help negotiating with
King Sigismund of Hungary. It did not survive contact with the
Bosnian nobility. When Ragusa's ambassadors returned that spring to
learn who the new king would actually be, Hrvoje - who evidently never
seriously intended to install a Ragusan client on a throne he himself
could not simply take - deflected their questions back onto the one
principle the whole negotiation had never actually challenged: that
only a Kotromanić could rule Bosnia. In June 1404 a noble assembly
did exactly that, choosing not Ostoja, not Radišić, but Tvrtko I's own
son, and Hrvoje wasted no time informing Venice that Tvrtko II
Tvrtković was now king.

## A pattern, not an incident

The 1403-04 crisis was not a one-time aberration but the shape Bosnian
kingship would keep for the next several decades: a magnate class -
the Hrvatinić and Kosača families chief among them - capable of
deposing kings, brokering their replacements, and courting foreign
powers against the throne itself, while the throne's own occupants
rotated among a narrowing pool of Kotromanić claimants who owed their
crowns more to noble consent than to any settled rule of inheritance.
Tvrtko II himself would be deposed and restored more than once in the
years that followed. What had been, under Tvrtko I, a kingdom strong
enough to make magnates was becoming, one negotiated succession at a
time, a kingdom the magnates made instead.
