Ban Kulin appears in the historical record in 1180, receiving a friendly
letter from the pope that treats him as a dutiful son of the Church, and
for the following two decades nothing in the surviving evidence suggests
he was anything other than a conventional Catholic ruler. His 1189
charter to the merchants of Dubrovnik opens with an invocation to the
Trinity in typical Catholic style. Two churches built during his reign
carry inscriptions asking God to give the ban and his wife, Banica
Vojislava, health and long life - the kind of ordinary, this-world piety
expected of a Catholic patron rather than any hint of unorthodox belief.
In 1194 the Archbishop of Dubrovnik traveled to Bosnia at Kulin's own
invitation and personally consecrated two churches there, apparently
finding nothing amiss.

## An accusation from a rival with reasons of his own

Everything changed in 1199, when Vukan, the ruler of neighboring
Dalmatia and Dioclea, wrote to the pope claiming that heresy had taken
root in Bosnia on a massive scale - naming Ban Kulin himself, his wife,
his sister, and more than ten thousand of his Christian subjects as
adherents. The timing and the source of the accusation both invite
scepticism: Vukan had his own political rivalry with Kulin, and framing
a neighboring ruler as a protector of heretics was an effective way to
draw papal and Hungarian pressure down on him. Nothing in the record
before 1199 supports the charge, and a great deal of it - the churches,
the letters, the archbishop's own untroubled visit five years earlier -
argues against it.

## A renunciation that settled nothing

Whatever the truth of Vukan's claim, the papacy took it seriously enough
to act. A legate was dispatched, and on 6 April 1203, at a gathering
called Bilino Polje, the priors of Bosnia's own religious houses formally
renounced a list of unspecified errors and pledged obedience to Rome,
with Kulin himself present as a witness. The document settled the
immediate diplomatic crisis. It did nothing to settle the much larger
question the accusation had opened - what, if anything, the religious
communities operating in Bosnia's own mountains actually believed - a
question that would occupy scholars, popes, and Bosnia's own neighbors
for the rest of the kingdom's existence.
