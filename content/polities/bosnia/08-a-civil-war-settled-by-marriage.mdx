Stefan Tomaš came to Bosnia's throne in 1443 to a kingdom already at
war with itself, spent his first three years fighting the most powerful
magnate in the country, and ended the fight not on a battlefield but at
his own wedding.

## A king the country's strongest lord refused to recognize

When the nobility chose Stefan Tomaš, King Ostoja's son, to succeed
Tvrtko II in 1443, the great Herzegovinian magnate Stefan Vukčić
Kosača refused to accept him. Vukčić had taken no part in the election,
and he answered it by declaring for the new king's own brother Radivoj
instead - the same rival the Ottomans had once put forward as an
anti-king a decade earlier. Civil war followed almost immediately. The
timing could not have suited the papacy less: Rome was trying to
assemble a unified Christian front against the Ottomans that same year,
and a Bosnia consumed by its own internal war had nothing to contribute
to it. Vukčić, isolated and searching for allies, turned to Alfonso of
Naples and accepted vassalage under him, receiving in return not the
troops he wanted but membership in a chivalric order - a token gesture
that left him no better armed than before. When the Ottomans, watching
the war from outside, saw an opportunity to weaken a kingdom the papal
alliance was courting, they began supplying Vukčić directly, and Turkish
support proved considerably more useful to him than a foreign king's
ceremonial belt. The war dragged on into 1446, entirely indifferent to
the larger struggle unfolding around it: in these same years the
Hungarian commander Hunyadi's armies were driving the Ottomans back
through Serbia and Bulgaria, forcing a ten-year truce in 1444 -
gains Bosnia's own civil war meant it played no part in securing.

## A peace sealed at the altar

The war ended in early 1446 the way many Bosnian succession quarrels
did: through a marriage rather than a decisive victory. Stefan Tomaš
and Stefan Vukčić agreed to a peace confirmed by the king's marriage to
Vukčić's own daughter Katarina, celebrated as a Catholic wedding that
May. The timing was not incidental. Stefan Tomaš's own religious
position had been ambiguous up to this point - his father Ostoja had
supported the Bosnian Church, and the new king had likely followed the
same allegiance before his own conversion - but a papal letter from
July 1446 credited a Franciscan, Thomas of Hvar, with having converted
the king to Catholicism outright. Whatever the precise sequence, by the
time of his wedding Stefan Tomaš had committed himself and his kingdom
firmly to Rome, in a way none of his Kotromanić predecessors, however
personally Catholic, ever had.

## What the conversion cost the Bosnian Church

The consequence, over the years that followed, was the persecution of
the same Bosnian Church that earlier kings had tolerated without
incident. Stefan Tomaš's own reign is the point at which papal sources
first begin describing that church in the language of organized
heresy rather than mere schism - a shift serious enough that scholars
studying the record still cannot agree on whether it reflects a real
change inside the church itself or a campaign by papal agents and
Franciscans determined to see one there regardless. What is clear is
that the king who had spent his first three years fighting for his own
throne spent the rest of his reign trying to prove, to a papacy whose
support he needed against the Ottomans, that Bosnia's Catholicism no
longer needed watching.
