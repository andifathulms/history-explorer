Between the last trace of Ban Kulin's own reign and the next Bosnian
ruler the sources allow historians to name with any confidence, there
is a stretch of roughly thirty years in which almost no contemporary
document survives - and the family that would eventually rule Bosnia
for two centuries only comes into clear view on the far side of it.

## A gap the sources themselves admit

Kulin's own attested rule ends around 1203, the year of the Bilino
Polje renunciation. The next Bosnian ruler securely documented is Ban
Matej Ninoslav, first recorded in a papal letter of 1233. What happened
in the intervening decades is not preserved in any surviving charter or
chronicle - a silence long enough that historians studying the
Kotromanić dynasty's own origins have had to treat it as a genuine gap
in the record rather than a puzzle waiting on a document nobody has
yet found. When Ninoslav wrote to Pope Gregory IX that year defending
his right to rule, he invoked "ancestors" who had governed Bosnia "from
the old times" - a claim vague enough to paper over exactly the
decades nobody can otherwise account for.

## Ninoslav's ancestors, real or invoked

Ninoslav's own relationship to Kulin cannot be established from anything
that survives: he mentions Kulin exactly once in his charters, without
specifying any kinship, and the pope's own chancery seems to have simply
repeated back the claims Ninoslav's own letter had made. Whether Ninoslav
descended from Kulin directly or merely from the same wider kindred,
his position - like every Bosnian ruler's after him - rested less on a
documented bloodline than on belonging to the one family group from
which the country's rulers were drawn, whoever within it happened to
hold power at a given moment. Ninoslav ruled into the 1250s. Despite
having sons of his own, he was succeeded not by one of them but by a
kinsman named Prijezda - the first ruler historians can connect with
real confidence to the Kotromanić line that would eventually produce
Tvrtko and every Bosnian king after him.

## Prijezda's sons, and a family forced from the throne

Prijezda ruled into the 1280s, and his sons Stjepan and Prijezda the
younger appear together in the charters of 1287, sharing power in a
country where sole rule was rarely absolute in practice. It was the
elder son, Ban Stjepan, who held Bosnia into the new century - and who,
sometime in the early 1300s, lost it. A powerful Croatian noble kindred,
the Šubići of Bribir, forced Bosnia's own ruling family from power and
governed the country themselves for roughly two decades, reducing the
Kotromanići to junior partners in territory that had once answered to
them directly. What the surviving record does not preserve is exactly
how the takeover happened or what Šubić rule inside Bosnia actually
looked like during those two decades - only that it happened, and that
it did not last.

It was Ban Stjepan's own son, Stjepan II, who reversed it. With
Hungarian royal support, he overthrew the Šubići and restored his
family to Bosnia's throne in his own right in 1322 - the same ruler
whose long reign would go on to rebuild Kotromanić prestige, marry his
daughter into the Angevin royal house of Hungary, and lay the ground for
his nephew Tvrtko's own coronation as king a generation later.
