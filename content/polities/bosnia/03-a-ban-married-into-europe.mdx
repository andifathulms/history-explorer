The Kotromanić family that would eventually give the kingdom its royal
line did not hold Bosnia continuously even in the generations after
Kulin. Early in the fourteenth century they were forced to surrender
their position to the counts of Bribir, a powerful Croatian kindred,
and spent roughly two decades as junior partners in territory that had
once answered to them directly. It was Ban Stjepan II, ruling from the early 1320s, who restored the
family's own supremacy and then built on it: a
rapid territorial and economic expansion of the state that, within a
single reign, turned a family recently dependent on a rival house back
into Bosnia's own undisputed rulers.

## A genealogy assembled to matter

Stjepan II backed that recovery with a dynastic claim built for a
European audience. He was a grandson of the Serbian king Dragutin
Nemanjić, and traced further descent - real or diplomatically useful -
from the Hungarian Árpáds, the Byzantine houses of Komnenos, Angelos
and Laskaris, and the Venetian Dandolos, an assembly of connections
Filipović reads as a deliberate construction of dynastic prestige rather
than simple genealogical accident. The claim was put to direct use in
1353, when Stjepan II married his daughter Elizabeth to Louis the
Great, the Angevin king of Hungary - a match that placed the
Kotromanići, in Filipović's own assessment, "almost on equal footing
with the most potent European dynasties of the time," and set the
stage for what came next: Stjepan II's own nephew Tvrtko, who
succeeded him that same year and would go on, a generation later, to
take the crown Stjepan II's own reign had made thinkable.
