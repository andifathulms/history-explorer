Frederick's record as emperor came, by the end of his reign, in for a
harder-edged assessment than his own passivity in most crises might
suggest. Coxe judges him unfairly blamed for every civil war that
afflicted Germany during his decades on the throne - languid and
inactive by temperament, certainly, but the same reign also produced
real, lasting reforms to how the empire actually governed itself.
Frederick's own government laid out, for the first time, a genuine
system for raising military contingents from the empire's princes and
states rather than leaving the matter to whatever power and
self-interest happened to apply in a given crisis, funded by fixed
assessments collected through imperial commissaries - about the most
workable system, Coxe judges, the empire's own patchwork of jealous,
semi-independent territories could realistically support.

The right of private warfare proved harder to solve: under it, any
noble, town or even guild could formally declare feud against any
other party over a grievance real or imagined, with no higher
authority required to approve it, and the abuse reached genuinely
absurd extremes - associations of tradesmen and household servants
exchanging formal declarations of war "on the most ridiculous
pretences." One surviving challenge came from a private individual
addressed directly to Frederick himself: "I will no longer obey your
grace, but will be the enemy of your country, and your subjects, and
will do them as much harm as possible." Frederick's own early instinct
had been to suppress the regional leagues that towns and nobles formed
for mutual defense, on the theory that any organized association not
answerable to the emperor was a threat to his own authority - he had
personally worked to dissolve the Swiss confederacy on exactly this
reasoning. Decades of failure eventually pushed him toward the
opposite strategy: build a confederacy loyal to the crown instead.

## Built on an old shield

Swabia offered the ideal testing ground, dense with imperial towns and
countless small lordships answerable to nobody but the emperor
himself. Frederick proclaimed a ten-year public peace and convened the
Swabian states around an old noble league called the Shield of St.
George, widened far beyond nobility alone to include princes, towns
and vassals together. It began modestly and grew fast, reaching
twenty-two towns, thirteen prelates, twelve counts and three hundred
fifty knights within a year, helped along by the threat of fines and
the imperial ban for anyone who refused to join; powerful princes
followed, and the league's own combined weight finally gave Frederick
something his personal authority had never managed alone - the
strongholds of robber-nobles came down by the hundred, and even the
feuding House of Bavaria was forced into line.

That new authority let Frederick settle a personal grievance he could
never have pressed alone. In 1485 Albert of Munich seized Regensburg,
a city his own ancestors had once held, and - without so much as
informing Frederick - married the emperor's own daughter Cunegunda,
securing through her a claim on the reversion of Tyrol. Maximilian
brokered a first reconciliation, but Albert simply refused to honor
his own promise to restore the city, and Frederick, now backed by the
full strength of the empire and the Swabian League, published the ban
against both Albert and Regensburg outright. An army of more than
twenty thousand assembled with unusual speed, more than a third of it
supplied by the league itself, and once again it was Maximilian's
mediation rather than the army's presence that actually settled the
matter: Albert restored Regensburg, reaffirmed his renunciation of
Tyrol, and brought his wife and daughters to visit Frederick at Linz,
where the aging emperor, his own health failing, found real comfort in
a reconciliation that finally brought both family peace and public
order to his final years.

## A sound peasant

The reconciliation with Albert of Munich was very nearly Frederick's
last act as a ruling sovereign. Soon after returning to Austria he
handed over administration of both his hereditary lands and the empire
entirely to Maximilian, retiring to Linz to pursue his own favorite
studies and enjoy, for the first time in a long and turbulent reign,
real tranquility. He was seventy-eight, and had for some time suffered
from a cancerous ulcer in his leg; amputation was the only remedy his
surgeons could offer, and when the operation had to be repeated a
second time due to their own inexperience, Frederick bore both
procedures with the same characteristic calm that had carried him
through decades of political humiliation. Holding up his own severed
limb afterward, he told those present, "What difference is there
between an emperor and a peasant? or rather, is not a sound peasant
better than a sick emperor? yet I hope to enjoy the greatest good which
can happen to man; a happy exit from this transitory life."

He seemed at first to be recovering, but a rigidly kept religious fast
combined with an excessive appetite for melons - the same indulgence
that had already killed his own son Albert V decades earlier - brought
on a dysentery his weakened body could not survive. He died at Linz on
19 August 1493, seventy-eight years old, having reigned fifty-three
years - longer, Coxe notes, than any emperor since Augustus himself.

Frederick was tall and handsome, plain in his everyday dress but
genuinely fond of jewels and splendor on ceremonial occasions;
religious, chaste, faithful to his word, and warmly affectionate
toward his own family and household. His defining trait, though, was
the same one that had shaped his entire reign for better and worse: an
almost stoic imperturbability in the face of injury and insult, which
Coxe judges too often invited exactly the contempt and fresh
aggression it was meant to absorb. It was this same unshakeable calm
that let him survive exile, siege, imprisonment of his own son, and
the loss of an entire kingdom without ever losing his throne outright
- the very passivity his critics condemned was also, in the end, the
quality that outlasted every crisis it failed to prevent.
