The gratitude Frederick owed Poděbrady for breaking the siege of
Vienna lasted only a few years before papal politics pulled him into
an alliance against his own rescuer. Pope Pius II, who as a cardinal
had helped negotiate the compacts protecting Bohemia's Calixtin
majority, reversed course completely once he held the tiara himself,
reviving the church's old claim to total authority over Bohemian
religious practice. When Bohemia's Catholics demanded Poděbrady go
further than concession and annul the compacts entirely, and Pius
refused even to reconfirm an agreement his own predecessors had
already approved, Poděbrady summoned a diet and spoke plainly: the
compacts were the only thing that had ended Bohemia's religious civil
war, and "I and my wife have followed them from our childhood, and I
am determined to maintain them till my death." When the papal legate
answered with a tirade, Poděbrady had him thrown out and imprisoned on
bread and water. Pius annulled the compacts outright in 1463 and
threatened excommunication - a crisis defused only when Poděbrady
apologized and Frederick, still mindful of his own debt, personally
interceded to suspend the sentence.

Frederick's gratitude did not survive Pius's own death the following
year. His successor pursued Poděbrady with even greater hostility,
and by 1467 imperial ambassadors were openly backing a papal call for
a crusade against Bohemia; though the diet itself refused the
proposal, the pope formally deposed Poděbrady anyway and let crusade
preachers operate freely across German and Austrian territory.
Poděbrady, furious at being betrayed by the very ally his own troops
had once saved from starvation, declared war outright and invaded
Austria as far as the Danube.

## A mock diet at Olomouc

Unable to muster real support from the empire's own princes, Frederick
turned to a more dangerous solution: he offered the Bohemian crown
itself to Matthias Corvinus, King of Hungary, in exchange for turning
against the man who had once ransomed him from Bohemian captivity and
made him king in the first place. Matthias, dazzled despite everything
he owed Poděbrady, invaded Bohemia while papal intrigue simultaneously
stirred the kingdom's Catholic minority into open revolt - but
Poděbrady, still capable at defending his own throne even as an old
man, crushed the uprising and threw back the invasion, forcing a peace
in 1469. It lasted barely any time: persuaded that no agreement with a
"heretic" king carried any real obligation, Matthias resumed the war,
overran Moravia and Silesia, and staged what Coxe pointedly calls a
"mock diet" at Olomouc, where a hand-picked assembly of Catholic
loyalists elected him King of Bohemia on the spot - acting for all
purposes as its rightful sovereign even while Poděbrady still held
Prague and the loyalty of most of the kingdom. Frederick, who had
handed Matthias the pretext for the whole campaign, now got cold feet
at the thought of a single ambitious rival ruling both Hungary and
Bohemia together, and quietly withheld the support he had promised,
disguising his retreat as a pilgrimage to Rome.

## A sovereign conversant with their language

Poděbrady answered both rivals with a single stroke, proposing as his
own successor Vladislaus of Poland, a candidate who satisfied nearly
everyone: Bohemia's Catholics welcomed a prince of their own faith,
the Calixtins saw the surest way to keep both Frederick and Matthias
off the throne, and the wider nation liked the idea of a king who
actually spoke their language. The estates approved the nomination
without real opposition. Matthias, furious at being passed over so
completely, kept fighting until the two armies - each led personally
by a capable and experienced king - fought each other to exhaustion
and agreed to a truce in July 1470. Poděbrady did not live to see it
hold for long, dying the following year; Frederick, sensing one more
opportunity, put himself forward as a candidate a second time and
found even less support than before. Vladislaus was duly elected, and
Frederick, unable to win the crown himself, settled for backing him
over Matthias instead - a policy that would keep stoking Hungarian
unrest for years, even as it settled nothing of Frederick's own much
larger ambitions.
