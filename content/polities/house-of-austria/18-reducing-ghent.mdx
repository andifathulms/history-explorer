Mary of Burgundy's death on 26 March 1482, still young and leaving two
infant children, undid much of what her marriage had secured.
Flanders's own turbulent population had never fully accepted
Maximilian as anything but a foreign consort, and once Mary was gone
they treated his continued war against France as personal ambition
rather than a defense of their own interests. The Flemish estates
seized custody of the young Philip, appointed his guardians
themselves, and shut Maximilian out of his own son's upbringing
entirely - a dispute Louis XI exploited immediately, negotiating
directly with the Flemish states over Maximilian's head. The
resulting Treaty of Arras, concluded that December, forced terms
Maximilian had no real power to refuse: his infant daughter Margaret
was betrothed to the French dauphin and sent to France as his intended
bride, carrying Franche-Comté and Artois as her dowry, while Philip's
guardianship remained with the Flemish estates alone.

The estates' own suspicion of Maximilian only deepened from there -
his continued presence nearby, his preference for foreign advisors,
his resentment at losing any share in governing Flanders. War followed,
with Flemish rebels backed openly by France, but Maximilian pressed
his own military advantage hard enough that by July 1485 Ghent itself
submitted, its burghers forced to recognize him as guardian of his son
and governor of Flanders in exchange for his promise not to remove
Philip from the Netherlands. With Flanders reasserted, Maximilian
turned toward France itself, allying with the Duke of Orléans and the
Duke of Brittany against the French regency - a coalition that
collapsed entirely once the young King Charles VIII took the field in
person, recovered Thérouanne, overran Brittany, and crushed the rebels
outright at Saint-Aubin. Orléans was captured, and on 28 August 1488
the Duke of Brittany signed a peace reducing his own duchy to French
vassalage.

## A city rises against its own king

Ghent's own submission proved no more permanent than any earlier peace.
Foreign garrisons, the misconduct of German troops Maximilian had
brought in, and fresh demands for money on top of what he already
refused to account for turned discontent into open insurrection once
again. When the people of Bruges summoned him, he went in person,
trusting a governor's promise that thousands of loyal burghers stood
ready to back him. Instead, the morning after his ceremonial entry on
9 February 1488, word arrived that Ghent's own rebels had taken Ypres
and Courtrai; as Maximilian tried to leave the city, the population
rose as one, seized the gates and market square, and demanded he
dismiss his foreign troops and hand over the counselors they accused
of corruption.

A false rumor that an avenging army was already marching on Bruges
tipped the crowd into open fury: they rang the alarm bell, raised the
banner of Brabant, and very nearly stormed the palace to kill
Maximilian outright before their own leaders talked them down. When
Maximilian addressed the crowd directly the next day - "Here I am,
behold me ready to live and die with you" - the majority dragged him
instead to an apothecary's shop and held him there for days while
torturing, beheading or exiling his own ministers in the market square
outside. Ghent executed thirteen more of his supporters and, together
with Bruges, formally stripped him of his son's guardianship, setting
up a new government in Philip's name with the King of France
recognized as its overlord. Moved afterward to a nobleman's house and
confined like a common criminal, Maximilian was at least spared
delivery into French hands by the people of Bruges themselves, who
resisted Ghent's own demands to hand him over - and throughout the
ordeal, Coxe records, he bore himself with a courage and dignity that
did more than any army to cool his captors' rage.

## No trace of resentment

Neighboring provinces petitioned for his release, but it was Frederick
who finally forced the issue - abandoning, for once, the indolence
that had defined most of his own reign to fight for his son with real
urgency. He assembled fifteen thousand men at Cologne within weeks and
pushed into Brabant, terrifying the Flemings into releasing Maximilian
on 16 May 1488, though only after extracting a renewed promise to
honor the Treaty of Arras in full and withdraw every foreign soldier
from the country.

Maximilian showed no anger at any of it. He went straight to church to
give thanks, telling the crowd outside, "We are now at peace," then
visited the very shop where his captivity had begun and read an
insulting inscription scrawled on its walls without any visible
reaction, forgiving the people who now knelt before him begging
pardon. Back with his father, he quietly talked Frederick out of
further war against the rebels; when Frederick pressed on regardless,
besieging Ghent for three months before handing the campaign off and
returning home, it was diplomacy rather than the siege that finally
closed the conflict. King Charles VIII, eager to pursue his own
designs on a newly vulnerable Brittany, concluded the Peace of
Frankfurt in July 1489 - restoring Breton conquests, confirming the
Arras settlement, and pressing the Flemish rebels into finally
accepting Maximilian's guardianship of his own son.
