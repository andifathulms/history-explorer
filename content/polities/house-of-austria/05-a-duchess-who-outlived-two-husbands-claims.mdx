Carinthia's own duke died in 1335 without a son, leaving his
territories to an only daughter, Margaret, nicknamed Maultasch for the
size of her mouth - and touching off a dispute over Carinthia and
Tyrol together that would run, on and off, for three decades and two
generations of Habsburg dukes. John of Bohemia had already secured
Margaret's hand for his own eight-year-old son, expecting both
duchies to pass to his family along with her; Emperor Louis of
Bavaria, newly reconciled with his old Austrian rivals, simply revoked
the decree allowing female inheritance and granted Carinthia to Albert
and Otho of Austria instead. Austria took it without much resistance.
Tyrol's own nobles refused the transfer outright, and John, furious at
losing what he had already paid for, ravaged Austrian territory until
a negotiated peace left Carinthia in Habsburg hands for good and Tyrol
with Margaret - the family's second permanent territorial gain, and
proof, a generation after Rudolf's own conquest of Austria and Styria,
that the dynasty could hold what it won.

## A marriage dissolved by decree

Margaret's own marriage curdled into open hatred within a few years;
she had her husband imprisoned, escaped her own confinement, and threw
herself on Louis's protection instead. Louis, eager for Tyrol on his
family's behalf now, accepted her, betrothed her to his own son, and -
over the Pope's furious objections - dissolved her first marriage by
imperial decree. King John, blind, aging, and never reconciled to the
insult, spent his last years building the coalition that would put his
own eldest son on the imperial throne, and died fighting for the
French crown at Crécy without ever recovering the province he
considered stolen from his family.

Two decades later, the province came to Austria not by war but by a
death that Rudolf IV moved fast to exploit. Margaret's second marriage
had never won papal approval, leaving her son Meinhard's own
legitimacy in doubt; Rudolf secured the pope's recognition for the boy,
married him to his own sister, and in return won Margaret's promise
that Austria would inherit Tyrol outright should her husband and son
both die without heirs. They did, within two years of each other, and
Rudolf crossed the Alps in the depths of winter to secure her
agreement in person before anyone could reconsider - pressing for
immediate possession, well aware how changeable her temper could be,
and dazzling her with enough of his own court's splendor at Vienna
that she agreed to leave her mountain duchy behind for good.

## Confirmed twice over

Securing the emperor's own approval proved harder than securing
Margaret's, since Rudolf and his brothers were, at that very moment,
allied against Charles IV in his capacity as King of Bohemia - old
rivalry outliving the marriage tie meant to end it. It was Rudolf's
own wife, Charles's daughter, who arranged the peace: a meeting at
Brünn in 1364 where Charles confirmed Margaret's grant and formally
invested Rudolf with a province no Habsburg duke had ruled before. The
house of Lower Bavaria, cut out of an inheritance it had once
expected, contested the transfer by force until the Pope - more
interested in German troops for his own Italian war than in one
Alpine province - brokered a truce that left Tyrol in Habsburg hands.
Bavaria renewed the claim once more after Margaret's own death in
1366, seizing several towns before three years of negotiation produced
a final settlement at Scharding in 1369: Bavaria renounced Tyrol for
good in exchange for a cash payment, and Austria kept undisputed
mastery of a duchy rich in silver mines and command of the roads into
Italy - a childless heiress's inheritance, fought over by two rival
dynasties across two Habsburg generations, settled at last by a treaty
neither original claimant lived to see.
