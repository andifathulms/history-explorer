While Frederick fought his own brother for Austria, a separate crisis
in Germany threatened his hold on the imperial crown itself. Frederick
the Victorious, regent of the Palatinate, had assumed full electoral
authority with the estates' approval and the pope's confirmation -
approval the emperor alone withheld, earning himself a formidable
enemy. The elector spent years building a coalition against him,
drawing in two archbishop-electors, a landgrave, and - most damaging
of all - Frederick's own brother Albert and cousin Sigismund, and won
George of Poděbrady's support outright with the offer of the imperial
crown itself. In 1461 the confederates convened rival diets without
the emperor's consent and moved toward formal deposition, sending
Frederick a scathing letter blaming Germany's disorder squarely on his
own weakness and fifteen years of absence from the diets he was
supposed to preside over.

Threatened with the loss of everything his family had spent nearly two
centuries reclaiming, Frederick abandoned his usual indolence and
fought back with intrigue rather than arms - splitting his enemies
apart one ambassador and circular letter at a time, holding
Brandenburg through its own distrust of the Palatinate, securing
Saxony despite its ties to Bavaria, and even peeling away Poděbrady
himself by offering to mediate the Bohemian king's worsening quarrel
with Rome. His strongest ally proved to be Pope Pius II, who had every
personal reason to keep his own benefactor on the throne. A related
war over the archbishopric of Mainz spilled into open battle - the
Elector Palatine crushed a rival coalition at Seckingen, taking a
bishop and a margrave prisoner - but battlefield losses proved almost
irrelevant next to the diplomatic collapse of the league itself. One
by one the confederates made separate peace, and the Elector Palatine
abandoned the deposition scheme entirely in exchange for narrower
territorial gains of his own.

## An empire too divided to fight the Turks

Frederick kept his crown, but Coxe's own verdict on the whole affair is
withering: the constant infighting among German princes left the
empire unable to answer a genuine external threat, as Ottoman armies -
already masters of Constantinople - made their first incursions into
German territory during Frederick's own reign. The Elector Palatine
spoke for most of his peers when he said he had "enemies nearer home
than the Turks," and Frederick himself, Coxe notes with open
disapproval, often treated Ottoman pressure on Hungary as a convenient
check on his own rival there rather than a danger worth uniting
against. Even Pius II's own zeal for a crusade collapsed into
factionalism: when Poděbrady offered to lead an army against the Turks
himself, the papal legate argued it would serve Christendom better to
turn German arms against the "heretic" Bohemian king instead - a
preference Pius's successor pursued even harder, trading a war against
the Ottomans for one against Bohemia.
