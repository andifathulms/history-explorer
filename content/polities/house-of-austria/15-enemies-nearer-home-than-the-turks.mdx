While Frederick fought his own brother for Austria, a separate crisis
in Germany threatened his hold on the imperial crown itself. Frederick
the Victorious, regent of the Palatinate, had assumed full electoral
authority with the estates' approval and the pope's confirmation -
approval the emperor alone withheld, earning himself a formidable
enemy. The elector spent years building a coalition against him,
drawing in two archbishop-electors, a landgrave, and - most damaging
of all - Frederick's own brother Albert and cousin Sigismund, and won
George of Poděbrady's support outright with the offer of the imperial
crown itself. In 1461 the confederates convened rival diets without
the emperor's consent and moved toward formal deposition, sending
Frederick a scathing letter blaming Germany's disorder squarely on his
own weakness and fifteen years of absence from the diets he was
supposed to preside over.

Threatened with the loss of everything his family had spent nearly two
centuries reclaiming, Frederick abandoned his usual indolence and
fought back with intrigue rather than arms - splitting his enemies
apart one ambassador and circular letter at a time, holding
Brandenburg through its own distrust of the Palatinate, securing
Saxony despite its ties to Bavaria, and even peeling away Poděbrady
himself by offering to mediate the Bohemian king's worsening quarrel
with Rome. His strongest ally proved to be Pope Pius II, who had every
personal reason to keep his own benefactor on the throne. A related
war over the archbishopric of Mainz spilled into open battle - the
Elector Palatine crushed a rival coalition at Seckingen, taking a
bishop and a margrave prisoner - but battlefield losses proved almost
irrelevant next to the diplomatic collapse of the league itself. One
by one the confederates made separate peace, and the Elector Palatine
abandoned the deposition scheme entirely in exchange for narrower
territorial gains of his own.

## An empire too divided to fight the Turks

Frederick kept his crown, but Coxe's own verdict on the whole affair is
withering: the constant infighting among German princes left the
empire unable to answer a genuine external threat, as Ottoman armies -
already masters of Constantinople - made their first incursions into
German territory during Frederick's own reign. The Elector Palatine
spoke for most of his peers when he said he had "enemies nearer home
than the Turks," and Frederick himself, Coxe notes with open
disapproval, often treated Ottoman pressure on Hungary as a convenient
check on his own rival there rather than a danger worth uniting
against. Even Pius II's own zeal for a crusade collapsed into
factionalism: when Poděbrady offered to lead an army against the Turks
himself, the papal legate argued it would serve Christendom better to
turn German arms against the "heretic" Bohemian king instead - the
same preference Pius's successor pursued even harder against the very
ally who had just helped save Frederick's own throne.

## No compact with heretics

The gratitude Frederick owed Poděbrady lasted only a few years before
papal politics pulled him into an alliance against his own rescuer.
Pope Pius II, who as a cardinal had helped negotiate the compacts
protecting Bohemia's Calixtin majority, reversed course completely
once he held the tiara himself, reviving the church's old claim to
total authority over Bohemian religious practice. When Bohemia's
Catholics demanded Poděbrady go further than concession and annul the
compacts entirely, and Pius refused even to reconfirm an agreement his
own predecessors had already approved, Poděbrady summoned a diet and
spoke plainly: the compacts were the only thing that had ended
Bohemia's religious civil war, and "I and my wife have followed them
from our childhood, and I am determined to maintain them till my
death." When the papal legate answered with a tirade, Poděbrady had
him thrown out and imprisoned on bread and water. Pius annulled the
compacts outright in 1463 and threatened excommunication - a crisis
defused only when Poděbrady apologized and Frederick, still mindful of
his own debt, personally interceded to suspend the sentence.

Frederick's gratitude did not survive Pius's own death the following
year. His successor pursued Poděbrady with even greater hostility,
and by 1467 imperial ambassadors were openly backing a papal call for
a crusade against Bohemia; though the diet itself refused the
proposal, the pope formally deposed Poděbrady anyway and let crusade
preachers operate freely across German and Austrian territory.
Poděbrady, furious at being betrayed by the very ally his own troops
had once saved from starvation, declared war outright and invaded
Austria as far as the Danube.

## A mock diet at Olomouc

Unable to muster real support from the empire's own princes, Frederick
turned to a more dangerous solution: he offered the Bohemian crown
itself to Matthias Corvinus, King of Hungary, in exchange for turning
against the man who had once ransomed him from Bohemian captivity and
made him king in the first place. Matthias, dazzled despite everything
he owed Poděbrady, invaded Bohemia while papal intrigue simultaneously
stirred the kingdom's Catholic minority into open revolt - but
Poděbrady, still capable at defending his own throne even as an old
man, crushed the uprising and threw back the invasion, forcing a peace
in 1469. It lasted barely any time: persuaded that no agreement with a
"heretic" king carried any real obligation, Matthias resumed the war,
overran Moravia and Silesia, and staged what Coxe pointedly calls a
"mock diet" at Olomouc, where a hand-picked assembly of Catholic
loyalists elected him King of Bohemia on the spot - acting for all
purposes as its rightful sovereign even while Poděbrady still held
Prague and the loyalty of most of the kingdom. Frederick, who had
handed Matthias the pretext for the whole campaign, now got cold feet
at the thought of a single ambitious rival ruling both Hungary and
Bohemia together, and quietly withheld the support he had promised,
disguising his retreat as a pilgrimage to Rome.

## A sovereign conversant with their language

Poděbrady answered both rivals with a single stroke, proposing as his
own successor Vladislaus of Poland, a candidate who satisfied nearly
everyone: Bohemia's Catholics welcomed a prince of their own faith,
the Calixtins saw the surest way to keep both Frederick and Matthias
off the throne, and the wider nation liked the idea of a king who
actually spoke their language. The estates approved the nomination
without real opposition. Matthias, furious at being passed over so
completely, kept fighting until the two armies - each led personally
by a capable and experienced king - fought each other to exhaustion
and agreed to a truce in July 1470. Poděbrady did not live to see it
hold for long, dying the following year; Frederick, sensing one more
opportunity, put himself forward as a candidate a second time and
found even less support than before. Vladislaus was duly elected, and
Frederick, unable to win the crown himself, settled for backing him
over Matthias instead - a policy that would keep stoking Hungarian
unrest for years, even as it settled nothing of Frederick's own much
larger ambitions.
