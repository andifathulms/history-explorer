The Treaty of Senlis had left one piece of business deliberately
unresolved: France was to hold Burgundy itself until its rival claims
were settled, returning three named towns once Philip, Maximilian's
own son, turned twenty. Philip, reaching that age, demanded not just
the towns but every territory France had carved from the old
Burgundian inheritance, and Charles VIII simply stalled until his own
death in 1498 left the question to his successor, Louis XII - who
divorced his own wife to marry Anne of Brittany, the same woman
Maximilian had once wed before she was taken from him. Philip's
renewed appeals went no better than before, and a mixed force of
Austrian soldiers and Swiss mercenaries sent into Burgundy collapsed
once the Swiss contingent mutinied over unpaid wages; Louis XII, whose
own ambitions had already turned toward conquering Milan, offered
peace on terms Philip accepted readily - the three towns restored, and
any further claim to Burgundy itself abandoned to the judgment of a
French court that was never going to rule against its own king.

Guelderland and Friesland, meanwhile, reopened almost as soon as they
seemed settled. The young pretender Charles of Egmont, driven out once
already, took up arms again when the new tax established at Worms
provoked real discontent among his own former subjects, and recovered
most of the duchy with quiet French backing; Friesland's own people,
encouraged by their neighbors' success, mounted a parallel resistance
and placed themselves under Guelderland's protection instead. Duke
Albert of Saxony struggled to suppress both uprisings at once, and once
Maximilian's own dispute with France was finally resolved, he marched
into Guelderland himself only to find Egmont's French backing too
strong to overcome - another regional conflict that ended not in
victory, but in a settlement France had quietly shaped from the start.

## Excommunication and the ban

Facing constant French pressure over Burgundy, Naples and Milan alike,
Maximilian recognized how much the Swiss cantons' own military
reputation could matter as allies, and worked to pry them away from
their existing alignment with France - demanding, as head of the
empire rather than as a neighboring rival, contingents for the
Burgundian war, formal submission to the new Imperial Chamber Court,
and contributions to the taxes that funded it. The Swiss refused every
demand and declined to acknowledge themselves subjects of the empire
at all. Maximilian escalated in response: he had the Pope excommunicate
them as French collaborators and let the Imperial Chamber turn its
full coercive weight against them, placing two Rhaetian nobles under
the imperial ban. Rather than intimidating the cantons into submission,
the pressure drove the whole confederacy together - seven of the old
cantons allying with two Rhaetian leagues, themselves already
alienated by separate disputes with Tyrol's own government, leaving
only Bern still working to avoid an open break with Austria. Into this
already volatile standoff, Louis XII assumed the title Duke of Milan
and began preparing to conquer the duchy outright; Maximilian warned
the Swiss against aiding the move and demanded six thousand men to
back his own son's claims in Burgundy, a request they refused outright
until the Chamber's pressure on them was lifted, promising strict
neutrality in the meantime. Both sides stood in a state of mutual
irritation that needed only a spark.
