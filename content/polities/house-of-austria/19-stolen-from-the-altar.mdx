While Maximilian fought for Austria and Hungary, a second inheritance
dispute in Brittany would test his family's fortunes just as severely.
Duke Francis II died in September 1488 leaving only two daughters,
with thirteen-year-old Anne inheriting a duchy immediately torn
between pro-French and pro-English factions. Like Mary of Burgundy
before her, Anne became the object of intense competition, and
Maximilian - whose interest in the match was as much about checking
French power as securing the duchy outright - opened negotiations even
before her father's death, pursuing the courtship in careful secrecy
to avoid provoking French intervention. Anne, won over as much by
Maximilian's own reputation as by any rival's qualities, agreed to a
prince whose rank and alliances seemed capable of actually protecting
her duchy; the marriage was solemnized by proxy, without Maximilian
himself present, sometime around the start of 1490. The secrecy that
protected the negotiation could not protect what came next.

## A mock treaty

Charles VIII, having just turned nineteen and taken the reins of
government himself, moved against Brittany with a duplicity Coxe
traces straight back to his own tutelage under the regent Anne of
Beaujeu. Recognizing that conquering the duchy outright would be
brutally difficult against a duchess backed by the empire and England
both, he pivoted to a subtler plan: marrying Anne himself, despite his
own existing marriage contract with Maximilian's own daughter
Margaret, who had lived at the French court as queen-in-waiting for
seven years already. He kept faith scrupulously with the Peace of
Frankfurt's terms regarding Flanders to lull Maximilian's suspicions,
stalled on the Brittany provisions with excuses about English troops
still in Breton service, and when the marriage between Maximilian and
Anne was formally announced, used the shock of it to peel away her
remaining allies - buying off one rival suitor with the surrender of
Nantes and pressing French troops forward against Rennes, the last
stronghold left to the duchy.

Maximilian scrambled for help but moved far too slowly against a
French court working with speed and total secrecy. Anne, who still
detested the French for the suffering they had brought her country
and found Charles himself personally repellent, held out until her own
generals and counselors, bought or intimidated, left her without hope
of relief and her capital under blockade. She finally agreed to terms
that looked generous - French withdrawal, arbitration, safe passage
into Germany - terms the French honored to the letter, right up until
Maximilian's own ambassadors, sent to escort their new queen home,
learned instead that she had gone to Langeais and married Charles VIII
there. Brittany was absorbed into the French crown in the same stroke.

## An indemnity, not a victory

Maximilian's fury was total - his own daughter publicly humiliated
after seven years as France's presumptive queen, and his own wife
taken from him after their marriage had already been solemnized. He
built a coalition with England and Aragon, but found exactly the kind
of half-hearted support that had failed him at Bruges: the Swiss
offered only mediation, German troops arrived too slowly, and
England's Henry VII sold his own neutrality to France for cash and a
pension rather than fight, while Aragon's Ferdinand accepted a
territorial bribe to abandon the coalition as well. The resulting
Treaty of Senlis returned Margaret and compensated Maximilian with
Franche-Comté, Artois and several lesser territories in place of
Brittany - honorable terms, in Coxe's own judgment, but a settlement
that could not undo the personal insult of losing a wife to the very
kingdom he had spent a decade fighting. It was, Coxe notes, the moment
a rivalry with no natural cause - France and Austria had shared no
real border and no real quarrel before Maximilian's marriage to Mary
of Burgundy planted the two powers against each other - hardened into
a hostility between the houses of Habsburg and Valois that would go on
soaking Europe in blood for more than three centuries.
