Ottokar of Bohemia had spent decades assembling, through inheritance,
war, purchase and a canny sequence of dynastic marriages, the largest
territory of any prince in Europe - Bohemia and Moravia by hereditary
right, Austria and Styria seized from a tangle of rival claimants after
the old Babenberg ducal line died out, and Carinthia and Carniola
bought outright from a dying, childless duke over his own brother's
prior claim. His dominions by the 1270s ran from the borders of
Bavaria to Raab in Hungary, and from the Adriatic to the Baltic. Beside
this, Rudolf's own hereditary lands - scattered fragments of Alsace,
Swabia and the Alpine foothills - looked almost trivial, and Rudolf
sat on what Coxe calls "a tottering throne," raised to the imperial
dignity by princes who meant him to be their chief in name more than
in power. When Rudolf's diet declared Ottokar guilty of contumacy for
refusing to surrender the disputed duchies and do homage for the rest,
open war followed.

## A kneeling king, and a legend refuted

Rudolf's campaign succeeded faster than anyone expected - the city of
Vienna itself opened its gates to him - and by November 1276 Ottokar
was forced to terms: he renounced every claim to Austria, Styria,
Carinthia and Carniola, and on 25 November crossed the Danube to
Rudolf's camp to kneel and do homage for what remained to him,
receiving in return the investiture of Bohemia and Moravia alone. A
popular story, repeated by many historians, held that Rudolf agreed to
let Ottokar perform this humiliating ceremony privately inside his own
tent, then had the tent's sides thrown open at the crucial moment to
expose the kneeling king to both watching armies - the calculated
public insult that supposedly drove Ottokar to break the peace soon
after. Coxe, citing a scholar who wrote a treatise specifically to
refute it, calls the story "idle and improbable" and rejects it
outright; whatever actually turned Ottokar back to war, it was not
stage-managed humiliation. Rudolf, having secured the disputed duchies
as fiefs reverted to the empire, moved his own family's household to
Vienna and set about winning the loyalty of the Austrian nobility by
confirming their privileges and letting them rebuild the fortresses
Ottokar had torn down.

## The battle by the March

The peace held for less than two years. Ottokar broke camp and
advanced with a large army in August 1278, and at dawn on the 26th
Rudolf's own forces crossed the stream near Weidendorf and attacked in
a crescent formation against both of Ottokar's flanks at once. The
fighting was desperate on both sides - at one point Rudolf himself was
unhorsed and nearly killed, saved only by the timely charge of his own
reserve commander - but the imperial army eventually broke Ottokar's
lines and drove the fleeing Bohemians into the river, where more than
fourteen thousand were killed, drowned or captured. Ottokar refused to
leave the field even after his army's total collapse, fighting alone
until he was overpowered, unhorsed and killed on the spot by Austrian
and Styrian nobles avenging relatives he had put to death. Rudolf,
who had tried and failed to send help in time to spare his rival's
life, praised Ottokar's courage afterward in his own letter to the
Pope - "that magnanimous monarch lost his life at the same time with
the victory" - and took possession of Moravia without a fight,
though he left the Bohemian throne itself to Ottokar's eight-year-old
son under a regency, having gotten what he actually wanted: undisputed
mastery of Austria and Styria.
