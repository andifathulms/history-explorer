While his brother Leopold fought and died against the Swiss, Albert
III governed the Austrian heartland in a wholly different register:
suppressing banditti, restraining nobles who mistook his mildness for
weakness, and pouring his real energy into the university his own
brother Rudolf had founded, endowing new chairs and deepening its
rivalry with Prague. He showed almost none of his predecessors'
appetite for war - a single expedition to Prussia alongside the
Teutonic Knights stands out precisely because it was so unlike him -
and kept his main residence at Laxenburg specifically to escape the
pomp of court life, gardening with his own hands and studying
classical treatises on rural economy for pleasure.

Age did not change that pattern until, in 1395, Bohemia's collapsing
politics pulled him in regardless: King Wenceslas IV's erratic rule
had driven a faction of nobles to seize and imprison him, and Albert
led an army into the kingdom on their behalf, his first real campaign
in nearly two decades of peaceful rule. He fell mortally ill in camp
before the fighting resolved anything, and died at Laxenburg on 29
August 1395, forty-six years old.

## "Our true father"

Vienna mourned him as it had mourned no Habsburg duke before: crowds
gathered around his body crying "We have lost our friend, our true
father!" A contemporary chronicler remembered him as "a lover of
peace, a pious and meek prince," and he earned two lasting nicknames
from his own subjects - "the Astrologer," for a genuine fascination
with judicial astrology, and "Albert with the Tress," for a lock of
hair he wore braided into his own and used to found a chivalric
society modeled on the gesture. An oddly gentle set of epitaphs for a
family whose dukes were more often remembered for the battles that
killed them.
