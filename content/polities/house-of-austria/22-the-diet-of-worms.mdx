To rally German support for the league against France, Maximilian
broke with tradition by summoning the empire's diet not to Nuremberg,
the customary site for the first assembly of a new reign, but to
Worms, closer to both the Netherlands and the Italian theater of war.
The gathering that opened on 26 May 1496 drew more princes and nobles
than any diet in memory, and Maximilian pressed two demands with
everything his own eloquence could muster: a nominal request for help
against renewed Ottoman raids into Croatia, and his real object, an
appeal for troops to drive the French out of Italy altogether. He
warned the assembled princes that French control of both Brittany and
Naples would let Charles VIII crush the church's and the empire's own
liberties outright, paraded ambassadors from Naples's exiled king as
living proof, and let a papal legate describe the Pope's own flight
from Rome in vivid detail. Coxe judges this the one moment when direct
imperial intervention might genuinely have driven France from Italy
for good - a rare alignment of motive, moral case and opportunity all
at once. None of it worked. The German states had grown numb to
complaints about Turkish incursions after decades of hearing them, and
proved no more moved by arguments about the European balance of power
than they had been by any of Frederick's own earlier appeals - an
empire that had learned, across generations of unanswered requests, to
treat its own emperor's foreign wars as somebody else's problem.

## A court that could not be dismissed

What the German states actually wanted, after generations of failed
attempts by earlier emperors, was a permanent end to private warfare
and a real tribunal to enforce it - and they used the leverage of
Maximilian's own urgent need for troops to finally extract concessions
no predecessor had managed to win from them. Maximilian, eager enough
for their support to accept the trade, agreed with unusual speed: by 7
August the ordinances establishing a perpetual public peace and a new
supreme tribunal, the Imperial Chamber Court, had his signature and
the diet's own approval. Private warfare was formally abolished, with
a heavy fine and the imperial ban threatened against any noble or town
that dared declare it again. The new court broke real ground in its
own design - a president and sixteen assessors split evenly between
knights and trained jurists, chosen by the emperor from candidates the
states themselves nominated, and, for the first time in any German
institution of this kind, members who could not be removed from office
once appointed. Its authority was limited at first, and it struggled
in its early years for members, funding and any real means of
enforcing its own rulings, but the foundation held: moved successively
through half a dozen German cities before finally settling at Wetzlar
in 1698, it grew into one of the empire's most durable institutions -
a genuine, lasting achievement born from a diet Maximilian had
convened for an entirely different purpose.

## Money that never came

The gratitude Maximilian expected in return for these concessions
never translated into the war funding he actually needed. The diet,
after long hesitation, voted a standing army grandly described as
"sufficient to check the progress of the French" and funded it with
just a hundred fifty thousand florins, a third from the Habsburgs'
own hereditary lands and the rest raised through a new levy called the
common penny - wrapped in conditions that bound Maximilian's own hands
as tightly as the tax bound the states'. The law was precise; the
collection was not. Suabia and Franconia's own states simply withheld
or delayed payment, and after repeated new ordinances trying to force
compliance, barely half the promised sum was ever actually collected -
and even that arrived so slowly that Charles VIII could have conquered
the whole of Italy before German gold reached anyone capable of
raising troops with it. Rather than leading nine thousand men into
Italy as he had promised, Maximilian could scrape together only three
thousand - a fraction of what the diet had voted, and nowhere near
enough to let him take personal command of the war he had spent so
much political capital convening the diet to fight.

## A romantic expedition

Germany's own paralysis at Worms turned out not to matter as much as
Maximilian feared: Charles VIII abandoned Naples almost as fast as he
had taken it, the moment he learned of the league forming against him
and, worse, Ludovico Sforza's own defection to it. Leaving a viceroy
to hold what he could, Charles marched north with eight thousand
troops, passed again through a Rome now abandoned by the Pope, and
crossed the Apennines toward Parma - only to find the league's own
combined army waiting for him at Fornovo, on the Taro river. Despite
commanding the smaller force, Charles attacked on 6 July 1495 and,
after a hard-fought engagement, forced his own way through the
confederate line and reached the safety of Asti, making peace with
Sforza on the spot. Naples turned against its French occupiers almost
as soon as Charles was gone, and the exiled Neapolitan king, returning
with Spanish reinforcements under Gonzalo de Córdoba, reclaimed his
kingdom - though he did not live to enjoy it long. Charles, undeterred,
was already planning a second Italian expedition before the year was
out, this time aimed at Milan itself.

Ludovico Sforza's own alarm at the threatened invasion was enough to
draw Maximilian back into Italy himself, though with a force of only
five hundred horse and eight companies of foot - a fraction of what
his own hopes for German reinforcement had led him to expect. He
crossed the Alps only to discover Charles had already postponed the
expedition that supposedly justified the trip; no German troops joined
him, and his own allies, no longer needing his presence, began hinting
broadly that he should go home. Unwilling to leave without some
visible achievement, Maximilian was eventually permitted only a lesser
consolation: a siege of Livorno, meant to pry Florence loose from its
French alliance. He blockaded the port by sea, but could not actually
stop supplies reaching the defenders, and when a storm scattered his
own ships he was forced into a land siege his allies undermined at
every turn - the Venetian generals in particular slowing the effort
until Maximilian, recognizing his coalition partners had no real
interest in his success, vented his fury on their ambassadors and
abandoned the whole enterprise in December 1496. Coxe judges the
episode harshly: a "precipitate and romantic" venture that cost
Maximilian real standing as both head of the empire and a prince of
genuine ability - the second time in two years his own Italian
ambitions had collapsed for want of the resources needed to match
them.
