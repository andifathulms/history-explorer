Albert V died leaving a pregnant widow and three thrones with three
different rules for succession: Austria passed only through male
heirs, while Hungary and Bohemia were both elective kingdoms whose
estates could, in principle, choose anyone. Hungary's nobles, dreading
a long regency with the Ottomans pressing their southern frontier,
pushed Elizabeth toward marrying the King of Poland and handing him
the crown outright - and the ambassadors carrying that offer had
barely left when Elizabeth gave birth to a son, baptized Ladislaus and
known ever after as Ladislaus Posthumus. Too late to recall the
embassy, she answered with audacity instead: in May 1440 she carried
the infant to Székesfehérvár, where the Archbishop of Esztergom
crowned him king of Hungary on his own mother's chest. Hungary split
into two camps, one behind the adult Polish claimant and his ablest
general John Hunyadi, the other loyal to the infant and his widowed
mother - strong enough to hold Upper Hungary but too weak to dislodge
Hunyadi's party from the rest of the kingdom. Elizabeth retreated to
Vienna, placed her son under Frederick of Styria's guardianship, and
mortgaged both a border city and the sacred Crown of St. Stephen
itself to secure his support; she died at Győr in December 1442, "not
without suspicions of poison," and the Polish claimant simply assumed
full royal title in the vacuum her death left behind.

Bohemia handled the same problem with a Habsburg twist of its own.
Elizabeth had claimed the crown for her infant son immediately, but
the Calixtin party balked - "a child cannot govern a kingdom" - and
offered it first to a Bavarian duke, who declined it as belonging to
Ladislaus by right, then to Frederick himself, who answered with the
same principle in almost the same words: "I will not rob my relation
of his right." The Bohemians named Ladislaus king in absentia and
governed through two rival co-regents until factional infighting
delivered sole power, in practice if not in name, to a rising figure
named George of Poděbrady. Hungary, meanwhile, was handed back to
Ladislaus by circumstance rather than negotiation once his Polish
rival died fighting the Ottomans at Varna in 1444 - though Frederick
still refused to surrender either the boy or the Hungarian crown he
held, leaving the kingdom to Hunyadi's regency instead.

## A ransom by siege

Frederick's reluctance hardened once his own fortunes rose: he took
Ladislaus to Rome for his own imperial coronation rather than release
him to any of the three kingdoms clamoring for his return, and left
him in papal custody afterward when he continued on to Naples. An
attempt by deputies from all three nations to help the boy escape was
discovered and blocked. Frustrated nobles finally gave up on
negotiation entirely: an Austrian noble named Ulrich Eyczing raised an
army of sixteen thousand men and laid siege to Frederick's own
residence at Wiener Neustadt, and in 1452, with no real choice left,
Frederick gave up his ward. Ladislaus entered Vienna to genuine public
celebration under a new three-way regency - Hunyadi governing Hungary,
Poděbrady governing Bohemia, and the boy's own maternal uncle, the
Count of Cilli, taking Austria - that finally gave each kingdom the
local authority it had been demanding for over a decade, even without
their sovereign's full attention. Cilli used his privileged access to
purge Austrian nobles from office and seed the young king's mind with
suspicion of both Hunyadi and Poděbrady, until Eyczing - the very man
who had freed Ladislaus from Frederick in the first place - turned on
Cilli in turn and drove him from court under threat from an angry
crowd. Ladislaus was crowned king of Bohemia that October, guided
entirely by Poděbrady's counsel, though his religious sympathies,
shaped by years among Catholic tutors, already ran against the
Calixtin majority he was now expected to rule.
