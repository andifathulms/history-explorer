Albert V's own guardians, uncles Leopold and Ernest, had actively
encouraged him toward hunting and idle amusement rather than
government - a young ward kept distracted was a young ward who did
not ask why his own inheritance was being plundered. His tutors
thought otherwise, giving him a genuine education in the sciences,
statecraft and religion despite his guardians' preferences, and when
Leopold died in 1411, Emperor Sigismund declared the fourteen-year-old
of age early rather than let Ernest's own misrule continue unchecked.
Vienna greeted him as a deliverer. Albert, who had watched his uncles'
feuding wreck the country for a decade, answered that welcome by
abandoning the hunting and amusements that had defined his childhood
and applying himself seriously to government - sound laws, close
attention to justice, a sustained campaign against the banditry that
had run unchecked through his uncles' chaotic regency. Austria, by its
own chroniclers' proud account, became an island of order while its
neighbors tore themselves apart, a striking reversal for a duchy that
only a few years earlier had been in what Coxe calls a "deplorable
state."

## Alone successful

Much of that transformation rested on one relationship: Albert's bond
with his father-in-law, Sigismund, King of Hungary and Bohemia and
Holy Roman Emperor, whose own turbulent path to three crowns had
taught him exactly how much a stable, capable ally was worth.
Bohemia's religious wars became Albert's proving ground. During
Sigismund's third campaign against the Hussites in 1431, the main
German army broke and fled the moment the Hussites appeared, while
Albert turned his own Austrian troops into Moravia instead, drove out
the raiding Taborites, and held the province securely for the rest of
the war - Coxe's own verdict blunt: while every other German
contingent panicked at the mere name of the Hussites, Albert "at the
head of his brave Austrians, was alone successful."

Sigismund's gratitude went further than praise. Over the opposition of
his own wife - who wanted her daughter's inheritance to go instead to
the King of Poland, whom she hoped to marry herself despite a
forty-year age gap - Sigismund worked to secure Albert the succession
to Hungary and Bohemia outright, arresting the empress when her
scheming grew too dangerous to ignore. Dying and thwarted at every
turn but this one, Sigismund summoned the nobles of both kingdoms to
his deathbed in 1437 and delivered what Coxe records as a direct plea
rather than a formal decree: "I give him my kingdom, or rather I give
him to my kingdoms, to whom I can give or wish nothing better." Those
present seized Albert's hand in tears and swore allegiance on the
spot. Sigismund died the next day.

## The crown his family had wanted for a century and a half

Hungary accepted him without real difficulty. Bohemia proved harder -
the Hussites rejected the succession outright, preferring a Polish
candidate they could "converse with... without an interpreter" to
another German ruler, and brought in Polish cavalry to back the claim
- but Albert moved faster than his rivals, was crowned at Prague amid
genuine celebration, and forced a truce that closed the contest for
good. In the midst of this fighting, the German electors turned to
him as well, judging his extensive territories and proven talents the
best available answer to the empire's own disorder and the rising
Ottoman threat together. He was never formally crowned emperor, but
Coxe judges he "obtained and deserved a place among the emperors" all
the same: for the first time since Rudolf I founded the House of
Austria on the imperial throne a century and a half earlier, and the
first time ever, a Habsburg duke held Austria, Hungary, Bohemia and
the German crown all at once.

He governed the empire itself with the same competence he had already
shown in Austria - reforming corrupt courts, curbing the secret
tribunals of Westphalia, sketching an early version of the public
peace and administrative "circles" system a later Habsburg emperor
would finish building. But the crisis that ended his reign came from
the east, where Sultan Murad II's armies, already masters of Smyrna,
Thessalonica and much of Greece, saw Sigismund's death as their
opening to strike before Albert could consolidate what he had just
inherited. He hurried to defend the Serbian fortress of Smederevo and
was still in the field when it fell and its garrison was massacred;
dysentery tore through both camps before the armies could properly
engage, forcing the Ottomans to retreat - and striking Albert just as
hard. Advised to rest, he pressed on toward Vienna anyway, repeating
"I shall recover if I can only once more behold the walls of Vienna,"
and worsened his own condition further by eating melons on the
journey. He died short of the city, in a village near Esztergom, on 17
October 1439 - forty-three years old, and not even two full years
into the reign that had, for one brief moment, reunited under a single
Habsburg ruler more territory than his family had ever held before or
would hold again for generations.
