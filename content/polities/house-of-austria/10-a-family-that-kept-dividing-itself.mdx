Albert IV, sixteen when his father died, spent his short reign
yielding ground rather than fighting for it - content to let his
cousin Leopold's branch of the family absorb every Habsburg territory
but Austria itself in the interest of peace, and settling a disputed
succession with his own cousin William on joint rule for life rather
than contest it. What his own reign is chiefly remembered for is a
pilgrimage: he traveled to Jerusalem and came home with the epithet
"the Wonder of the World," even though Coxe notes dryly that the
actual business of his rule offered "few remarkable events in the
sober pages of history." One campaign did reach him regardless - an
alliance with King Sigismund of Hungary against a Moravian warlord
ended in a surrender feast that turned out to be a poisoning; Albert
died of it at Klosterneuburg in 1404, twenty-seven years old, leaving
the Austrian succession, once again, to a child too young to rule.

## Four Habsburg administrations at once

That child, Albert V, was seven, and the cousin who took over his
guardianship, William, governed competently until his own sudden,
childless death in 1406 - the moment that actually broke the family
apart. An old prediction of Emperor Charles IV's, that the Habsburgs
would one day humble themselves by dividing their own inheritance,
played out in full: the house split permanently into an Albertine
line holding Austria for the child Albert V, and a Leopoldine line
descended from Leopold III, itself split three ways among his
surviving sons - Leopold the elder keeping Switzerland, Alsace and
Swabia, Ernest taking Styria, Carinthia and Carniola, and Frederick
receiving Tyrol. Four separate Habsburg administrations now governed
what had once been, however imperfectly, a single family patrimony.
Leopold the elder had already had his own costly adventure abroad,
losing an entire army at Lake Garda in 1401 and buying his own freedom
from Milanese captivity only by promising to abandon the campaign; he
returned to ordinary administration until William's death called him
back to Vienna to share young Albert V's guardianship with his brother
Ernest - a co-regency neither man wanted and neither could make work.

## A country left to banditry

The result was exactly what a shared, contested guardianship over a
distant child-heir usually produces: two rival factions, power
swinging back and forth between them as each gained temporary
advantage, and both using every swing to enrich themselves and punish
the other's supporters through what Coxe calls "a species of legal
tyranny." With no strong hand actually running the country, the
nobility reverted to the lawlessness earlier dukes had spent
generations suppressing - robbers again infested the roads, towns
were plundered with impunity, and Austria fell, in Coxe's judgment,
into a state of disorder worse than at any point since the House of
Habsburg had first taken possession of it more than a century earlier.
Leopold died in 1411, forty years old and childless, leaving Austria's
disorder to whichever of his brothers, or his own young ward, could
finally impose the order four generations of dividing and subdividing
the family's own inheritance had made impossible to keep.
