---
title: Reducing Ghent
drafted_from: coxe-austria-1847

Mary of Burgundy's death on 26 March 1482, still young and leaving two
infant children, undid much of what her marriage had secured.
Flanders's own turbulent population had never fully accepted
Maximilian as anything but a foreign consort, and once Mary was gone
they treated his continued war against France as personal ambition
rather than a defense of their own interests. The Flemish estates
seized custody of the young Philip, appointed his guardians
themselves, and shut Maximilian out of his own son's upbringing
entirely - a dispute Louis XI exploited immediately, negotiating
directly with the Flemish states over Maximilian's head. The
resulting Treaty of Arras, concluded that December, forced terms
Maximilian had no real power to refuse: his infant daughter Margaret
was betrothed to the French dauphin and sent to France as his intended
bride, carrying Franche-Comté and Artois as her dowry, while Philip's
guardianship remained with the Flemish estates alone.

The estates' own suspicion of Maximilian only deepened from there -
his continued presence nearby, his preference for foreign advisors,
his resentment at losing any share in governing Flanders. War followed,
with Flemish rebels backed openly by France, but Maximilian pressed
his own military advantage hard enough that by July 1485 Ghent itself
submitted, its burghers forced to recognize him as guardian of his son
and governor of Flanders in exchange for his promise not to remove
Philip from the Netherlands. With Flanders reasserted, Maximilian
turned toward France itself, allying with the Duke of Orléans and the
Duke of Brittany against the French regency - a coalition that
collapsed entirely once the young King Charles VIII took the field in
person, recovered Thérouanne, overran Brittany, and crushed the rebels
outright at Saint-Aubin. Orléans was captured, and on 28 August 1488
the Duke of Brittany signed a peace reducing his own duchy to French
vassalage.
