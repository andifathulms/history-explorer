While Frederick wandered Germany as a landless exile, he pursued a
diplomatic project that would ultimately matter more to his family's
future than anything he lost to Matthias: a marriage between his son
Maximilian and Mary, heiress of Burgundy - by then the richest
territorial bloc in Western Europe, stretching from Provence to the
North Sea through generations of dukes who had accumulated Flanders,
Artois, Holland, Brabant and more through purchase, inheritance and
outright extortion. Duke Charles the Bold offered a bargain: his only
daughter and heiress for Maximilian, if Frederick would elevate
Burgundy into a kingdom in its own right. Frederick traveled to Trier
with the fourteen-year-old Maximilian, formally invested Charles with
several imperial territories, and watched Charles prepare every
regalia for a royal coronation - a throne built in the cathedral, the
princess already affianced - before abruptly leaving under a flimsy
excuse. The real cause, Coxe explains, was French interference: Louis
XI had worked on Frederick's own naturally suspicious temperament,
warning that a crowned Charles would never be satisfied and would soon
revive his old ambitions for the imperial crown itself. Neither side
fully trusted the other to begin with, and the standoff collapsed the
arrangement without a wedding or a coronation.

Charles the Bold died in battle at Nancy on 5 January 1477, and his
death threatened to unravel everything at once. Louis XI moved
immediately, claiming Mary's hand for his own son while seizing
Burgundy and parts of Artois and Flanders outright; Mary's own
counselors, bought or terrified, urged her to accept the French match,
while the people of Ghent rose in revolt and effectively held their
own sovereign hostage to extract political concessions. By every
practical measure, Maximilian's cause had no real support anywhere in
Mary's own court or country.

## A letter and a ring, kept

What saved the match was the impression Maximilian had made at Trier:
Charles had returned from that failed ceremony full of praise for his
prospective son-in-law, calling him the most promising prince in
Europe - words that had lodged deeply enough in Mary's own memory
that when she became free to choose for herself, they mattered more
than any councilor's advice. When the Elector of Mainz presented the
letter and ring she had once sent Maximilian at her own father's
direction, she recognized them as tokens of a promise she intended to
keep, and no amount of protest from her own advisors could move her.
The marriage was solemnized by proxy in April 1477.

Maximilian arrived in person that August, and it was what he did next
that secured his position for good. Only eighteen, with no army of his
own, he rallied the Burgundian Netherlands' defense, held off the
French advance, and won his new subjects' loyalty by confirming their
traditional privileges rather than ruling by force; pressure from the
German diet forced Louis to restore several imperial territories he
had seized, freeing Maximilian to besiege Thérouanne and win a bloody,
decisive victory at Guinegate on 7 August 1479 that stabilized
Burgundian rule over the Low Countries for good - proof, delivered on
a battlefield rather than in a marriage contract, that the Habsburg
claim to Burgundy could actually be defended.
