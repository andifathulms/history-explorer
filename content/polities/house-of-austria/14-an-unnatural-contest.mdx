The 1458 partition of Austria - Lower Austria to Frederick, Upper
Austria to his brother Albert VI, a slice of Carinthia to their cousin
Sigismund, with all three sharing Vienna as a joint residence - solved
nothing. Frederick's own indolence made him unpopular with the
capital's citizens, and Albert, never satisfied with his own share of
the inheritance, fanned that discontent into open war. Vienna split
into factions, the senate backing the emperor and the populace
rallying behind a demagogue named Wolfgang Holzer in Albert's cause.
Frederick sheltered in the citadel with barely two hundred men,
resolved, in his own words, to be buried under its ruins before he
would surrender to his own rebellious subjects - and when a messenger
reached the empire's assembled princes warning that the garrison had
only three weeks of food left, they voted to send help too slowly, as
always, to matter. What actually saved him was faster and closer to
home: George of Poděbrady, King of Bohemia, marched thirteen thousand
troops to break the siege in person and then personally arbitrated a
settlement between the warring brothers.

## The end of the feud

The arbitration lasted no longer than any earlier settlement between
them. Albert soon tried to claim Lower Austria outright and took
Vienna's own oath of allegiance; Frederick answered by securing both
the imperial ban and a papal excommunication against his own brother.
Mediators from every direction tried to stop what Coxe calls an
"unnatural contest" between siblings, and Albert's own governance in
the interval did more to undo him than Frederick's diplomacy ever
managed - arbitrary rule and constant exactions alienated the very
Viennese who had once backed him, and even Holzer, the demagogue who
had led the earlier revolt against Frederick, switched sides and began
working for the emperor's restoration. Albert discovered the plot,
had Holzer executed, and confiscated the property of everyone
connected to it - a final, vindictive act before he died suddenly on 4
December 1463, in the middle of preparing to renew the war one more
time. With his death Frederick lost an "active and turbulent rival,"
in Coxe's own phrase, without having to defeat him outright - a war
that had cost Vienna two sieges and dragged in a Bohemian king as
arbitrator, closed not by a settlement either brother had wanted, but
simply because one of them stopped living long enough to keep
fighting it.
