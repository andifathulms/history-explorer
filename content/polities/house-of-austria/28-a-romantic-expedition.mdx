Germany's own paralysis at Worms turned out not to matter as much as
Maximilian feared: Charles VIII abandoned Naples almost as fast as he
had taken it, the moment he learned of the league forming against him
and, worse, Ludovico Sforza's own defection to it. Leaving a viceroy
to hold what he could, Charles marched north with eight thousand
troops, passed again through a Rome now abandoned by the Pope, and
crossed the Apennines toward Parma - only to find the league's own
combined army waiting for him at Fornovo, on the Taro river. Despite
commanding the smaller force, Charles attacked on 6 July 1495 and,
after a hard-fought engagement, forced his own way through the
confederate line and reached the safety of Asti, making peace with
Sforza on the spot. Naples turned against its French occupiers almost
as soon as Charles was gone, and the exiled Neapolitan king, returning
with Spanish reinforcements under Gonzalo de Córdoba, reclaimed his
kingdom - though he did not live to enjoy it long. Charles, undeterred,
was already planning a second Italian expedition before the year was
out, this time aimed at Milan itself.

## A blockade that never worked

Ludovico Sforza's own alarm at the threatened invasion was enough to
draw Maximilian back into Italy himself, though with a force of only
five hundred horse and eight companies of foot - a fraction of what
his own hopes for German reinforcement had led him to expect. He
crossed the Alps only to discover Charles had already postponed the
expedition that supposedly justified the trip; no German troops joined
him, and his own allies, no longer needing his presence, began hinting
broadly that he should go home. Unwilling to leave without some
visible achievement, Maximilian was eventually permitted only a lesser
consolation: a siege of Livorno, meant to pry Florence loose from its
French alliance. He blockaded the port by sea, but could not actually
stop supplies reaching the defenders, and when a storm scattered his
own ships he was forced into a land siege his allies undermined at
every turn - the Venetian generals in particular slowing the effort
until Maximilian, recognizing his coalition partners had no real
interest in his success, vented his fury on their ambassadors and
abandoned the whole enterprise in December 1496. Coxe judges the
episode harshly: a "precipitate and romantic" venture that cost
Maximilian real standing as both head of the empire and a prince of
genuine ability - the second time in two years his own Italian
ambitions had collapsed for want of the resources needed to match
them.
