Rudolf IV, who won Tyrol for his family by moving faster than anyone
else expected, spent much of the rest of his short reign on an
ambition no treaty could satisfy: making his own family look, on
parchment and in seal-wax, as ancient and exalted as it had just
become in fact. He combed the "musty records" of Austria's earlier
dukes and revived every privilege and title any predecessor had ever
held or merely claimed, commissioning seals stamped with invented
hieroglyphs and occult characters - one showed a man's head with five
faces - and an alphabet mixing Greek, Runic, Roman and Arabic letters
for the mottoes cut into them. He rarely signed his own letters with
his name, closing them instead with his age, his regnal year, and the
phrase Hoc est verum - this is true.

## A title too far

What actually provoked the emperor was wounded pride rather than any
of the antiquarian invention itself. Charles IV's own recent Golden
Bull had ranked the seven electors above every other prince in the
empire, and Rudolf, denied a vote he had no real claim to, answered by
assuming the title Archduke Palatine, citing an old grant supposedly
made by Frederick I to the earlier Babenberg dukes of Austria - a
document no source outside Rudolf's own chancery had ever seen.
Charles at first dismissed the whole affair as antiquarian
eccentricity, but Rudolf's growing ostentation eventually forced his
hand: he summoned his own son-in-law before the diet repeatedly and
ordered him to drop the invented arms and return to those his father
had actually borne. Rudolf gave up the fanciful seals and the
"Palatine" in public use, but never fully let go of "Archduke" itself.

The title outlived the rebuke that tried to kill it. Confirmed for the
House of Austria in earnest by a later emperor, Frederick III, decades
after Rudolf's own death, it became the family's own standing style -
a fabrication invented to answer one Golden Bull's insult that ended
up outlasting the document that provoked it.

## What else the same reign left behind

Rudolf's shorter-lived successes were more conventional. A dispute
with the Patriarch of Aquileia over contested fiefs in Styria,
Carinthia and Carniola ended in an imperial ban against the patriarch,
several fortresses taken by force, and a peace that installed an
Austrian judge and garrison in Friuli. And in Vienna he founded a
university meant to rival Prague's own - which within a few years
became the second such institution in Germany, and outlasted every
invented seal and disputed title from the same reign as the one
genuine monument Rudolf left behind. He died of a fever in Italy in
1365, twenty-six years old and without an heir, having spent a brief
reign trying to manufacture on parchment the standing his own conquest
of Tyrol had already won him in fact.
