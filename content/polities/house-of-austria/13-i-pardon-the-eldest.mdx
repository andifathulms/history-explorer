Ladislaus's own dependence on his regents met its sternest test at
Belgrade, the fortress guarding Hungary's southern approach, where
Sultan Mehmed II laid siege with an army and a river flotilla in 1456.
John Hunyadi, still regent in all but title, broke the Ottoman ships
blocking the Danube and Sava and reached the garrison in time to
resupply it before the sultan's main assault came - an assault that
nearly succeeded anyway, Ottoman troops planting their own banners on
the ramparts before Hunyadi's presence turned a collapsing defense
into a counterattack that drove the Turks out with heavy losses and
wounded Mehmed himself. The siege broke after a day and a night of
fighting, and the Ottoman army withdrew having lost some thirty
thousand men. Hunyadi did not long survive his own greatest victory -
a fever brought on by the exertions of the siege killed him at Zemun
that August, leaving two sons, Ladislaus and Matthias, whom Coxe
credits with promise to match their father's fame.

## A murder, a pardon, a betrayal

Ladislaus Posthumus felt the loss chiefly as relief: freed from a
regent more powerful than the king himself, he transferred his
patronage to Count Cilli, his own maternal uncle, and nominated him
governor of Hungary - inflaming the nobles who had idolized Hunyadi's
memory. The mutual suspicion broke into open violence in March 1457,
when Cilli and the elder Hunyadi brother met by chance, traded bitter
accusations, and Cilli seized a sword from a bystander and wounded
Ladislaus Hunyadi in the head; Ladislaus's own followers rushed to his
defense, and Cilli was mortally wounded in the brawl that followed.

Hunyadi's widow met the king soon after, dressed in mourning with her
younger son Matthias at her side, and threw herself at his feet
begging forgiveness for her elder son's act. Ladislaus raised her up
himself, promised to treat her as a mother and her sons as brothers,
pardoned the elder Hunyadi outright, and sent the family away laden
with gifts after a day of shared festivity. Reassured, both brothers
followed the king back to Buda - where, once they had let their guard
down completely, German troops sealed the palace gates, arrested both
young men, and beheaded Ladislaus Hunyadi on the spot. The betrayal of
a solemn pardon turned Hungary against its own king outright: Hunyadi's
widow rallied her husband's old supporters and threatened to hand
Hungary's border fortresses to the Ottomans unless Matthias was freed,
forcing Ladislaus to flee the kingdom under public curses, taking the
surviving brother with him to Vienna as a hostage.

## Deliver us from evil

Ladislaus's own reign ended before the consequences could fully play
out. He traveled to Prague to marry a French princess, fell suddenly
and mortally ill in the midst of the wedding preparations, and died
within thirty-six hours, seventeen years old, reciting the Lord's
Prayer and asking, as a final renunciation of worldly vanity, that his
own prized golden hair be cut off. Coxe's own verdict on his short
life is far less generous than the mourning that followed it: a
"feeble and irresolute character" who never once showed the courage
his father had displayed at the same age, and whose betrayal of the
Hunyadi family would, had he lived, likely have kept Hungary in open
revolt. German rumor blamed his sudden death on poison and pointed
directly at Poděbrady, who went on to claim the Bohemian throne once
it fell vacant; Coxe sides with the Bohemian historians who
investigated the charge and concluded the actual cause was plague -
though the accusation outlived any proof for it, and the fight over
what Ladislaus left behind was only just beginning.
