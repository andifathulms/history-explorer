In January 1641 Portuguese Melaka fell to a Dutch company siege, after a
hundred and thirty years.

Johor supplied ships, men and the blockade. The dynasty that had been driven
out of that city in 1511 helped take it back — and did not get it. The company
kept Melaka, and then set tariffs and enforcement that drove the traffic away
from it, and much of that traffic went to Johor.

It is about as close as any polity in these waters came to recovering what a
European power had taken from it — and what was recovered was a share of the
trade rather than the place.

What Albuquerque had taken in 1511 was a customs house rather than a trade, and
the commerce had dispersed to Aceh, Johor, Banten, Brunei and Patani. The same
thing now happened to the Dutch. They took the city, the trade left again, and
the polity that received it the second time was the one that had helped them
take it.

## The orang laut, for the third time

Johor's fighting strength at sea was the *orang laut* — the sea peoples of the
straits, estuaries and islands.

They had served Srivijaya. They had served Melaka. They served Johor. A
thousand years on the same water, the same arrangement each time: the strait
was safe because the people who could have made it unsafe were attached to
whoever was claiming it.

Very little in these centuries persists like that. Institutions of this kind
normally last a dynasty and are replaced; this one outlasted the polity it
belonged to twice, transferring each time to whoever held the Melakan line.

That is not a metaphor about loyalty. Andaya's account makes it a specific and
fragile arrangement, and the next chapter is about what happened when the thing
they were attached to stopped existing.
