Johor's court did not sit still. In its first two centuries the capital
moved along the Johor River more than half a dozen times, almost always in
response to an attack: Sayong Pinang from the founding in 1528, Johor Lama
from 1535, and then, after the Portuguese burned Johor Lama in 1587, a new
seat thirty-five kilometres upriver at Batu Sawar - too far from the coast
for a Portuguese warship to reach with its heavy guns, and close enough to
the tide that trading vessels could still ride the current in and out.

## What a Dutch admiral found there

When the Dutch admiral Cornelis Matelieff visited in 1606, he found a
walled town of roughly four thousand fighting men, houses built on stilts
along the river, and a second settlement, Kota Seberang, on the opposite
bank. He also found a capital he judged badly exposed: its defensive
works stood on ground that flooded at every spring tide, and Johor's own
nobles, though they had promised to build the bulwarks he recommended,
let the work lapse the moment his fleet sailed on.

## A court divided between two patrons

The vulnerability was not only physical. Sultan Ali Jalla bin Abdul Jalil
had died around 1597 leaving four sons to share power - a nominal
Yang di-Pertuan, and his brothers Raja Siak, Raja Laut and Raja Bongsu -
and the court split between them into factions backing, respectively, the
Portuguese at Melaka and the newly arrived Dutch. Portugal answered
Johor's part in the 1603 seizure of the Portuguese carrack *Santa
Catarina* with a riverine blockade that dragged into 1604, and imposed
another for most of a year from 1609 to 1610. Each time, trade that would
have passed through Johor went instead to Patani. Peace was finally
struck with Portugal in October 1610 - a victory for the pro-Portuguese
faction at court, and a defeat for Raja Bongsu, the one brother the Dutch
themselves regarded as reliable.

## What Aceh took while everyone was looking at Melaka

That peace was the pretext, or perhaps only the occasion, for what
followed. At the end of June 1613 Sultan Iskandar Muda of Aceh, ambitious
for dominance on both shores of the strait and unsettled by an arrangement
between his two rivals, launched a seaborne assault on Johor with a fleet
whose numbers overwhelmed the town's defences. Singapore, base of Johor's
own navy, and Batu Sawar itself were burned. The Dutch factory's own losses
alone were reckoned at 45,000 guilders in pepper, cloth, gold and cash. The
old Yang di-Pertuan was carried off and is said to have died either a
captive in Aceh or a broken man on the island of Bintan.

Raja Bongsu - the pro-Dutch brother, and by Matelieff's account the only
one of the four with any credit left - was married to a sister of Iskandar
Muda and sent back to rule Johor as an Acehnese client under the name
Sultan Abdul Hammayat Shah. He rebuilt Batu Sawar, invited the Dutch in
1614 to scout a site for a fort of their own, and spent his first years
trying to pull the kingdom back together. It did not last: in November
1615 the Acehnese burned the rebuilt capital a second time. Winstedt's
verdict, that the fall of Batu Sawar closed one chapter of Johor's history
just as the fall of Johor Lama had closed the one before it, is the
judgement the Dutch factors on the spot reached in real time - they shut
their own Batu Sawar factory for good not long after, and moved their
business to Banten, Jambi, Aceh and, for a few more years, Patani. Sultan
Abdullah spent the rest of his reign moving from island to island and died
on Tambelan Besar in 1623, the last king of a capital that no longer
existed.
