In January 1641 Portuguese Melaka fell to a Dutch company siege, after a hundred
and thirty years.

Johor supplied ships, men and the blockade. The dynasty that had been driven out
of that city in 1511 helped take it back — and did not get it. The company kept
Melaka, and then set tariffs and enforcement that drove the traffic away from it,
and much of that traffic went to Johor.

It is the closest this corpus comes to a polity recovering what a European power
took from it, and the recovery is a share of the trade rather than the place.

The Melaka record's last chapter makes the point from the other end: what
Albuquerque took in 1511 was a customs house, not a trade, and the commerce
dispersed to Aceh, Johor, Banten, Brunei and Patani. Two of those five now have
pages here. What that chapter could not know is that the same thing happened to
the Dutch: they took the city, the trade left again, and the polity that received
it the second time was the one that had helped them take it.

## The orang laut, for the third time

This record's `military_basis` is `client-levy`, and the client is the *orang
laut* — the sea peoples of the straits, estuaries and islands.

They served Srivijaya. They served Melaka. They served Johor. Three records on
this site, spread across a thousand years on the same water, carrying the same
value in the same field for the same reason: the strait was safe because the
people who could have made it unsafe were attached to whoever was claiming it.

Nothing else in this corpus persists like that. Institutions in these fields
normally last a dynasty and get replaced; this one outlasted the polity it
belonged to twice, transferring each time to whoever held the Melakan line.

That is not a metaphor about loyalty. Andaya's account makes it a specific and
fragile arrangement, and the next chapter is about what happened when the thing
they were attached to stopped existing.
