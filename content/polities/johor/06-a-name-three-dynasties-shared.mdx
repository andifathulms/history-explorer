---
title: A name three dynasties shared
drafted_from: andaya-1975

The bendahara who took the throne in 1699 kept everything he could of what he
had just ended: the name Johor, the capital's regalia, the court offices his
own family had filled for generations under the sultans he served. What he
could not keep was the one thing that had made those things worth having — a
line whose descent nobody could dispute.

## A posthumous son, real or not

Within twenty years a challenger arrived who understood exactly where the new
dynasty was weakest. Raja Kecil of Siak claimed to be the posthumous son of
Mahmud Shah II, the sultan killed in 1699, and pressed that claim with an army
rather than a genealogy: he took Johor outright in 1718. Whether his birth
story was true or invented, it worked because it was aimed at the one
vulnerability the bendahara line could never fully answer — that it was not,
whatever else it was, the blood that had made Johor's throne legitimate for
three hundred years.

## Newcomers who did not bother with the claim at all

The bendahara family recovered the throne from Raja Kecil, but the fight had
shown every ambitious neighbour that Johor's kingship was now negotiable by
force rather than settled by descent. Bugis adventurers from Sulawesi, already
established as soldiers and traders throughout the strait, moved into that
opening directly. Rather than manufacture a genealogy of their own, they made
themselves the real power behind the throne at Riau, holding the office of
viceroy and running the state's actual affairs while a nominal sultan reigned
over what the Bugis allowed him to.

## What the name covered by the end

By the early nineteenth century "Johor" named a loose confederation split
between a Malay court largely ceremonial in its authority and a Bugis
leadership that actually governed, spread across the peninsula's southern tip
and the Riau-Lingga archipelago beyond it. In 1824 an Anglo-Dutch treaty
settled a colonial rivalry that had nothing to do with any Malay court's own
wishes, drawing a line through waters the sultanate had once claimed as a
single connected world and assigning each side to a different European
sphere. What had been one contested inheritance became, on paper, two
countries' worth of provinces — modern Johor in Malaysia and the Riau Islands
in Indonesia, both still carrying pieces of a name that had first meant the
Melaka dynasty in exile.
