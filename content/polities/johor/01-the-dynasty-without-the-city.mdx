Johor is what happened to Melaka's dynasty after it lost Melaka.

Alauddin Riayat Shah II was the second son of Mahmud Shah, the sultan who lost
Melaka to Albuquerque in 1511. His father had withdrawn up the peninsula and
died on Bintan; in 1528 Alauddin established himself on the Johor river with
the Melakan regalia, the Melakan court offices — bendahara, laksamana,
temenggung, in the same order — and the claim that this was the same sultanate
in a different place.

And with a chronicle. The *Sejarah Melayu*, the *Malay Annals*, was composed
here, at this court, a century after the city fell, to make exactly that
argument. It is the successor's own account of the thing it says it is
continuing — elegy written by the survivors, and a legal brief.

## A line, and nothing else

Most dynasties argue their right to rule from more than one direction. Johor
could argue it from exactly one.

There was no caliphal grant, no Ming seal, no Ottoman guns, no distinctive
religious authority of the kind Aceh built up the coast out of the ulama and
the pilgrimage traffic. What Johor had was a line of descent, and the line was
the whole argument. The whole seventeenth-century politics of the strait — who
could command the orang laut, who could summon the Malay lords, whose daughter
was worth marrying — ran on where a claimant stood relative to Melaka's
dynasty.

Which is why the last chapter here is what it is.
