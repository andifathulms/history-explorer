In 1673 Jambi, a Malay polity on the Sumatran side of the strait, sacked Johor
Lama. The court fled and re-established itself in the Riau islands.

It is worth stopping on, because it does not fit the story usually told about
this century.

The seventeenth-century strait is generally narrated as Asians and Europeans —
Portuguese Melaka, then Dutch Melaka, then the company's monopolies, with the
Malay states arranged around them. In the middle of the decade that had been
Johor's recovery, it was nearly destroyed by a neighbour of its own kind, over
a quarrel about precedence and trade in which no European had any part.

The same holds a few hundred miles north. Aceh spent the sixteenth century
besieging Melaka and fighting other Sumatran ports for the pepper coast, and
its worst single defeat, in 1629, came at Portuguese hands only because Aceh
had chosen to attack them.

A chronology organised around European arrival misses most of what these states
were actually doing, which was competing with each other.

## Riau

The court moved twice: Johor Lama from 1540, the Riau islands from 1673.

<Figure id="penyengat-island" />

The second move matters for what comes after. The islands at the mouth of the
strait were better placed for the trade than a river town up the Johor, and the
second half of the sultanate's life is the more prosperous one. The court that
was killed in 1699 was richer than the court that was sacked in 1673.
