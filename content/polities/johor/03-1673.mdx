In 1673 Jambi, a Malay polity on the Sumatran side of the strait, sacked Johor
Lama. The court fled and re-established itself in the Riau islands.

The chapter is here as a corrective. The seventeenth-century strait is usually
narrated as Asians and Europeans — Portuguese Melaka, then Dutch Melaka, then the
company's monopolies, with the Malay states arranged around them. In the middle
of the period this record's previous chapter calls a recovery, Johor was nearly
destroyed by a neighbour of its own kind, over a quarrel about precedence and
trade that had nothing to do with any European.

The same holds a few hundred miles north. This corpus's Aceh record spends the
sixteenth century besieging Melaka and fighting for the pepper coast against
other Sumatran ports, and its most consequential single defeat, in 1629, was
inflicted by the Portuguese only because Aceh had chosen to attack them.

A site organised around polities is fairly good at catching this and a narrative
organised around European arrival is not, which is one of the arguments for
organising it this way.

## Riau

The capital move is on the record: Johor Lama from 1540, Riau from 1673.

It matters for what comes after. The islands at the mouth of the strait were
better placed for the trade than a river town up the Johor, and the second half
of this record's life is the more prosperous one. The court that was killed in
1699 was richer than the court that was sacked in 1673.
