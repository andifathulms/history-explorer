Mahmud Shah II was killed by his own nobles in 1699, on his way to the mosque,
and the bendahara — the chief minister — took the throne.

The Melaka royal line, unbroken since about 1400, stopped in a road outside the
capital.

`ended.type` on this record is `internal usurpation` and `ended.by` is empty,
because what replaced the dynasty was its own chief minister's family, ruling
under the same name, in the same place, with the same regalia. There is no second
polity to name. This site's `successor_claims` on this record reads one, and the
item is those bendahara sultans: a claim to continue the thing made by the people
who ended it, which rule 1 counts without comment.

## Why a killing was a rupture

The previous chapter's institution is the answer.

Andaya's argument is that the *orang laut* were attached to the Melaka line
personally rather than to the office of the sultan — to a specific descent, which
this record's `legitimation` field carries as its only value. When the nobles
killed the last man in that line, the fleets that had made Johor enforceable at
sea went away, and the bendahara sultans could not get them back.

So an institution that had survived the fall of Srivijaya and the fall of Melaka,
transferring intact across a thousand years and three polities, ended because a
dynasty did. It is the clearest demonstration in this corpus that the coded
institutions on these pages are not free-standing facts about a place. They are
attached to something, and the schema has no field for what.

## What the century afterwards argued about

Everything. Johor's own bendahara sultans, the Bugis who took power at Riau,
Selangor, Perak, Pahang, Terengganu, and Raja Kecil of Siak — who claimed to be
Mahmud's posthumous son and took Johor on the strength of it in 1718 — all argued
their standing in terms of a line that had stopped in 1699.

That is where this record ends, and the reason it ends there rather than at the
Anglo-Dutch treaty of 1824 is the one this corpus's Dai Viet record gives on its
own first page. The name Johor continues for another hundred and twenty-five
years across three unrelated dynasties and a partition. Running one span over all
of it would produce a record about a country, whose succession field, revenue
field and ending would each have to be an average of things that contradict one
another.

The Melakan dynasty is the object. It began in 1400 on this site's Melaka record
and it ends here, three hundred years and two capitals later, and the two records
between them cover it end to end.
