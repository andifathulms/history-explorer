Mahmud Shah II was killed by his own nobles in 1699, on his way to the mosque,
and the bendahara — the chief minister — took the throne.

The Melaka royal line, unbroken since about 1400, stopped in a road outside the
capital.

Nothing was conquered. What replaced the dynasty was its own chief minister's
family, ruling under the same name, in the same place, with the same regalia —
and claiming, as the men who had just killed the last sultan, to be continuing
what they had ended.

## Why a killing was a rupture

The previous chapter's institution is the answer.

Andaya's argument is that the *orang laut* were attached to the Melaka line
personally rather than to the office of sultan — to a specific descent, which
was the whole of Johor's claim on them. When the nobles killed the last man in
that line, the fleets that had made Johor enforceable at sea went away, and the
bendahara sultans could not get them back.

So an arrangement that had survived the fall of Srivijaya and the fall of
Melaka, transferring intact across a thousand years and three polities, ended
because a dynasty did. It had never really been an institution of the state. It
was a personal attachment to a family, and it died with the family.

## What the century afterwards argued about

Everything. Johor's own bendahara sultans, the Bugis who took power at Riau,
Selangor, Perak, Pahang, Terengganu, and Raja Kecil of Siak — who claimed to be
Mahmud's posthumous son and took Johor on the strength of it in 1718 — all
argued their standing in terms of a line that had stopped in 1699.

The name Johor continues for another century and a quarter after that, across
three unrelated dynasties and a partition, and it is a different object under
the same word. What ends in 1699 is the older thing: a line that began at a
river mouth on the far side of the peninsula around 1400 and ran three hundred
years and two capitals before its own ministers ended it in a road.
