The Shammar are a large camel-herding confederation of northern Arabia, and
Jabal Shammar — the mountain country around the oasis of Ha'il — is their
country. In 1836 Abdullah ibn Rashid, a Shammar chief who had been in Saudi
service, took Ha'il and made himself its amir, with the second Saudi state's
acquiescence and initially as its client.

<Figure id="jabal-aja" />

A polity beginning with a subordinate who keeps what he was given to hold is the
most ordinary opening there is. The Tulunids and Ikhshidids did it to the
Abbasids in Egypt; the Rasulids did it to the Ayyubids in Yemen.

<Figure id="shammar-camp" />

Al-Rasheed's book is an anthropologist's study as much as a political history,
and its central question is the one to hold on to: why did a
camel-herding confederation, which had existed for centuries without a state,
become susceptible to political centralisation at that particular moment?

<Figure id="hail-south" />

Her answer is about position and money rather than about a great man. Ha'il sat
on the roads — the pilgrim route from Iraq and Persia to the holy cities, and
the caravan traffic between the Gulf, the Najd and Syria — and an amir who could
guarantee safe passage across Shammar territory had something to sell that no
tribal chief had been able to sell before, because the routes had not been that
valuable and the Ottomans had not been that interested in paying for them.

<Figure id="fayd-cistern" />

So the emirate was funded from outside: transit dues on what crossed its roads,
and subsidies from an empire that wanted a client in the interior it did not
have to garrison. It is the same arrangement the sharifs of Mecca lived on, in
poorer country and for a smaller price.
