By 1918 the emirate at Ha'il was being kept alive by the allies of the men
trying to destroy it, and starved by nobody in particular. The British officer
sitting in Riyadh trying to make sense of it called the whole arrangement, in a
phrase he plainly enjoyed, a regular Gilbertian situation.

His own instructions were the first absurdity. He was in the middle of Arabia,
twelve to fifteen days from any source of accurate information, charged with
preventing Ibn Sa'ud from attacking the neighbours who were Britain's allies —
the sharif of Mecca, the Ajman, Kuwait — and with inducing him to attack the
Shammar of Ha'il, who were allied to the Turks.

<Figure id="gulf-sketch-map" />

The neighbours he was to protect were the ones supplying the enemy.

## The blockade that leaked

Ha'il's lifeline ran east to the Gulf. The shaikh of Kuwait, consistently
hostile to Ibn Sa'ud, and the Ajman tribes, were conniving at the passage of
provisions and other necessaries up to Ha'il; a large caravan had recently gone
on from there to Damascus. The Ajman also raided into Ibn Sa'ud's territory
whenever a reasonable opportunity offered.

<Figure id="pilgrim-caravan" />

When the blockade restrictions at Kuwait were lifted, the port received the news
with joy. In the Najd it was taken for granted that the British, for some
strange reason, positively wished the Turks to get what they needed. And the
conclusion drawn there was immediate and correct: Ha'il would live again, and
could face the coming Wahhabi attack with equanimity, now that its enemy could
no longer use the economic weapon.

Ibn Sa'ud declined all further responsibility for the leakage and demanded that
everything intended for the Najd be landed at Bahrain instead.

## The other war

Meanwhile the sharif of Mecca's son was negotiating a treaty of peace and
friendship with Ibn Rashid — the Turks' ally — while his brothers prosecuted the
war against the Turks in the north, and while he himself prepared an expedition
against the Najd with arms and ammunition supplied by the British.

His agents turned up in the Qasim buying camels for that campaign and were sent
about their business. Circulars arrived announcing the peace with Ibn Rashid, an
impending meeting to settle the affairs of north and south, and directions for
the tribes to rally to the sharifian standard, with some offensive remarks about
Ibn Sa'ud included.

Every one of the parties in this was a British ally.

## What it tells you about Ha'il

Strip out the farce and a hard fact is left underneath.

The Rashidi emirate at this date could not feed itself from its own country. It
had been built on the caravan and pilgrim roads and on Ottoman subsidies, and by
1918 the roads were a war zone and the subsidising empire was losing. What kept
it going was the calculation of third parties — a Gulf shaikh who feared Ibn
Sa'ud more than he disliked Ha'il, tribes who profited from the traffic, and a
British government weighing the Najd against Mecca and finding for Mecca.

<Figure id="desert-chiefs" />

A state whose survival depends on which way other people's interests happen to
point is not, at that moment, a power. Ha'il had been the paramount power in
central Arabia twenty-five years earlier and had held Riyadh itself. By 1918 it
was an object in other people's arithmetic, and the arithmetic was about to
change.
