This record's `succession_rule` is null, and the corpus has a good many of
those. This is the one where the emptiness of the field is itself the subject.

Between Muhammad ibn Rashid's death in 1897 and the fall of Ha'il in 1921 the
emirate had roughly eight rulers. Most were killed. They were killed by cousins,
by nephews, by brothers, in the palace and on campaign, in a sequence that
al-Rasheed traces in detail and that reads, set out end to end, less like a
succession crisis than like a mechanism.

Her explanation is structural and is the reason her book is a study of tribal
politics rather than a chronicle. The Rashidi state was a lineage that had
acquired the resources of a state — subsidies, transit dues, a palace, a
treasury — without acquiring any of the things that ordinarily determine who
gets them. In a tribal confederation, leadership is contingent and negotiated
and can be withheld; the position is not property. Once it became property, and
a very valuable one, every member of the lineage with a plausible claim had both
a motive and a right to press it, and there was no institution above them to
decide.

The corpus keeps arriving at this from different directions and the Arabian
region has three versions of it side by side. The Zaydi imamate had a doctrine
that made the office elective and watched it become hereditary in the Qasimid
family, and the doctrine was revised to suit. The Omani imamate had the same
doctrine, watched the Al Bu Sa'id stop calling themselves imams, and the
interior spent two centuries reconstituting the office in protest. The Rashidis
had no doctrine at all — only a lineage, a town and an income — and what
happened is what happens when there is nothing to appeal to.

That is why the field is null rather than `factional`, which the rulebook
reserves for polities where a source describes the *absence* of a rule as the
arrangement. The Mamluks had no succession rule and everybody knew it and the
system worked on that basis for two and a half centuries. Ha'il had no rule and
nobody had decided that; it simply had not occurred to anyone that one would be
needed until the amir who did not need one had died.
