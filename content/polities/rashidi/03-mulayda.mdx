The second Saudi state destroyed itself in the war between Faysal's sons, and
Muhammad ibn Rashid was the arbitrator both sides appealed to. He arbitrated in
his own favour, by degrees, over fifteen years: taking the Qasim, installing
governors, holding Saudi princes at Ha'il as guests who could not leave, and in
1887 taking Riyadh outright.

In 1891, at Mulayda in the Qasim, the last serious Saudi attempt to recover the
position was beaten in the field. The Al Saud left central Arabia for Kuwait.

For about a decade after that Muhammad ibn Rashid was the paramount power in
central Arabia — Ha'il, Jabal Shammar, the Qasim, Riyadh, and influence from the
Syrian desert to the edge of the Empty Quarter. A family that had taken its oasis
in 1836 as clients of the Al Saud held the Al Saud's capital and had driven them
out of the country.

It is as complete a reversal as this corpus records anywhere, and this site's
`ended.by` on the Emirate of Nejd record points at it.

What Ha'il could not do was hold it. The emirate had no administration outside
its own oasis, no revenue from Riyadh that did not require a garrison to
collect, and no way of making the Qasim towns — which were commercial,
settled and had their own quarrel with Shammar nomads — content to be governed
from a Shammar capital. Muhammad ibn Rashid spent the last years of his reign
campaigning against risings in territory he had already conquered.

He died in 1897 without a son, which brings this page to the thing it is really
about.
