In January 1902 Abd al-Aziz ibn Abd al-Rahman Al Saud crossed the desert from
Kuwait with about forty men, went over the wall of Riyadh at night, waited
outside the Rashidi governor's house until morning, and killed him as he came
out. The town was his by the end of the day.

The twenty years that followed are the contest between the two families, and
the Rashidis lost it in stages. In 1906 at Rawdat Muhanna the amir Abd al-Aziz
ibn Mut'ib was killed in the field, and after that the emirate never had a ruler
who held it steadily long enough to fight a war. The Qasim went. The Ottomans,
who had been the Rashidi patron, were increasingly unable to subsidise anybody
and then, after 1918, did not exist. Ibn Saud had a British subsidy and, from
1912, the Ikhwan — settled bedouin organised in agricultural colonies around
the doctrine, which gave him something none of his predecessors in this region
had ever had, which was infantry that did not go home.

Ha'il fell in 1921 after a siege. The Rashidi family was taken to Riyadh, where
its surviving members were treated well and married into the Al Saud, which is
the customary end for a defeated Arabian house and is more than the second Saudi
state's imam received from Ibrahim Pasha.

The state that took Ha'il took the Hejaz in 1925, and the Kingdom of Saudi
Arabia was proclaimed in 1932. Central Arabia had spent two centuries producing
states that rose out of one oasis, took a great deal of country, and were
destroyed by somebody who could afford a longer war. The one that came out of
Riyadh in 1902 was the first that nobody finished — the Al Bu Sa'id dynasty
in Oman is the only other Arabian house from this same long pattern still on
its own throne, still without an ending to write.

Central Arabia's rulers across two and a half thousand years run from an
incense kingdom that built a dam to a camel confederation that lost an oasis,
and almost none of them succeeded one another in any real sense. Most of
these states had no successor and no predecessor at all. They are a
peninsula's worth of separate
answers to the question of how to hold ground that nobody else can be bothered
to hold, and the answers have almost nothing to do with each other.
