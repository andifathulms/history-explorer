Two English travellers crossed the Nafud to Ha'il in the winter of 1878 and
stayed as guests of Muhammad ibn Rashid. On the way, their Shammar guide told
them how the emirate had got rid of its garrison.

Soldiers had occupied Ha'il in the time of the first Ibn Rashid and been left
there afterwards as a garrison. Then either their government could not
communicate with them or forgot them; men died; and the remaining five hundred
or so were glad enough to agree to march home to Damascus under the escort of
Ubayd, the emir's brother, who had already decided to destroy them.

They rode out of Ha'il and followed their Shammar guides into the sand. To every
question about where the next wells were, the answer was a little further, a
little further on. Then the guides left them.

They seem to have been brave men. The last that was heard of them was a chorus
they sang as they struggled forward: *nahnu askar ma nahnu atasha — we are
soldiers, we are not thirsty, we soldiers want no water.* By noon they had lost
heart and lay down under the bushes for what shade there was, and that is how
they were found afterwards, scattered through the hollows.

Some of their horses got back to Jubba on their own and became the property of
whoever could catch them. They were sold for a few sheep or goats each.

*It is a ghastly tale*, the traveller wrote, and left it there.

## What the emirate was built out of

That is the founding transaction of Rashidi independence, and it sets the terms
for everything after it.

The family began as clients. Abdallah ibn Rashid, described to the Blunts as at
that time a mere private individual of the Abda section of Shammar, took service
with the Al Saud of the Najd and was appointed lieutenant of Jabal Shammar by
the Wahhabi amir. He was a great warrior, and with his brother Ubayd he reduced
the whole country to order.

So a Saudi appointee pacified a province for his employers, and his brother then
arranged the deaths of the garrison the outside power had left. Nothing was
declared. The next generation simply held Ha'il, and the one after that took
Riyadh.

## The reach

Blunt heard the sequence from people in Ha'il and set it down against what
Palgrave had published fifteen years earlier, noting where they disagreed.
Abdallah died in 1843. His son Talal took the title of amir and made himself
nearly independent of the Wahhabi government. Talal's brother and successor
Mit'ab conquered Jawf and Ithra to the north.

The names people at Ha'il actually talked about were not Talal's. It was Ubayd
they told stories about — hospitality, generosity, courage, the three cardinal
virtues, and the man who had counselled the destruction of the soldiers in the
Nafud. He was never amir. After his brother's death he effectively ruled the
country, and lived to a great age.

He left no property at all, having given everything away in his lifetime:
nothing but his sword, his mare and his young wife. These he left to his nephew
Muhammad, the reigning emir, with the request that the sword remain undrawn, the
mare unridden and the wife unmarried for ever afterwards.

Two of the three wishes were respected. Muhammad took the wife into his own
household.
