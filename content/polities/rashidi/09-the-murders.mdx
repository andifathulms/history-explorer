Between Muhammad ibn Rashid's death in 1897 and the fall of Ha'il in 1921 the
emirate had roughly eight rulers. Most of them were killed.

They were killed by cousins, by nephews, by brothers, in the palace and on
campaign, in a sequence that al-Rasheed traces in detail and that reads, set out
end to end, less like a succession crisis than like a mechanism.

## Why

Her explanation is structural, and it is why her book is a study of tribal
politics rather than a chronicle.

The Rashidi state was a lineage that had acquired the resources of a state —
subsidies, transit dues, a palace, a treasury — without acquiring any of the
things that ordinarily determine who gets them. In a tribal confederation,
leadership is contingent and negotiated and can be withheld: the position is not
property. Once it became property, and a very valuable piece of it, every member
of the lineage with a plausible claim had both a motive and a right to press it,
and there was no institution standing above them to decide between them.

## Three answers to one problem

The Arabian peninsula has three versions of this side by side, and Ha'il is the
one with nothing to fall back on.

The Zaydi imams of Yemen had a doctrine that made the office elective, watched it
become hereditary in the Qasimid family, and revised the doctrine to suit. The
Ibadi imams of Oman had much the same doctrine, watched the Al Bu Sa'id quietly
stop calling themselves imams, and the interior then spent two centuries
reconstituting the office in protest. Both had something to argue from.

The Rashidis had no doctrine at all. A lineage, a town and an income, and no
account of who was entitled to them.

It is worth distinguishing that from the Mamluk arrangement, which also had no
rule of succession and is often described the same way. The Mamluks had *decided*
they had no rule. Everybody knew it, the system was built around it, and it
worked on that basis for two and a half centuries. Ha'il had not decided
anything. It simply had not occurred to anyone that a rule would be needed, until
the amir who had never needed one was dead.
