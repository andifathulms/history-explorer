Doughty spent months at Ha'il in 1877 as the amir's guest, refusing to pretend he
was anything but a Christian, and was eventually sent away. What he wrote down
about the town is the best physical description of a central Arabian capital
there is, and what he wrote down about being a guest is a description of how the
Rashidi state worked.

<Figure id="doughty" />

The guest system first, because it is the state. Facing the castle across the
open square stood nine *makhzans*, lodging-rooms with wooden locks and a small
court, each with a dark guest-chamber behind it about fifteen feet by twelve. A
guest lived there at the amir's expense. If he wanted firewood in the cold
weather, he had to ask for it from the amir, sitting in the public *mejlis*.

<Figure id="kasr-plan" />

That last detail is the whole arrangement in one sentence. Hospitality on this
scale is a political instrument — feed every traveller, tribesman and merchant
who comes, and the roads and the tribes have a reason to prefer you — and the
instrument is administered face to face, in public, by the ruler in person, down
to the level of a bundle of sticks.

<Figure id="coffee" />

The town Doughty describes is new and prospering. Eleven wards, one-storey
houses built of thick clay layers bedded with sun-dried brick blocks set leaning,
copes and window sills finished with a stepped pinnacle he thought resembled the
carved cornices at Petra. No seeming of decay, he says, but rather of newness,
and thriving, and spending. Over many doorways he noticed small closed frames
like right-angled triangles, timbered above the entrance: shooting-down sconces,
machicolations for the defence of a single household's door.

<Figure id="hail-street" />

And then the administration, in a sentence that should be quoted whenever a
state is called primitive. There are no dues at Ha'il for the upkeep of the ways
or for public lighting — which, Doughty notes, is unknown even at Damascus — nor
so much as for watchmen; and yet the streets are clean and the refuse is carried
out to pits.

<Figure id="feast" />

His own position was less comfortable. Called for after the *mejlis* one
afternoon, he was roused out of his lodging with haste and violence by the
prince's man, stopped to pick up his cloak and headcloth, and had that held
against him: the slave told the amir that Khalil had not been willing to follow
him. Muhammad ibn Rashid sat in another man's place in the coffee room with his
light gold-hilted scimitar leaned against the wall beside him, in a cotton tunic
with bare feet, his sandals set out in front of him. Doughty gave the greeting
and got no answer. He greeted two other men he knew and they returned it
cheerfully. Then the amir asked him where he had come from, and what he found to
do all day, and what he had seen in his time at Ha'il — and was it well?

Doughty, worn out after a year of famine and hard travelling, did not use any of
the honorifics a man is supposed to use when a prince says his name, and kept
silent. The amir turned to the others and asked how it was that this man returned
no word to somebody speaking to him.

An amir whose entire business was safe passage had every reason to be hospitable
to Europeans who would write about him, and generally was. He also required, in
his own coffee hall, the forms. Doughty was fed, housed, protected on the roads,
and put out of the country, and he recorded all four without deciding which of
them was the real one.
