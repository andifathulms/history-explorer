The attack on Ha'il in September 1918 failed. The man who had spent that summer
pressing for it wrote later that it was a failure if not a fiasco.

Then he wrote the sentence that is the emirate's real epitaph. The failure
committed Ibn Sa'ud to a policy of imperial expansion. He had thrown down the
gauntlet to the most dangerous of his rivals, and the issue lay on the knees of
the gods; probably at no point in his long career was he more distracted with
doubts and fears about the wisdom of what he had done. And yet no single act of
that career led so inevitably to the final glory.

The final glory was the coronation in the Great Mosque at Mecca on the eighth of
January, 1926.

## What Ha'il did to its conqueror

That is a strange thing for a state to bequeath, and it is worth stating plainly
because it is the most consequential thing the Rashidis ever did.

Before 1918 Ibn Sa'ud was the ruler of the Najd with a claim to eastern Arabia,
occupied with the recovery of his family's old position. Ha'il was the obstacle
to that and nothing more. The decision to go for it, and the fact that the first
attempt did not work, turned a regional recovery into a campaign that could only
end with one power holding the peninsula. Ha'il fell in 1921. The Hejaz followed
within five years.

So the emirate that had beaten the Saudis in 1891, driven them into exile in
Kuwait and held Riyadh for eleven years was, in the end, the thing that made
their third state into a kingdom. Had Ha'il been weaker, it would have been
absorbed as a province and forgotten. Being formidable enough to need a real war
is what committed the other side to fighting one.

## The prince at the rival's court

There is a scene from that summer that says the rest of it.

At Riyadh, in Ibn Sa'ud's entourage, was Faysal ibn Rashid — a man of the ruling
family of Ha'il, well over forty, living at the court of the power that would
take his relatives' capital three years later. The Englishman happened to
mention that he had been reading Doughty's account of Ha'il, written forty years
before, when Faysal had been an infant and may well have been one of the
unnamed children in the story.

Faysal was very interested, and asked one question.

*Was he the vaccinator, of whom we used to hear tell?*

That is what remained. The great traveller's book, which fixed the emirate's
image in English for a century, had reached Ha'il as a family rumour about a
foreigner who gave inoculations — and the man asking was a prince of a house
about to be dispossessed, sitting in the household of the man who would do it,
trying to place a European he had been too young to remember.

## Afterwards

What happened to the family is prefigured in that scene. Faysal ibn Rashid was
already living at Riyadh, in the rival's household, three years before Ha'il
fell — which is what absorption looks like when it starts before the conquest
does.

What was extinguished was not the family but the position. For eighty-five years there had been two
powers in central Arabia, one in the north at Ha'il and one in the south, and
each had at different times held the other's capital. After 1921 there was one.
