---
title: Ha'il
drafted_from: al-rasheed-1991

Muhammad ibn Rashid ruled from 1869 to 1897 and made Ha'il into something no
central Arabian oasis had been: a court.

He built a palace and a great walled compound at the centre of the town; he kept
a guest house where any traveller, tribesman or merchant was fed at the amir's
expense, which is a traditional obligation performed at an untraditional scale
and was a deliberate instrument of politics; he maintained the pilgrim road,
guaranteed the caravans, and collected the dues. He acknowledged Ottoman
suzerainty, took Ottoman titles and Ottoman subsidies, and governed as though
neither existed.

Ha'il in this period is also, oddly, among the best-described places in Arabia,
because Europeans went there. Georg Wallin, Carlo Guarmani, Charles Doughty —
whose *Arabia Deserta* is one of the strangest books in English — and Wilfrid
and Lady Anne Blunt, whose *A Pilgrimage to Nejd* describes the court and the
amir's stables in detail, all reached it and wrote about it. They went because
the Rashidis let them, which is the point: an amir whose business was safe
passage had a reason to be hospitable to people who would say so in print.

The horses are part of the same account. The Rashidi stud was famous, the Blunts
went partly to buy from it, and Arabian bloodstock in Europe descends
substantially from animals bought at Ha'il in this period.

None of that is in a field on this record and it should be said where it can be.
A corpus that measures extent and counts influence would show this polity as
null across the board, and what it actually was is a town on a road that made
itself worth stopping at.
