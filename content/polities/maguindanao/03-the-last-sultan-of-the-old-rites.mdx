Kudarat's line did not hold the sultanate together indefinitely. A
disputed succession two generations later collapsed into a civil war
that lasted, by some accounts, more than thirty years - the palace
at Magindanao was plundered and burned, much of the town of Siangan
destroyed alongside it, and one sultan, Manamir, was killed by his
own nephew before the fighting finally burned itself out under the
capable Pakir Mawlana, who restored the sultanate's prosperity and
kept up its old pirate raids into the Celebes against Dutch and
English shipping alike.

## A treaty and a subordinate title

That recovery did not survive the return of Spanish strength. As
Spain's naval power revived through the early nineteenth century,
successive sultans found themselves negotiating from steadily
weaker positions - a treaty in 1794, another cession of coastal
territory in 1843 - until the reign of a young ruler remembered as
Sultan Kudrat II, in honour of his formidable ancestor. He proved to
be, as the source itself puts it, the last sultan who observed every
custom and rite of the sultanate in full.

## The downfall begins

In 1837 Sultan Kudrat II signed a treaty submitting to Spanish
sovereignty, accepting the subordinate title of Feudatory King of
Tamontaka in place of independent rule. Spain began appointing his
successors directly and regulating the shipping that passed along a
coast the first Kudarat had once held closed to Spanish ships by
force alone. The submission marked, in the source's own words, the
beginning of the sultanate's downfall - a slow eclipse rather than a
single conquest, completed over the following decades as Spanish
garrisons advanced steadily up the Pulangi valley that Kabungsuwan's
storm-scattered fleet had first reached more than three centuries
before.
