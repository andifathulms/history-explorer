Almost everything Europe knew about the lower Volga before the Russians took it
came from one book, and the book is wrong about how the story ends.

## How Herberstein knew what he knew

Sigismund von Herberstein was an imperial envoy, not a traveller. His *Notes upon
Russia* were compiled from what he could get out of Muscovite officials, out of
Russian chronicles read to him in translation, and out of merchants who had been
where he had not. He was careful, he names his disagreements, and he says when he
is guessing — which is why the book survived as the standard account of Muscovy
and its neighbours for the better part of two centuries.

He also had one piece of evidence nobody else had: he had met the last khan of
the Great Horde.

Sheikh Ahmed, driven out of the steppe by the Crimeans, had fled to Lithuania and
been detained there. Herberstein describes his reception, the Polish council that
agreed on a war to restore him, the unreasonable time the Poles took over
mustering, the khan's attempt to slip away, and his recapture — and then, flatly,
that he was taken back to the castle of Trakai four miles from Vilnius, *where I
saw him and dined with him*.

The senior Jochid line, holder in name of the throne of Batu, eating in a Baltic
fortress with an Austrian diplomat.

## The error

From that dinner Herberstein drew a conclusion:

> This was the termination of the reign of the kings of Savolha, and together with
> them ended the race of the kings of Astrachan, who derived their origin from the
> same royal line.

The first half is right. The second is not. The khans of Astrakhan did descend
from the same line — Mahmud, who founded the khanate, was the brother of the Ahmad
who lost the Great Horde — but they were still reigning in the delta when
Herberstein wrote, and would go on reigning for another thirty years. He knew the
city well enough to describe its trade and argue about its position, and he
believed its dynasty extinct.

## Why it is worth noticing

Because it is exactly the mistake the sources make about small states at the far
end of a trade route. A visible dynasty collapses; the invisible branch of it that
continues five hundred miles downriver is assumed to have gone with it.

Astrakhan spent ninety years being assumed to have been settled by whoever had
last marched through — the Crimeans in 1523, the Nogai after them, Moscow in 1554
— and each time it was still there. What finally ended it was not another
conqueror riding away but a garrison that stayed.
