Sigismund von Herberstein went to Moscow twice as an imperial ambassador, in 1517
and 1526, and wrote up everything he had been able to establish about the country
and the lands beyond it. He never saw the lower Volga. What he recorded is what
Moscow knew about it in the 1520s, which is itself the point: the delta was close
enough to matter and far enough to be uncertain.

## What he was told

> Astrachan is a wealthy city, and the great emporium of the Tartars, which gives
> its name to all the surrounding country.

He places it on the near bank of the Volga, near the mouth, ten days' journey
below Kazan, and admits the disagreement among his informants about whether it
stood on the mouths or some days above them. His own judgement is that the city
must lie at the point where the Volga breaks into its branches — seventy of them,
he had heard — and makes its islands and falls into the Caspian by as many mouths,
with so much water that from a distance it looks like a sea.

That is an accurate description of the delta arrived at by reasoning from
merchants' reports, and it is the kind of knowledge a trade route produces:
precise about the goods and vague about the ground.

## The goods

Herberstein's Russians knew the emporium by what came out of it. Silks and the
finer wares of Persia reached Moscow through Astrakhan; Shemakha in Shirvan, six
days from the city, supplied the silk dresses its weavers were famous for. The
caravan trade to Bukhara went out across the Ust-Urt. Azov on the Don, seven days
away, connected the same commerce to the Black Sea and Constantinople.

Between Kazan and Astrakhan, he writes, along the Volga and west as far as the
Dnieper, lie desert plains inhabited by Tatars with no fixed abodes. The khanate
was a point of settlement in an enormous emptiness, and the emptiness was the
reason it was worth holding and the reason it was impossible to defend.

## What the khanate took

Tolls on what passed, and a share of what the delta produced — salt from the
lakes, and fish on a scale that later visitors found hard to credit.

No land tax worth the name is recorded, because there was almost no land: the
islands would not bear corn, and the pasture that the khanate's own Tatars used
was shared with the Nogai, who were far more numerous. Astrakhan lived on transit
and on water, which made it prosperous in peace and worthless in a siege.
