Astrakhan's own named peak-era khan held the throne through two separate
reigns, driven off it once and restored once, in a decade when the city
changed ruler almost as often as it changed allies.

## A khan abducted, a khan deposed

Kasim, who had made peace overtures to Moscow in 1532, lost his throne
and very nearly his life within the same year: Circassian raiders fell
on Astrakhan, carried him off, killed a number of the city's princes and
people, and plundered their corpses before installing Ak Kubek in his
place. Kasim seems to have died in the raiders' hands soon after. Ak
Kubek, his cousin, lasted only a few months before the Nogai - the
power that had made and unmade Astrakhan's khans since the horde first
killed Ahmed Khan on the same steppe - deposed him in turn.

## Abd al-Rahman

Abd al-Rahman, probably a nephew of the deposed Ahmed line rather than
one of its direct descendants, was already on the throne by 1533 and
opened his reign the way every Astrakhan khan had to: with a friendly
letter to Grand Prince Vasili. It bought him four years. By 1537 the
regency council ruling Moscow during the young Ivan IV's minority was
writing to warn him that both the Crimean Tatars and the Nogai had
turned against him, and within the year the Nogai drove him out and put
Dervish Ali, a descendant of the old Ahmed line, on the throne instead.

Dervish did not hold it long. By September 1539 Abd al-Rahman was back,
exchanging friendly embassies with Moscow again - a Russian envoy sent
to inquire after his health, his own envoy Kudaliar returning the
courtesy the following year, and in July 1541 an Astrakhan mission that
also arranged for a stray Chinggisid prince, Yadigar, to enter Russian
service at Kasimov rather than stay and complicate the succession
further. Abd al-Rahman's own second reign ends where the sources simply
stop naming him: the next time Astrakhan is mentioned, the throne
belongs to a new khan, Yamgurchi, with no account of how the change was
made.

## What the throne actually was

Four khans in roughly a decade, two of them the same man twice over,
none of them installed or removed by a battle anyone thought worth
describing in detail - that is the whole political shape of Astrakhan's
so-called peak. The city sat on the richest single crossing of the
lower Volga, and every neighbour with cavalry wanted a khan there who
owed them something. What none of them wanted, evidently, was to hold
the throne itself; a kingdom worth fighting over four times in ten
years was still, to Crimea and the Nogai alike, not worth the trouble
of actually keeping.
