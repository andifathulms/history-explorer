Of the states that came out of the Golden Horde, Astrakhan was the one with the
least of everything — the smallest army, the shortest reach, the poorest hinterland
— and the best address on the continent.

## What broke, and what was left

The Horde did not fall in a battle. Through the fifteenth century it came apart
along the lines of its own princely families: Kazan on the middle Volga in the
1430s, the Crimea in the 1440s, the Uzbek and Nogai groupings east of the river,
and in the middle of them a rump still calling itself the horde of the Jochids —
what the Russians called the Great Horde — riding the grass between the Don and
the lower Volga.

Astrakhan separated from that rump. Mahmud, son of Kuchuk Muhammad, lost the
contest for the Great Horde to his brother Ahmad and withdrew downriver in the
1460s to the old trading town of Hajji Tarkhan, near the head of the delta. What
he founded was a khanate in the full sense — a Chinggisid of the senior line,
minting, receiving embassies, treated as a sovereign by his neighbours — with a
territory that amounted to one town and the fisheries around it.

## Why the address mattered

Everything that moved between the Caspian and the north moved past that town.

Silk and spices coming up from Shirvan and Gilan, Persian and Armenian merchants,
the caravan road east to Khwarazm and Bukhara, the salt of the lakes, the sturgeon
of the delta, and above all the Volga itself, which runs from the Caspian into the
whole forest zone of Russia and does not stop until it is a few days' portage from
the Baltic and the Arctic drainage. Astrakhan sat on the Caspian end of that
system.

A state on such a spot does not need to be strong to be rich, and does not get to
choose whether it is important. Every power on the steppe wanted the delta:
the Crimea, the Nogai mirzas across the river, the Ottomans behind the Crimea,
and — increasingly and then decisively — Moscow.

## The permanent condition

The khanate had no answer to any of them and never pretended to have one. Its
rulers were made and unmade by outsiders from the first generation; its safety
lay entirely in the fact that no one of its neighbours would let another have it.

That is not a weakness peculiar to Astrakhan. It is what happens to a small state
sitting on something several large ones need, and it worked, in its fashion, for
ninety years.
