Two years after the annexation an English merchant reached the delta on his way to
Bukhara and wrote down what the place was like under its new owners. It is the
first eyewitness account of Astrakhan in English and it is not a happy one.

## The town

Anthony Jenkinson came downriver in July 1558, passed the ruins of the old town on
his right hand and arrived at the new one — a settlement on an island, on a
hillside, with a castle inside it walled about with earth and timber, *neither
fayre nor strong*, and the town likewise walled with earth. Except for the
captain's lodging and a few gentlemen's houses, the buildings were *most base and
simple*.

The island was destitute and barren of wood and pasture, and the ground would bear
no corn. The people lived on fish, and chiefly on sturgeon, having great scarcity
of flesh and bread. They hung their fish up in the streets and houses to dry,
which bred such an abundance of flies as he had never seen in any land, to their
great plague.

Of trade he found *a certaine trade of merchandize there vsed, but as yet so small
and beggerly, that it is not woorth the making mention* — though merchants did
come, from Shemakha and elsewhere, and when he offered them English kerseys they
told him they could buy as cheap at home.

Herberstein's great emporium of the Tatars, thirty years on and under new
management.

## The famine

What Jenkinson mostly saw was the destruction of the Nogai.

Civil war among the mirzas, followed by famine and pestilence, had consumed above
a hundred thousand of them in that year by his reckoning — *the like plague was
neuer seéne in those parts* — leaving a country of great pasture unpeopled, to the
great contentation of the Russians, who had fought them a long time.

The survivors came in to Astrakhan in great numbers to surrender to their enemies
and seek relief, and were ill entertained. They died of hunger all through the
island, lying in heaps, unburied and like beasts, *very pittifull to beholde*.
Many were sold by the Russians; the rest were driven off the island. Jenkinson,
who was travelling on the tsar's safe-conduct and was in no position to object,
recorded that he could have bought as many Tatar children as he wanted from their
own fathers and mothers — a boy or a girl for a loaf of bread worth sixpence in
England — but that his party had more need of victuals than of such merchandise.

## What replaced it

Astrakhan, he notes, was the furthest hold the tsar had taken from the Tatars
towards the Caspian, and was kept very strong, with men, victuals and timber for
the castle sent down every year.

That is the shape of the aftermath: a fortified terminus, supplied from the north,
with a broken steppe around it. Going back up the river in June 1559 he took six
weeks to reach Kazan, because in all that way there was no habitation. The
khanate's world had not been conquered so much as emptied, and the emptiness took
two centuries to fill.
