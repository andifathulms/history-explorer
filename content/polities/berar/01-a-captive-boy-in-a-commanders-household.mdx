The man who founded Berar's ruling house began life a prisoner. Taken
captive as a boy in one of the Bahmani sultanate's wars against
Vijayanagara, he was raised among the household guard of Khan Jehan,
the Bahmani commander-in-chief and governor of Berar, who noticed his
ability and courage as he grew and advanced him through offices of
increasing trust. After his patron's death he moved to the Bahmani
court itself, where the same minister who reorganized the whole
sultanate's provinces, Mahmud Gawan, secured him the title Imad-ul-Mulk
and command of the Bahmani forces stationed in Berar.

## A declaration ahead of its neighbors

In 1485, Fateh Ullah Imad Shah declared his own independence from the
Bahmani court at Bidar — earlier, by this source's own dating, than
the secessions of Ahmadnagar, Bijapur or Golconda, all still years away
from breaking openly with a sultanate not yet visibly beyond saving. He
died not long after, leaving the throne to his eldest son, Ala-ud-Din,
who took the same royal title his father had claimed and settled a
capital at Gawilgarh.

## A kingdom that held its ground more than it grew

Berar's early history, as the source narrates it, was one of
continuous contest with its larger neighbors rather than steady
expansion. Ala-ud-Din seized the forts of Mahur and Ramgir from a
rival's sons during one period of confusion, only to lose them again
to Ahmadnagar's own forces soon after; alliances by marriage with
Bijapur's ruling house offered periodic support but rarely arrived in
time to matter. Of the five kingdoms that rose from the Bahmani
sultanate's own collapse, Berar was consistently the smallest and the
least able to act without a stronger neighbor's help.
