Mamuq did not stay to rule the khanate he had just founded, and the four
rulers who followed him at Sibir held it for the better part of sixty
years without a single one of them ever fighting a war worth naming to
anyone outside their own family.

## The khan of two thrones

Mamuq's own reign at Kashlik lasted barely long enough to be called
one. Within a year of killing Ibak he marched south instead - not
against a rival for his own throne, but for Kazan's, where four
disaffected princes had invited him in against the Moscow-backed khan
Muhammad Amin. He took the city and ruled it briefly, harshly enough
that he imprisoned the very allies who had brought him in, and marched
out again to besiege the rebellious town of Arsk. While he was in the
field Kazan shut its gates against him. He turned for home and died on
the road, khan of nowhere, having held two Chinggisid thrones in barely
two years and kept neither.

## Three quiet successions

His brother Abalak inherited Sibir and is remembered for one act: in
1499, when a Russian army moved against the khanate's Ugrian
tributaries, Abalak marched in their defence in alliance with a Kazan
prince, then withdrew when Moscow sent reinforcements. Nothing else
about his reign survives.

Abalak's own son Aguish succeeded him, and then the khanship passed
sideways to Mamuq's son Kasim - a transfer between the two brothers'
lines that the sources record without explaining, the kind of
succession that on the steppe usually meant an agreement nobody thought
worth writing down because everybody involved already understood it.
Nothing is recorded of Kasim's reign at all beyond his name and his
sons.

## Two brothers, one tribute

Those sons, Bekbulat and Yadigar, ruled jointly, and it is only with
them that the khanate re-enters the written record in any detail - not
through a war, but through an argument over furs. In 1555, with Kazan
and Astrakhan already fallen to Moscow and the Bashkirs newly
submissive, Yadigar sent seven hundred sable skins as tribute and was
told it fell short; the following year his envoy returned with a
thousand sables, a hundred more for tolls and sixty-nine in place of
squirrel, along with a formal letter of submission. It is the same
negotiation the khanate's own economy chapter already tells in full -
what matters here is that it was Yadigar and Bekbulat, the fourth
generation after Mamuq, still ruling from the same walled town on the
Irtysh, who were the ones forced into it.

## Sixty years, one family, no war

From Mamuq's death to Yadigar's own, the Taibugid line held Sibir
without ever having to defend it in a war that made it into anyone
else's chronicle. What ended that run was not exhaustion but a rival
branch of the family Mamuq himself had displaced: Ibak's own grandson
Kuchum, who had spent those same six decades growing up in Bukhara
rather than on the Irtysh, and who came back for exactly the inheritance
his own family had lost.
