In 1563 an envoy of the khan Yadigar was still at Moscow with the year's tribute
when the news arrived that his master was dead. A prince from the south had come
into the country with an army, killed Yadigar and his brother Bekbulat, and taken
the khanate.

## Who he was

Kuchum was a Shibanid — a descendant of Jochi's fifth son, from the Uzbek world
south of the steppe, son of the khan Murtaza. A Siberian legend says the Tatars
sent for him because they would not be ruled by Yadigar's children; Howorth
dismisses it, on the grounds that the story exists to explain the name of
Yadigar's son Seydyak, and that everything else in the record says Kuchum came as
a conqueror and took the country by force.

Bekbulat's son Seydyak escaped to Bukhara, which is the fact that matters
twenty-five years later.

## What he changed

Two things, and the second outlasted the khanate.

He stopped the tribute. The Taybugids had paid Moscow in sable and apologised
when they were short; Kuchum did not, and by the 1570s his people were raiding the
Russian settlements on the Kama and killing the tsar's men.

And he brought Islam. The Siberian dynasty he displaced had been pagan — Howorth
makes the point that Taybuga's line were still heathens when Kuchum conquered
them — and Kuchum imported mullahs and imams from Bukhara and Urgench and set
them to converting his subjects. The Muslim Tatars of western Siberia date from
his reign and are still there.

## The height

At his strongest he was the only serious power between the Urals and the Ob. The
Nogai over the steppe sought him as an ally, and he married his son Ali to the
daughter of Din Ahmad, the Nogai chief; his name appears in Russian reports as a
prime mover behind Tatar raids on the Kama as late as 1583, when he had already
lost his capital. He had two iron cannon, each two ells long and firing a
forty-pound shot, got from Bukhara or from Kazan.

He was also, by the time the Cossacks reached him, blind.
