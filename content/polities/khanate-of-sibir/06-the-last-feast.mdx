Taking the capital did not end the khanate. It took another seventeen years, and
the decisive act was not a battle.

## The interval

Yermak drowned in 1584 and his survivors walked out of the country; Kuchum sent
his son Ali to reoccupy Sibir and Seydyak — Bekbulat's son, back from Bukhara —
drove him out and reigned there himself. For three years the khanate had a khan
in its own capital again, while its founder's displacer wandered the Baraba
steppe with a following.

What was different was on the other side of the Urals. Yermak's men had shown that
the crossing was easy, and the state took over from the adventurers. Three hundred
streltsy and Cossacks under Sukin and Myasnoy left Moscow in 1587; they built a
town at the old Tatar fortress of Chimgi on the Tura and called it Tyumen, the
first permanent Russian settlement east of the Urals. The next year Danilo Chulkov
came down to the Irtysh and laid the foundations of Tobolsk on the eastern bank,
opposite the mouth of the Tobol — a wooden fort and a church, within sight of
Seydyak's capital.

## The invitation

In the summer of 1588 Seydyak was out hawking on the Irtysh with the Kazakh
prince Uraz Muhammad and the murza Karacha and five hundred men. They came near
the new town, and the governor invited the three of them to a feast at which a
treaty might be arranged.

They accepted. The three chiefs and a hundred followers were let inside; the rest
stayed out. When the brandy had done its work the three were seized and the
hundred were killed.

The men camped outside fled when they heard. So did the inhabitants of Sibir. The
capital of the khanate was abandoned, and Howorth records the result in six words:
thenceforward Sibir remained uninhabited. The captives went to Moscow in September.

## The old man on the Ob

Kuchum refused every offer. Moscow held his eldest son Abulkhair and made him
write to his father praising the tsar's generosity; two more of his sons went over;
his nephew Mahmetkul's mother went over. He answered the Russian terms with a
sentence worth the whole correspondence: *I did not cede Siberia to Yermak,
although he conquered it; but, wishing peace, I demand as a frontier the banks of
the Irtysh.*

Tara was built in 1594 to contain him, garrisoned by a force whose composition is
its own commentary — streltsy from Moscow, Tatars from Kazan, Bashkirs, Poles,
Lithuanians, Circassians, Cossack exiles and three hundred tributary Siberian
Tatars sent against their own khan.

On 2 September 1598 Voyeikov found his camp on the Ob among cornfields in the
marshes and attacked before sunrise. The fight lasted all day. His brother and
one son were killed with six princes, ten murzas and a hundred and fifty of his
best men; more than a hundred drowned; eight of his wives and five of his children
were taken. Voyeikov burned what he could not carry and wrote to Boris Godunov
that the Russians were now undisputed masters of Siberia.

Kuchum was not among the dead. A Muslim priest sent to find him came on him in a
wood on the bank of the Ob, sitting under a tree among Tatar corpses with three
sons and thirty men, and offered him the tsar's clemency.

> I did not go to him in happier days, when I was rich and strong; shall I go now
> to find a disgraceful death? I am blind and deaf, poor, and alone.

He said he did not mourn his riches but his captured son Asmanak, that he would
send his family to Bukhara and go to the Nogai, and he asked the Chat people, who
had been his subjects and were now the tsar's, for a horse and warm clothes as
alms. Then he spent two days burying the dead in the priest's presence, mounted,
and rode off.

He reached the Nogai. They had been ill-used by his father Murtaza, and they
killed him.
