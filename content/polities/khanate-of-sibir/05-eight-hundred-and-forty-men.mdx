The force that destroyed the khanate was a band of river pirates hired by a
merchant family, and nobody agrees how many of them there were. Müller's
authorities say about five thousand at the outset; the Siberian sagas say eight
hundred and forty.

## The expedition

Yermak set out on 5 July 1579 with Zyrian guides, wintered on the Serebryanka,
lost so many men to disease that a muster the following spring counted 1,636, and
crossed the low spurs of the Urals into the Tobol watershed on 12 May 1580.
Everything about the campaign was improvised: boats built on the Tagil when the
old ones would not serve, winter quarters wherever they stood in November, food
taken from the people whose land they were in — including Voguls who had received
them hospitably, and who thereafter raised the country behind them.

By the time they reached the khanate proper the force was down to a little over a
thousand.

## The defence

Kuchum did what a Tatar ruler could do. He sent his nephew Mahmetkul with an army
to meet them; Karamzin puts it at ten thousand horse, and it fought for five days
at Babasan and lost. He had an iron chain stretched across a narrow reach of the
Tobol with men waiting to shoot into the boats as they fouled it; Yermak dressed
bundles of birch poles in Cossack coats to hold the watchers' attention, landed
the rest of his men and took the ambush from behind. He dug a new ditch round
Kashlik, ordered his subject princes to fortify their towns, and threw up an
entrenchment on the Irtysh below the hill called Chuvash.

It was there, on 26 October 1581, that the thing was decided. His people came out
through three gaps in the rampart at the Cossack ranks; Mahmetkul was wounded and
carried across the river; the Ostyak chiefs ran and the Tatars followed. The
Russians lost a hundred and seven men, whose names were read in the cathedral of
Tobolsk for generations afterwards. Kuchum threw his two cannon into the Irtysh
and fled towards the Ishim.

On 7 November Yermak walked into the capital. There was a great booty of furs in
it and, Karamzin says, no food at all.

## Why it went that way

Howorth's explanation is the one the campaign itself keeps demonstrating: bows
and arrows were no match for firearms, and the Siberian annals fill the gap
between the two with omens — a white wolf and a black hound fighting at the mouth
of the Tobol, a Christian town with bell towers appearing in the air over the
future site of Tobolsk, a crowned king on a throne driving the Tatars before him
while their bowstrings snapped.

They are stories told afterwards by the losing side, and the historian who quotes
them says so. What they record accurately is the experience of being on the wrong
end of a technology, which is the same thing they recorded in Peru.
