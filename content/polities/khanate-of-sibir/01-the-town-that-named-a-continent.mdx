title: The town that named a continent

There was a small wooden town on a bluff above the Irtysh, about twenty versts
upstream of where Tobolsk now stands. Its own people called it Kashlik or Isker.
The Permians and Zyrians west of the Urals called it Sibir, and from them the
Russians took the name — first for the khanate, then for the country beyond the
mountains, then for a third of Asia.

## Before the khanate

The dynasty that ruled the Tobol and the Tura before it was not Chinggisid and
was not Muslim. The Siberian annals trace it to a prince called Taybuga, and give
him a genealogy — a khan called On, murdered by a common Tatar called Chingis —
that is a folk memory of the Kerait chief Ong Khan and of Chinggis himself,
attached to the name of a town. What can be said is that the Taybugids held
Chimgi-Tura on the river Tura, that their subjects were Tatars and Ugrians, and
that they were still pagan.

That made them a target from two directions at once.

## The Shibanids

East of the Volga the descendants of Shiban, the fifth son of Jochi, had a claim
to the whole eastern half of the Jochid inheritance and no fixed country to
exercise it in. One of them, Ibak, made himself a power around Tyumen in the
1480s: he was one of the two men who rode into the camp of the Great Horde in
1481 and killed its khan, and in 1489 he was corresponding with Moscow about a
Kazan prisoner.

Ibak married the sister of Mar, the Taybugid ruler, and then killed him and took
his kingdom. Mar's grandson Mamuq gathered what people he could, killed Ibak in
his turn, and recovered what he called the patrimony of his ancestors.

Howorth will not date this. Ibak is not heard of after 1493, he says, and the
killing was probably soon after that year. By May 1496 Mamuq was strong enough
to be attacking Kazan, where the Russian chronicles call him the tsar of Shiban.

## The move

Mamuq did not go back to Chimgi-Tura. He built himself a new capital downstream
on the Irtysh, at Kashlik, and that is the moment the khanate of Sibir begins:
a Muslim Turkic state on the Siberian rivers with a walled seat of its own.

Müller, who saw the site in the eighteenth century, found no trace of any
building — they had been wood. It had barely room inside its bounds for the
khan's house and his dependents', with the Irtysh on one side, the Sibirka brook
on another, and a rampart round the rest.

That was the capital. The country it governed reached from the Urals towards the
Ob, and its wealth was in animals nobody in it had to farm for.
