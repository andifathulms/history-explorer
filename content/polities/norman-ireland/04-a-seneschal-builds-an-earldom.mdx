---
title: A seneschal builds an earldom

The century after Henry II's submission at Waterford was the lordship's
own territorial peak, and no single family did more to reach it than
the de Burghs, whose conquest of Connaught turned a province still
ruled by its own Irish kings into the last great Anglo-Norman earldom
on the island.

## A kingship inherited like a fief

When the Connaught king Cathal Crobderg died in 1224, his son Aedh
succeeded him in a manner the Irish annals themselves treated as
unusual - not through the acclamation of the Uí Briúin's own
chieftains by ancient custom, but as something closer to a hereditary
fief passing from father to son, backed by the English justiciar's own
support. The succession was contested almost immediately. Rival
claimants among Cathal's own kinsmen, backed by the king of Tyrone,
rose against Aedh within the year, and the fighting that followed drew
in Anglo-Norman forces from both the north and south of the province -
among them Richard de Burgh, recently appointed seneschal of Munster,
whose own family had held a hereditary claim on Connaught since the
previous generation. Aedh could not do without Norman help, and the
Norman lords who supplied it did not do so for nothing.

## A conquest paid for in installments

What followed was not a single campaign but decades of intervention,
each round of internal Connaught politics giving Anglo-Norman lords a
fresh pretext to intervene, extract concessions, and entrench their own
following further into the province's own territory. Richard de Burgh
built his own position methodically rather than by outright conquest,
marrying into the local nobility, securing royal grants confirming his
claims, and gradually converting a role as an Irish king's indispensable
ally into direct lordship over most of the province. By the time his
family's hold on Connaught was fully secured, the de Burghs held not
only that earldom but, through the same generations, the earldom of
Ulster as well - two of the largest Anglo-Norman territorial blocks in
Ireland, controlled by a single family whose founder had begun as one
lord among many competing for influence at an Irish king's court.

## A peak the lordship would not reach again

By the last decades of the thirteenth century, Anglo-Norman lordship
stretched, in some form of overlordship or direct control, across most
of the island - Leinster under royal administration, Munster divided
between the Butlers and the Geraldines, Ulster and Connaught under the
de Burghs, with only the most remote and mountainous Irish-held
territories remaining fully outside the reach of English law and
English lords. It was the widest extent the lordship would ever
achieve. The murder of the last de Burgh Earl of Ulster in 1333 - by
his own Anglo-Norman kinsmen, in a killing that fractured the earldom
and pushed many of his own family into adopting Irish custom rather
than defending English lordship - marked the point from which the
territory so painstakingly assembled began, permanently, to contract.
