Strongbow's own campaign succeeded faster and more completely than
anyone, Henry II included, had quite expected. He landed at Waterford
in August 1170, stormed the town, and married Aoife, Diarmait's
daughter, among the ruins that same day - a wedding conducted, by
every account, with the ordinary business of a battle's aftermath
still visible around it. Dublin fell soon after. When Diarmait died
the following spring, Strongbow claimed the whole of Leinster as his
own inheritance through his wife, exactly as their agreement had
promised.

## An earl grown too independent

A Norman baron ruling an Irish kingdom in his own right, answerable to
no one, was not the outcome Henry II had authorized when he wrote his
permissive letter at Diarmait's request. Alarmed at what his own
subject was building across the Irish Sea, Henry crossed to Waterford
in person that October, at the head of a force large enough to leave
no doubt about who held the greater power between them.

## Submission, and a synod to match it

Strongbow submitted at once, surrendering Dublin and the coastal towns
to the crown and holding the rest of Leinster only as the king's own
tenant thereafter. Irish provincial kings, watching an army larger
than any yet seen in Ireland, largely followed his example rather than
risk finding out what resistance would cost. A synod convened at
Cashel that winter brought the Irish church's own practices - on
marriage, tithes and the sacraments - into closer conformity with Rome
and, by extension, with the reforming programme Henry's own bishops
were pursuing in England. What had begun as one exiled king's private
bid to reclaim his throne ended, within five years, as a lordship of
the English crown spanning most of the island's eastern half.
