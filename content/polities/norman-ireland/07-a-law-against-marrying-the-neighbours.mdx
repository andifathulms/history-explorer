By the 1360s the gap between how much of Ireland the English crown
claimed and how much it actually governed had grown too wide to
ignore. Lionel of Clarence, sent as viceroy and married into the
earldom of Ulster, found even the country immediately around Dublin
difficult to hold - his own soldiers cut off by local raiders more
than once - and spent much of his time in England rather than in the
lordship he had nominally come to strengthen. In 1367 he returned to
summon a parliament whose enactments gave legal recognition to a
reality that had been building for decades.

## Naming the Pale by not yet naming it

The Statute of Kilkenny drew a formal line between shires still
answering reliably to English law and territory beyond it - ten
counties required to render account at Dublin, others excused
altogether because of distance. The word "Pale" itself would not come
into use for another century, but the statute's own text already
described exactly the thing that word would later name: a narrow,
defended core of English government surrounded by land the crown no
longer administered directly.

## Treason defined as intermarriage

The statute's substance went well beyond administrative boundaries. It
recited that the English in Ireland had once spoken English and lived
as Englishmen, but that many had since adopted Irish language and
custom to the crown's own detriment - and it made marriage, fosterage,
or even friendly social bonds with the Irish into acts of high treason
for the colonists still living inside the shrinking zone of direct
rule. Englishmen were required to keep English names, English dress,
and the English tongue on pain of forfeiture. That such a law needed
writing at all was itself the clearest evidence of how far the
colony's own assimilation into the country it ruled had already gone.
