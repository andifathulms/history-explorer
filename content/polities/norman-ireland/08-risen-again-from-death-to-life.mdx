By 1541 the title Henry II had claimed at Waterford three hundred and
seventy years earlier - Lord of Ireland, granted in the shadow of a
papal bull - had come to look like a liability rather than an honour.
Friars sympathetic to Rome had spent years encouraging the idea that
the English crown's own authority in Ireland was merely delegated from
the pope, a notion the recent break with Rome in England made
considerably more awkward than it had once been.

## A parliament that voted itself out of one name

Anthony St. Leger, the king's deputy, summoned a parliament to Dublin
that summer, attended by the Anglo-Irish peerage and, unusually, by a
number of Gaelic lords as observers or agents. On the appointed day a
bill declaring Henry VIII King of Ireland, rather than merely its
lord, was read aloud in both English and Irish, to what the record
calls general acclamation. It passed its three readings the same day
and was sent to the Commons, where it passed just as quickly; the
Lords, St. Leger noted, subscribed it at once, "lest they should
thereafter be tempted to deny their consents."

## What a change of title actually settled

St. Leger himself, writing to the king afterward, described the honour
of presiding over the change as being no less to his own comfort than
"to be risen again from death to life." The language was not simply
flattery. The new royal title answered, at a legal and symbolic level
at least, the very question the friars had been raising - that English
authority in Ireland rested on the pope's grant and could therefore be
dissolved by the pope's displeasure. A kingdom, unlike a lordship,
claimed no such derivative status. The unanimous vote that created it
settled nothing about how much of Ireland the crown actually governed,
which remained, as it had for two centuries, considerably less than
the whole island - but it closed, on paper at least, an argument about
where that authority had come from in the first place.
