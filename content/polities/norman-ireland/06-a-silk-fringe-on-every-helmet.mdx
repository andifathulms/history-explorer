By the last decades of the fifteenth century, the lordship the
Statutes of Kilkenny had tried to wall off from Irish influence had
shrunk to a fortified enclave around Dublin, the Pale, defended by a
brotherhood of thirteen landowners with barely two hundred paid
soldiers between them. Real power over what remained of English
Ireland belonged, for most of two generations, to a single family: the
Earls of Kildare.

## A house too useful to remove

Under Edward IV and Richard III, the House of Kildare eclipsed its old
rival the Butlers so completely that the crown found it simpler to
govern through the Fitzgeralds than around them. Successive kings
confirmed Kildare earls as viceroy or deputy governor not because
London trusted the family, but because no one else could hold the
lordship's shrunken territory together as cheaply or as effectively.
The arrangement suited Kildare as much as the crown: the earls
governed Ireland largely as they pleased, answerable to a king an
ocean away who had neither the money nor the inclination to install a
rival administration.

## A young man given a naked sword

That arrangement ended in 1534, when the ninth Earl, summoned to
London to answer charges of misgovernment, handed the sword of his
own deputyship to his twenty-year-old son and heir, Thomas Lord
Offaly - advising him to be guided by older, cooler heads among his
own kinsmen. The advice went unheeded. A false rumor reached Dublin
that the old earl had been executed in the Tower, and his son,
convinced his father was already dead, resolved on open rebellion
before the truth could catch up with him. On St Barnabas's Day he rode
into Dublin at the head of a hundred and forty armed retainers, each
wearing a fringe of silk on his helmet - the flourish that gave him
the name by which he has been remembered ever since, Silken Thomas.
Interrupting a meeting of the royal council at St Mary's Abbey, he
publicly renounced his father's office and threw down the sword of
state, ignoring the Archbishop of Dublin's own tearful warning that
"they are not yet born that shall hereafter feel the smart of this
uproar." His cousin Lord Butler, offered a share of Ireland in exchange
for joining the revolt, refused in writing, declaring he would rather
die Thomas's enemy than live as his partner.

## An eclipse the family did not recover from

Silken Thomas held the field against royal forces for over a year,
laying siege to Dublin Castle and rallying Irish allies to his cause,
but the rebellion collapsed once an English army under Skeffington
arrived in strength. He surrendered in 1535 on a promise of mercy that
the crown did not honor: after an act of attainder stripped him of his
title, he and five of his uncles were taken to Tyburn in 1537 and
executed together, ending the Kildare ascendancy in a single afternoon.
The rebellion's real casualty was the whole system of governing
Ireland through a semi-independent Anglo-Irish magnate family. Henry
VIII drew the obvious lesson: within a few years the crown would
govern Ireland directly, through English-appointed deputies backed by
an English garrison rather than through any Irish family's own private
loyalty - the change of policy that led, by 1541, to Henry claiming
not merely to be Ireland's lord but its king.
