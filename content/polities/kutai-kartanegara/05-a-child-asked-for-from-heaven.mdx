Kutai's own tradition tells its founder's origin as a story rather
than a biography. A childless couple living on a mountain, after
decades without an heir, prayed to the gods of the heavens for a
child of their own to carry on the line at Jaitan Layar. Their prayer
was answered: a baby, understood to be a divine gift rather than an
ordinary birth, was given the name Aji Batara Agung Dewa Sakti - the
name the kingdom's own royal line would trace itself back to.

## A wandering king

Once grown and ruling as king in his own right, Aji Batara Agung Dewa
Sakti met a princess of the heavens, Putri Karang Melenu, and their
union produced a son, Paduka Nira, raised according to customs the
tradition says became a fixed part of how Jaitan Layar's own royal
household cared for its heirs from then on. The king himself, however,
was restless. Tradition remembers him repeatedly leaving his own
kingdom to travel and to compete - including, in one especially vivid
episode, a cockfight against a Chinese prince, which Aji Batara Agung
Dewa Sakti won.

## A queen's return, and a kingdom passed on

The story ends not in triumph but in loss. Not long after his return
from that journey, his wife Karang Melenu departed back to the
heavenly realm she had come from, and the grieving king followed her
rather than remain to rule. What he left behind was a working
succession: his son Paduka Nira, raised from infancy according to the
customs the tradition itself credits to this founding family, took the
throne of Jaitan Layar in his father's place.

## What a myth like this is actually for

Read as history, none of this can be verified, and it was never meant
to be - a divine birth, a heavenly bride, a wandering warrior-king,
and a return to the sky are the vocabulary of foundation myth, not
biography. Read as politics, the story does real work: it gives the
dynasty a claim to divine origin distinct from ordinary human descent,
exactly the kind of legitimacy device many of the archipelago's
kingdoms built for their own founding rulers, and one that would
matter increasingly little once the dynasty's later kings staked their
own authority on a different and more verifiable claim - conquest of
the ancient kingdom upriver, and the name that conquest let them take.
