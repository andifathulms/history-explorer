Upriver from Kutai Kartanegara's own territory, at Muara Kaman, stood a
kingdom far older than the one now approaching it - a Hindu polity
attested by seven stone inscriptions written in Sanskrit and Pallava
script, dated to roughly the fifth century and naming three
generations of kings: Kudungga, his son Aswawarman, and his grandson
Mulawarman. These inscriptions, known as the Yupa, rank among the
oldest surviving written records anywhere in the Indonesian
archipelago. By the time a Kutai prince who would take the honorific
title Pangeran Sinum Panji Mendapa led an expedition up the Mahakam in
the early seventeenth century, that ancient dynasty's own kingdom had
survived, in some reduced form, into an age when its Hindu origins
sat increasingly at odds with an Islamizing coast around it.

## A war that lasted a week

The campaign that followed was fought at close quarters and cost both
sides heavily: seven days and seven nights of continuous fighting,
with substantial losses on each side, ended in Kutai's own outright
victory. The kings of Martadipura, the old upriver kingdom, were killed
in the fighting, and the kingdom's own high officials submitted to the
victorious prince rather than continue resisting a fight that had
already claimed their own rulers' lives.

## A merger written into the crown itself

What followed the military victory was a deliberate act of political
absorption. Rather than simply annexing Martadipura's territory and
discarding its name, Kutai's own royal line folded that name directly
into its own, becoming Kutai Kartanegara Ing Martadipura - a title that
announced, every time it was used, that this dynasty now held the
territory, the prestige and the ancient legitimacy of the kingdom it
had just conquered. A war of one week ended a Hindu dynasty whose own
inscriptions had already stood on that ground for well over a thousand
years.
