Sultan Adji Muhammad Idris's own reign - the first to carry the title
Sultan at all - did not open a new relationship with the Dutch so much
as continue refusing one that had already failed three times before
he was born. The VOC first sought a trade monopoly with Kutai and the
neighboring sultanate of Pasir in 1634; a formal mission under Gerrit
Thomassen Pool followed in November 1635, arriving with five ships
and two hunting vessels from Banjarmasin to demand that Kutai pay
tribute to the Banjarmasin sultanate as a condition of trade - a
demand the reigning king rejected outright. A third mission under
Paulus de Back came in 1667. None of the three secured anything.

## A fourth refusal, and an alliance across the strait

The pattern held under Idris himself. When a fourth VOC mission
arrived in 1747 under Van der Heyden, seeking the same monopoly rights
Kutai had already refused three times over, it fared no better than
its predecessors - by which point Kutai's own resistance had outside
backing. Idris built an alliance with La Maddukelleng, Arung Matowa of
Wajo from 1736 to 1754, a Bugis commander whose own control of the
Makassar Strait with dozens of ships was substantial enough that a
Dutch resident described him outright as a pirate lord of the strait.
With that alliance behind him, and gunpowder and weapons smuggled in
from Brunei, Solok and Mindanao, Idris armed several hundred fighters
drawn from Kutai, Pasir, Sambaliung and Pangatan - not merely refusing
Dutch demands from behind Kutai's own borders, but joining a wider,
coordinated Bugis effort, alongside Gowa's own forces, aimed at
striking the VOC's regional stronghold at Fort Rotterdam in Makassar
directly.

## What the sources do not follow

Kutai Kartanegara's own sultanate endured for more than two centuries
after Idris, well into the twentieth century, on the same Mahakam
river ground its founders had first settled around 1300, its own
later history far less accessible in the sources than these opening
decades. What survives clearly of Idris's own reign is this: a
sultanate that had taken its name from one war two centuries earlier
spent its first decades under that title refusing, again and again,
to let a European trading company dictate the terms on which it did
business with anyone at all.
