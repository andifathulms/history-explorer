The unequal division of rewards did more than disappoint two loyal
commanders. It let discontent spread through the ordinary soldiers who
had actually fought the campaign, many of whom began muttering that
they had exchanged one set of overlords for the amusements of court
concubines and dancing girls, and would rather serve a shogun outright
than remain the restoration's neglected instruments. Ashikaga Takauji
cultivated this resentment carefully among the soldiers who preferred
war as a profession to the peace the restoration was supposed to bring.

## A father persuaded to condemn his own son

Go-Daigo had named his own son Moriyoshi shogun, and Ashikaga, jealous
of the appointment, found an instrument close to the emperor himself:
a concubine he had bribed into repeating, at every opportunity, that
Moriyoshi was plotting to seize the throne from his own father.
Go-Daigo, who by this account had lost in exile much of the judgment
that once marked his character while keeping all of his susceptibility
to flattery, believed her rather than his own ministers, who had
warned him plainly against Ashikaga from the start.

## The one command no rebel could survive

The emperor's decree branding Moriyoshi a rebel against the throne
carried a weight nothing else in the whole political order could match.
Whoever held the mikado's favour held, in effect, the law itself:
his enemies became traitors by definition, his own soldiers the loyal
army, regardless of what either side had actually done. Moriyoshi,
condemned by his own father's word, was seized, transported to
Kamakura, and murdered in an underground cell in 1335. Only afterward,
when it was already too late to matter, did Go-Daigo's suspicion turn
where it had belonged from the beginning - toward the flatterer who had
engineered his son's death from the safety of the throne's own favor.
