The first act after Kamakura's destruction in 1333 was to bring Emperor
Go-Daigo back from the island where the Hōjō had banished him two years
earlier. With the sovereign restored to full authority, it seemed to
many that Japan's ancient and rightful government - a single throne
ruling directly, without a military intermediary standing between the
emperor and his own people - was about to be permanently reestablished
after a century and a half of shogunal government.

<Figure id="kr-godaigo" />

## A debt that had to be paid in land

Go-Daigo's first duty as restored ruler was to reward the men who had
fought to bring him home. Here the new government revealed, almost
immediately, how thoroughly the habits of the system it meant to
replace had already taken root. Rather than devise some new form of
recognition suited to a genuinely restored imperial rule, Go-Daigo
followed the same feudal precedent the Minamoto and the Hōjō had
established before him, apportioning conquered military fiefs to his
own vassals exactly as a shogun would have done.

## A prize too large for the wrong man

The single richest reward - the provinces of Hitachi, Musashi and
Shimōsa - went to Ashikaga Takauji, a general of Minamoto stock whose
own family had every historical claim and popular standing needed to
found a shogunate of its own, should he ever choose to try. Kusunoki
Masashige and Nitta Yoshisada, the two commanders whose own sustained
loyalty had done the most to keep Go-Daigo's cause alive through his
exile, received smaller provinces by comparison. The distribution was
meant to close the war. It opened, instead, exactly the danger the
restoration existed to prevent.
