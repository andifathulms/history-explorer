Once Go-Daigo's suspicion finally turned against him, Ashikaga Takauji
did not wait to be condemned in turn. He slipped out of Kyoto by night,
followed by thousands of the same disaffected soldiers his own earlier
patience had cultivated, and made for Kamakura, which he set about
rebuilding as a base for exactly the shogunate the restoration had
fought to prevent. Nitta Yoshisada, cleared of Ashikaga's own earlier
accusations against him, received the emperor's commission to put the
rebellion down.

## A commission that could not be carried out

The campaign against Ashikaga ended in complete defeat for the imperial
forces. Go-Daigo, once the returning exile whose restoration had seemed
to promise permanent direct rule, became a fugitive in his own capital,
fleeing Kyoto with his loyal followers and carrying the sacred imperial
regalia rather than leave it behind for a victor he refused to
recognise as legitimate.

## A rebel who could not rule as one

Ashikaga's victory left him in an impossible position by his own
country's own political logic: however great his military resources,
he remained, in the eyes of the people, nothing more than a rebel
against the throne, and no rebel could expect lasting success under
that label. His solution was the one every earlier usurper in Japan's
history had already found effective in its own way - he made himself a
different Son of Heaven to serve. In 1336 he installed Kōgen, a prince
of the same imperial house, as a compliant new emperor, and Kōgen
promptly granted him the title of Sei-i Tai Shogun the entire
restoration had been fought to make unnecessary.

## Two courts where there had briefly been one

Go-Daigo, in exile from the capital but still holding the regalia and
his own claim to legitimacy, refused to recognise Kōgen's throne as
anything but Ashikaga's own invention. What had been, for two brief
years, a single restored mikadoate answerable to no military
intermediary split instead into two rival imperial courts, each
insisting it alone was the rightful one - a division that would run for
fifty-six years before either side could claim to have settled the
question the restoration's own failure had reopened.
