Sancho III came to a Navarre that already included Aragon, and spent
his reign extending it further still. When the Cordovan caliphate
finally collapsed into fractured taifa kingdoms, Sancho intervened
successfully in the wars among the Christian states themselves,
uniting Castile and León under his own authority. Since he already
ruled Navarre, Aragon and the Basque provinces on both the Spanish and
French sides of the Pyrenees, only Galicia - where the kings of León
had taken refuge - and the counties of Catalonia remained outside his
power in the entire Christian north.

## A moment that might have changed everything

For one brief moment, nearly the whole of Christian Spain answered to
a single king. It could have been, in the judgment of one historian,
an important turning point in the peninsula's own history - a step
toward a unified Spanish state centuries before such a thing actually
existed. But the idea of Spanish nationhood was not yet ready for it,
and neither, in truth, was Sancho's own confederation: it held
together only as long as he personally held it.

## Undoing the work of a lifetime

At his death in 1035, Sancho did what every strong king who preceded
him in this pattern seemed doomed to do - he divided his own
inheritance among his sons. Navarre, Castile and Aragon emerged from
that division as separate kingdoms, Castile and Aragon entering
independent existence for the first time as a direct result. The
peninsula's work toward unity had to begin again almost from nothing,
and Navarre itself, once the seed from which two future great powers
grew, would spend the centuries that followed shrinking in their
shadow rather than growing to match them.
