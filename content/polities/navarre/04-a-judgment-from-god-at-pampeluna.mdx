The house of Evreux that took Navarre's throne in 1328 held it for
nearly a century before the succession crisis that would install the
house of Foix, and the reign that dominates that century in the
chronicles is not a flattering one. Charles II, who came to the throne
in 1349, spent his rule scheming against the French crown he had once
been close to - plotting, warring, making peace, and plotting again -
and taxing his own Navarrese subjects hard enough that when he
summoned sixty deputies from the kingdom's towns to Pampeluna to
approve a new tax and they told him plainly the country could not
bear it, he had the delegation shut up in a walled orchard on short
rations until three of the most determined were beheaded to frighten
the rest into submission.

## An old man's ordinary night

Froissart records what happened next as a story told to him at Foix by
people who had been at Pampeluna, and frames it himself as something
that "seemed a judgment from God." Charles, then close to sixty and
in the habit of having his bed warmed with heated air to make him
sweat - a treatment for the effects of what Froissart calls his
debaucheries - returned from a night with his mistress shivering with
cold and asked his servants to warm his bed as usual. The sheets he
was wrapped in caught fire; he was burned, Froissart writes, "as far
as his navel," and lingered in agony for fifteen days before dying, no
surgeon or physician able to help him. His subjects, Froissart notes
dryly, at least escaped the tax he had meant to impose on them.

## A nickname the sources themselves argue about

Froissart's own translator adds that the story was not universally
accepted even at the time: a letter from the bishop of Dax, Charles's
own minister, to the king's sister Queen Blanche describes only the
great pain of his final illness, with no mention of any fire at all.
Later historians split over the question, some treating the burning
sheets as literal fact and others as a moralizing legend attached
after the fact to a king already notorious for his scheming. What both
sides agreed on, whatever killed him, was the name history had already
settled on him: Charles the Bad, a Navarrese king remembered less for
any single act of war or treaty than for a reputation for treachery
that had outrun the specifics of his own reign by the time he died.
His son, crowned Charles III soon after the funeral at Pampeluna,
inherited a throne considerably less trusted by its neighbors than the
one his father had received in 1349.
