The house of Foix that Leonor's marriage had installed proved, in the
end, exactly as short-lived as the circumstances of its own accession
suggested it might be. In 1512 Ferdinand of Aragon - already king of a
neighboring realm that had itself once been part of Navarre's own
domain, under Sancho the Great three centuries before - conquered the
Spanish portion of the kingdom outright, ending its independence south
of the Pyrenees for good.

## One kingdom, two fates

The mountains that had sheltered Navarre's own founders now divided
its ending as well. French Navarre, the smaller portion north of the
Pyrenees, was not part of Ferdinand's conquest and remained for a time
under the rule of the same house of Foix that had lost the larger,
Spanish kingdom. That rump state did not last as an independent
entity either - it presently became a part of the kingdom of France,
absorbed rather than conquered, the last piece of a kingdom that had
once briefly stretched across nearly the whole of Christian Spain
reduced, finally, to a province of its northern neighbor.

Spanish Navarre did not simply disappear into Aragon and Castile the
way the conquest's own finality might suggest. What it kept, and for
how long, is its own story.
