Charlemagne invaded Spain in 778 and reached Zaragoza before troubles
back in France forced him to withdraw. Recrossing the Pyrenees, his
army passed through the valley of Roncesvalles, where Basque
fighters - not Muslim ones, whatever a later French epic would claim -
fell on his rearguard and destroyed it in the mountains. No Muslim
force took part in the fight at all; the Basques who ambushed the
Franks were themselves Christians, hostile to any foreign army
crossing their own land regardless of its faith. It was this disaster
that gave the world the Chanson de Roland, a poem that turned a
Basque ambush into a war against Islam it had never actually been.

## A second Roncesvalles, and a kingdom's own beginning

Decades later, an army under the Frankish counts Eblus and Asenarius
took Pamplona and began withdrawing through the very same pass,
confident the danger was behind them. It was not. A combined force of
Basque mountaineers and allies from Muslim Córdoba - a treaty had
secured Córdoba's support in exchange for free passage south of the
Pyrenees - cut the retreating Franks to pieces a second time. This
victory, by one historian's own account, was contemporaneous with the
mountain country's assertion of real independence and political
organization of its own.

## A name without a secure date

Just when a kingdom actually emerged from these wars is not clear from
the sources: tradition credits an early king or chief named Íñigo
Arista, but the first ruler either source consulted can name with real
confidence is Sancho García, reigning from 905. Navarre and its
neighbor Aragon shared a common origin in the legends of the period,
and by the close of the tenth century Navarre had absorbed Aragon
outright - a union that would last only until the next century's own
great king divided his inheritance again.
