Minamoto no Yoritomo did not set out to seize the military control of
the whole empire. It was his chief minister, Ōe no Hiromoto, who saw
how a set of practical administrative fixes could be turned into
something far larger than their stated purpose.

## A tax that paid for permanence

Hiromoto's proposal was straightforward on its face: since the Kantō
region was already peaceful and prosperous under Yoritomo's own
government, a special levy across every province could fund a
permanent garrison, sparing the expense of repeatedly calling out the
Eastern army whenever trouble arose elsewhere. Attached to this
financial fix came two further innovations with far larger
consequences - a military governor, the shugo, placed in every province
alongside the existing civil governor, and a steward, the jito,
appointed directly from Kamakura to administer individual districts.
Yoritomo filled both kinds of office with his own kinsmen and loyal
retainers, answerable only to him.

## Why the court agreed to its own eclipse

The mikado's court had every reason to resist an arrangement that so
plainly diminished its own authority, and every reason, in the
circumstances, to accept it anyway. Half the empire already sat
tranquil under Yoritomo's military government while the rest remained
unsettled, and the reigning emperor, Go-Toba, owed his own throne in no
small part to the same family's victory. The government at Kamakura
took its name, bakufu - literally curtain government, after the cloth
that anciently surrounded a Chinese or Japanese general's field
headquarters - and the name stuck for the whole span of military rule
that followed.

## A father who could not pass on his own gift

Yoritomo held every civil function the Fujiwara had once monopolised
and more military power than the Taira had ever wielded, and in 1192
he received the title that crowned it: Sei-i Tai Shogun. He died seven
years later, and what followed proved that the office he had built
depended entirely on the man who held it. His own eldest son and heir,
Yoriie, unable to wield his father's personal authority, was pressured
within two years by his own mother and grandfather into surrendering
real power to an infant nephew and a twelve-year-old brother - forced
into a monastery and strangled there before he turned twenty-three. The
shogunate that would rule Japan for nearly seven centuries had not yet
completed its first decade before the pattern that would define most of
its history was already set: a nominal ruler on the seat of power, and
someone else's hand directing every real decision behind him.
