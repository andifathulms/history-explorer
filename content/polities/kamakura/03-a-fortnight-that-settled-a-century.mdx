The Hōjō's double usurpation had one obvious weakness: it rested on
force the throne itself had never formally sanctioned, and in 1221 the
retired Emperor Go-Toba decided to test it by war.

## An outlawry answered with an army

Go-Toba declared the regent Hōjō Yoshitoki an outlaw and summoned every
loyal subject to join in putting him down. Word reached Kamakura within
days. The Kwantō's leading vassals, uncertain whether loyalty to the
Hōjō or to the throne itself should come first, were gathered before
Yoritomo's widow, the nun Masako, who put the choice to them directly
and had their pledges within the hour. Against the counsel of those
urging a purely defensive stand behind the mountain barriers east of
the capital, two elder statesmen of Yoritomo's own generation argued
for an immediate offensive, and Masako backed them.

Three armies numbering a hundred thousand men converged on Kyoto by
three separate roads. The campaign lasted barely a month: Hōjō Yasutoki
left Kamakura on the thirteenth of June and was in possession of the
capital by the sixth of July, after a single hard-fought stand at the
Uji river was outflanked and broken. It looked, in retrospect, like an
easy Bakufu victory. It was closer than it appeared - a considerable
part of the population along the northern road had already declared
for Go-Toba, and had Kamakura simply held its passes and waited, the
rest of the country might well have rallied to the throne before the
Hōjō could respond at all. What decided it was the speed of the
counter-attack, ordered before the rebellion had time to spread.

## Three emperors sent into exile

The settlement that followed was harsher than the fighting. Go-Toba
himself was banished to the remote island of Oki; his son, the retired
Emperor Juntoku, who had actively supported the rising, was sent to
Sado; a third retired emperor who had taken no part in the plot at all
was moved first to Tosa and then to Awa, on the reasoning that his mere
presence in the capital might become a focus for future trouble. The
reigning child emperor was removed from the succession altogether and
was not even formally recognised as having reigned until 1870. Court
nobles judged complicit were executed on the road to Kamakura or exiled
outright.

It was the first time since the shogunate's founding that the court had
tried to overthrow it by force, and the first time the Bakufu had
answered by deposing and exiling emperors rather than merely managing
them from behind the throne. For the century that followed, no one at
court seriously attempted it again.

## Fifty-one articles for a country the old law never anticipated

The other half of what made Hōjō rule durable was written down eleven
years later. Japan already had law - a slow accumulation of Nara and
Kyoto edicts stretching back five centuries - but by 1200 there was
scarcely a working jurist left in the old capital competent to apply
any of it, and none of it had been drafted with a warrior class in mind
at all. The two statesmen who had argued for the offensive in 1221,
Ōe Hiromoto and Miyoshi Yasunobu, had carried their legal training out
of Kyoto's court society, which prized courtly manners over legal
knowledge, and into Kamakura, where it was put to use settling disputes
among a military class the old imperial code had never been designed
to govern.

In Kamakura the first half of every month was set aside for judicial
business, verdicts delivered on fixed days after the hardest cases had
been argued before the full Council of Government; a bell hung at the
gate of the Record Office, and a petitioner who struck it was guaranteed
a hearing. Working from precedent going back to Yoritomo's own day,
Hōjō Yasutoki and Miyoshi Yasutsura presented the finished code, the
Goseibai Shikimoku, to the council in 1232 - fifty-one brief articles
built not from the old imperial law but from the customs the warrior
class had actually been living under for two generations.

The Bakufu was careful to say the code bound only its own vassals and
its own domains, leaving the estates of the court, the great
monasteries and independent military houses to their own law. It did
not stay that way. Within a few generations, landholders outside
Kamakura's direct authority found the code's provisions better suited
to their own circumstances than the old imperial statutes, and adopted
them anyway - a warrior class's customary law quietly displacing the
law of the court that had once claimed to govern everyone.
