Sanetomo, Yoritomo's second son, took his murdered brother's place as
shogun at twelve years old, cared nothing for the military exercises
that had defined his father's life, and spent his time on poetry and
football while the Hōjō family - his own maternal grandfather's line -
governed in his name. He too was eventually killed, cut down at a
shrine by his own dead brother's son - the last of Yoritomo's direct
line to hold an office his father had built for a family that could
not keep it.

## Vassals of vassals

What the Hōjō built in the vacancy that followed amounted, in
substance, to a double usurpation. Properly, the Hōjō were vassals of
the shogun, who was himself a vassal of the mikado - a chain of formal
subordination running all the way to the throne. From that point
onward the Hōjō installed a succession of nominal shoguns too young or
too disconnected from military affairs to govern in fact, while the
actual government of Japan passed permanently into Hōjō hands without
the family ever claiming the shogunal title for itself. Twelve Hōjō
regents across seven generations governed this way, the ablest among
them devoted seriously to public administration in a way their later
descendants would not be.
