Nitta Yoshisada, a Hōjō vassal whose loyalty had already turned, raised
an army to march on Kamakura itself once Go-Daigo's cause had gathered
enough strength to make the attempt worthwhile. Before the final
assault, he assembled his warriors at the edge of the strand, removed
his helmet, and addressed the god of the sea directly: his sovereign
had been deposed by traitorous subjects and exiled to a distant island,
and he asked the tide to ebb and open a path for his army's advance. He
then cast his sword into the waves as an offering.

## A path that opened, whatever the actual cause

The tide had receded by the next morning, and Nitta's army, now
convinced it marched with heaven's own favour, advanced without
hesitation. Kamakura was attacked from three sides at once. The
fighting was severe, but every advantage went to the attackers, and
Nitta himself, after conspicuous feats of personal combat, ordered the
city set on fire. Within hours, Kamakura was ash.

## A retainer's death, and thousands who followed him

As the city burned, a Hōjō vassal named Andō, observing a hundred years
in which no retainer had died for his lord, resolved to end his own
life rather than survive the family's fall - and did so even after his
niece, Nitta's own wife, sent a letter begging him to surrender instead.
He wrapped her letter around the blade before driving it into his own
body. Thousands of other Hōjō vassals took the same path rather than
accept defeat, bringing the recorded toll of dead and self-killed to
nearly seven thousand in a single engagement.

## Two commanders, two cities, one restoration

While Nitta destroyed Kamakura in the east, Ashikaga Takauji had
already drawn his sword in Kyoto, joining Kusunoki Masashige to
re-establish imperial rule in the west on the very same campaign - two
separate attacks converging on the same result from opposite ends of
the country. The Hōjō domination that had run the actual government of
Japan for nearly a century and a half, through two Mongol invasions and
the extinction of the very shogunal line it had first served, ended in
this single coordinated collapse. What came next, at least in name,
was the restoration Go-Daigo had been fighting for since long before
his own exile - though how completely an emperor alone could actually
govern a country used to being run by someone else was a question the
Hōjō's destruction had settled without yet answering.
