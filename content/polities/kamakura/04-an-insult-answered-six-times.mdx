The most consequential test of Hōjō rule came from outside Japan
entirely. Kublai Khan, having conquered China and overthrown the Sung
dynasty, sent envoys through Korea demanding tribute and homage. Hōjō
Tokimune rejected six successive embassies, and when nine Chinese envoys
announced they would remain in Japan until receiving a definite answer,
Kamakura's reply was to strike off their heads. An initial Mongol
landing force of ten thousand men was driven back from Kyushu; further
envoys sent to demand tribute met the same fate as the first, their
heads falling at Dazaifu in 1279.

## A wind that ended the question for good

The Mongols answered with an armada, and in 1281 more than a hundred
thousand Chinese, Mongol and Korean troops arrived off the Japanese
coast. Individual Japanese sailors matched the invaders' superior
numbers and siege engines with personal daring - boarding enemy junks
under fire, cutting down crews in hand-to-hand combat - but could not
by themselves prevent the fleet from establishing itself offshore. What
finally destroyed the invasion was a typhoon that struck as the emperor
and ex-emperor sent prayers to the shrine at Ise, wrecking the Mongol
fleet against the coast and drowning the greater part of an army no
one on shore had yet managed to defeat outright. No army from the
continent would ever again attempt a landing on Japanese soil, and
Tokimune's own energy and valor in the crisis earned him credit, in the
words of one contemporary chronicler, sufficient to atone for whatever
his family's earlier seizure of power had cost it in legitimacy.
