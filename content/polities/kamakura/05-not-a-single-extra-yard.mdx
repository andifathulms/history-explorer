A Japanese historian's verdict on the great storm of 1281 is that it
did more than wreck a Mongol armada. Within two generations it had
shaken the whole fabric of Hōjō authority, because the wind had left
the Bakufu with a problem no earlier victory ever had: how to reward
an army for winning a war that produced no spoils.

## A defensive war pays nothing

Every Japanese war of the previous century had been a civil war, and
in a civil war the confiscated lands of the losing side had always
supplied ample recompense for the winners. The repulse of the Mongols
put not a single extra yard of soil at Kamakura's disposal, and the
years of keeping the whole nation, Kyushu above all, on a war footing
against a second invasion that never came had left the Bakufu's own
resources badly depleted before the reward question was even opened.

The temples and shrines whose prayers were credited with the typhoon
went first, and were treated generously - forfeited domains restored,
mortgaged estates cleared, new grants made on top. What that left for
the men who had actually fought was very little. Kamakura spent the
better part of two decades fielding petitions from Kyushu vassals for
recompense it could not provide, referring claims back and forth
between offices, and finally, near the end of the century, simply
declared the whole question closed. Meanwhile those same vassals were
still paying to keep the coast defences in repair and still standing
under arms at Hakata, decades after the invasion that had ruined them
was over.

## Extravagance, and the cure that made it worse

In Kyushu the vassals' own excuse - ruined by the burden of national
defence - was largely true. In the Kwantō it mostly was not. There the
greater cause was simply the fast, fashionable life Kamakura's own
warrior elite had taken to living, and to keep up with it needy
samurai mortgaged their family holdings to merchants, money-lenders
and even farmers who had managed to save money, at interest running as
high as ninety-six per cent. Foreclosure suits multiplied faster than
the courts could hear them.

The Bakufu's answer, after years of patchwork rules that were revoked
almost as fast as they were issued, was a tokusei - an "act of
benevolent government" - in 1297: all suits for the recovery of debt
interest forbidden, existing mortgages simply cancelled, and any
future sale or mortgage of a vassal's holding banned outright. It
solved nothing. Striking at the root of credit itself only made
lenders refuse to lend at all, and within a short time the same poorer
vassals the act was meant to protect were more deeply in debt than
before, evading the new restrictions through the same kind of
subterfuge that had evaded every earlier regulation. The one real
relief left to an indebted samurai family was to be on the winning
side of a civil war large enough to hand out confiscated manors again
- which was not a solution so much as an incentive.

## A bribed verdict in a border dispute

The same years produced a flood of lawsuits the Bakufu's own courts
had lost the competence to manage cleanly. Judgements that had once
run five or six pages now ran to scores of them, petitions sat
unresolved for years, and a verdict, when it finally came, was as
often as not deliberately "ambiguous" - leaving both sides exactly
where they had started, except poorer by whatever bribes they had
quietly sent the judge.

One such case, a succession dispute inside the Andō family of the
northern frontier around 1320, was settled this way by Nagasaki
Takasuke, the regent's own favourite and effective ruler of Kamakura
in Hōjō Takatoki's name: both sides paid, both sides received an
equally worthless judgement, and both sides went to war over it
instead, forcing the Bakufu to send an army north to restore order in
a province a competent court could have settled with a single honest
ruling. It was, by itself, a minor affair. It was also exactly the
kind of failure - financial, judicial, and entirely of Kamakura's own
making - that Go-Daigo's rebellion a decade later would find waiting
for it.
