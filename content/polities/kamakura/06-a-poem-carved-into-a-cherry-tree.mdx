The capable Hōjō regents who had governed through the Mongol crisis
gave way, in later generations, to descendants given to luxury and
carousal, neglecting public business while a horde of rapacious
officials drained the country's resources to support their own
comfort. The triple burden of supporting mikado, shogun and a
self-indulgent regental house on top of them became, in
the source's own words, intolerable.

## A prince who chose a monastery over a scaffold

The Emperor Go-Daigo, though he had been placed on the throne by the
same kingmakers he now resented, began working with his son Moriyoshi
to plan the Hōjō's overthrow as early as 1327. When spies uncovered the
plot, Moriyoshi escaped punishment only by retiring - ostensibly in
renunciation of worldly ambition - to a Buddhist monastery, from where
he continued to assist his father's planning in secret.

## A mountain fortress and a general who would not be captured

In 1330, with Buddhist support, Go-Daigo fortified the stronghold of
Kasagi in Yamato. Around the same time, Kusunoki Masashige rose in
Kawachi with the same restoration in mind. The Hōjō answered quickly:
their army burned Kasagi the following year, capturing Go-Daigo and
exiling him to the remote island of Oki. Kusunoki, besieged twice,
escaped both times and lived to fight again.

## A cherry tree that carried a message no guard could read

Go-Daigo's journey into exile produced one of the most enduring images
in Japanese art and memory. A young nobleman named Kojima Takanori
tried repeatedly to approach the imperial convoy and failed each time,
the Hōjō guard too vigilant to allow it. When his own followers gave up
and turned back, Kojima alone kept pace with the convoy for days until,
slipping into the garden of an inn where the party had stopped for the
night, he scraped the bark from a cherry tree and wrote on the exposed
white wood beneath a verse invoking an ancient Chinese king restored to
his throne through a loyal retainer's faithfulness - a message meant to
tell his exiled sovereign, without a single guard understanding what
had been left for him to read, that his cause was not yet lost.

## A defeat that did not end the cause

Go-Daigo's own first rising had failed in the field, and he himself
remained a prisoner on a distant island. But neither Kusunoki nor Nitta
Yoshisada, the Hōjō's own vassal who had not yet openly turned against
them, had abandoned the restoration Go-Daigo's exile was meant to have
ended. What the Hōjō had actually won at Kasagi was time, not victory -
and the family's own later tyranny was already making sure that time
would run out.
