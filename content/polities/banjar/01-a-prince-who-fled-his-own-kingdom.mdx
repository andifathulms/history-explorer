---
title: A prince who fled his own kingdom
drafted_from: ras-1968

The Sultanate of Banjar was founded by a man running away from the kingdom
he might otherwise have inherited.

Raden Samudera was, by the Hikayat Banjar's own account, a prince of Daha, a
Hindu kingdom of the Barito river system in southern Borneo, displaced from
his position by a rival branch of the ruling family. Rather than continue a
losing contest inside Daha's own court, he took his followers down the
Barito to the delta country near its mouth, and from there sought help from
the strongest Muslim power then operating in the archipelago: the sultanate
of Demak on Java's north coast.

## What Demak gave him

Demak supplied what Daha's own court had denied him: recognition and armed
support. In exchange, Raden Samudera accepted Islam, taking the title Sultan
Suriansyah — the chronicle dates the conversion to 1526 — and returned
upriver at the head of a force that defeated the uncle who had displaced
him.

What he founded on his return was not a restoration of Daha under new
management. It was a new capital, Banjarmasin, built in the delta rather
than upriver at Daha's own former seat, oriented toward the sea trade a
delta position made possible rather than toward the interior kingdom he had
just conquered. Daha's own former territory became a tributary of the new
Muslim state at the coast rather than its continuing centre.

## A conversion that was also a conquest

The Hikayat Banjar presents the sequence as one continuous act: acceptance
of Islam and military victory over the man who had wronged him arrive
together, backed by the same outside power. That is worth taking as
evidence about how the founding was actually experienced rather than
dismissing as chronicle convention. A new religion and a new dynasty were
not separate developments that happened to coincide in southern Borneo in
1526; they were, in the story the Banjarese themselves told about their own
state, the same event told from two angles.
