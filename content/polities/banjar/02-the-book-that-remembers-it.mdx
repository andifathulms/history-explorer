Almost everything Banjarmasin's own people recorded about their sultanate's
early history survives in one text, and the text itself has a history worth
setting out before using it for anything else.

## What the Hikayat Banjar is

The Hikayat Banjar — sometimes called the History of Lambung Mangkurat after
one of its earlier legendary figures — is a Malay-language chronicle
compiled from older Javanese and Malay materials, surviving in a form Ras
dates in its final layer to around 1663 or a little later, with older
sections behind it. It tells the story of Banjarmasin's founding, the
kingdom of Daha it grew out of, and the reigns that followed, in the
register every Malay court chronicle of this kind uses: genealogy, omen,
formal speech and battle narrated together, with no sharp line drawn between
what a modern reader would call history and what such a reader would call
legend.

Ras's study is not a retelling of the chronicle. It is an analysis of how
the chronicle itself was built — which passages derive from older Javanese
literary models, which from oral Banjarese tradition, and which from
material specific to the court that eventually commissioned the surviving
text — and of what that construction implies about which parts of the
chronicle's chronology can be used as history and which cannot.

## Why the founding survives better than what came before it

The material concerning Daha and its legendary founding figures is, in Ras's
reading, the least historically secure part of the text: distant, formulaic,
and built from narrative patterns that recur across many Malay chronicles
regardless of the specific kingdom being described. The material concerning
Raden Samudera's flight, his dealings with Demak, and his conversion is
treated as considerably firmer ground, closer in time to whenever the
underlying material was first set down and less dependent on inherited
literary convention to fill gaps in what was actually remembered.

That is the reason the founding of the sultanate can be dated with some
confidence to 1526 while nothing before it can be dated with confidence at
all: not because 1526 was somehow more memorable an event than anything
that happened at Daha, but because it happened close enough to living memory
when someone finally wrote the story down that invention had less room to
operate.
