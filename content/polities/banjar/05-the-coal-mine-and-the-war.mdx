The war that ended the sultanate began, in April 1859, as an attack on a
Dutch coal concession rather than as a battle for the throne, and the
distinction matters for understanding what kind of conflict followed.

## Antasari's attack

Prince Antasari, a member of the Banjar royal house with his own grievances
against the arrangement the Dutch had imposed in 1857, led an assault on the
Oranje Nassau coal mine at Pengaron. It was an attack on a specific symbol of
Dutch commercial and territorial expansion into the interior rather than a
conventional palace coup, and it opened a conflict that from its first
weeks ran on two tracks simultaneously: a Banjarese succession dispute
between Tamjidillah's Dutch-backed claim and Hidayatullah's testamentary
one, and a wider rebellion against Dutch expansion into southern Borneo that
drew in fighters with no particular stake in which prince ended up on the
throne.

Hidayatullah himself, who had briefly served the Dutch administration after
his own claim was passed over, resigned that position and joined the
resistance once it became clear how the Dutch intended to use their own
authority in the dispute.

## The abolition

The Dutch colonial government did not wait for the fighting to end before
settling the sultanate's fate on paper. In 1860 it issued a decree abolishing
the Sultanate of Banjar outright, ending the office both claimants had been
fighting over rather than deciding between them.

## A war that outlasted the state it was fought over

Removing the sultanate on paper did not remove the war. Fighting under the
name and cause of the abolished sultanate continued in the interior for
years afterward, led first by Antasari and, after his death from smallpox in
1862, carried on by other Banjarese leaders including Hidayatullah, who was
eventually captured and exiled. The Dutch had solved the legal question of
who was sultan by deciding nobody was; they had not solved the question of
whether the Banjarese population currently fighting them cared about the
distinction.
