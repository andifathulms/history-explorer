What made Banjarmasin worth a European trading company's attention, a
century and more after its founding, was two things the interior of southern
Borneo produced and the delta capital could ship: pepper and diamonds.

## The pepper trade

Pepper cultivation in the country behind Banjarmasin expanded through the
sixteenth and into the seventeenth century, feeding a European demand that
made the crop worth carrying halfway around the world. Banjarmasin's
position at the mouth of the Barito gave it the same function every river
capital on this coast performed: the point where an interior product met
the ships that would carry it onward, and the point where a sultan could tax
the meeting.

<Figure id="barito" />

The Dutch East India Company's interest followed the pepper directly. A
formal contract for pepper supply between the Company and the sultanate was
in place by the 1630s, one of a network of similar arrangements the Company
built across the archipelago's pepper-producing coasts, each one binding a
local ruler to sell at a fixed price to a single buyer in exchange for
recognition and, often, military support against rivals.

## Diamonds

<Figure id="stilt-houses" />

Southern Borneo's diamond fields, worked in the interior beyond the
pepper-growing country, supplied the sultanate with a second export that
carried an entirely different kind of prestige. Diamonds of exceptional size
found their way into the regalia of the Banjar court itself and into the
hands of foreign visitors and traders as gifts and purchases, and the
largest stones associated with the sultanate's own treasury became objects
of reputation well beyond Borneo, carried into European collections long
after the sultanate that had once controlled their source had stopped
existing.

## What that combination bought

A sultanate producing both a bulk commodity in continuous demand and a
luxury good of exceptional individual value had two different kinds of
leverage with the outside powers courting it: pepper made Banjarmasin worth
a standing commercial relationship, and diamonds made its court worth
treating as a source of genuinely valuable gifts and prestige objects rather
than merely a supplier. Both together kept European interest in the
sultanate active for more than two centuries before that interest turned, in
the nineteenth century, from trade to territory.
