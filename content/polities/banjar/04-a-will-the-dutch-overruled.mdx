By the middle of the nineteenth century, Dutch colonial interest in
Banjarmasin had shifted from the commercial arrangements described in the
previous chapter toward direct involvement in who actually held the throne.

Sultan Adam, ruling into the 1850s, left a will naming his grandson
Hidayatullah as his intended successor. When Adam died, the Dutch colonial
government — which by this point held enough leverage over the sultanate's
internal affairs to make its preference decisive — recognised a different
candidate, Tamjidillah, as sultan instead in 1857.

## Why the Dutch preferred Tamjidillah

The colonial government's reasoning, as later colonial and Indonesian
accounts of the episode largely agree, was straightforward: Tamjidillah was
judged the more compliant candidate, likelier to accept Dutch direction in
the sultanate's affairs than Hidayatullah, whose own conduct and connections
made him a less predictable partner. Overruling a sultan's own testamentary
choice of heir in favour of a candidate more agreeable to colonial interests
was not, by 1857, a new kind of intervention for the Dutch administration to
make in a Sumatran or Bornean succession; it was a well-established pattern
of the period, and Banjarmasin was simply the case where its consequences
were about to be worked out in full.

## What the substitution actually settled

Nothing, in practice. It answered the narrow legal question of who would
be recognised as sultan, and it left entirely open the question of whether
the Banjarese population, the customary chiefs and the disappointed
claimant himself would accept that answer. A colonial government that could
install a sultan by decree had demonstrated exactly the kind of authority
that made the office worth contesting rather than worth accepting quietly,
and the following two years produced exactly that contest.
