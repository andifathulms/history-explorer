Kedah's standing as a major tin port made it a target for every
power with a fleet nearby. In 1611 a Portuguese force sailing down
from Tenasserim under Dioge de Mendoca Furtado destroyed the towns
of Kedah "with fire and sword." Nominal Siamese overlordship did
nothing to prevent the attack, nor did it prevent Aceh's Sultan
Iskandar Muda, campaigning along the peninsula in 1619, from leading
Kedah's own Sultan Sulaiman Shah into captivity alongside the ruler
of Perak - an occupation of Achinese influence that lingered for
years afterward.

## A river kept under blockade

The Dutch East India Company proved just as persistent a problem.
In 1642 the reigning king agreed to let the Company have half of
Kedah's tin production at a fixed price and to admit no ship without
its permission - a concession Perak, protected by its own vassalage
to Aceh, had refused to make. When Kedah chafed at the arrangement,
the Company responded with force: instructions from Batavia ordered
the blockade of the Kedah river repeatedly through the 1660s, and
the correspondence of the Dutch governors shows the order renewed
again and again against a kingdom that, for all its wealth in tin
and gold and elephants, could not shake off the Company's grip on
its own coastline. By the early eighteenth century Kedah was
recorded as one of several ports formally subordinate to the Dutch
government at Malacca.
