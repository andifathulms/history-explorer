On 6 March 1957 the Gold Coast became independent and took the name Ghana.

The country is eight hundred kilometres from where the kingdom was, on the
Atlantic coast rather than the Sahel, and was never part of it in any sense
anybody has demonstrated. The choice was deliberate and it was an argument: that
the new state's ancestry ran back to an African empire that had traded gold with
the Mediterranean world while Europe was in its dark ages, and that the colonial
name — the Gold Coast, which described what Europeans had come for — was not
going to be kept.

That is the most consequential thing this kingdom ever did, and it did it nine
hundred years after it stopped existing, by having had a name worth taking.

## The name it did not have

Which is the joke underneath it, and al-Bakri supplies it.

*Ghana* was the ruler's title, not the country's name. The kingdom was called
something else — Wagadu, in the Soninke tradition — and the Arabic writers who
recorded the title as though it were a place did so because that is what the
merchants they questioned said when asked whose country it was.

So a twentieth-century republic named itself after a medieval empire, using a
word that was never the empire's name, transmitted by geographers in Spain and
Sicily who had never been anywhere near it, from traders who were reporting what
they called the man in charge.

## What is actually left

Very little, and none of it in the ground where the country now is.

There is a stone-built town at Kumbi Saleh in southern Mauritania, excavated in
stages since the 1910s, with a mosque, a cemetery and a merchant quarter of
two-storey houses. It is probably the Muslim half of al-Bakri's double capital.
The royal half — the town six miles away with the sacred grove and the king's
tombs — has not been found, and may not survive.

There is no Ghanaian document of any kind. No inscription, no coin with a legend,
no king-list, no chronicle. Everything anybody knows was written by people who
were not there, in Arabic, at Córdoba and Palermo and Cairo, out of what
merchants told them.

## And al-Bakri

The last thing to say about this kingdom is that it exists because of one man in
Andalusia who never travelled.

Al-Bakri finished his geography in 1068 and it is the single most important text
about West Africa before the sixteenth century. His account of Ghana — the double
capital, the audience, the taxes on salt, the royalty on nuggets, the sister's
son, the dogs in gold collars — is not corroborated by anything except itself,
and it is detailed, internally consistent, and specific in exactly the way
invention is not.

A kingdom left no records, was destroyed by a state that left no records, and is
known because a Spanish Muslim who never crossed the Sahara wrote down carefully
what the men who did told him.
