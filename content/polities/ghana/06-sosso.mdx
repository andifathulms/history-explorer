What ended Ghana was not the Almoravids and not a single event. It was a
tributary that stopped being one.

Sumanguru's kingdom of Sosso took Kumbi in the first years of the thirteenth
century, more than a hundred and thirty years after the conquest that did not
happen. By then the empire al-Bakri described had been shrinking for a century:
provinces detaching, the Saharan trade rerouting eastward to newer termini, the
southern goldfields shifting from Bambuk to Bure, further from Ghana and closer
to the country that would be Mali.

## The version that survives

Almost everything known about Sosso comes from the oral tradition of its
enemies.

Sumanguru is the sorcerer-king of the Sundiata epic, the tyrant Mali's founder
overthrew at Kirina in about 1235 — a figure with an iron-bodied invulnerability
and a magical balafon, defeated by an arrow tipped with a white cockspur. That is
not a chronicle. It is the founding story of the state that beat him, performed
by griots whose hereditary function was to perform it, and Sumanguru is in it
because a founding story needs somebody to have been founded against.

Whether Sosso was a serious power that briefly held the western Sudan, or a
Ghanaian successor state that Mali's tradition inflated to make its own victory
worth having, cannot be settled. Both are possible and the sources for
distinguishing them do not exist.

## What Mali took

Not just the territory, which was in any case not the valuable part.

It took the trade position: the two directions of the salt-and-gold road, the
customs posts at the desert edge, and the royalty on nuggets that al-Bakri had
described in 1068. Every element of Ghana's fiscal system passed intact to Mali,
and from Mali to Songhai, and went on working for four hundred more years under
three dynasties and four capitals.

Which is the honest way to describe this ending. The state stopped. The thing
the state had been built to do carried on without interruption, because the
gold and the salt did not care who was collecting the duty.
