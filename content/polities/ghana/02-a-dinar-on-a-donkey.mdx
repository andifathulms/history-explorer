Al-Bakri's passage on Ghana's revenue is the most quoted paragraph in the early
Arabic material on West Africa, and it is quoted because it is specific in a way
almost nothing else about this polity is.

A dinar of gold is taken on every donkey-load of salt that enters the country,
and two on every load that leaves it. Copper pays five *mithqals*, other goods
ten. And all nuggets of gold found in the mines of the kingdom belong to the
king; only the gold dust is left to the people, because if he did not take the
nuggets, gold would become so plentiful as to be worthless.

That is the kingdom's entire fiscal system, set down by a geographer in Córdoba
who had it from merchants: a transit duty on everything moving in either
direction, and a royalty on the metal itself.

## The salt goes the other way

The asymmetry in the salt duty is the trade in a sentence. One dinar to bring a
load in, two to take one out.

Salt came south from the Sahara, where there was too much of it and nothing else,
into a savanna that had none and needed it. Gold went north. Ghana sat between
and charged in both directions, and charged more on the way out because that was
the direction the gold was going.

There is a whole class of states that lived on a transit duty and nothing else —
the Nabataeans on the incense road, Palmyra, Characene, the toll fortress at
Derbend on the Caspian gate, Srivijaya on the Malacca strait, Trebizond at the
end of the Tabriz road. They are states that owned nothing but a position, and
they lasted exactly as long as the traffic did.

Ghana was not one of them, and the second half of al-Bakri's paragraph is why.
It had a position *and* a claim on what came out of the ground behind it, which
is the difference between an empire and a town on a pass.

## The nugget rule outlives the state

The second half of al-Bakri's paragraph is the part that matters for the rest of
this region.

All nuggets to the king, only dust to the people, and an explicit reason: to keep
gold from becoming worthless. It is a monetary policy, stated as one, in the
eleventh century.

Two hundred years later the mansas of Mali were running exactly the same
arrangement, and two hundred years after that so was Songhai. In none of the
three cases did the empire own the goldfields. The gold came out of Bambuk and
later Bure, worked by people who were not the empire's subjects in any
meaningful sense, and every one of these states taxed the product rather than the
production — because taxing the product requires a customs post and taxing the
production requires an occupation.

The instrument outlasted three empires, four capitals and six hundred years. It
is the single most durable thing in the political history of the western
Sudan.
