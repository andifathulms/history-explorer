Al-Bakri's passage on Ghana's revenue is the most quoted paragraph in the early
Arabic material on West Africa, and it is quoted because it is specific in a way
almost nothing else about this polity is.

A dinar of gold is taken on every donkey-load of salt that enters the country,
and two on every load that leaves it. Copper pays five *mithqals*, other goods
ten. And all nuggets of gold found in the mines of the kingdom belong to the
king; only the gold dust is left to the people, because if he did not take the
nuggets, gold would become so plentiful as to be worthless.

That is this record's `revenue_basis` field — `trade-toll, mining` — set out by a
geographer in Córdoba who had it from merchants.

## The salt goes the other way

The asymmetry in the salt duty is the trade in a sentence. One dinar to bring a
load in, two to take one out.

Salt came south from the Sahara, where there was too much of it and nothing else,
into a savanna that had none and needed it. Gold went north. Ghana sat between
and charged in both directions, and charged more on the way out because that was
the direction the gold was going.

This corpus tracks a specific type: polities whose `revenue_basis` reads
`trade-toll` and *nothing else*. There are eight — the Nabataeans on the incense
road, Palmyra, Characene, Derbend at the Caspian gate, the Zurayids at Aden, the
Rustamids, Srivijaya on the Malacca strait and Trebizond — and the Characene
record's chapter on them argues that eight instances arrived at one page at a
time, by a rule that never asked the question, is a type rather than a
coincidence.

This record is not one of them, and the reason is the second half of al-Bakri's
paragraph. Ghana taxed transit *and* took a royalty on production, so the field
reads `trade-toll, mining` and the polity sits just outside a category it
otherwise belongs to. The eight are states that had nothing but a position.
Ghana had a position and a claim on what came out of the ground behind it, which
is why it was an empire and Derbend was a town on a pass.

## The nugget rule outlives the state

The second half of al-Bakri's paragraph is the part that matters for the rest of
this region.

All nuggets to the king, only dust to the people, and an explicit reason: to keep
gold from becoming worthless. It is a monetary policy, stated as one, in the
eleventh century.

Two hundred years later this corpus's Mali record describes exactly the same
arrangement under the mansas, and that record's second chapter argues that the
royalty on nuggets is what makes `mining` codeable for a state that did not own
the mines. It did not own them here either. The gold came from Bambuk, outside
the kingdom's direct administration, and Ghana taxed the product rather than the
production.

So the institution passes from this record to the next and then to Songhai, and
survives every dynasty and every capital in the region for six hundred years. It
is one of the clearer cases in this corpus of a fiscal instrument outlasting the
states that used it, and it is legible only because the corpus now carries all
three of them.

## And the numbers to distrust

The same author gives the king two hundred thousand men, forty thousand of them
archers, and describes dogs at the audience wearing collars of gold and silver.

This record's `military_basis` reads `conscript` on that passage and the data
comment says the figure should be read as *many*. Arabic geography about distant
places produces round numbers of this kind routinely, and the useful part is not
the total but what it implies: a levy raised from a population, rather than a
paid force, in a state whose revenue is described in customs receipts and gold
royalties and never in wages.
