For most of the twentieth century the standard account of this polity's ending
was that the Almoravids took Kumbi Saleh in 1076, forced the kingdom to Islam,
and broke it — and that everything afterwards is a decline into the arms of Mali.

It probably did not happen.

## What the account rested on

One late source, and a habit of reading.

The claim traces to Ibn Khaldun, writing three hundred years after the event, and
to a reading of earlier notices that does not survive being looked at closely.
Al-Bakri, writing in 1068 — eight years before the supposed conquest — describes
a functioning kingdom. The contemporary Almoravid material does not claim the
conquest. Archaeology at Kumbi Saleh has produced no destruction layer of the
right date.

What the earlier evidence supports is something less dramatic and more
interesting: pressure, alliance, commercial competition, and a conversion of the
Ghanaian ruling house to Islam somewhere in this period that appears to have been
negotiated rather than imposed. The relationship between the Almoravids — this
corpus carries them in the Islamic West — and Ghana looks, on the surviving
evidence, like two powers with a shared interest in the same trade routes.

The dismantling of the conquest story is largely the work of the 1970s and after,
and Levtzion, whose book supplies this record's narrative, is one of the people
who did it.

## Why the entry is here anyway

This record carries 1076 as a turning point and marks it `contested: true`, which
requires a word of defence because coding rule 4.2 excludes events that did not
matter and rule 4.4 says `contested` marks a dispute about whether something was
a hinge.

Neither quite fits. The dispute here is about whether the event happened.

The entry stands because the non-event shaped the field for a hundred years. An
Almoravid conquest of Ghana in 1076 is in a great many books, it supplied a tidy
causal story — Berber jihad destroys pagan African empire, Mali picks up the
pieces — and the story's tidiness is a good part of why it lasted. A record that
simply omitted it would leave a reader with the version they arrived with.

This corpus has used the flag this way twice before: on the Kleidion entry of the
First Bulgarian record, where the famous detail is partly a later construction,
and on the Kosovo entry of the Serbian one, where the outcome is disputed. Three
uses slightly outside the flag's written scope is worth noticing, and each of the
three says so on the page rather than letting a reader infer a rule that is not
in the book.

## What actually ended it

Sosso, around 1203, and then Mali.

Sumanguru's kingdom took Kumbi in the first years of the thirteenth century, and
the account of it comes mostly from the same oral tradition that supplies this
corpus's Mali record with its own founding — the Sundiata epic, in which
Sumanguru is the sorcerer-king the hero overthrows at Kirina in 1235.

Mali took what remained. This record's `ended.type` is `gradual absorption` and
`by` names the Mali record, and the edge `ghana -> mali` is the second internal
edge this corpus's Africa region has ever had.

What Mali absorbed was not only territory. It was the trade position, the two
salt-and-gold directions, and the royalty on nuggets that the second chapter
describes — an institution that outlived this state by four hundred years and
three empires.
