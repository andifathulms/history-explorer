Al-Bakri, writing at Córdoba in 1068 from what merchants told him, sets out how
the kingdom of Ghana passed its throne, and gives the reason he was given.

The king is succeeded not by his own son but by his sister's son, because a man
can be certain that his sister's child is of his own blood and cannot be certain
of his wife's.

## What that is actually solving

It is a serious answer to a real problem, and it is worth taking on its own
terms rather than as an exotic detail.

Every hereditary system has to decide what it is tracking. A patrilineal one
tracks a claim that cannot be verified: it rests on the fidelity of the ruler's
wife, which nobody can guarantee and everybody can allege. A matrilineal one
tracks something observable — the king's sister is his sister, and her children
are demonstrably her children.

The cost is that a king does not hand the throne to his own son. That requires a
court to accept that the ruler's children are not the ruler's heirs, which most
dynasties find intolerable, and which produces a permanent tension between a
reigning king who wants his son to succeed and a rule that says his nephew will.
A good many West African successions turn on exactly that fight.

## Ibn Battuta finds it, and disapproves

Three hundred years later and further east, Ibn Battuta came into the western
Sudan and met the same arrangement among the merchants of Walata, and it
irritated him considerably:

> No one is named after his father, but after his maternal uncle; and the
> sister's son always succeeds to property in preference to the son: a custom I
> witnessed nowhere else, except among the infidel Hindoos of Malabar. But these
> are Mohammedans, who retain their prayers by memory, study theology, and learn
> the Koran by rote.

That is the whole of his objection. He can accept the practice among Hindus and
cannot accept it among Muslims who know the Quran by heart, because Islamic
inheritance law is explicit and this is not it.

He went on to be scandalised by the women, who did not veil, prayed alongside
the men, and kept male friends whom their husbands did not mind. He remonstrated
with a judge he had befriended about a young woman sitting alone with him; the
judge said *this is our custom, nor is there any suspicion from our being in
society together*; Ibn Battuta says he did not benefit by the advice and that he
did not visit him again.

## Where else it turns up

Ghana in the eleventh century, Mali in the fourteenth, Makuria on the Nile from
the seventh to the fourteenth, Elam in the third millennium BC, and the Asante
on the Akan goldfields into the twentieth century.

Five instances, on two continents, spread across four thousand years, with no
possibility of contact between the earliest and the rest. The convergence is not
diffusion. It is several societies independently deciding that the reliable
question is *who is this woman's child* rather than *who is this man's*.

## What it says about the state

One thing worth drawing out. A rule of that kind cannot be improvised, and it
cannot be enforced against a determined king without an institution behind it —
somebody who says whose turn it is and is obeyed.

Al-Bakri is describing an eleventh-century state with a stated constitutional
rule about the succession, running in a country that had no writing of its own.
The rule was known well enough that traders crossing the Sahara could repeat it
accurately to a geographer in Spain who never went anywhere near it.
