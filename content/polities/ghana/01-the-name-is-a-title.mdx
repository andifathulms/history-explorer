Ghana is what the Arabic writers call the king. Al-Bakri says so directly: the
ruler's title is *ghana*, and the country is called after him. The Soninke
tradition calls the polity Wagadu.

The modern republic that took the name in 1957 is eight hundred kilometres away
and was never part of it, and chose the name deliberately as a claim on a West
African imperial past. It is called Ghana here because everything ever
written about it is written under that word, and the word is a title rather than
a name: al-Bakri says plainly that *ghana* is what the ruler was called, and that
the country's own name was something else.

## Everything here was written by people who were not here

Al-Fazari, in the eighth century, knows of Ghana as the land of gold. Al-Ya'qubi
and Ibn Hawqal add to it. Al-Bakri, writing at Córdoba in 1068, produces the
description everything else depends on — the court, the taxes, the succession,
the double capital — and al-Bakri never left al-Andalus. Al-Idrisi, working for a Norman king at Palermo a century
later, largely copies him.

That is the evidentiary situation, and it is why the Arabic accounts are best
read printed side by side rather than as a narrative: laid out together it
becomes visible which passages are independent testimony and which are one author
copying another, and for this kingdom a great deal is the second.

Nothing was written from inside. There are no Ghanaian documents, no
inscriptions, no coins, and the kings were not Muslim, so the literate community
in the country wrote about trade rather than about the state.

## Kumbi Saleh

Al-Bakri describes a double town. One half is where the Muslims live — merchants,
with twelve mosques, salaried imams and muezzins, and jurists and scholars. The
other half, six miles away, is the king's, with the royal residences, the domed
buildings, a grove where the priests and the idols are, and the tombs of the
kings.

Excavation at Kumbi Saleh in southern Mauritania found a substantial stone-built
town of about the right period, and did not find the royal half. The
identification is probable and not certain, and it has been argued about for
seventy years.

## What the two halves were for

They are the whole political arrangement, and the difference between Ghana and
the empires that followed it.

Mali and Songhai had Muslim dynasties. Ghana did not. What it had was a state
that hosted a Muslim commercial and legal community, paid its clergy, used its
literacy, employed Muslims as ministers by al-Bakri's account — and kept its own
king in the other town with the grove and the idols, buried under a dome with his
retainers and his weapons.

That arrangement lasted for centuries, and it worked precisely because the state
carried nothing outward. It did not convert its subjects, did not send
missionaries, and did not require its trading partners to be anything. It kept
the two things six miles apart and let the caravans do the rest, which is a
considerably more sophisticated arrangement than it is usually given credit
for.
