title: Dogs in collars of gold

Al-Bakri's description of the Ghanaian court is the only one anybody has, and
its details are the kind a merchant would have brought back because they were
worth repeating.

The same author gives the king two hundred thousand men, forty thousand of them
archers, and describes dogs at the audience wearing collars of gold and silver.

Two hundred thousand should be read as *many*. Arabic geography about distant
places produces round numbers of that kind routinely, and no eleventh-century
West African state fielded two hundred thousand men.

What the passage is useful for is what it implies rather than what it counts. An
army described as a proportion of a population, rather than as a number of men on
a payroll, is a levy — and Ghana's revenue is described everywhere in customs
receipts and gold royalties and nowhere in wages. The king could call people out.
He was not paying them to stand about.
