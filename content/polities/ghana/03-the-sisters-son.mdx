Al-Bakri, describing the kingship, says that the ruler is succeeded not by his
own son but by his sister's son, and gives the reason he was given: the king is
certain that his sister's child is of his blood, and cannot be certain of his
wife's.

That is a stated succession rule from a datable source, and this record's
`succession_rule` field now reads `matrilineal`.

## It did not, for a while

When this chapter was written the field was null, and the paragraph here said
why: the seven values available were primogeniture, tanistry, appanage,
nomination, election, acclamation and factional, and matrilineal succession was
none of them. `tanistry` was the nearest and was not close — it means selection
from among a defined group of eligible kin, which is a rule about *choosing*,
and this is a rule about *descent*.

The chapter then counted the cases and said the note was there so that a fourth
had somewhere to arrive. Four arrived, then a fifth, and the value was added.

The reasoning is kept above rather than replaced, because the argument is the
useful half. A reader who finds `matrilineal` in the field and nothing else would
have no way of knowing that this site's succession vocabulary was built out of
European, Islamic and steppe practice and had to be told that descent through
women exists.

## And the Mali record is still null

Which is the best evidence that the change was worth making.

This corpus's Mali record is empty in the same field, two hundred kilometres and
two hundred years away, and it was always empty for a different reason. Mali's
succession has no stated rule. What it has is a pattern — the throne going to
brothers, to sons, and to sisters' sons in no order anybody records — and Ibn
Battuta, at the court in 1352, noting with disapproval that among these people a
man's heir is his sister's son rather than his own.

Mali's field was empty because nobody wrote the rule down. Ghana's was empty
because somebody did and the site had no word for it.

Before the value existed, both fields rendered identically and a reader had no
way to tell those two situations apart. Now one is filled and one is not, and the
difference between a stated rule and an observed practice is visible in the data
instead of only in a chapter.

The Median record in this corpus sets out four kinds of null and the Ur III
record adds a fifth. This page has just stopped being an example of the second
kind, and the coding rules now say in terms that a pattern is not a rule here as
everywhere.

## Three cases, and not a proposal

The corpus has now met avuncular or matrilineal succession three times.

Here, as a rule stated by al-Bakri. On the Mali record, as a practice a visitor
found scandalous. And on the Ur III record's last chapter, which notes that the
Elam of Awan and Shimashki passed the throne from brother to brother and then to
a sister's son, and calls it one of the more genuinely unusual constitutional
arrangements in the ancient world.

Three records, three continents' worth of distance, no contact between them.

This site changed a closed vocabulary once, when nine records in four regions had
recorded on the page that `legitimation` could not say what they needed. Three is
not nine. No value is proposed here, and this paragraph exists so that a fourth
case has somewhere to arrive — which is the same discipline the Attalid record
applies to a kingdom left by will and the Zand record to a ruler claiming
authority on behalf of the governed.

## What it says about the state

One thing beyond the bookkeeping.

A polity that passes the throne through the female line is organised around a
different idea of what a dynasty is, and al-Bakri's informants gave a reason that
is about certainty rather than about status. The Soninke arrangement was not an
oddity of Ghana's: it belongs to a wider West African pattern that the Arabic
visitors kept noticing because it violated their own assumptions, and it is one
of the few things about this polity's internal constitution that the sources
record at all — precisely because it surprised them.
