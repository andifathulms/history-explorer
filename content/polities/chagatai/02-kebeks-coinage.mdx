For seventy years the Chagatai khans governed the richest cities in Central Asia
without going near them, and the moment one of them tried to, the trouble
started.

## The arrangement they inherited

Chagatai's ulus ran from the Ili valley across Transoxiana to the Tarim. It
contained Bukhara and Samarkand, and its rulers lived on the steppe north-east
of them and came nowhere near.

The towns were farmed out. Mahmud Yalavach and after him his son Mas'ud Beg —
Khwarazmian merchants in Mongol service — administered Transoxiana on behalf of
the imperial government rather than the local khan, remitting revenue and
keeping the cities functioning through a period in which the khans themselves
were fighting each other and, repeatedly, the Great Khan.

That produced the strangest fiscal arrangement in the Mongol world: a khanate
whose territory contained enormous urban wealth and whose rulers drew very
little of it, because the collection apparatus reported somewhere else.

## What Kebek did

Kebek came to the throne in about 1318 and made the obvious decision. He built a
residence near Qarshi — Qarshi is the Mongol word for palace, and the town is
named after it — which meant a khan living in Transoxiana rather than visiting
it.

Then he reformed the coinage. A unified silver dinar and its half, struck to a
consistent standard across the khanate, replaced the patchwork of local
issues; the coins were good enough that the name survives in the Russian
*denga* and eventually in *dengi*, money.

And he reorganised the provinces into *tümens* for administration and taxation
— which is a nomadic decimal unit being used to govern farmland, and is exactly
the kind of hybrid a state in this position produces when it starts trying.

## Why it was dangerous

Because every one of those steps moved the centre of gravity from the men on
horses to the men with ledgers.

A khan on the steppe is first among the tribal leaders who elected him and
depends on distributing what he takes. A khan in a palace at Qarshi with a
uniform coinage and a provincial tax administration does not need them in the
same way — and they can see it.

Kebek himself was not deposed. His successor Tarmashirin went further, and was
overthrown for it in about 1334 by an assembly of the very people the reforms
had been making redundant — which is the point at which the khanate stopped
being able to hold its two halves together.

## What was actually at stake

Not religion, though the sources put it in religious terms, and not culture.
Revenue and command.

The eastern nomads were not objecting to Islam as such — they converted
eventually themselves. They were objecting to a khan whose income came from
somewhere they had no share in, administered by people they had no hold over,
which would within a generation have made them irrelevant.

They were right, and the western half is what happened when they lost. By the
1360s the Transoxianan khans were ceremonial and the Turkicised tribal amirs of
the region held the power — which is the vacancy an amir of the Barlas walked
into.
