Ibn Battuta left the Chagatai khanate on good terms with its ruler, and then heard
what happened to him. The explanation he was given is the only clear description
anybody wrote down of the constitutional machinery of a Chinggisid state.

## The book

Everything rested on the *yasak* — the code attributed to Chinggis, which Ibn
Battuta understood as a book, and which he says enacted that whoever opposed any of
its statutes should be put out of office.

He may or may not be right that it existed as a written text; that is still argued
about. What matters is that everybody in the khanate believed it did, and behaved
accordingly.

## The day

One of the statutes concerned an annual assembly, which the Mongols called the
feast.

On a fixed day each year the descendants of Chinggis, the governors of the
districts, the wives of the nobles and the general officers of the army were all to
come together. And if the ruler had altered any of the statutes in the year, the
nobles were to stand up and say so:

> Thou hast done so and so upon such and such a day, and hast made an alteration in
> the statutes of the yasak — and therefore thy deposition is a necessary
> consequence.

They were then to take him by the hand, remove him from the throne, and put another
descendant of Chinggis in it. On the same occasion, any noble who had committed a
crime was to be judged.

An annual review of the sovereign, by an assembly with the power to remove him for
breach of the fundamental law, and a standing court for the nobility — described by
a Moroccan judge who had just spent some days as the ruler's guest.

## What Tarmashirin did

He abolished the day.

Ibn Battuta states it in one flat sentence and lets it do its own work. *Now,
Tarmashirin had entirely abolished the observance of this day, which gave very great
offence.*

He had reason. Tarmashirin was a Muslim — by some accounts the first Chagatai khan
to make Islam general among his people — who spent his time in Transoxiana, kept
the company of doctors of law, and was praised by Ibn Battuta for the justice of
his laws and reckoned by him one of the four great kings of the world, whose
territories occupy the middle station and to whom the kings of China, India, Iraq
and the Turks all send presents.

He was, in other words, becoming a settled Muslim sovereign. The assembly was the
institution through which the Mongol amirs of the eastern pasture controlled a khan,
and it required him to come east once a year and stand still while they inspected
him.

## What happened

> Some time, therefore, after we had left the country, the Tartars, together with
> their nobles, assembled and deposed him, appointing for a successor one of his
> relations; and to such an extent was the matter pressed, that Tarmashirin took to
> flight and was put to death.

The machinery worked exactly as described, on a ruler who had tried to switch it
off.

## The quarrel in one sentence

This is the fracture that broke the khanate thirteen years later, and it is worth
stating plainly because it looks like a religious quarrel and was not.

The eastern amirs did not depose Tarmashirin for being a Muslim; the khans who
followed in the east became Muslims themselves within a generation. They deposed
him for governing from the wrong place, out of the wrong institutions, with the
wrong people, in a way that made the annual inspection impossible and their consent
unnecessary.

Every steppe polity in this history faced the same choice and most of them made it
by drifting. The Chagatai are the one case where the two answers were argued out in
a formal assembly with a written rule and a named procedure — and where the party
of the steppe won, killed the khan, and then split the country in half anyway.
