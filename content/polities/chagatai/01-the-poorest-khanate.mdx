Chagatai's inheritance was Transoxiana and the Tarim — the old Karakhanid and
Kara Khitai lands, containing Bukhara and Samarkand, two of the wealthiest cities
in the Islamic world, and a very large amount of pasture.

<Figure id="cg-chagatai" />

The khanate spent a century unable to decide which of those it was. Its rulers
were pulled between the settled Muslim towns that generated revenue and the
nomadic Mongol military that generated power, and every attempt to move toward
the towns — Kebek minting a settled coinage and building a residence near
Qarshi in the 1320s, Tarmashirin converting to Islam in the 1330s — produced a
revolt from the steppe. Tarmashirin was overthrown for it.

The khanate split in the 1340s into a western half, Transoxiana, that became
Muslim and settled, and an eastern half, Moghulistan, that stayed nomadic. This
is the case that shows what the Ilkhanate and the Golden Horde avoided by
converting decisively: the Chagatai state fractured along exactly the seam that
every steppe polity governing farmland has to bridge. The Liao had found the
alternative solution two centuries earlier — two administrations, deliberately
kept apart — and the Chagatai khans never tried it.

Out of the western half, and out of a minor Turkicised Mongol clan within it,
came Timur.

## The two countries

The ulus Chagatai received in the 1220s was not one country but two that happened
to be adjacent, and the khanate spent its whole existence failing to be both.

South of the Tian Shan and west of the Pamirs lay Transoxiana: Samarkand, Bukhara,
Khujand, the Zarafshan valley — irrigated, urban, Persian-speaking, Muslim, and the
richest agricultural land between Baghdad and China. North and east lay the steppe
and the mountain pasture, the Ili and the Chu and the Yulduz, which grew nothing and
supported horses.

The khan's army came from the second. The khan's revenue came from the first. No
other Mongol successor state had the two in such equal measure. The Golden Horde
had grass and treated its farmers as tributaries at a distance. The Ilkhans had
cities and went to live in them. The Chagatai had both, in one inheritance, with a
mountain range between.

## What the amirs wanted

Not the same thing the khan wanted, and this is the mechanism that wrecked every
reign.

A tribal amir with his own following on the pasture had no interest in his khan
becoming a settled ruler with a capital, a bureaucracy and a standing revenue,
because such a khan would not need him. The amirs' position depended on the khan
remaining what he had been: a first among equals who lived in a tent, moved with
the seasons, and distributed what he took.

So the khanate developed a rule, unwritten and lethal: a khan who spent too long in
Transoxiana, who built, who minted, who governed, was a khan who would be removed.
Kebek tried it and is remembered fondly by the townsmen and hardly at all by the
Mongols. Tarmashirin tried it and was deposed and killed. When the khanate finally
broke in 1347 it broke on exactly that line, and the two halves went in exactly the
two directions.

## Poorest, and longest

For all that, it lasted half as long again as the Ilkhanate, and its eastern
successor outlasted the Golden Horde.

The Ilkhans took the richest country any Mongol conquered, governed it properly,
converted, reformed the taxes, patronised the best science in the world — and were
gone in seventy-nine years. The Chagatai stayed poor, stayed mobile, stayed
divided, and lasted a hundred and twenty-one; and their line was still ruling in
the Tarim oases in the seventeenth century.

Being unable to become a state turned out to be survivable. Becoming one did not.
