Ibn Battuta crossed the Chagatai khanate in the early 1330s, a hundred and ten
years after Chinggis had passed through it, and what he records is not a ruined
country. It is a country in which some places had recovered and some had not, and
he says which.

## Bukhara

He came out of Khwarazm after eighteen days of sandy and uninhabited desert, through
Kath and Wabkana, and reached Bukhara — *the principal city of the country beyond
the Oxus*.

> After it had been ravaged by the Tartars, it almost entirely disappeared: I found
> no one in it who knew any thing of science.

That is a scholar's judgement and it is the harshest thing in his book. Bukhara had
been the intellectual capital of the eastern Islamic world; a traveller who went
there specifically to meet its jurists found nobody worth talking to.

## Balkh

Across the Oxus, a day and a half over a desert with no house on it, he came to
Balkh — and here the destruction was still total.

> It now lies in ruins. It has not been rebuilt since its destruction by the cursed
> Jengiz Khan. The situation of its buildings is not very discernible, although its
> extent may be traced. It is now in ruins, and without society.

Its mosque had been one of the largest and handsomest in the world, with
incomparable pillars — three of which Chinggis had pulled down, because somebody
told him the mosque's treasure was hidden inside them.

A hundred and ten years, and the outline of the city was still visible in the
ground and nobody was living in it.

## Samarkand

And then, twenty days' ride from Balkh, the other half of the answer.

> I then proceeded to Samarkand, which is a very large and beautiful city.

No qualification, no ruins, no lament. Samarkand had come back.

## Why some and not others

The difference is not how hard each place was hit. It is what each place needed in
order to work again.

A city on a river that runs by itself — Samarkand on the Zarafshan, with its canals
short and its water arriving without help — can be repopulated. A city that depends
on a long artificial system needs the system rebuilt before anyone can live there,
and rebuilding it costs more than founding a new town somewhere easier. Balkh's
water came a long way. Merv's came further.

And a city whose value was its scholars is a different case again, because the
scholars are not replaced by repopulation. They are replaced by two generations of
teaching, by libraries, by endowments, and by somebody wanting to come. Bukhara had
people in it when Ibn Battuta arrived. What it did not have was anybody who knew
anything, because the men who knew things had been killed or had left for Delhi and
Cairo and Anatolia, and the books had burned, and the endowments that paid the
teachers had been confiscated.

## What the khanate did about it

Very little, for a century, and that is the charge against it.

The Ilkhans rebuilt: Ghazan's reforms, the observatory at Maragha, Rashid al-Din's
suburb at Tabriz with its hospital and its sixty thousand books. The Chagatai khans
mostly did not, because their army did not want them to and their revenue never
stayed in one pair of hands long enough.

When the rebuilding of Transoxiana finally came it came under Timur, sixty years
after Ibn Battuta's visit — and Timur did it by carrying off the craftsmen of every
city he took and putting them to work at Samarkand.
