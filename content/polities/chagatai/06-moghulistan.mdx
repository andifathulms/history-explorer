The split of the 1340s gave the khanate two futures, and both happened.

The western half, Transoxiana, went settled and Muslim. Its khans became
increasingly ceremonial and real power passed to the Turkicised tribal amirs of
the region — which is precisely the vacancy that Timur, an amir of the Barlas who
could not himself be khan, stepped into. The Timurid empire is the western
Chagatai khanate's continuation under management that had stopped pretending.

The eastern half, Moghulistan, stayed nomadic and stayed on the steppe, its
rulers keeping Chinggisid descent and converting to Islam later and less
completely. It lasted longer as a Chinggisid polity and left far less behind. Its
successor states in the Tarim persisted into the seventeenth century.

The two halves are the cleanest controlled comparison there is for the question
every steppe conquest state faces. Come inside and you get wealth,
literacy, administration, and a ruling family that ceases to command the men who
put it there. Stay outside and you keep the army and the legitimacy and remain
poor and peripheral.

The Liao tried to have both with two administrations and got two hundred years.
The Jin came inside and got a hundred and nineteen. The Yuan came inside
half-way and got eighty-nine. Nobody found a third answer.

## What actually broke in 1347

Qazan Khan was killed in battle by his own amirs in 1347, and no khan afterwards
ruled both halves.

In the west the amir Qazghan took power and did not take the title, ruling through a
Chinggisid figurehead — the arrangement that Timur would inherit and perfect. In the
east, the Dughlat amirs found a boy who was said to be a son of the old khan, raised
him at Aksu, and had a country to run within a few years.

Both halves therefore ended up governed by tribal families holding a Chinggisid in
front of them. The difference was what those families wanted.

The western amirs wanted the revenue of Transoxiana, which meant order in the
cities, which eventually meant a ruler strong enough to impose it and a khan who was
a signature. The eastern amirs wanted the pastures and the right to make khans, and
had no cities to speak of until Sultan Said took Kashgar in 1514.

## The comparison

The two halves are the cleanest controlled comparison there is for the question
every steppe conquest state faces.

Come inside and you get wealth, literacy, administration, and a ruling family that
ceases to command the men who put it there. Stay outside and you keep the army and
the legitimacy and remain poor and peripheral.

The Liao tried to have both with two administrations and got two hundred years. The
Jin came inside and got a hundred and nineteen. The Yuan came inside half-way and got
eighty-nine. The Ilkhans came all the way inside and got eighty.

The eastern Chagatai stayed outside, and their line was still ruling in the Tarim
when the Dzungars arrived in the seventeenth century — three hundred and fifty years
later, having done nothing anybody would call an achievement, and outlasting every
one of them.
