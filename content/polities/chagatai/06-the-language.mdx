The Chagatai khanate is the least successful of the four Mongol successor states
and the only one that gave its name to a literary language, an empire in India,
and a modern nation's idea of itself.

## Chagatai Turkic

The western half of the khanate produced, over the fourteenth and fifteenth
centuries, a literary language: Turkic in the Arabic script, heavily loaded with
Persian and Arabic, and called Chagatai after the khanate it grew up in.

It became the literary standard for the whole Turkic-speaking world east of the
Ottomans, and it stayed that way for four hundred years. Ali-Shir Nava'i wrote
in it at the Timurid court in the 1480s and argued in a famous treatise that it
was capable of everything Persian could do — a case that had to be made, in
Persian-dominated Central Asia, and that he substantially won.

Babur wrote his memoirs in it. So did the khans of Khiva and the Uzbek courts of
Bukhara. Modern Uzbek and modern Uyghur both descend from it directly.

A khanate that could not decide whether to be a steppe polity or a settled one
is the ancestor of the literary language of every settled Turkic people in
Central Asia, and it acquired that role after it had ceased to exist.

## Mughal

The eastern half is where the other name comes from, and the route is
roundabout.

Timur came out of the western Chagatai lands, and his descendants ruled
Transoxiana until the Shaybanid Uzbeks pushed them out at the beginning of the
sixteenth century. One of those displaced Timurid princes, Babur, went south,
took Kabul, and then took northern India.

He called his dynasty Timurid and thought of himself as a Chagatai — his
memoirs use the word for his own people. Everyone else called the dynasty
Mughal, which is simply the Persian for Mongol, and the name stuck to the
largest and richest empire in Indian history.

So the term by which a European or an Indian refers to the Delhi and Agra of
Akbar and Shah Jahan traces back through a displaced Timurid prince to the
second son of Chinggis Khan, whose own khanate had been the poorest of the four.

## Moghulistan and after

The eastern half kept the Chinggisid line longest. Its rulers converted to Islam
later, moved into the Tarim oases, and the states they founded there — Yarkand
above all — went on being ruled by descendants of Chagatai into the seventeenth
century, when they passed under the authority of the Dzungars and eventually of
the Qing.

The name of the region in Persian and Turkic sources was Moghulistan, the land
of the Mongols, and the Turkic-speaking Muslim population of the Tarim was
called Moghul by its neighbours for centuries after any of them were.

## What the split proved

Come inside and you get the wealth, the literacy and the administration, and
your family stops commanding the men who put it on the throne. Stay outside and
you keep the army and the legitimacy and remain poor.

The Chagatai khanate is the only one of the four that did both, in two halves,
at the same time, and the results are the controlled experiment.

The western half went settled and its khans became ornaments; within a
generation the real ruler was an amir who was not a Chinggisid and had to marry
one. The eastern half stayed nomadic and kept its Chinggisid khans for another
three centuries, and produced nothing anybody outside the Tarim had to take
account of.

Both halves were right about the other one.
