Qaidu barely held a title inside the Chagatai khanate at all. He was an
Ögedeid, not a Chagatayid, and
for more than thirty years he was the actual power behind whichever khan of
Chagatai's line happened to be sitting on the throne he had put them on.

## A rival Great Khan on the empire's own western half

Qaidu's own claim ran through Ögödei, and by the Mongol empire's own
founding rule the chief khanship belonged to Ögödei's line - which made
Qaidu, in his own eyes and in the eyes of everyone who accepted the
argument, the legitimate Great Khan against Kublai's own usurpation of an
office that belonged to a different branch of Chinggis Khan's house
entirely. He never held Karakorum and never seriously expected to. What he
built instead, over three decades of war against Kublai's Yuan, was
authority over the whole Chagatai ulus exercised through a rapid
succession of client khans he installed, deposed and replaced as it suited
him - Nikbey, then Toka Timur, then, from the late 1270s, Dua.

## Talas, 1268

The mechanism by which Qaidu first secured this position was a peace, not a
conquest. Baraq, the Chagatayid khan then ruling Transoxiana, had been
defeated in the field by Qaidu's own ally Möngke Temür of the Golden Horde
and driven back with his army broken. Qaidu offered terms instead of
finishing him: a meeting at Talas in 1268, at which the two princes agreed
that Baraq would keep two-thirds of Transoxiana while the remaining third
went jointly to Qaidu and Möngke Temür, that none of the three would tax
the ruined territory further, and that flocks would be pastured away from
the cultivated land until it recovered. The peace was sealed by rinsing
gold in the cup they drank their oaths from. For Qaidu the substantive
prize was smaller than it looked and larger than it sounds: an implicit
recognition from a reigning Chagatayid khan that Qaidu himself was the
rightful Great Khan of the Mongols, a claim the Chagatai khans would go on
treating as settled for a generation.

Baraq did not long survive his own defeat. Talked into a disastrous
invasion of Khorasan on Qaidu's own encouragement, he returned with his
army in ruins, fell out with the younger princes he blamed for the
failure, and died before Qaidu - who had marched to his camp with twenty
thousand men, ostensibly to help him punish the deserters - could even
reach him. The Chagatai nobility swore allegiance to Qaidu on the spot.
From that point the throne of Chagatai was his to fill.

## The frontier at the edge of the desert

What Qaidu built with that authority was a functioning state in its own
right, with Dua - installed after two shorter-lived predecessors - as his
loyal and long-serving partner rather than his rival. A frontier forty
days wide separated Qaidu and Dua's own territory from Kublai's, watched by
Mongol garrisons on both sides; Marco Polo, who travelled through the
region, records the Uyghur city of Karakhodja sitting neutral between the
two powers. In 1275 the two princes together besieged the Uyghur ruler in
his own capital, trying and failing to force him into an alliance against
Kublai. Two years later, when discontented Yuan princes seized two of
Kublai's own sons in a palace conspiracy, they handed one captive general
straight to Qaidu - proof of how completely his camp had become the
alternative court for every prince with a grievance against the Great
Khan's own line.

## Forty-one battles

Qaidu fought the Yuan for three decades without ever losing his own
position, and fought the Golden Horde on his western frontier at the same
time - fifteen battles, by one count, against Nayan's own Jochid line
alone. In 1301 he invaded the heartland of the empire itself in company
with Dua and forty princes of the houses of Ögödei and Chagatai, meeting
the Yuan army between Karakorum and the Tamir river. The battle's outcome
is contested in the sources that survive it, but its aftermath is not:
Qaidu died shortly after, mourned, in the record's own words, "by his
subjects for his humanity and by his troops for his bravery." He had
fought forty-one battles against the Great Khan and other rivals over his
career, and won most of them - the reason, more than any formal
recognition he never received, that so wide a stretch of Central Asia had
spent a generation answering to a man Karakorum never once had to call
its own ruler.

His own daughter, a wrestler of some fame in Marco Polo's telling, is said
to have staked her hand in marriage on the outcome of a match no suitor
ever won against her, and to have fought at her father's side in his wars
afterward rather than accept a husband she hadn't been thrown by.
