Ibn Battuta reached the Chagatai khanate in about 1333, at the one moment when
it looked as though it was going to work, and he records both the height and
the exact constitutional reason it did not last.

## The prince

Ala al-Din Tarmashirin he describes as *a powerful prince, who has at his
command a large army, and is remarkable for the justice of his laws* — a Muslim
khan who kept the prayers, received a visiting jurist properly, and ruled a
territory the traveller places among the four great kingdoms of the world,
alongside China, India and Iraq.

He also gives the cities, and the two entries do not match. Bukhara he found
wrecked: after the Tartars ravaged it, he writes, it almost entirely
disappeared, and *I found no one in it who knew any thing of science* — a
hundred and ten years after the sack, in what had been one of the great centres
of Islamic learning. Samarkand, a few days east, he calls a very large and
beautiful city.

That is the khanate in two sentences. Enormous wealth, next to a ruin nobody had
rebuilt.

## The statute

Then he explains, better than any chronicle does, what a Chinggisid khan was
actually bound by.

One of the statutes of the *yasa*, he says, was that on a certain day each year
— they call it the feast — the descendants of Chinggis, the governors of the
districts, the wives of the nobles and the general officers of the army must all
assemble. And if the emperor had altered any of the statutes:

> the nobles should stand up and say, Thou hast done so and so upon such and
> such a day, and hast made an alteration in the statutes of El Yasak, and
> therefore, thy deposition is a necessary consequence. They are then to take
> him by the hand, and remove him from the throne, and to place in it another
> of the descendants of Jengiz Khan.

An annual assembly with the power to depose the sovereign, by naming the
specific day on which he broke the law, and to replace him from within the same
family. Any noble who had committed a crime was tried on the same occasion.

## What Tarmashirin did about it

> Now, Tarmashirin had entirely abolished the observance of this day, which gave
> very great offence.

Some time after Ibn Battuta left, the account continues, the Tartars and their
nobles assembled anyway, deposed him, appointed a relative in his place, and
pressed the matter so far that Tarmashirin fled and was put to death.

The charge is usually reported as apostasy from Mongol custom, and this version
is more precise and more damning. He did not merely stop attending the assembly.
He cancelled the one procedure by which he could be held to account, and the
people it belonged to held it without him and used it on him.

## Why that is the peak and not the recovery

Everything Kebek and Tarmashirin had built — the residence in Transoxiana, the
coinage, the provincial administration, the conversion — pointed the khanate at
the settled south. All of it made the annual assembly on the steppe less
necessary to the khan and more clearly the only leverage the steppe had left.

So the reform programme and the thing that destroyed it were the same programme.
A khan who succeeds in becoming a Muslim sovereign of Transoxiana has, by
succeeding, given the men who elected him nothing to do but depose him.

Within a decade the khanate had split in two, and neither half was ever again
worth a visiting traveller's chapter.
