The eastern Chagatai khans became Muslims in the middle of the fourteenth
century, and the fullest account of how is by a man whose own great-grand-uncle
was in the room. Mirza Haydar Dughlat wrote the history of the Moghuls two
centuries later because nobody else had, and the conversion scene is the one he
tells at most length.

## The dogs

Tughluq Timur was not yet khan. He was out hunting when his men brought in some
strangers who had not turned up for the hunt — a shaykh from a ruined town and
his followers, who said they knew nothing about the hunt or its ordinances and
had therefore transgressed nothing. He ordered them released.

He was at that moment feeding his dogs with pig's flesh, and asked the shaykh
angrily:

> Are you better than this dog, or is the dog better than you?

The reply is the hinge of the whole story:

> If I have faith I am better than this dog; but if I have no faith, this dog is
> better than I am.

The prince withdrew, then sent a man to put the shaykh on his own horse and bring
him back with all respect. The detail Mirza Haydar keeps is that the shaykh, on
seeing the saddle stained with pig's blood, said he would walk — and when the
Moghul insisted, spread a clean handkerchief over the saddle and mounted.

He found Tughluq Timur standing alone in a retired spot with traces of sorrow on
his face, and was asked: *What is this thing that renders man, if he possess it,
better than a dog?* The shaykh said Faith, and explained what it was. The prince
wept, and promised that if he ever became khan with absolute authority the shaykh
must come to him and he would become a Muslim.

Then the shaykh died, having charged his son to hold the young man to it.

## Collecting the promise

Tughluq Timur became khan. The son, Arshad al-Din, went into Moghulistan and
could not get an audience, so he took to calling the prayer every morning near
the khan's tent.

One morning the khan sent a man to find out who it was. The messenger seized the
muezzin by the neck in the middle of the call and dragged him in, and the khan
demanded to know who he was to disturb his sleep at that hour every morning. He
answered that he was the son of the man to whom the khan had once promised to
become a Muslim.

> Ever since I ascended the throne I have had it on my mind that I made that
> promise, but the person to whom I gave the pledge never came. Now you are
> welcome. What must I do?

He was given the ablution, declared his faith, and was circumcised.

## The examination

The two of them then decided to put the princes of the ulus to the question one
by one: those who accepted would be well, and those who refused *should be slain
as heathens and idolaters*.

The first in was Amir Tulik, whom Mirza Haydar identifies as his own
great-grand-uncle. Asked whether he would embrace Islam, he burst into tears and
said he had been converted by holy men at Kashgar three years earlier and had
been a Muslim ever since, *but, from fear of you, I did not openly declare it*.
The khan got up and embraced him.

They went through the princes in the same way until one named Jaras refused, and
set a condition: he had a man called Sataghni Buka, and if the Tajik could throw
him he would believe. The khan and the amirs protested that this was absurd. The
scholar said let it be so — *if I do not throw him, I will not require you to
become a Musulman.* Jaras warned him that the man had been seen to lift a
two-year-old camel.

A crowd assembled. The wrestler came on with a conceited air and the scholar
looked small and weak beside him. They closed, the scholar struck him full in the
chest, and he fell senseless; when he came round he fell at the scholar's feet
professing belief. On that day, Mirza Haydar says, a hundred and sixty thousand
people cut off the hair of their heads and became Muslims.

## What the story is doing

It is a conversion narrative written by a member of one of the families in it,
two hundred years later, and every element of it is doing work.

The khan is shown converting out of conviction rather than policy, in private,
years before he had power — which answers the obvious suspicion that a Chagatayid
became Muslim because his subjects already were. The author's own ancestor is
placed first in the examination and revealed to have been a secret Muslim
already, which puts the Dughlats ahead of the dynasty. The one refusal is
resolved by a miracle in front of a crowd, which converts a political decision
into a public demonstration.

What is not in question is the outcome, and Mirza Haydar states it flatly at the
end: Islam was disseminated through the country of Chagatai, and has continued
fixed in it ever since. The western half of the ulus had converted a generation
earlier under Tarmashirin, and the split between a Muslim west and a still-pagan
east had helped break the khanate apart. After Tughluq Timur there was no longer
a religious frontier inside the house of Chagatai — only a political one, which
turned out to be enough.
