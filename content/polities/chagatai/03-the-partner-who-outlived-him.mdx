Dua held the Chagatai throne for close to thirty years, longer than any
other khan in the dynasty's whole history, and for most of that time he
ruled as Qaidu's own loyal partner rather than as an independent sovereign
in his own right. What he did with the seven years he outlived his patron
is a different, sharper story.

## A funeral, and a nomination

When Qaidu died in 1301, it was Dua who managed the succession. He
proposed to the assembled princes that Qaidu's eldest son Chapar, then
absent from the camp, be recognised as heir - a debt of his own, since
Chapar had argued Dua's own claim to the Chagatai throne years earlier
when it fell vacant. The nomination carried without opposition, and when
Chapar returned to take up the succession, Dua was the first to pay him
homage.

Then Dua did something Qaidu himself had spent thirty years refusing to
do: he suggested to Chapar that the two of them recognise the Yuan emperor
Temür as the legitimate Great Khan, ending the war their fathers' whole
generation had fought over exactly that question. Chapar agreed, and
envoys went to the Yuan court to offer submission on behalf of both the
houses of Ögödei and Chagatai.

## The submission that was not one

Chapar's own submission did not hold. Within the year he had quarrelled
with Dua, who by most readings had simply grown tired of a nominal
overlord asserting an independence Dua no longer thought Chagatai owed
him. Dua offered the Yuan an alliance against his own former ally and set
about the war methodically: a first battle near Samarkand went to Chapar,
a second to his own general reversed it, and when Dua proposed a peace
conference at Tashkent to identify who was really responsible for the
fighting, his own envoy arrived with an army instead and destroyed the
force Chapar's general had disbanded in good faith to attend it. At the
same moment, coordinated with Dua though Chapar did not know it, the Yuan
army broke through the Altai and caught Chapar's own hundred-thousand-man
camp by surprise. Deserted by most of his own men, Chapar fled with three
hundred horsemen - to Dua's own camp, of all places, where his father's
old partner received him with formal honour and then quietly absorbed the
greater part of what remained of his territory.

## What thirty years of loyalty bought

Dua died within the year, in 1306, not long after finishing the work of
dismantling the house his own patron had spent a lifetime building
alongside. His son Kebek's predecessor, Kunchek, held the throne only
briefly before Chapar - still alive, still contesting his own diminished
claim - was finally broken for good by a further alliance between the Yuan
and the Chagatai khans, ending the war of succession that had opened with
Kublai's own break from Ariq Böke half a century earlier. Chapar himself
lived out his remaining years doing homage at the Yuan court, the last
Ögedeid prince to matter in Central Asian politics.

What Dua's own reign actually settled was less about who held legitimate
authority than about which of the two rival houses would survive as a
territorial power once the argument was finally over. The house of Ögödei,
Qaidu's own line, disappeared into scattered clans - absorbed by the
Chagatai khans, or drifting west to join the Golden Horde's own
confederacy, where they would later be counted among the founding strength
of the Uzbeks. The house of Chagatai kept the throne, the territory, and -
until Timur's own generation found it useful to keep a Chagatayid
figurehead on it regardless - the title that had once been Qaidu's to give
away three khans at a time.
