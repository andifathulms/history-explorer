title: The amir who could not be khan

The western half of the Chagatai inheritance produced the last world conqueror, and
he spent his entire career unable to use the only title that would have made his
conquests legitimate.

## Where he came from

Timur was a Barlas — one of the Turkicised Mongol tribes of Transoxiana, the
military class of the western ulus — and he first appears in the record as a
junior officer deciding which side to take.

Mirza Haydar, quoting the *Zafar-Nama*, puts him at the crossing of the Khojand
river in March 1360, when Tughluq Timur brought the Moghul army down out of the east
to retake Transoxiana. Timur's own chief, Amir Haji Barlas, collected troops from
Kesh and Qarshi to oppose them, thought better of it, and withdrew to Khurasan.
Timur did not go with him.

That is the whole man in one decision. The country was going to be taken; the
question was who would be running it afterwards.

## What he inherited and what he could not

He inherited a country that had been without an effective khan for a generation,
with a tribal aristocracy accustomed to making and unmaking figureheads, and a
Moghul army coming over the mountains every few years to assert a claim that
nobody in Transoxiana wanted honoured.

What he could not inherit was the title. Timur was not a descendant of Chinggis
Khan, and by the rule that governed the whole Turco-Mongol world he could therefore
never be khan.

He did what Edigü would do on the Volga a generation later and what the Manghits at
Bukhara would still be doing four centuries later: he kept a khan.

A Chinggisid puppet of the house of Ögödei sat on the throne. Coins were struck in
the puppet's name. Decrees went out in the puppet's name. Timur called himself
*amir* — commander — and *güregen*, son-in-law, because he married a princess of
the Chinggisid line and that was the nearest thing to legitimacy available to him.

He conquered from Delhi to Ankara and from Moscow to the Persian Gulf as somebody
else's officer.

## What he did with Transoxiana

He rebuilt it, by the only method available to a man with an army and no revenue
worth the name: he took the craftsmen.

The masons, tile-workers, weavers, armourers and glassmakers of every city he
stormed were sent to Samarkand. The wealth of Delhi went to Samarkand. The scholars
went to Samarkand. Ibn Battuta had found Bukhara with nobody in it who knew anything
about science sixty years earlier; within a generation of Timur his own grandson
Ulugh Beg was running an observatory at Samarkand whose star tables were being
copied in Europe two centuries later.

It is the exact reverse of the Ilkhanid method. Ghazan tried to make a conquered
country productive by reforming the way it was taxed. Timur made one city
magnificent by stripping every other city he could reach.

## Why he belongs here

Because the Chagatai khanate did not end in 1347. It divided, and both halves went
on — and the western half's continuation is not a khanate at all but the Timurid
empire.

The elements are all Chagatai elements: the Turkicised Mongol tribal army of
Transoxiana, the puppet khan, the Chinggisid legitimism, the amirs who had been
deposing khans since Kebek, and the permanent quarrel with the Moghuls in the east.
What changed is that one amir was strong enough to stop pretending that the
pretence mattered.

His descendants ruled Transoxiana for a century. One of them, driven out of it,
took Kabul and then Delhi, and the dynasty he founded in India was still there when
the British arrived — and it was called, by everyone including itself, the Mughal:
the Persian word for Mongol.
