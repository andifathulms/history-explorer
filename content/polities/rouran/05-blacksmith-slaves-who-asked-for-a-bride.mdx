The Turks had served the Jeu-jen for generations as ironworkers,
subject craftsmen rather than a people anyone expected to rule
themselves. In 546 their chief, Tumen, asked Anakwe for a Jeu-jen
princess in marriage - a request for the kind of recognition a subject
people did not typically receive. Anakwe's answer was to make war on
him instead. It was a catastrophic miscalculation: Anakwe's army was
utterly defeated, and the king who had spent his whole reign clawing
his way from exile to something like parity with the Chinese court
killed himself rather than survive the defeat.

## A throne that could not survive its own founder

Anakwe's heir, Amrodjin, fled to the newly founded Qi empire and was
given land in northern Shanxi, only to revolt against his own hosts
soon after - a rebellion that ended with his entire horde broken up
and scattered. A cousin of Anakwe was elected to succeed him, but
found no better fortune: repeated defeats at Turkish hands forced him
to seek refuge with a warlord who was, in practice, running the
Western Wei emperor's own court from behind the throne.

## A massacre outside the gates

The Turks were not content to let their former masters live on
anywhere, even as refugees. Using the influence their own military
power now commanded, they insisted on the wholesale butchery of the
Jeu-jen who had sought asylum at the Western Wei court - a massacre
carried out by, or in the presence of, the Turkish ambassador himself
outside the gates of the capital at Xi'an. Only the younger males were
spared, and only to be distributed as slaves into princely and ducal
households. From that point on, the Jeu-jen go unmentioned in the
Chinese record even as a subject tribe under their Turkish and Uyghur
successors - a silence the historian reading it calls as complete a
destruction as any people of the steppe ever suffered.
