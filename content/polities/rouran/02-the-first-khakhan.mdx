The "sixth grandson" who first led the Jeu-jen north of the desert
was succeeded, a generation on, by his own grandson Zarun - a man
Gibbon, misled by French translators, called Toulun. Where his
ancestors had scraped together vagabonds and raided neighboring
tribes, Zarun organized a formidable military system and built an
empire that ran from Korea in the east to the oasis of Harashar in
the west, and south to the Tuyuhun country and modern Gansu - holding
every road west except the main one, his courts established near the
old waystations later travelers would know from Marco Polo's own
account of the Silk Road.

## A title no steppe ruler had claimed before

Zarun marked his own new standing with a new title: Kudovar Khakhan,
"the governing and spreading emperor" - the Chinese record's own
account of the first time the word Khakhan was used by any ruler of
the steppe, a title later empires from the Turks to the Mongols would
all eventually claim for themselves. To the northwest of his own
territory lay the scattered remnants of the once-dominant Xiongnu,
and Zarun absorbed them one by one as his own power grew, until his
empire stood as the unrivaled power of the eastern steppe - for the
moment, at least, unchallenged by the Toba dynasty newly seated on
the throne of northern China, a neighbor Zarun had not yet chosen to
provoke.
