Tughochir's grandson Cheunu gave the Jeu-jen their first real recovery
in generations, punishing the rebellious Kankalis to the west in 516
and raising his country once more to real power. It did not survive
his own succession. His younger brother Anakwe could not take the
throne without opposition, and fled for his life to the Toba court,
where he was received well. In his absence a cousin bearing the
striking, un-Turkic name of Brahman took charge of the horde and
hunted down Anakwe's own supporters, until the Kankalis pressed him
hard enough in turn that Brahman himself had to surrender to a Toba
governor.

## A quarrel two rival beasts were kept apart to fight

The Toba court debated what to do with two rival claimants of the
same defeated people, and settled on a policy of calculated
separation: quarter them far enough apart that neither could
cooperate against their hosts, encourage them to weaken each other,
and step in only if either became dangerous. Brahman was sent to the
far west near modern Hami with a Chinese resident ostensibly to
assist, in practice to watch him; he later conspired to rebel anyway,
was taken to China, and died there. Anakwe was settled in the east,
given the title Duke of Shuofang and formally recognized by the Tobas
as king of the Jeu-jen - a position from which his own family went on
to build marriage alliances with both the Toba court and the rival
Tartar dynasty of Qi.

## A vassal who stopped calling himself one

Anakwe's real opportunity came from his patron's own weakness. As the
Toba Wei dynasty collapsed into anarchy, Anakwe used the chaos to
consolidate a power the Chinese court could no longer check, and
simply stopped calling himself a vassal. He kept Chinese literate men
and eunuch clerks in his own service, built an official government on
the Chinese model, and began presuming to an equality with the very
dynasty that had once arbitrated his own claim to the throne.
