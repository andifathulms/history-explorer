Between the end of the Hasmonean state and the twentieth century, the number of
kingdoms anywhere in the world that can be called Jewish is very small, and the
two clearest cases are a thousand years and three thousand kilometres apart.
They are not the same kind of case, and the difference is the useful part.

Adiabene is a converted dynasty. A king and a queen mother became Jews in the
first century, after arguments with two teachers about what conversion actually
required. Their descendants were Jews for at least two generations and went to
fight and die in Judaea. What is not visible anywhere is a state.

There is no Adiabenian royal inscription that changes its gods. There is no
decree, no policy, no evidence of what the population of the country between
the Zabs believed or was expected to believe, and no confessional foreign
alignment beyond the family's own personal involvement with Jerusalem. Josephus
is interested in the palace because a palace conversion is what his argument
needs, and the palace is what he shows us. Below it the record is silent.

## The other one

Himyar in South Arabia is the stronger case, and the comparison shows why.

There the royal inscriptions themselves change. Around 380 the old South Arabian
gods stop being named and the formulas begin invoking Rahmanan, the Merciful,
and referring to Israel and the people of Israel. The change holds for a century
and a half across several reigns, which is longer than any individual king's
enthusiasm. By the sixth century the confession is the axis of the kingdom's
foreign relations and the stated reason two Christian empires combined to
destroy it.

That is a public religion — something the state says about itself, in its own
documents, consistently, at a cost.

## Why the distinction is worth keeping

Because collapsing it flatters both cases and describes neither.

Call Adiabene a Jewish state and you assert a confessional population and a
religious policy that no evidence supports, and you flatten the thing Josephus
actually recorded, which is more interesting than a policy: a live disagreement
between two Jewish teachers, conducted in front of a king who wanted to do the
thing properly, about whether reading the commandment was enough.

Call Himyar merely a dynasty with a private faith and you cannot explain the
inscriptions, the persistence across reigns, or the war.

One is a family that converted and stayed converted through a rebellion, a
famine relief operation and a tomb outside the walls of Jerusalem. The other is
a state that changed what it said about God in public and was eventually killed
for it. Both are rare. They are rare in different ways.
