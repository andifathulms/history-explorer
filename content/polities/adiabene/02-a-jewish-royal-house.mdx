Josephus devotes a long passage of the twentieth book of the *Jewish
Antiquities* to the conversion of the royal family of Adiabene, and it is the
fullest account of a first-century conversion to Judaism that survives.

The queen, Helena, was taught by a Jewish merchant named Ananias while her son
Izates was away in Characene, at the head of the Gulf, where he was being taught
by the same man. Both accepted the God of Israel. Izates wanted to
be circumcised; Ananias advised against it on the grounds that his subjects
would not accept a king who had adopted foreign customs, and that worshipping God
mattered more than the rite. A second teacher, Eleazar, arrived from Galilee,
found him reading the Law, and told him that reading the commandment without
performing it was worse than not knowing it. Izates was circumcised.

That exchange is one of the most-discussed passages in the study of ancient
Judaism, because it is a live debate about what conversion required, conducted
between two Jewish teachers in front of a Parthian client king, and reported by
a Jewish historian writing for Romans.

Helena moved to Jerusalem. She arrived during the famine of the 40s — the one
Acts also refers to — and bought grain in Egypt and dried figs in Cyprus and
distributed them, which the rabbinic tradition remembers her for. She built a
palace in the city and a monumental tomb outside the northern wall, which
survives and is known as the Tomb of the Kings and was for a long time thought to
be a Judaean royal burial. She and Izates were buried in it.

Her descendants fought in the revolt of 66. Josephus names Adiabenian princes
among the defenders of Jerusalem, and records that they were taken to Rome as
hostages after it fell. A Mesopotamian dynasty on the other side of the Roman
frontier sent its royal family to die in somebody else's war because it had
become part of the people fighting it.

How far the conversion went beyond the palace is not known. There was a Jewish
population in Adiabene before and after, and the kingdom was a Parthian vassal in
a region with Zoroastrian, Aramaean, Greek and later Christian communities. What
is established is that its kings were Jews for at least two generations.
