Ardashir annexed Adiabene around 226, in the same sequence of campaigns that
took Characene, Elymais and the Parthian empire itself. It became a Sasanian
province and its dynasty disappears from the record.

That sentence is the whole of the ending, and the disproportion between it and
what the country turned into afterwards is the point of the chapter that
follows this one.

By 226 the kingdom had already been two things it is worth naming in one place.

It had been a Parthian vassal for something over two centuries: a small state
between two rivers, supplying troops, quarrelling with Armenia, and being walked
over by Roman armies with somewhere else to be. It had a Jewish royal house for
at least two generations of that, whose members were buried outside the walls of
Jerusalem and whose princes died in the revolt of 66.

And by the time Ardashir arrived it had been Christian, in its towns and among
some of its gentry, for about a hundred years — long enough for its bishops to
have been martyred, imprisoned and educated abroad, and for the see at Arbela to
have become the senior church east of the Roman frontier.

The kingdom stopped in 226. Almost nothing else did.
