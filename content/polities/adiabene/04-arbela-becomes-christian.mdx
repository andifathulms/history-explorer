Ardashir annexed Adiabene around 226, in the same sequence of campaigns that
took Characene, Elymais and the Parthian empire itself. It became a Sasanian
province and its dynasty disappears from the record.

What the country became afterwards is the reason the ending is not the end of
the interest.

Adiabene was one of the earliest and most substantial centres of Christianity
east of the Roman frontier. The *Chronicle of Arbela* — a Syriac text whose
authenticity has been disputed at length, which is worth saying — gives the see
a line of bishops from the second century; whatever its reliability, the
independent evidence for a large Christian population in the region from the
third century is not in doubt. Arbela was a metropolitan see of the Church of
the East, and Adiabene supplied a substantial share of that church's bishops,
scholars and martyrs.

This site's Sasanian record has a chapter on that church: a Christian community
descended partly from Roman deportees, persecuted in the fourth century for
being potential partisans of the enemy, and then organised in 410 under royal
authorisation into a hierarchy that declared itself independent of the Western
patriarchates in 424 — legal precisely because it answered to nobody in Roman
territory, and sending missions that reached Tang China.

Adiabene is where a great deal of that came from.

So the country in this record is Jewish in its royal house in the first century,
substantially Christian by the fourth, Sasanian and then Muslim afterwards, and
Kurdish now. Erbil is the capital of the Kurdistan Region of Iraq and the
citadel mound the Assyrians built on is a UNESCO site.

The corpus has no way of recording that either. `religions_carried` on this page
is null because the kingdom was converted rather than converting; there is no
field for what a place becomes after the polity in it stops. The chapters carry
it, which is what this site has now said on a dozen records, and the count of
those records is itself the finding.
