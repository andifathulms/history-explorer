The country outlasted the kingdom by a very long way, and what it became is
better recorded than anything the kingdom itself did — because the people
writing it down were interested in bishops rather than kings.

The source has to be introduced with a warning. Nearly everything known about
early Christian Adiabene comes through the *History of the Bishops of Adiabene*
by Mshikha-Zkha, a sixth-century compiler from the province who says openly that
he is working from earlier authorities now lost. Its authenticity has been
disputed at length, and Wigram, writing in 1910, believes it more readily than a
modern reader should. What follows is what that tradition says, which is a
different thing from what happened, and it is the only connected account there
is.

## The first bishops

Pqida was consecrated first bishop of Adiabene in 104, and he had been born a
slave in a Magian household and raised in that religion. He got his freedom, was
converted, and was persecuted for apostasy — not by his owner, the tradition is
careful to say, but by the owner's family. He escaped them and spent five years
travelling with his teacher before being made bishop.

His successor Samson was killed by Magians in 123. Wigram notes what that makes
him: the first man to die for the Christian faith in a country which afterwards
supplied, he thinks, more martyrs than any other.

The third, Isaac, converted a Zoroastrian landholder of some standing named
Raqbokt. The mobeds sent men to kill the convert; the men arrived, found him
away from home, and rather than go back empty-handed took the bishop instead and
shut him up for a while in a dark pit. The same punishment had been used on
Pqida's account earlier. It is a small detail and it says a great deal about the
period: an organised priesthood with enough standing to send out killers and not
enough authority to have the killing sanctioned, improvising on the day.

Because that is the pattern for the whole Parthian period. Persecution in
Adiabene came from the Magian clan, not from the government. Formal
state persecution of Christians in the east does not begin until the Sasanians,
and then not until Constantine had made Christianity the official religion of
the rival empire and every Christian in Iran a potential partisan of the enemy.
The Syriac historians make the point sharply: there was no organised persecution
in the east until its day was over in the west.

## Which made it a refuge

So for two centuries the traffic ran the wrong way.

While Christians were being killed for their religion inside the Roman empire,
Adiabene was comparatively safe, and people crossed the frontier eastwards and
settled. Later the Sasanian kings would add to the population by force, carrying
off whole towns from Roman Syria and resettling them in Mesopotamia. The
combined effect, Wigram argues, was to keep an isolated church in some contact
with theological argument in the west — not close contact, but not none.

Traffic went the other way too, on ordinary business. Abd-Mshikha, bishop of
Arbela from 190 to 225, had become a Christian while at Antioch as a student,
and came home with it.

## The general election

By 225 the church east of the frontier had more than twenty bishops. Arbela
appears to have been the only see in Adiabene itself, which is why it was
recognised as a metropolitan seat when the eastern church organised itself into
a hierarchy at the council of 410 — the arrangement that made it legally
independent of every patriarch in Roman territory, and that would eventually
send missions as far as China.

And then there is the detail that puts the whole record in proportion.

Mshikha-Zkha, writing the history of his own province, reaches the year 225 —
the year the Parthian empire that had ruled Adiabene for three centuries was
destroyed and replaced by the Sasanians — and mentions it in passing. Wigram's
comparison is that he notices it about as much as an English writer notices a
general election.

That is what a country looks like from the inside once the kingdom has stopped
mattering to it. The dynasty that Josephus made famous is gone, the empire it
was a client of has just fallen, and the man writing the local history records
the change of régime as background to an episcopal succession, because in Arbela
the succession was the news.
