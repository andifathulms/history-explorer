Adiabene — Hadyab in Syriac — is the country between the Greater and the Lesser
Zab, east of the Tigris: the old Assyrian heartland, with Nineveh in ruins on one
side and Arbela as its capital. Arbela is Erbil, and it has been continuously
inhabited for longer than almost anywhere on earth; the citadel mound stands
thirty metres above the plain and is made of the debris of its own occupation.

<Figure id="erbil-citadel" />

The kingdom appears in the record in the last two centuries BC as one of the
vassal kingdoms of the Parthian empire. Nobody founded it as far as the record
shows; it is simply there, and the century in which it became a kingdom cannot
be fixed closer than a hundred years.

The structure it belonged to was loose by design. A Parthian king of kings sat
over great noble houses with their own armed followings and over a ring of
vassal kingdoms — Persis, Elymais, Characene, Adiabene, Media Atropatene,
Armenia — each with its own dynasty and its own coinage, supplying contingents
when called.

Adiabene's kings played that part unremarkably. They fought in Parthian wars,
quarrelled with Armenia, and were periodically overrun by Roman armies passing
through on the way to Ctesiphon; Trajan took Arbela in 116 and made the country
into a short-lived province called Assyria, which his successor abandoned along
with everything else east of the Euphrates. One of them, Narses, was drowned in
the Zab around 190 by his own overlord for sympathising with a Persian revolt,
and the country was plundered as a punishment for its king.

That is the political history, and it is not why anybody remembers Adiabene.
What people remember is a passage in Josephus.
