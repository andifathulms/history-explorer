Adiabene — Hadyab in Syriac — is the country between the Greater and the Lesser
Zab, east of the Tigris: the old Assyrian heartland, with Nineveh in ruins on one
side and Arbela as its capital. Arbela is Erbil, and it has been continuously
inhabited for longer than almost anywhere on earth; the citadel mound stands
thirty metres above the plain and is made of the debris of its own occupation.

The kingdom appears in the record in the last two centuries BC as one of the
vassal kingdoms of the Parthian empire, and its origins are not established —
which is why this record's span opens with a hundred-year range and why its
`founder` field is null.

This corpus's Parthian record describes the structure it belonged to and codes
the empire `client-levy` for it: a king of kings over great noble houses and
vassal kingdoms in Persis, Elymais, Characene, Adiabene, Media Atropatene and
Armenia, each with its own dynasty and its own coinage. Four of the six are now
on this site. Adiabene is the one whose interest is not political.

Its kings were Parthian clients who fought in Parthian wars, quarrelled with
Armenia, and were periodically overrun by Roman armies passing through on their
way to Ctesiphon — Trajan took Arbela in 116 and made Adiabene into a short-lived
province called Assyria, which his successor abandoned with the rest.

None of that would justify a page. What does is a passage in Josephus.
