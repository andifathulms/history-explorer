title: A cringing prince

Liu Bei, ruler of the southwestern kingdom that styled itself the true
continuation of the Han, had no sooner proclaimed himself an independent
prince than he turned his mind to vengeance rather than strategy. His target
was not Wei, the strongest of the three successor states, but Sun Quan's
kingdom of Wu — a personal grudge that his own advisers warned him was the
wrong war to fight, since Wei was by far the more dangerous neighbour and an
alliance with Wu against it the more sensible course. Liu Bei ignored the
advice and invaded anyway.

## An alliance bought cheap

The danger forced Sun Quan's hand. Rather than fight Wei and Shu at once, he
accepted Cao Pi as emperor and was confirmed in return as Prince of Wu — a
cringing posture, and one Sun Quan had no intention of maintaining a moment
longer than the immediate crisis required, but it cost Cao Pi nothing and
bought Wei a secure eastern flank while its two rivals exhausted themselves
against each other. Liu Bei's army pressed to the frontier of Wu regardless,
and Sun Quan's general Lu Xun, distrusted by Cao Pi as much as either of his
neighbours but proving the better ally than either, held the line through
twelve months of neither side willing to strike first.

## The night attack

Time favoured the defender. Liu Bei's army, fighting far from home and
worn down by inaction, grew dispirited while Lu Xun's forces, fighting on
their own ground with a friendly population behind them, stayed fresh. When
Lu Xun judged the moment right, he struck at several points at once in a
night assault that broke Liu Bei's army outright — his best generals killed
or captured, thousands of soldiers dead, the entire baggage train taken. Liu
Bei himself barely escaped the field, and died three years later never
having recovered from the shock of the defeat, leaving his young son
Liu Shan a claim far larger than the power left to sustain it.

## What the victory changed, and what it didn't

Cao Pi's response to the news was to raise his demands on Sun Quan at once,
and the two former enemies found in Wei's renewed pretensions a stronger
reason to set their quarrel aside than either had found in years of open war
— the same logic of self-preservation that had produced Sun Quan's cringing
submission in the first place, now working to end it. Cao Pi died not long
after, succeeded by his son Cao Rui, and it fell to the new emperor to press
Wei's advantage on every front still open to him.

## Liaodong

Cao Rui's clearest triumph came not against either southern rival but on his
own northern frontier, where Gongsun Yuan, King of Liaodong, had grown
confident enough in his isolation to defy the court at Luoyang outright.
Cao Rui sent a large army under the general Sima Yi to settle the matter.
Gongsun Yuan defended himself with real resolution and won an early success,
but reinforcement after reinforcement tipped the balance against him; shut up
in his own capital, he was killed attempting to cut his way out, and his city
was handed over to the victorious soldiers to plunder. The whole of Liaodong
became a Wei province, and Cao Rui's reputation stood higher among his
neighbours than at any point before or after — though he did not live long
to enjoy it. Within days of Sima Yi's triumphant return from the campaign,
Cao Rui was dead, leaving an eight-year-old heir to a general his own
deathbed instructions had just finished making indispensable.
