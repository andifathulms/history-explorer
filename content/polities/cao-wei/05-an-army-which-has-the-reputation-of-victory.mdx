Sima Zhao lost nothing by Cao Mao's failed attack — if anything it removed
the one obstacle still willing to contest his authority directly — and he
installed a third of Cao Rui's nephews, Cao Huan, on the throne in his
place. The southern kingdom of Shu, still ruled in the name of the Han it
claimed to continue, had by then outlived every advantage its founder's
early campaigns had won it, and Sima Zhao turned the whole of Wei's military
weight against it.

## The end of the Han that called itself the Han

The general Deng Ai carried the invasion through with a speed the southern
court had no answer for. A council of despair debated the options at Chengdu
— some counselling flight, some resistance to the last man — and the
southern ruler, Liu Shan, accepted his minister's advice to throw himself on
Sima Zhao's mercy rather than fight on. His own son argued for a different
ending, that a dynasty on the point of extinction should at least finish
with its lives rather than its dignity, but was overruled; Liu Shan
surrendered in person while his son ended his own life and his family's in
the ancestral temple rather than watch the surrender completed. It is in the
son's act rather than the father's submission, Boulger writes, that the last
scion of the great house of Han actually vanishes from history. Shu was
absorbed into Wei's territory, and Deng Ai wrote to Sima Zhao urging him to
press on at once against Wu as well — "an army which has the reputation of
victory flies from one success to another" — advice Sima Zhao chose not to
take. The conquest of Wu was left for nearly twenty more years.

## The sword changes hands one last time

Sima Zhao died in 265, and his son Sima Yan inherited both his father's
accumulated authority and his father's patience run out. He deposed the
nominal emperor — Boulger names him Yuandi — without ceremony beyond what
was needed to make the transfer official, and proclaimed a new dynasty, the
Jin, taking the name Emperor Wu in his own right.

## What Cao Pi's family had built, and lost, in forty-five years

The rivalry that had once divided the Han inheritance three ways ended in
favour of the dynasty Cao Pi had founded — but only for as long as it took
one loyal general's descendants to do to the Cao family exactly what the
Cao family had done to the Han. Cao Wei had held the recognised capital, the
largest population, and the strongest claim to being the empire's actual
government for forty-five years. None of that had been enough to keep the
throne in the hands that had first taken it, because the same accumulation
of trusted, indispensable, generation-spanning service that had made Cao
Cao's rise possible in the first place was, in the end, exactly what Sima Yi
and his sons had been allowed to build for themselves.
