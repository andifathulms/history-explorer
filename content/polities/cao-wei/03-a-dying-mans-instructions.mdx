Cao Rui died a few days after Sima Yi's victorious return from Liaodong,
leaving the throne to his nephew Cao Fang, a child of eight. His last
instructions to Sima Yi asked the general to be as faithful to the boy as he
had always been to Cao Rui himself — a request that assumed the loyalty Sima
Yi had shown a living emperor who could reward or punish him would transfer
intact to an eight-year-old who could do neither.

## An emperor who could not yet rule

The years that followed tested that assumption without quite breaking it.
Sun Quan, watching the succession of a child as the opportunity he had waited
for, attempted to press an advantage while Wei's government was distracted;
Sima Yi's arrangements foiled the attempt, and Sun Quan withdrew baffled from
the contest. A separate war with the southern kingdom of Shu ended the same
way, Wei's frontiers held rather than pushed forward, but held all the same
— competent defence rather than conquest, which was as much as a regency
government generally manages, and more than most.

## A minister who mistook the moment

The one real internal crisis of these years came from inside Wei's own
court rather than from either rival kingdom. A minister — Boulger names him
Cao Shuang — judged the child emperor's minority the moment to seize the
governing power for himself, and moved to displace the arrangement Cao Rui
had left in place. Sima Yi curbed him. The sources give the episode little
space, which is itself telling: whatever resistance Cao Shuang mounted, it
was not enough to threaten Sima Yi's position, only enough to demonstrate
that unseating him from the outside had already stopped being a realistic
possibility.

## What "restoring order" actually meant

Sima Yi died in 252, the same year as his old rival Sun Quan, and his
position passed to his son Sima Shi as naturally as Cao Rui's throne had
passed to Cao Fang thirteen years earlier — the difference being that no one
now needed to ask whether the son would inherit his father's actual power
along with his title. Every crisis of the boy emperor's reign had been
answered by the same family reasserting the same indispensability, and each
time the answer had cost the throne a little more of what it still
controlled directly. Cao Fang himself would not survive contact with that
arithmetic much longer.
