Emperor Xian had been a puppet for as long as he had been emperor. Placed on
the throne in 191 after a palace massacre that killed ten thousand eunuchs
and the general who had opposed them, he passed from the hands of one
strongman to the next — first Dong Zhuo, who had installed him and issued
edicts in his name until his own overreach made him too many enemies, then
Cao Cao, who added military skill and personal courage to Dong Zhuo's
appetite for power and gradually gathered every strand of real authority
into his own hands. Boulger's verdict on the exchange is blunt: Emperor Xian
found that in trading one master for another he had not become more
independent, only changed the name of the man who ruled through him.

## Twenty years of war for a third of the empire

Others refused to accept Cao Cao's ascendancy without a fight, and two
decades of near-constant warfare followed. Yuan Shao proclaimed himself
emperor outright and was broken by Cao Cao for the presumption; Sun Quan and
Liu Bei carved out separate domains in the south and held them. Cao Cao won
almost every battle he fought, and still ended his life in command of only a
third of the old Han territory — the other two-thirds split between rivals
he could defeat in the field but never dislodge entirely. When Cao Cao died,
his position passed to his son Cao Pi in the ordinary course of inheritance,
in the year 220.

## An abdication with nothing forced about the ceremony

Emperor Xian, apprehensive of what refusal might cost him, abdicated in
favour of Cao Pi. There was no battle to force the issue — thirty years of
accumulated warlord control had already settled the substance of the
question, and the ceremony that followed simply gave it a name. Xian retired
to private life with the title Prince of Shanyang, and outlived the man who
deposed him. It was, Boulger writes, the ending of "the brilliant dynasty of
the Hans which had ruled China for more than four hundred years with
splendour and wisdom" — armies that had marched across the breadth of Asia,
provinces and vassal states won in every direction, cities and canals and
roads that still stood to testify to what the dynasty had built.

## What Cao Pi actually inherited

What Cao Pi took possession of was the name of that inheritance and the
smallest workable fraction of its substance: the recognised capital at
Luoyang, the central and northern provinces, and the diplomatic status that
came from being the state every foreign embassy and every claim to legitimate
succession had to reckon with first. Two rival princes, in the south and the
southwest, held the rest between them, each convinced his own claim to the
Han inheritance was the true one. China had not been reunified. It had
simply acquired, in place of one fiction of unity, three competing claims to
it — and Wei's advantage lay less in strength on any single battlefield than
in being the one all the others had to answer to.
