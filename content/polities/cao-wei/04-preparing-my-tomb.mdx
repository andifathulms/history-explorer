Sima Shi did what his father never had: he deposed an emperor outright.
Internal disturbances gave him the pretext, and Cao Fang — twenty-one years
old by then, no longer the child his regency had begun with, but no more
able to govern in fact than he had been at eight — was set aside in favour
of another of Cao Rui's nephews, Cao Mao. Sima Shi did not long survive the
act; his death passed the family's accumulated authority to his brother
Sima Zhao, and the real governing power in Wei remained exactly where it had
been, only under a different name.

## A war fought for its own sake

The rivalry between Wei and its southern neighbours dragged on through these
years without producing a decisive result on either side, until 257, when
Sima Zhao's general Wang Ji won a string of victories and took the town of
Zhuquan with its garrison of a hundred and fifty thousand men — a heavy blow
struck in Wei's favour, and one Sima Zhao made sure was credited entirely to
himself. Over Cao Mao's protests, he had himself proclaimed governor of the
empire with the title Prince of Jin.

## A boy who would not stay a cipher

Cao Mao was not without courage, whatever else the office had cost him. He
gathered a handful of companions he trusted enough to confide in, and set
out for Sima Zhao's own palace with the plain intention of ridding the
empire of an over-mighty subject by his own hand. It was a bold plan, and it
failed completely. There was, in the event, a killing that day — but Cao
Mao himself was the one killed, not Sima Zhao.

## A different commander's answer

Years earlier, in a different theatre entirely, a Wei commander named
Zhangde had faced his own version of the same arithmetic and answered it
with more room to manoeuvre than Cao Mao ever had. Besieged at the small
fort of Xincheng for ninety days by a southern army under Zhuge Ke, his
walls breached in numerous places and no relief in sight, Zhangde tricked
his besiegers into a hundred-day truce by inventing a law that supposedly
pardoned a garrison which surrendered without hope of relief after holding
that long — then used the borrowed time to rebuild his ramparts from the
ruins rather than to prepare his surrender. Asked what he thought he was
doing, he answered plainly: "I am preparing my tomb, and to bury myself
under the ruins of Xincheng." Zhangde's ruse worked; the relief he had never
really expected arrived in the form of his own besiegers' exhaustion and
withdrawal. Cao Mao, cornered in his own palace with no walls left to
rebuild and no truce worth inventing, had made the same choice of dying
rather than submitting - without anything like Zhangde's luck to survive
having made it.
