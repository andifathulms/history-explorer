Tattooed Serpent fell mortally ill in the spring of 1725, and his death
threatened to take a portion of the nation with him: he and the Great
Sun, his own brother, had sworn since childhood to follow each other
into death, and the whole nation understood what that promise would cost
if the Great Sun kept it. A French colonist who counted the war chief as
an intimate friend was summoned, along with the fort's commandant, to
talk the grieving sovereign out of killing himself - a vigil that lasted
through the night, with the Great Sun's own household standing guard at
his hut door and hoping, as much as any Frenchman did, that he would
keep the calmer resolution he professed by morning.

## A funeral bed already prepared

Before the night's vigil began, the mourners found Tattooed Serpent laid
out in his own hut exactly as tradition required: dressed in his finest
clothes, face painted with vermilion, shod as if for a journey, a
feather crown on his head, his weapons fastened to the bed beside him,
and every calumet of peace he had ever received arranged around it. A
pole planted nearby carried forty-six painted rings of cane, one for each
enemy he was credited with killing in life. His household - two wives,
his chancellor, his physician, his chief servant, his pipe-bearer, and a
noblewoman who volunteered to join them out of friendship rather than
obligation - waited beside him to be strangled at the interment, still
served meals at the customary hours as though he had not yet died.

## A procession that circled back on itself

On the day of interment, mourners appointed to die were paraded twice
daily before the temple to act out their own deaths in advance, each
attended by eight relatives who would serve as their executioners and
who earned noble rank by doing so. A captured runaway who had once fled
Natchez service for the French was found, restored to the ranks of the
condemned, and then spared a second time when three elderly kinswomen
offered to die in his place - after which, the source that records it
notes dryly, he grew insolent, having learned enough at New Orleans to
make dupes of his own countrymen. The funeral procession itself circled
the dead chief's hut three times before winding its way to the temple in
overlapping loops, a child already strangled by its own parents thrown
before the bearers at each turn so they might walk over it. Tattooed
Serpent and his two wives were buried together within the temple; the
other dead were interred separately, and his hut was burned to the
ground behind them, the fullest display of Natchez royal ceremony any
source describing this nation records.
