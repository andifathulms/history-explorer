The Natchez traced their own laws to a single lawgiver who, by their own
account, descended among a people scattered and constantly at war with
their neighbors, and offered to rule them on conditions of his own
choosing. He would lead them to a better country, he said, if they
promised to live by his instructions and acknowledge no sovereign but
him and his descendants forever after. His people agreed, and in the
presence of the whole nation he brought fire down from the sun onto
prepared walnut wood - the eternal flame that would burn afterward in
two temples, tended day and night by eight chosen keepers under penalty
of death, so that if one fire ever went out it could be rekindled from
the other.

## A rule passed through women

The lawgiver's most consequential decision concerned who would follow
him. Noble rank, he ruled, would descend through women rather than men:
his own eldest son might marry as he pleased, but that son's children
would be commoners, while his eldest daughter's children would be
princes and princesses, and her own eldest son would inherit the
sovereignty itself - a rule that held even if she married a commoner, and
even if her sons themselves eventually lost their rank while her
daughters kept theirs. It was a law the Natchez were still living under
two centuries later, when a French colonist recorded it directly from
the reigning Great Sun's own mouth.

## A fire that could not be allowed to fail

The Natchez told of one moment when the system nearly broke: a temple
guardian who let the eternal fire die while on watch relit it in secret
with ordinary flame rather than confess his failure, and a plague that
followed - four years long, killing many of the five hundred Suns the
nation counted at its height - was remembered as the fire's own
judgment. Only a deathbed confession and a ritual transfer of flame from
the second temple, "snatched" by violence rather than simply carried,
was said to have ended it. Whether or not the plague and the broken vigil
were truly connected, the story itself did the work its keepers needed:
it made the fire's survival a matter the whole nation had reason to
watch.
