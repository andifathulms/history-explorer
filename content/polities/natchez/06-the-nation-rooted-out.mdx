New Orleans took months to organize a response, but when it came it came
in force. A French army marched on the Natchez, who by then had built a
fort of their own to withstand a siege they knew was coming. When French
artillery fire happened to land among the women and children sheltering
at its center, their screams broke the defenders' resolve faster than
any breach in the walls could have: the men, the source that records it
says plainly, "made the sign to capitulate" at the sound of their own
families' terror. Negotiations dragged on through a night the Natchez
hoped to use for one more escape, as they had managed once before against
an earlier French commander, but this time French sentries watched the
gate too closely; only a few who had been out hunting managed to slip
away and reach the Chickasaw. The rest surrendered outright - the Grand
Sun, the female Suns, warriors, women, young people and children alike.

## A colony's answer to a colony's fear

The French did not simply hold their prisoners. Shipped first to New
Orleans and imprisoned there, the captives were moved to the royal
plantation once overcrowding threatened disease, and from there put
aboard ships bound for the French colony of Saint-Domingue and sold into
slavery. Among the women deported was the Tattooed Arm, the same
Grand Sun's mother who had tried and failed to warn the French of the
massacre she could not prevent - a French colonist who had known her
personally recorded hearing the full story of her attempts directly from
her own mouth before she was shipped away for good.

## The nation the French called irreplaceable, and destroyed anyway

The deportation was not incidental to the punishment; it was the
punishment's whole point. So few Natchez had escaped the siege, and so
few women remained among even those who had, that the nation lacked any
realistic means of rebuilding its own numbers - which was, in the
recorded judgment of the French colonist who had lived among them for
years, exactly why Saint-Domingue rather than a Louisiana plantation was
chosen as its destination: a permanent removal, meant to root the nation
out for good. He called the Natchez, in the same passage, the most
useful and the most conspicuous native nation the French colony of
Louisiana had ever known. Within a single year of a dispute over a
cornfield, that nation had ceased to have a political existence of its
own.
