Two hundred miles up the Mississippi from the Natchez, on the Illinois
side across from present-day St. Louis, stands the largest earthwork ever
built north of Mexico: the Cahokia mound, more than treble the volume of
any other true mound surveyed in the United States by the time the
Smithsonian's Bureau of Ethnology mapped the site in 1882. Whoever built
it stood, by any honest reckoning, at the head of the same broad
Mississippian mound-building tradition the Natchez still practiced when
Europeans arrived - centuries after Cahokia's own great mound had already
gone quiet.

## A quarrel over who built them

For much of the nineteenth century, American writers preferred to
believe the mounds of the Mississippi valley were the work of a
"lost race" - a vanished, more civilized people driven out or exterminated
long before the arrival of the ancestors of the Indian nations Europeans
actually met. The Bureau of Ethnology's own 1894 survey argued otherwise,
and pointed directly at the Natchez as living proof: a documented nation,
still building and using mounds in the exact same manner the ancient
earthworks displayed, observed and described in detail by named French
witnesses within living memory of the survey itself.

## A mound raised, and abandoned, with every reign

The proof lay in a Natchez custom no invented "lost race" was needed to
explain. Both the temple and the house of the Great Sun stood atop
earthen mounds roughly eight feet high, built by hand and maintained by
the living nation - the temple mound about thirty feet square, the Great
Sun's own house mound some sixty feet across. A French missionary who
witnessed it directly recorded that when a Great Sun died, his cabin was
torn down and an entirely new mound raised to receive his successor's
house, since Natchez custom forbade a new ruler from ever occupying his
predecessor's dwelling. Multiply that practice across the centuries a mound-building tradition
endured, and the sheer number of mounds scattered across the Mississippi
valley stops looking like evidence of a mysterious vanished civilization,
and starts looking like exactly what it was: the accumulated record of
ordinary succession, one new mound at a time, built by the ancestors of
the same nations still living in the valley when Europeans arrived.
