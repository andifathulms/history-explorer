The man who founded the principality of Antioch is described at length by the
daughter of the emperor he cheated, and Anna Komnene's portrait is worth having
precisely because she is not pretending to be fair.

## Supper at Cosmidium

Bohemond arrived at Constantinople in 1097 with the crusade, and was lodged and
given a rich table. The cooks also brought in uncooked meat and birds and
explained that the food had been prepared in the usual way, but that if it did
not suit him, here was raw meat to be cooked however he liked.

That was on Alexios' instructions. He had guessed that Bohemond would suspect
poison, and he was right. Bohemond would not touch the cooked dishes or even
put a fingertip to them; he pushed them away and distributed them among his
attendants — appearing to do them a kindness, Anna says, and in fact handing
them a cup of death. His own cooks prepared the raw meat in the Frankish
manner.

The next morning he asked the men who had eaten the supper how they felt. They
said they felt exceedingly well. So he explained himself: remembering his wars
with the emperor and that terrible battle, he had been afraid of poison.

The emperor then had a room in the palace heaped with treasure — cloth, stamped
gold and silver — until there was no walking across it, and the doors thrown
open suddenly. Bohemond exclaimed that if all this were his, he would have made
himself master of many countries long ago; and was told it was a present. He
thanked them and went to his lodging. When the treasure was delivered he had
changed his mind and said he had never imagined the emperor would insult him
so, and sent it back. Alexios, Anna writes, quoted the proverb about bad things
returning to their owner — and when Bohemond saw the porters carefully packing
it all up again, he changed his mind once more and gave them pleasant looks,
*like a polypus that changes its form in an instant*.

Her verdict: in roguery and courage he was superior to every Latin who came
through, as he was inferior to them in troops and money.

## The document

Eleven years later Bohemond had taken Antioch, kept it against his oath, gone
home to Europe, raised a war against the empire, invaded the Balkans and been
comprehensively beaten. The terms he accepted at Devol in 1108 are transcribed
in the *Alexiad* in full, and they are extraordinary reading, because the voice
is his.

The previous agreement, he begins, is annulled: he declared war and broke it,
and that cancels the charges as well as the promises. Then, on the new one:

> *now as if moved by remorse and like a stricken fisherman I have recovered my
> sanity and, I might almost say, been rendered more discreet by thy spear.*

He will be the emperor's liegeman — "or to express it more clearly and plainly,
thy menial and subject". He will hold his lands as a gift under a golden bull
signed in red ink. He will take arms against anyone who lifts a hand against
the empire, Christian or pagan. He will receive two hundred pounds a year of
the coinage of the emperor Michael, collected by an envoy sent from Syria. And
he swears it on the Gospels, the Cross, the crown of thorns, the nails and the
lance.

## What a treaty is worth

Bohemond never went back to Syria. His nephew Tancred, who was actually in
Antioch, treated the document as being about somebody else, and in the ordinary
sense it governed nothing at all.

In another sense it governed the next sixty years, because it existed. Two
emperors after Alexios came to Syria in person with armies to collect what was
written in it, and both collected. A text that was a dead letter the day it was
signed turned out to be the most durable thing Bohemond ever produced — more
durable than the war he lost, and more durable than anything he actually held.
