Antioch was not Edessa. Where the county was a thin Frankish crust over an
Armenian and Syriac countryside, the principality's capital was one of the
great cities of the eastern Mediterranean — a patriarchal see, a Greek city
with Greek institutions, walls running for miles over a mountain, and a working
port at St Symeon at the mouth of the Orontes.

So Antioch lived on customs as well as on land, which Edessa inland never
could. It is also why the principality's central internal quarrel was
ecclesiastical. Antioch had a Greek patriarch. The Latins installed their own
and spent a century in a dispute that was never merely about liturgy, because
the return of the Greek patriarch was one of the standing imperial demands and
conceding it meant conceding the larger claim. Greek and Armenian went on being
languages of administration alongside Latin, and not as a concession to
tolerated minorities: these were the people who ran the city.

The principality's own expansion ran east towards Aleppo and north into
Cilicia. It ended in a single morning in June 1119, which the next chapter is
about.
