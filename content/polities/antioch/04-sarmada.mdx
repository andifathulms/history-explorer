In June 1119 Roger of Salerno, ruling Antioch for the boy prince, marched out
to meet the Artuqid lord of Aleppo without waiting for the king of Jerusalem,
who was coming.

Ibn al-Qalanisi, in Damascus, describes the army he took. Franks and Armenian
footsoldiers gathered from every province and district of the principality;
more than twenty thousand horse and foot besides camp followers; an immense
army, he says, completely equipped and perfectly armed. It encamped at Sarmada,
between Antioch and Aleppo. This is Antioch at its largest — every man the
principality could put in a field, in one place.

The Turkmens had been assembling from every quarter, in his phrase, like lions
seeking their prey and gerfalcons hovering over their victims.

## The morning

The two armies came in sight of each other and the Muslims charged at once,
surrounding the Franks and closing on them with swords and arrows.

Not one hour of Saturday 28 June 1119 had passed, Qalanisi writes, before the
Franks were on the ground in one prostrate mass, horse and foot together with
their animals and their weapons, and not one man of them escaped to tell the
tale. Roger was found stretched out among the dead.

Men who had been there afterwards walked the field to look at what had
happened, and what they remembered was the horses: lying on the ground like
hedgehogs, because of the quantity of arrows standing in them.

## What was destroyed

The Franks called it the Field of Blood, and the name has stuck to it in every
language since.

What was killed there was not a garrison or an expedition but the knighthood of
the principality as a body — the class that held the castles, ran the frontier
and supplied the field army. Antioch itself did not fall; Ilghazi did not
seriously try, and the king of Jerusalem arrived and stabilised the frontier
within weeks.

But the principality never expanded again on its own account. From 1119 its
eastern boundary stopped moving, and for long stretches it was governed by
regents sent from Jerusalem, because there was nobody in Antioch to govern it.

Half a century of northern Syrian history follows from one morning, and the
morning happened because a prince would not wait a week for his ally.
