Antioch was taken in June 1098 after an eight-month siege in which the besiegers
came closer to starving than the besieged. How that happened is told most often
from inside the Frankish camp. Anna Komnene tells it from Constantinople, at
second hand, and the distortions in her version are as useful as the facts.

## Hunger, and a bishop

Her account begins where the crusade's own accounts do: the Latins terribly
pressed by famine and blockade, going to their bishop for counsel.

The bishop she names is Peter — the man, she notes with satisfaction, who had
been beaten at Helenopolis. He tells them the trouble is their own: they had
promised to keep themselves pure until they reached Jerusalem, they have broken
that promise, and God is therefore no longer helping them as before. They must
repent in sackcloth and ashes, with tears and vigils, and he will spend his own
time propitiating the Deity on their behalf.

They obey. A few days later the bishop, *inspired by a divine voice*, assembles
the chief counts and tells them to dig on the right side of the altar, where they
will find the Holy Nail.

They dig, find nothing, and come back discouraged. He prays harder and sends them
to search more carefully. They go again, find it, and carry it to him *overcome
with joy and awe*. It is entrusted to Raymond of Toulouse to carry into battle,
as the holiest of them.

## Two mistakes worth keeping

Anna has the object wrong — it was the Lance, not the Nail, and her translator
says so in a footnote. She also has the man wrong: the visions belonged to Peter
Bartholomew, a poor Provençal, and she has attached them to Peter the Hermit, the
only Peter of the crusade whose name had reached her.

Both errors tell you what the Byzantine court knew and how it knew it. The story
had crossed to Constantinople as a story — a relic found under an altar after a
fast, and a famous preacher — and arrived with the roles compressed the way
rumour compresses them.

What is striking is what she does *not* do with it. Anna Komnene despises
Bohemond, thinks the crusade broke its oath, and writes the whole episode in her
own book without irony. The relic was found. The prayer worked.

## The battle, and the man who asked first

Then the sortie from the secret door. Robert of Flanders asks to ride out ahead
with three companions only; he dismounts, prostrates himself three times, prays,
and they charge Kerbogha where he stands on his hillock, shouting *God with us*.

The Turks break before the battle has properly begun. Most of the fugitives are
caught in the eddies of the river and drowned, and Anna says those coming behind
crossed on the bodies. The baggage and booty were so great it took thirty days to
carry into Antioch.

And then, in a single sentence, the founding of the principality:

> They took thought for Antioch and looked for a man to guard it. This man was
> Bohemund who had asked for this position even before the city was captured.

The rest of the army went on to Jerusalem, took several coastal fortresses on the
way, passed by the ones that would have needed a long siege, and stormed the city
within a lunar month.

Anna gives no comment at that point, and none is needed. She has just recorded,
as a matter of administrative convenience, that the man who wanted Antioch before
it fell was given it by the colleagues he had sworn an oath with — and her whole
book is an argument about what that oath was worth.
