Antioch was stormed in May 1268 and burnt to the ground. Baybars then wrote to
its prince, who had not been in the city, to tell him what had happened to it.

He addressed him as *count*. Bohemond VI was still count of Tripoli; he was no
longer prince of anything, and the salutation is the first blow of the letter.

## What the letter says

It opens by reminding him that he has been looking on "like a man in a mortal
swoon" while piece after piece of his territory was taken away. Then it
describes the sack, sparing him nothing:

> *Hadst thou but seen thy knights trodden under the hoofs of the horses! thy
> palaces invaded by plunderers and ransacked for booty! thy treasures weighed
> out by the hundredweight! thy ladies bought and sold with thine own gear, at
> four for a dinar! hadst thou but seen thy churches demolished, thy crosses
> sawn in sunder, thy garbled gospels hawked about before the sun; the tombs of
> thy nobles cast to the ground; the monk, the priest, the deacon slaughtered on
> the altar; the rich abased to misery; princes of royal blood reduced to
> slavery! could'st thou but have seen the flames devouring thy halls... then
> would'st thou have said, "Would God that I were dust!"*

And then the turn, which is the reason the letter is famous:

> *This letter holds happy tidings for thee: it tells thee that God watches over
> thee to prolong thy days, inasmuch as in these latter days thou wert not in
> Antioch!*

It closes on the detail that makes the whole thing work. As not a man has
escaped to tell him the tale, the sultan tells it; as no soul could bring him
word that he alone is safe while all the rest have perished, the sultan brings
it.

## The same sentence, a hundred and forty-nine years apart

A Damascene chronicler used almost exactly that phrase about Antioch once
before: after the Field of Blood in 1119, *not one man of them escaped to tell
the tale*.

In 1119 it described the destruction of the principality's army and Antioch
survived. In 1268 it described the destruction of Antioch, and this time the
formula was not a chronicler's flourish but a piece of psychological warfare
posted to the man it concerned, with a covering note explaining that he was
lucky to have been elsewhere.

## Afterwards

There was no principality left to lose. Latakia held out until 1287 as an
appendage of Tripoli; the princes of Antioch after 1268 were princes of
nothing, living at Tripoli and keeping the title.

The city did not recover. Its marble went where the marble of other razed
Syrian towns went in those years, into the decoration of mosques in Cairo. The
patriarchate of Antioch, one of the ancient sees of the Christian world, has
been a title held elsewhere ever since; nobody has governed Syria from Antioch
since the afternoon Baybars' men came over the wall.
