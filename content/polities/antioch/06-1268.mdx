Baybars came up from Egypt in the spring of 1268, took Jaffa in March and
Beaufort in April, and arrived before Antioch in the middle of May. Bohemond VI
was not there; he was at Tripoli. The constable Simon Mansel rode out, was
captured, and was sent to tell the city to surrender. It refused.

The city was stormed on 18 May after four days. The gates were shut on the
Mamluk order so that nobody could escape, and what followed inside was a
massacre and an enslavement on a scale that appalled contemporaries who were
professionally accustomed to both. Baybars afterwards wrote to Bohemond VI a
letter describing in detail what had been done to his city and his people,
which survives, and which is one of the more studied documents of the period
precisely because of how deliberately it was composed to wound.

Antioch had been a great city without interruption for a millennium and a half
— Seleucid capital, Roman metropolis, one of the five patriarchates, Byzantine
frontier fortress, Norman principality. It stopped being one in that week and
has never been one since. The site is a provincial town in southern Turkey.

The principality ended with it. There was no field army left, and no territory
to speak of: Latakia held out until 1287 as an appendage of Tripoli, and the
princes of Antioch after 1268 were princes of nothing, ruling at Tripoli and
keeping the title. Nothing was conquered by Tripoli and nothing was inherited.
The same man simply went on ruling the one thing he had left.

Antioch in 1268, Tripoli in 1289, Acre in 1291. Read in order, those are not
three separate wars but one programme, worked through over twenty-three years
by a state that had decided the Franks in Syria were a solved problem and was
going down the list.
