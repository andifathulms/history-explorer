The Khazars came out of the wreck of the western Turkic khaganate. When that
state came apart in the 650s under Tang pressure, the ruling house on the lower
Volga did not disappear with it: it kept the Ashina claim, kept the title
qaghan, and turned a wing command into a state of its own between the Caspian
and the Black Sea.

That is a common enough way for a steppe polity to begin. What the Khazars did
with the position afterwards is not.

## The position

The lower Volga is not a place anybody would choose for a capital on
agricultural grounds. It is a place you choose if what you want to tax is
movement.

Four systems crossed there. The Volga carried furs, wax, honey and slaves down
from the northern forests. The Caspian carried the trade of Iran and Khurasan.
The steppe road ran east into Central Asia and west into the Pontic grasslands
and beyond them to the Danube. And the Caucasus passes, above all the one at
Derbend, were the gate between all of that and the Muslim world.

A state sitting on the junction of those four does not need to produce
anything. It needs to be trusted by everyone using them, and it needs to be
able to shut the Caucasus.

## Between two universalisms

By the eighth century the Khazars had a Christian empire on one side and a
Muslim caliphate on the other, and both of those states were organised around
the proposition that everyone ought eventually to belong to them.

A pagan steppe khaganate between them was a natural object of missionary
attention from both, and conversion to either would have meant something
specific and unattractive: a Christian Khazar khagan becomes a junior partner of
Constantinople, a Muslim one becomes a client of the caliph. Both religions came
with a hierarchy attached, and in both hierarchies the convert enters at the
bottom.

Somewhere in the eighth or ninth century the ruling elite converted to Judaism
instead.

The evidence is real and comes from three directions that had no reason to
agree: Arab geographers, Byzantine accounts, and a Hebrew correspondence
between a Khazar king and a Jewish official in Muslim Spain. How deep the
conversion went below the elite is not recoverable and was probably not very
deep.

The obvious reading is the political one. A monotheism with no imperial sponsor
made the khaganate legible to both neighbours — a people of the book, with a
law and a scripture, arguable with in terms both understood — without making it
subordinate to either. It is the third option, and the Khazars are the only
state that ever took it.

## What it bought them

Three hundred years, in the most contested corridor in western Eurasia, as a
power both empires had to negotiate with rather than absorb.

It also cost them a place in anybody's story afterwards. A state that stops the
Arab advance into eastern Europe is a hero of Christendom; a state that holds
the Caucasus against Byzantium is a bulwark of Islam. A state that did both
while being neither belongs to no later tradition, and gets remembered by
nobody's chroniclers except as a neighbour.
