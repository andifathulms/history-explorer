In the 950s Hasdai ibn Shaprut, a Jewish physician and diplomat in the service of
Abd al-Rahman III at Cordoba, wrote to the king of the Khazars asking whether it
was true that there existed a sovereign Jewish kingdom. A reply survives,
attributed to King Joseph, describing the conversion, the royal genealogy and the
extent of the realm.

The authenticity of the exchange has been argued over for a century and the
current position is broadly that it is genuine, with later editing. What is
striking regardless is the geography of it: a Jewish official of an Umayyad
caliph in Spain corresponding with a Turkic khagan on the Volga, in Hebrew, about
a conversion that had happened two centuries earlier. Both of those polities are
on this site.

The Khazar conversion has attracted more speculation than evidence, most of it in
the twentieth century and much of it politically motivated in one direction or
another. What the sources support is an elite conversion, a court that
adjudicated between Muslim, Christian, Jewish and pagan communities, and a
capital where all four had standing.

The polity that stopped the Arab advance into eastern Europe therefore did it
while being neither Christian nor Muslim, which is why neither surviving
tradition made much of it. It is the clearest case in this corpus of a state
being important and unremembered because it did not belong to anyone's later
story.
