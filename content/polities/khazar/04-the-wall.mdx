The Arab-Khazar wars of the seventh and eighth centuries are the least discussed
of the caliphate's frontiers and arguably decided the most. Umayyad armies pushed
north through the Caucasus repeatedly; the Khazars pushed south; the fighting was
severe, and in 730 a Khazar force destroyed an Arab army and killed its commander
in Azerbaijan. Neither side broke through permanently, and the frontier settled
in the Caucasus rather than on the Volga or the Don.

The consequence is that the caliphate did not expand into eastern Europe. The
steppe north of the Black Sea, and the Slavic populations beyond it, developed
outside the Islamic world for reasons that have a good deal to do with Khazar
military capacity.

The khaganate's other distinction is that its ruling elite converted to Judaism,
at some point in the eighth or ninth century. The evidence is real — Arab, Byzantine
and Hebrew sources, and a correspondence between a Khazar king and a Jewish
official in Cordoba — though the depth and extent of the conversion below the
elite is not recoverable. The obvious reading is political: a power sitting
between a Christian empire and a Muslim one, whose commerce depended on being
trusted by both, took the third option.

Kievan Rus took Sarkel on the Don and then Atil itself between about 965 and
969, and the khaganate stops appearing in the sources as a power.
