Al-Masudi, writing at Baghdad in 943, gives the fullest description anybody
wrote of the Khazar capital while it was standing, and what he describes is not
a nomad camp. It is a constitutional arrangement.

## The city

Atil, he says, is cut in three by a great river coming down from the high
country of the Turks, one branch of which runs off towards the Bulghars and into
the sea of Maeotis. The town is built on both banks, and in the middle of the
river is an island holding the seat of government. The king's castle stands at
one end of the island, joined to one bank by a bridge of boats.

The population is Muslims, Christians, Jews and pagans. The king, his court and
everyone of Khazar race practise Judaism, which had become the dominant religion
of the state, he says, since the caliphate of Harun al-Rashid.

He adds a specific and datable reason why there were more Jews there than there
used to be: in the year 332 of the Hijra — the year he was writing — the emperor
Romanos at Constantinople was persecuting the Jews of his empire to convert them
to Christianity, and a great many had left Rum for the Khazars.

## The seven

Then the sentence that makes the record worth having:

> It is an invariable rule in the capital of the Khazars that there should be
> seven judges in all: two for the Muslims, two for the Khazars, who decide
> according to the Torah, two for the Christians, who decide according to the
> Gospel, and one for the Slavs, the Rus and the other pagans.

Not toleration as a policy announced from above. A fixed bench, with a fixed
number of seats, allocated by community, each applying its own law — and one
seat for people with no book at all.

Nothing like that existed in either of the empires on the Khazars' borders. The
caliphate had a developed law for non-Muslim communities and it was a law of
subordinate status. Byzantium had no equivalent at all. Here four legal
traditions sat at the same level in the same city because the city's business
required all four to keep coming.

## The Muslim guard, and its terms of service

The most extraordinary thing al-Masudi reports is the army.

The Khazar king's guard was Muslim: the *Larisiyya*, originally from the
neighbourhood of Khwarazm, who had come to the Khazar realm not long after the
appearance of Islam, fleeing war and plague. Seven thousand of them served as
the king's horse-archers, in cuirass, helmet and mail, some with lances,
equipped as Muslims usually are. They had judges of their own religion.

They had also negotiated their terms, and al-Masudi lists them: free exercise of
their religion, permission to build mosques, the call to prayer to be made
publicly, and the vizier always to be chosen from among them. The vizier in his
own day, he notes, was a Muslim named Ahmad ibn Kuwayh.

And one clause more:

> It was agreed that whenever the king of the Khazars should be at war with the
> Muslims, those who serve in his army should stand aside and not fight their
> co-religionists, but should march against all other infidels.

A Jewish king, whose standing army is Muslim, whose chief minister is
constitutionally required to be Muslim, and whose army has a written exemption
from fighting Muslims.

## What kind of state that is

Not an empire in any sense the word usually carries. A state of this shape
cannot conquer in a direction its own guard is excused from fighting in, and it
does not need to.

What it can do is hold a junction and charge for it, and guarantee to four
different legal communities that their contracts will be enforced by a judge
they recognise. That is the whole product, and it was worth enough that people
came from Khwarazm and from Constantinople to be inside it.
