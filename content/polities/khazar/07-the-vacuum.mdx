Most states that end leave a successor arguing about them. This one left a hole,
and the hole has been filled, repeatedly, by people who needed it to contain
something.

## What actually followed

Politically, the answer is straightforward and unglamorous. The Pontic steppe
passed to the Pechenegs and then the Cumans; the Volga route passed to the Rus,
who had just taken it by force and had no reason to keep paying anybody for it;
the Volga Bulghars, who had been Khazar tributaries and had converted to Islam
in 922, went on as an independent Muslim state on the middle Volga until the
Mongols.

Khazar communities are mentioned here and there for a century or so afterwards
— in Crimea, on the Taman peninsula, in a Byzantine document or two — and then
they stop being mentioned. There is no Khazar successor state, no Khazar
diaspora that anybody traced at the time, and no later dynasty anywhere that
claimed descent from them, which is unusual: steppe pedigrees were valuable and
were routinely claimed.

## Why the silence is loud

Consider what the khaganate did. It held the Caucasus against the Umayyads for
about eighty years, in a war severe enough that in 730 a Khazar force destroyed
an Arab army and killed its commander in Azerbaijan. The caliphate never got
past it, and the steppe north of the Black Sea — and the Slavic populations
beyond — developed outside the Islamic world.

Any state that had done that as a Christian power would be a fixture of European
historical memory. As a Muslim power it would be a fixture of the other one.
Being neither, it belongs to no continuous tradition that had an interest in
remembering it, and the Rus who destroyed it wrote the account that survives
best.

## What the vacuum attracted

An unremembered Jewish kingdom of uncertain fate is an almost perfect vessel,
and the twentieth century treated it as one.

It has been used to argue that Ashkenazi Jews descend from converted Turks
rather than from the ancient Near East, a claim advanced with various motives —
some of them by people trying to detach European Jews from any connection to
Palestine, some by people trying to detach them from Europe. It has been used in
the other direction as evidence of Jewish statehood in exile. Genetic and
philological work has not supported the descent claim, and the argument has
continued regardless, because it was never really about the Khazars.

What the sources actually support is narrower and more interesting than any of
it: an elite conversion, a court that adjudicated between four communities, a
capital where all four had standing and their own judges, and a Muslim guard
with a written exemption from fighting Muslims.

## The one thing that lasted

The word *qaghan* came west with them. Rus princes used it of themselves for a
while afterwards — it appears for Vladimir and Yaroslav in eleventh-century
Kievan texts — which is the ordinary way a conquering power borrows the
vocabulary of the state it replaced.

A title, in somebody else's chancery, for about a century. That is the
inheritance, and it is a fair measure of how completely a state can vanish while
its neighbours go on standing exactly where it put them.
