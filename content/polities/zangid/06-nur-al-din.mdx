---
title: Making the counter-crusade a programme
drafted_from: hillenbrand-1999
phase: peak
---

Zangi took Mosul, Aleppo and Edessa, and made himself the first ruler of the
period with a reputation as a fighter of Franks. His son Nur al-Din did the
more consequential thing. He turned the recovery of
Jerusalem from an objective into an ideological programme: sponsoring jihad
literature, endowing madrasas and Sufi lodges across Syria to build a Sunni
religious establishment that supported it, commissioning a minbar to be
installed in the Aqsa mosque when the city was retaken, and unifying Muslim
Syria under one authority as the precondition.

He did not live to use it. He died in 1174, having sent an expeditionary force
to Egypt that ended the Fatimid caliphate and left his own officer's nephew in
charge there. Saladin took Damascus from Nur al-Din's heirs within months, and
inherited both the unified Syria and the programme. That is why the Zangids are
hard to date an ending to: the Ayyubids did not destroy them so much as replace
them branch by branch over two generations, while carrying on their project.
