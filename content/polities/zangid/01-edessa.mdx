Imad al-Din Zangi was an *atabeg* — a Turkish officer appointed guardian to a
Seljuk prince, an office this site's Great Seljuk page describes inverting with
total predictability into a dynasty. He got Mosul in 1127 and Aleppo the year
after, and for sixteen years he did what the Turkish commanders of Syria and
the Jazira did: fought other Muslim rulers, mostly, for cities.

In December 1144 he took Edessa.

It was the first of the crusader states to be founded, in 1098, and it was the
most exposed — a Frankish county east of the Euphrates with Muslim territory on
three sides, held by a small ruling class over an Armenian and Syriac Christian
population. The count was away; Zangi came fast, invested it for four weeks,
mined the walls and took the city.

What followed matters more than the siege. In the Islamic world, Zangi became
the first ruler of the crusading period with a reputation as a fighter of
Franks. He had done a great deal else — this corpus does not have to pretend
he was a holy warrior by temperament, and the sources closest to him describe
a man of exceptional brutality who was murdered in his tent by his own servants
two years later — but the reputation was real and it was politically usable.

In Europe, the fall of Edessa produced the Second Crusade. Bernard of Clairvaux
preached it, two kings led it, and it achieved nothing at all: the armies
crossed Anatolia badly, arrived in Syria, decided to attack Damascus — which
was a Muslim city hostile to Zangi's son and therefore the one place in Syria
that had been useful to them — besieged it for four days and went home.

That failure is the hinge this page turns on. It destroyed the Damascene
appetite for accommodation with the Franks, and it delivered Damascus to Nur
al-Din, who took it in 1154 without a fight. Syria unified because a crusade
attacked the one Muslim power that had been prepared to deal with the crusader
states.
