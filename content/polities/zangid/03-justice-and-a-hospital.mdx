The previous chapter describes Nur al-Din turning the recovery of Jerusalem
into a programme. This one is about the rest of the programme, which was
domestic and is the part that outlasted the campaign.

He built madrasas across Syria in numbers — Damascus and Aleppo especially —
endowing a Sunni religious establishment that this site's Great Seljuk page has
Nizam al-Mulk inventing the instrument for a century earlier. He built Sufi
lodges. He restored the walls and the water supply of Damascus.

Two of the foundations are worth naming individually.

The **Bimaristan al-Nuri** at Damascus, endowed in 1154, was a hospital and a
teaching hospital: wards, a pharmacy, salaried physicians, and instruction. It
went on functioning as a hospital and a medical school into the nineteenth
century, seven hundred years, and the doctors attached to it wrote a
substantial medical literature. Ibn al-Nafis, who described the pulmonary
circulation of the blood three centuries before Europe did, trained there.

The **Dar al-Adl** — the house of justice — was something new. Nur al-Din built
the first one at Damascus around 1163: a hall in which the ruler himself sat, in
public, on fixed days, to hear grievances brought by his subjects against his
own officials and soldiers, with the jurists present. The institution spread —
the Ayyubids and then the Mamluks on this site's pages built their own — and
what it was for is legible. A Turkish military ruler with no lineage, governing
an Arab population, asking it to pay for a long war, made his answerability the
public face of his government.

That is the same problem this corpus keeps recording and a different solution
to it. The Marinids on the Islamic West page answered it with madrasas, the
Almohads with a doctrine, the Fatimids with a mission. Nur al-Din built a room
in which he could be complained to.

His reputation followed accordingly. The chroniclers of the next century,
writing under the Ayyubids and Mamluks, made him the model just ruler — pious,
austere, accessible — against whom later sultans were measured. Saladin, who
took his state, spent a good deal of effort being seen to continue it.
