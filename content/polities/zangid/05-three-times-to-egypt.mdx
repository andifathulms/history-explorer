title: Three times to Egypt

Bar Hebraeus, a Christian bishop writing a century later with no side to take,
gives the Egyptian expeditions as what they were: a bidding war for the richest
country in the region, conducted by three parties who all knew the stakes, in
which the winner was the one who kept turning up.

The instrument was a Kurdish officer. Shirkuh and his brother Ayyub, sons of
Shadhi, came from Dvin in Armenia and had been in the service of the eunuch amir
of Tikrit; Shirkuh killed a Christian of Tikrit whom the amir was fond of, and
the two brothers fled to Zangi at Mosul and prospered there. Ayyub held the
citadel of Baalbek for Zangi, surrendered it to Damascus after Zangi's murder,
and later helped hand Damascus to Nur al-Din. Shirkuh was given Homs. When
Shawar, the Fatimid vizier, came north in flight from a rival and asked for
troops, Nur al-Din looked at the men available and judged Shirkuh the best of
them.

**1163.** Shirkuh went south with Shawar and restored him, and Shawar worked out
from his behaviour that he intended to take the country, so he made peace with
the Franks instead and paid Shirkuh none of the gold or the towns he had
promised. Shirkuh took Bilbays; Shawar brought the king of Jerusalem down with a
large Frankish army; Egyptians and Franks together shut Shirkuh up in Bilbays
for three months. What released him was news from Syria — the Franks had been
broken and captured at Harim — and the king of Jerusalem, needing to go home,
persuaded Shirkuh to march out unmolested and leave Egypt to its owners.
Shirkuh, says Bar Hebraeus, agreed readily.

**1167.** He went back, taking his nephew Saladin, crossed to the west bank of
the Nile and went up into Upper Egypt. Shawar sent for the Franks again, and a
combined army crossed after him. Shirkuh's officers advised retreat: they were
outnumbered, the peasants and the soldiers and everyone in the country were
enemies, and there was nowhere to fall back to. Then a slave of Nur al-Din named
Bungush said that if they did not fight they would have to go back to Nur al-Din
and explain themselves, and he would stop their rations and take back what they
had been eating for years, because a man who trembles at the prospect of a
battle is not a king's soldier but a field labourer, or else should live in the
house with the women. Shirkuh said he was of the same opinion.

What he then did was a trick, and Bar Hebraeus describes it as one. He put
Saladin and all the baggage in the centre of the line to make the numbers look
bigger, and told him that the enemy would throw their whole weight at the centre
thinking that was where he was; Saladin was not to fight hard, but to give ground
little by little and not to worry about pursuit, because Shirkuh would be behind
them. He kept for himself a body of trained hunting men chosen for stamina. The
centre gave way, the Franks and Egyptians chased it, Shirkuh chased the chasers,
the runaways turned around, and the pursuers were caught between. Bar Hebraeus
puts Shirkuh at two thousand and the allies at ten. He then took Alexandria
without a fight.

The terms he accepted for leaving are the clearest measure of what Egypt was
worth. Fifty thousand dinars for Shirkuh to go home; Alexandria handed back to
the Egyptians; and a hundred thousand dinars a year to the Franks, who were to
keep an officer and a troop of horse posted at the gates of Alexandria.

**1168-69.** The Franks came back for the rest, and Shawar tried to buy them off
with a million dinars, of which he paid a hundred thousand down. Bar Hebraeus
gives his reasoning to them in the first person: you know what I owe you, and if
I thought the Arabs would let me hand you Egypt I would hand it over now, but
they would destroy me for it — so take as much gold as you like, go home, and be
my tax collectors as before, and do not let Nur al-Din come and take the country,
because then you will have neither the country nor the tribute. The Franks took
the down payment and left.

Nur al-Din sent Shirkuh anyway. Bar Hebraeus explains why in eleven words: it was
not his object to do good to the Egyptians, but to rule over them.

What followed in Cairo was three months of everyone plotting to murder everyone
else and being prevented by procedure. The caliph received Shirkuh honourably and
then, because Shawar ran the administration, put him off with empty words and
provided nothing for him or his men; the caliph was planning a feast at which to
seize Shirkuh and his nephew, and was stopped by Shawar's son. Saladin wanted to
kill Shawar and was stopped by his uncle. Then one day Shawar came to call and
found Shirkuh out — he had gone to get a blessing at a holy man's tomb — and
Saladin, riding with him and talking, threw himself off his horse and took hold
of him, and could not kill him without his uncle's order, and said so. They put
it to the caliph, who by then had no authority over his own vizier at all, and
the caliph told them to do it.

Shawar was killed and his house was plundered and Shirkuh became vizier in his
place, with the titles the Fatimid viziers carried — King, and Captain of the
Host. He held the office two months and died of strangury. His nephew took it,
and made every soldier in the army his friend by the size of his gifts, and ruled
Egypt.
