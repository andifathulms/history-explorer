The three expeditions to Egypt are the reason the Zangids do not exist.

Saladin spent the five years after his uncle's death as the nominal servant of
two masters and the actual master of one country. He ended the Fatimid caliphate
in 1171, having the Friday sermon at Cairo pronounced for the Abbasid caliph
instead. Nur al-Din, watching an officer's nephew acquire Egypt in his name and
remit very little of its revenue, was preparing to march south and settle the
question when he died in 1174.

His heir was eleven. Saladin took Damascus within months, and over the
following twelve years took Aleppo and then Mosul's obedience from the
remaining Zangid branches.

The dynasty did not end there, which is why its extinction is dated nearly
eighty years later: Zangid branches went on ruling at Mosul and in the Jazira
as Ayyubid vassals until the middle of the thirteenth century. The Ayyubids did
not destroy the Zangids. They inherited the state, its army, its religious
establishment, its programme and even its furniture, and then replaced its
family one branch at a time.

It is the same mechanism that carried the Ghaznavids out of Samanid service and
the Ghurids out of theirs, with one difference. The instrument that walked away
here walked away with the whole project intact, and finished it. Saladin took
Jerusalem thirteen years after Nur al-Din died, and put Nur al-Din's pulpit in
the mosque.
