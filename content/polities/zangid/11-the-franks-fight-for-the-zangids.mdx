Nur al-Din died in 1174 leaving an eleven-year-old heir, and the officer he had
sent to Egypt came back for the rest of it.

Saladin's case was that he was acting for the boy. Lane-Poole describes the
ride: seven hundred picked horsemen across the desert to Damascus, which was
handed over, and then north through Homs and Hama to Aleppo — where the
child-king's vizier shut the gate in his face, because nobody in Aleppo
believed the protestations of loyalty.

They were right not to. But what they did next is the part that gets left out
of the story.

## The alliance

The Zangids fought Saladin with the help of the Franks and the Assassins.

An attempt was made on Saladin's life by emissaries of the Old Man of the
Mountain. Count Raymond of Tripoli brought a Frankish army into the field to
create a diversion in favour of Aleppo — his Muslim ally, as Lane-Poole calls
him without embarrassment. The siege of Aleppo was raised and Saladin was
checked, and had to content himself with Syria south of it.

Nor was he left alone there. The Zangid atabeg of Mosul sent an army out of
Mesopotamia to combine with his cousin at Aleppo, and the two of them marched
on Hama.

So the dynasty that had made the war against the Franks into a programme, that
had preached it from the pulpits of Aleppo and Damascus for thirty years and
built the institutions to pay for it, spent its last decade fighting the man
who was going to carry it out, in alliance with the crusaders and the
Assassins.

Nobody involved thought this was strange. A dynasty's programme is one thing
and a dynasty's survival is another, and when the two came apart the Zangids
chose the second, the way anybody would.

## The long swallowing

It took Saladin twelve years and he did it piece by piece.

Damascus in 1174. Then a decade of campaigning in which the Franks were mostly
left alone. In 1182 and 1183 he went into Mesopotamia and subdued the whole
country except the city of Mosul itself. Aleppo he did not storm; he bought it,
by exchange, in 1183. Mosul he besieged twice without success, and in 1186 it
consented to become his vassal, which brought northern Mesopotamia and part of
Kurdistan into his empire permanently.

Then, with the north settled and Mosul's contingents available to him, he
turned on the kingdom of Jerusalem. Hattin was the following summer.

## The order of operations

The sequence is worth stating plainly, because it is usually told the other way
round.

Saladin did not take Jerusalem and then consolidate Syria. He spent thirteen
years consolidating Syria — most of it against fellow Muslims, and specifically
against the family whose war he was continuing — and took Jerusalem in the
fourteenth, in a single campaigning season, once there was nobody behind him.

The Zangids of Mosul went on ruling as vassals into the thirteenth century, and
their branch in the Jazira outlived Saladin's own dynasty in Egypt. What ended
in 1186 was not the family. It was the possibility that the war Zangi's sons
had started would be finished by Zangi's sons.
