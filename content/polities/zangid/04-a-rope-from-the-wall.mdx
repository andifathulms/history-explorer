---
title: A rope from the wall
drafted_from: ibn-al-qalanisi
phase: expansion
---

Damascus was the largest city in Syria, it had held out against Zangi for twenty
years, and on Sunday 25 April 1154 it fell in a morning to a man who then
abolished the tax on vegetables.

Nur al-Din had been attacking it day after day. That morning the Damascus
*askar* came out as usual, his troops pressed from the east, and the defenders
fell back to the wall near the Kaysan Gate. And then, in Qalanisi's account —
he was inside the city, and he is scathing about it — there was not a living
soul on the wall, troops or townsmen, except a negligible handful of Turks on
one tower, which he ascribes to the bad management of the authorities. A
footsoldier walked up to the wall. A Jewish woman standing on it let down a
rope. He climbed up unnoticed and a number of others followed him, and they
planted a standard on the wall and shouted *Ya Mansur*.

At that point the resistance simply stopped — because of the affection the
troops and citizens felt for Nur al-Din, says Qalanisi, and his justice and good
reputation. A woodcutter ran to the East Gate with his axe and broke the bolts
off it. The Thomas Gate was opened too. The army came in and went through the
main streets and not one man opposed them, and then Nur al-Din came in himself,
to what the chronicler calls the joy of all the people — who had been suffering
famine and high food prices and were afraid of being besieged by the Franks.

Mujir al-Din, the last of the Burid rulers, shut himself in the citadel, was
sent a guarantee of his life and property, came out on it, and was given
districts around Homs to hold instead. There was some rioting and looting in the
markets by what Qalanisi calls the baser sort, and it was stopped. Two days
later the leading jurists and merchants were summoned and told what the new
regime intended.

Then the decree. The duties on the melon market and the vegetable market were
abolished, and so was the farming-out of the canals; it was written up and read
from the pulpit after the Friday prayers. Qalanisi — a Damascene official of the
administration that had just been removed, writing about the man who removed it
— says that the husbandmen and the cultivators and the women and the journeymen
raised their voices in the open to pray for his long life and the victory of his
standards.

It is worth being clear about what that passage is. It is not neutral: he is
writing under the new government and he had spent the previous decade recording
the Zangids as a threat. But the detail is specific in a way propaganda usually
is not — the melon market, the vegetable market, the canal farms — and it names
exactly the classes that a market-duty abolition would benefit. A reputation for
justice was the instrument that took the walls, and it had been built for years
before it was used.

With Damascus, Muslim Syria was under one authority for the first time since the
First Crusade. In the north the Artuqids had been brought in by a different
method: in 1149 Zangi's son Sayf al-Din took Dara from Timurtash of Mardin and
then besieged Mardin itself, and the peace that ended it was sealed by Timurtash
giving him his daughter — and when Sayf al-Din died, his brother Qutb al-Din
married her too. Later the lord of Mardin and the lord of Hisn Kayfa appear in
the field alongside Nur al-Din, in a coalition that also included the Armenians
of Cilicia and the Danishmendid of Sivas, assembled against the Seljuk sultan of
Konya.

Nobody in that coalition was a subject. They were houses that had concluded it
was better to be inside Nur al-Din's arrangements than outside them, which is
what the unification of Syria actually consisted of.
