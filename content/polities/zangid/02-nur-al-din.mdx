title: Making the counter-crusade a programme

Imad al-Din Zangi was a Seljuk *atabeg* — a Turkish officer appointed guardian to
a Seljuk prince, an office that repeatedly turned into a dynasty — who held Mosul
from 1127 and took Aleppo the year after. In 1144 he captured Edessa, the first
of the Crusader states to be founded and the first to fall, which prompted the
Second Crusade and made him the first Muslim ruler of the period with a
reputation as a fighter of Franks.

His son Nur al-Din did the more consequential thing. He turned the recovery of
Jerusalem from an objective into an ideological programme: sponsoring jihad
literature, endowing madrasas and Sufi lodges across Syria to build a Sunni
religious establishment that supported it, commissioning a minbar to be installed
in the Aqsa mosque when the city was retaken, and unifying Muslim Syria under one
authority as the precondition.

He did not live to use it. He died in 1174, having sent an expeditionary force to
Egypt that ended the Fatimid caliphate and left his own officer's nephew in
charge there. Saladin took Damascus from Nur al-Din's heirs within months, and
inherited both the unified Syria and the programme. The site records the Zangid
end as `gradual absorption` for that reason: the Ayyubids did not destroy them so
much as replace them branch by branch over two generations, while continuing
their project.
