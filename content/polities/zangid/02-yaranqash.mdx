Zangi was killed in his sleep on the night of Saturday 14 September 1146, in
his own camp, while besieging a castle on the Euphrates.

The man who did it was an attendant of Frankish origin named Yaranqash, for
whom Zangi had a particular affection and in whose company he took pleasure,
and who had been nursing a grudge over some injury done to him earlier. He
found his moment when his master was off guard in his drunkenness, and he had
help from some of the other attendants.

Zangi had taken every precaution. He surrounded himself with armed men and
weapons and kept a strong guard posted around the enclosure of his tent. He was
cut down on his bed with a number of effective blows to the vital parts, and
nobody knew anything about it until the killer had got away — into the fortress
Zangi had been besieging.

## What happened to the army

It scattered, in Ibn al-Qalanisi's phrase, like the bands of Saba. His money
and his treasuries were plundered. He was buried on the spot without a shroud,
and his body was afterwards moved, so it was said, to a tomb near Raqqa.

The conqueror of Edessa, two years after the most celebrated victory in the
Islamic world in half a century, was left in the ground unwashed by his own
soldiers while they went through his baggage.

The roads became unsafe at once, and the Turkmen and the brigands were loose in
the country districts after what Qalanisi calls a grateful period of security
and a noteworthy respect for authority — which is what a Damascene official
says about a man his own government had spent twenty years trying to keep out.

## The division

His two sons split the inheritance without fighting over it, which is the
unusual part.

Sayf al-Din Ghazi rode for Mosul, where the governor kept him out for a few
days until terms were agreed, and then opened the gate; he took his father's
place. Nur al-Din Mahmud went to Aleppo, and the moment he was inside he began
assembling troops and paying them, and order was restored.

That is the whole of Nur al-Din's accession: he arrived, he paid the soldiers,
and the city was his. He was about twenty-nine, he had the smaller and more
exposed half of his father's state, and the half he had was the one facing the
Franks.

Everything the dynasty is remembered for — the unification of Syria, the
madrasas, the justice halls, the recovery of Jerusalem carried out by somebody
else with his furniture — comes from the younger son getting Aleppo because his
brother wanted Mosul.
