---
title: A pulpit made in advance
drafted_from: hillenbrand-1999
phase: aside
---

Nur al-Din commissioned a minbar — a mosque pulpit — at Aleppo in 1168 or 1169,
carved in wood with an inscription naming him, to be installed in the Aqsa mosque
in Jerusalem when the city was retaken. Jerusalem had been in Frankish hands for
seventy years and there was no immediate prospect of taking it.

The object is the counter-crusade in miniature: a public, dated, expensive
commitment to an outcome not yet achieved, made to be seen by people in Aleppo
who were being asked to pay for the campaign that would achieve it. Nur al-Din
also endowed madrasas across Syria to produce a religious establishment that
preached the recovery of Jerusalem, and sponsored a literature of *fada'il
al-Quds*, the merits of Jerusalem, that gave the city a religious weight in Sunni
piety it had not carried at the time of the First Crusade.

He died in 1174 without taking the city. Saladin took it in 1187 and had Nur
al-Din's minbar brought from Aleppo and installed, with the original inscription
naming a man who had been dead for thirteen years. It stood until 1969, when an
arson attack destroyed it.

That transfer is the clearest single illustration of what this site records as
`absorbed remnants of`: the Ayyubids did not merely replace the Zangids, they
inherited the project and completed it using the objects it had already made.
