Nur al-Din died in 1174 leaving an eleven-year-old son and an officer in Egypt
who had been sent there to be got rid of. What followed was not a conquest of the
Zangid state. It was a slow substitution, and it can be tracked in the mint.

## What Saladin struck, and when

Lane-Poole sets out the sequence, and it is a document in itself.

As vizier of Egypt, Saladin struck coins in the name of the Fatimid caliph
al-Adid — 1169 to 1171. When the Fatimid caliphate was abolished he struck them
in the name of **Nur al-Din**, from 1171 to 1174. In neither period did he put
his own name on anything.

Then Nur al-Din died. Saladin occupied Damascus, and on the copper coinage he put
the name of al-Salih, Nur al-Din's son — *adding his own as well*.

And in 1175, for the first time, gold coins from Cairo and Alexandria appeared
with the titles of *the king strong to aid, Joseph son of Ayyub*.

Four stages, each one legible from a distance, none of them a declaration. He
never repudiated the Zangids; he stood beside them on the metal, and then the
metal stopped mentioning them. Lane-Poole notes the technical consequence
precisely: Saladin's independent sovereignty dates from the moment he issued
coins in his own name, not from any battle.

## Standing next to the heir

The legal position Saladin occupied for a decade was that he governed on behalf
of Nur al-Din's son, whose family he was in the process of dispossessing.

It let him take Damascus in the boy's name. It let him campaign against Aleppo,
where the boy actually was, as the loyal servant rescuing him from bad advisers.
It let him treat the Zangids of Mosul as rebels against a settlement he was
upholding.

The Zangids could not find an argument against it, because the argument would have
had to be that a subordinate who conquers in his master's name is a usurper — and
that is precisely how the Zangids themselves had begun, as atabegs governing in
the name of Seljuk princes.

Peace eventually reigned between Saladin and the house of Zangi, in Lane-Poole's
phrase, and it was the peace of a family that had been reduced to one province.
Mesopotamia was annexed. Mosul survived as a Zangid city and nothing more.

## The other side of the ledger

The years in which the dynasty was being absorbed were quiet ones on the Frankish
frontier, and that is not a coincidence.

There was a nominal truce with the crusaders, negotiated by Humphrey of Toron,
whose friendship with Saladin had gone as far as admitting him to the rank of
knighthood. The organisation of the new dominions and the fortification of Cairo
took up the time — new stone walls laid out, and the Citadel begun, though it was
not finished until his nephew al-Kamil's reign thirty years later. The founder's
inscription can still be read over the Gate of the Steps.

So while the Zangid inheritance was being consolidated, the counter-crusade Nur
al-Din had built the state around was in abeyance, and its new master was being
knighted by a Frankish baron and building a citadel.

## What was actually inherited

The thing to see is that almost nothing was destroyed.

The army was Nur al-Din's army. The doctrine of the holy war, the network of
madrasas, the reputation for justice, the argument that Syria and Egypt had to be
under one hand before Jerusalem could be recovered — all Zangid, all continued,
all now attached to a different family name.

Jerusalem was recovered in 1187 with the instrument Nur al-Din assembled,
thirteen years after Nur al-Din died and by a man he had sent to Egypt to be got
out of the way. How that officer came to be in Egypt at all is the thing that
decided it, and it happened a decade before any of this.
