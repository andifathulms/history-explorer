Between 1164 and 1169 Nur al-Din sent three expeditions to Egypt, and they are
the reason his dynasty does not exist.

The Fatimid caliphate was in the condition this site's Fatimid page describes:
a caliph who reigned while viziers ruled, and rival viziers each inviting
outside help against the other — the Crusader kingdom of Jerusalem on one side,
Nur al-Din on the other. The stakes were obvious to everyone. Egypt's revenue
would decide whether Syria could be held, and neither Damascus nor Jerusalem
could afford to let the other have it.

Nur al-Din's commander was a Kurdish officer named Shirkuh, who took his nephew
with him. Shirkuh won the contest, became Fatimid vizier in 1169, and died two
months later. The nephew succeeded him in the post.

Saladin then spent five years as the nominal servant of two masters and the
actual master of one country. He ended the Fatimid caliphate in 1171 — the
Friday sermon at Cairo pronounced for the Abbasid caliph, which is where this
site's Fatimid page stops. Nur al-Din, watching an officer's nephew acquire
Egypt in his name and remit very little of it, was preparing to march south and
settle the question when he died in 1174.

His heir was eleven. Saladin took Damascus within months, and over the
following twelve years took Aleppo and then Mosul's obedience from the
remaining Zangid branches.

This record types the ending `gradual absorption` and dates it to 1250, which
is nearly eighty years after Saladin took Damascus, because Zangid branches
went on ruling at Mosul and in the Jazira as Ayyubid vassals for that long.
That is the accurate shape and it is also the point of the type: the Ayyubids
did not destroy this dynasty. They inherited its state, its army, its
religious establishment, its programme and — as the next chapter describes —
its furniture, and then replaced its family one branch at a time.

It is the same mechanism this corpus records on the Samanid, Ghaznavid and
Ghurid pages, with one difference. The instrument that walked away here walked
away with the whole project intact and finished it. Saladin took Jerusalem
thirteen years after Nur al-Din died, and put Nur al-Din's pulpit in the mosque.
