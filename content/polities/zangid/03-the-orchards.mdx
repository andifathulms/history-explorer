---
title: The orchards, and the field at Inab
drafted_from: ibn-al-qalanisi
phase: expansion
---

Nur al-Din got Syria because the Second Crusade came to take Damascus and
failed, and Ibn al-Qalanisi, who was an official of the city that was being
besieged, wrote down both halves of it.

The Frankish armies came up to Damascus in July 1148. On Saturday 24 July the
city's troops, its militia bands, its volunteers and its *ghazis* drew up
against them in the orchards south-west of the walls and were pushed back: the
Franks had the numbers and the equipment, took control of the water, spread
through the orchards and camped in them, and then moved up and occupied a
section of ground which, Qalanisi says, no army in ancient or recent times had
managed to hold. The Malikite jurist Yusuf al-Findalawi was killed by the water
near al-Rabwa, having refused to fall back in front of them; so was the ascetic
Abd al-Rahman al-Halhuli. The Franks spent that night cutting down the fruit
trees and building stockades out of them, and the people of the city spent it,
in his phrase, discouraged and straitened in spirit through fear because of the
horror of what they had witnessed.

They came out again on the Sunday and did better, and within four days the
siege was over. The Franks pulled back and were pursued with arrows, and what
Qalanisi remembers of the ground afterwards is the burial pits and the dead
horses and a stench that almost overcame the birds in the air. Then he records
the thing that mattered. Mu'in al-Din, the regent who had spent years keeping
Damascus independent by dealing with the kingdom of Jerusalem against Aleppo,
joined forces with Nur al-Din in the middle of September and campaigned with
him, and the two of them took the castle of Arayma near Tripoli and captured a
Frankish king's son and his mother in it, with a thousand horse sent down from
Mosul to help.

A crusade raised to avenge Edessa had spent its strength on the one Muslim city
in Syria that was not hostile to it, and pushed that city into the arms of the
man it had been holding off for a decade.

The following June came the other half. Nur al-Din, reinforced by Turkmen
cavalry and a detachment of the Damascus *askar*, moved into the territory of
Antioch with about six thousand horse against roughly four thousand Frankish
lancers and a thousand foot. They met at Inab. The Franks made their famous
charge; the Muslims broke into detachments, came at them from several sides at
once, and the whole thing was decided inside a cloud of dust. Almost nobody got
away. Raymond of Poitiers, prince of Antioch — whom Qalanisi describes as one
of the Frankish knights famous for gallantry, valour, cunning and great stature,
and noted for the dread he inspired and his excessive ferocity — was found lying
among his own guard and knights; his head was cut off and carried to Nur al-Din,
who paid the man who brought it. It was Wednesday 29 June 1149.

Nur al-Din then rode to Antioch and camped at the gate. The city had been
emptied of its soldiers and had nothing in it but townspeople, and he offered
terms: surrender, and your lives and property are guaranteed. They answered that
they could not do such a thing until they had given up hope of a rescuer. They
had not yet given up hope, and he did not take the city.

That is the shape of the next five years in one sentence. He could destroy any
Frankish field army that came out to meet him, and he could not take the walls
behind it. What he could take was Damascus.
