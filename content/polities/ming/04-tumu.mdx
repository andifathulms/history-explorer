In 1449 a Ming field army was destroyed at Tumu, sixty miles north-west of
Beijing, and the emperor was captured alive by an Oirat Mongol force perhaps a
tenth its size.

<Figure id="mg-yingzong" />

The court did not pay. It enthroned his brother, declared the captive a retired
emperor, and declined to negotiate — after which the Mongols, holding a prisoner
of no value, sent him home. He spent seven years under house arrest in his own
palace, then deposed his brother in a coup and reigned again.

## What it settled

The early Ming had gone onto the steppe. The Yongle emperor led five campaigns
into Mongolia in person and died on the last of them, and the capital had been
moved to Beijing partly in order to run
that war from close up.

After 1449 none of that happened again. The strategy became the one the next
second chapter describes: a line of walls, garrisoned, paid for out of the salt
monopoly through the *kaizhong* system, under which merchants who delivered grain
to the frontier were paid in salt certificates rather than cash.

<Figure id="mg-juyong" />

That is worth stating as an institutional fact rather than a military one. The
Ming financed its northern frontier by issuing licences to sell a commodity it
monopolised. The salt monopoly was not simply a source of revenue; it was the
instrument through which the northern defence was paid for, which meant that
anything that disturbed the salt administration disturbed the frontier.

## The counterpart

The Yuan court had not been destroyed in 1368. It withdrew to the steppe with
the seals and the archives and went on issuing the dynasty's reign titles for
two more centuries, as the Northern Yuan.

Tumu is what that claim looked like from the Ming side, eighty-one years after
the withdrawal. The two states are one argument seen from both ends: a dynasty
that says it is still the dynasty, and a dynasty that has to keep an army in the
field about it.
