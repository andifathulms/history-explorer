Zhu Yuanzhang was born to a tenant farming family in Anhui in 1328. In 1344
plague and famine killed his parents and his eldest brother inside a month; he
was sixteen, and buried them, the accounts say, in a strip of ground a
neighbour gave him because the family owned none. He entered a monastery
because it fed him, and when the monastery ran out of food he was sent out to
beg.

Nobody else in Chinese history goes from that to the throne. Liu Bang, the
founder of the Han, was a village headman — minor, but an official. This is the
only Chinese dynasty founded by a man who had been a begging monk.

## What he joined

The Red Turbans were one of several risings in the collapsing Yuan south, and
they were millenarian: White Lotus devotion, the coming of the Buddha Maitreya,
and a claimant descended from the Song imperial house. Zhu joined in 1352, rose
through the movement, married the commander's adopted daughter, and by the
1360s had his own base at Nanjing.

Then he stopped being a Red Turban. He beat the other rebel leaders one at a
time — the largest battle, on Lake Poyang in 1363, was a naval action of several
hundred thousand men on a fresh-water lake — took the movement's figurehead
under his protection, and had him drowned in a river crossing in 1366.

In 1368 he declared the Ming dynasty and sent an army north. The Yuan court left
Dadu without a siege and rode back to Mongolia.

## What he did with it

The Hongwu reign is thirty years of a man who trusted nobody, and the
institutions reflect it exactly.

He abolished the Central Secretariat and the office of chancellor in 1380, after
executing the incumbent for treason, and never replaced them: from that point
the six ministries reported to the emperor directly, with no coordinating layer
between them. That is a workload no ordinary ruler could carry, and later Ming
emperors dealt with it by not carrying it — which is how the Grand Secretaries
and then the eunuch bureaucracy filled the gap.

The purge that followed that execution ran for years and is reckoned in tens of
thousands of deaths. A second one in 1393 killed a comparable number. The people
who had won him the empire were, by the end, almost all dead.

He also rebuilt the countryside on paper: the *lijia* system organising
households into groups of a hundred and ten for tax and labour, the Yellow
Registers of population, the Fish-Scale Registers of land, and hereditary
occupational categories — military, artisan, civilian — that a household could
not leave.

## The instructions

The most revealing thing he left is a set of ancestral injunctions for his
descendants, in which he told them what not to change.

Among them: never restore the chancellorship. Never invade a list of named
neighbouring countries, which he enumerated, because they were poor, distant,
and not worth the men. Keep the tribute embassies limited.

A man who had begged for food set out to build a state that would never need to
be adaptable, and told his heirs in writing that it was finished. Most of what
happened to the dynasty afterwards is what happens when a design like that
meets three centuries.
