In 1575 an Augustinian friar named Martín de Rada led a Spanish embassy into
Fujian and happened to be in Fuzhou for the annual provincial graduation, and what
he watched is the fullest eyewitness account of a Ming examination ceremony that
survives from anyone who was not Chinese.

## The examination

The provincial inspector, having finished touring his circuit — punishing
wrongdoers, rewarding the deserving — proclaims in the chief city that any student
who judges himself ready may present himself to be examined for the degree of
*juren*, which Mendoza's translator renders *Loytia*: among the Chinese reckoned
the making of a gentleman, among Europeans understood as a doctorate.

All the candidates give their names; a day is set; on it, every graduate already
holding the degree in that city joins the inspector to examine the new candidates
together, with great rigour, favouring above all those skilled in the laws of the
country, *by which they do govern all other faculties whatsoever* — and requiring,
besides the learning, that a man be good and virtuous. Those who pass are entered
in a second register and a day is appointed for their commencement.

## The oath

Before anything is given to them, they swear.

> That in all offices committed unto them, they shall with all care and diligence
> do justice equally unto all men, and that they shall not receive any bribes or
> presents whatsoever: that they shall be true and loyal unto the king, and that
> they shall not conspire in any confederacy or treason against him.

Only after the oath does the inspector — standing, in the ceremony, for the person
of the king himself — invest each man with the sash and the cap of his new rank,
and embrace him.

## The procession

Then the city turns out, and Mendoza's account of it runs for a page because
Rada's party watched the whole eight hours of it.

Soldiers march first with drums and trumpets; then maces are borne; then the
established graduates, some on horseback, some in litters; then the sponsors; then
the new men themselves, bare of any upper garment as the custom required, mounted
on white horses draped in cloth of gold, each with a tippet on his shoulder and a
cap trailing two small tippets behind, in the manner — the friars could not help
noticing — of a bishop's mitre. Before each new graduate go six frames of satin,
each carried by four men, on which are written in gold letters his subject, his
degree, and his family's arms.

The whole procession takes eight hours to pass. The city keeps the day as a
festival, with dancing and games, and for three or four days afterward the
better families take turns feasting the new graduates and courting their goodwill,
because from that day forward any of them is qualified to hold office, and every
family in the city has a reason to want to be remembered by one.

## What it bought the state

An officer class recruited on a written law rather than on blood, sworn in public
under oath before it received a single privilege, whose qualification a foreign
friar — with no stake in the outcome and no access to the examination room —
could nonetheless watch being tested, in front of the whole city, by men who had
already passed it themselves.

Mendoza's own comment, filtered through Rada's report, is that no Christian
kingdom examines its magistrates so rigorously before trusting them with office.
It was not, on the evidence of the rest of his book, a comment he made carelessly:
the same government that produced this ceremony also produced the whip Pereira
watched used on thieves a generation earlier, in the same province, under men
raised by exactly this process.
