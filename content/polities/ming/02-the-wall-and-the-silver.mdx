title: A wall, and other people's silver
drafted_from: mote-twitchett-1988

The Ming built the Great Wall — not the earlier rammed-earth works, but the
brick-and-stone construction most people picture, put up mainly in the sixteenth
century after repeated failures to settle the steppe frontier by campaign or by
trade agreement. It was expensive, and it was an admission: the dynasty could
neither destroy the Mongol confederations nor buy them off the way the Song had
bought the Liao.

The other defining pressure came by sea. Ming China ran on silver, taxed
increasingly in silver after the sixteenth-century fiscal reforms, and did not
produce much. It came from Japan and then, in enormous quantity, from Spanish
mines in the Americas via Manila. For roughly a century the Chinese fiscal system
depended on a supply it did not control and could not see.

When the flow contracted in the 1630s — for reasons in Japan, Manila and Europe —
Chinese silver prices moved, tax obligations became crushing in real terms, and
the resulting distress met a run of failed harvests and an epidemic. The rebellion
that took Beijing in 1644 opened the way for the Manchus.

This is the earliest polity in this corpus whose fate turns visibly on a global
economy rather than on its own frontier. It is also the point at which this
site's four measures become least adequate: no axis here registers a state's
exposure to a monetary system it does not control, and that is arguably the most
important fact about the late Ming.
