The Ming were founded by a peasant who had been a mendicant monk, expelled the
Mongols from China, and set about restoring what they understood as the proper
Chinese order — the examinations restored, the Yuan legal categories abolished,
the capital eventually at Beijing facing the steppe the threat still came from.

Between 1405 and 1433 the Yongle emperor and his successor sent seven fleets into
the Indian Ocean under the admiral Zheng He, a Muslim eunuch from Yunnan. They
reached Sumatra, Ceylon, Calicut, Hormuz and the East African coast, carried tens
of thousands of men in ships larger than anything Europe would build for
centuries, installed and deposed rulers, and brought back tribute and a giraffe.

<Figure id="mg-ship" />

Then the programme stopped. The court decided the expense was not justified, the
records were reportedly destroyed, and the shipyards fell out of use. Sixty years
later Portuguese ships entered the same ocean from the other direction.

It is worth being careful about what that comparison proves. The voyages were
tribute-system diplomacy, not commerce or colonisation, and they achieved what
they were designed to achieve. The Ming did not fail at something they were
attempting; they stopped doing something they had chosen to do, for reasons that
were internal and, in fiscal terms, defensible. The retrospective drama belongs
to people who know what happened next.
