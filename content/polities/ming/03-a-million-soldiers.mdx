`military_basis` on this record reads `land-grant`. Forty-two records on this
site carry that value and two of them are in this region — this one, and the Jin,
whose version of it is the subject of that record's fourth chapter.

The Hongwu emperor built the army out of hereditary military households — the
*weisuo* — settled on state land in self-supporting garrisons. A household
supplied a soldier in every generation, in perpetuity, and farmed the land that
fed him. He is supposed to have said he could keep a million soldiers without
spending a grain of the people's rice.

For about a century it did roughly what it was meant to. Then it did what every
hereditary land-for-service arrangement in this corpus does.

## The failure that has its own field

The garrison households lost their land — to officers, to civilian landlords, to
debt — and once the land was gone the service was an unfunded obligation on
people who could not meet it. Desertion ran at rates the Ming's own inspectors
recorded and could not fix. By the sixteenth century a Ming campaign was fought
with hired troops, paid in silver.

Forty-two records on this site are coded `land-grant` and some version of the
same story is on most of them, from the Ur III record to the Ottoman. What makes
the Ming instance worth a chapter is that the state had the diagnosis in writing
and kept the institution anyway, for two hundred more years, because Hongwu's
Ancestral Injunctions forbade changing what he had set up.

## Which is also the succession rule

`succession_rule` here is `primogeniture`, and it is one of very few records on
this site where the rule was written down as law rather than inferred from
practice. The same Ancestral Injunctions fixed the throne on the eldest son of
the empress.

The Wanli emperor spent fifteen years trying to get round it in favour of a
younger son he preferred. The bureaucracy refused, citing the text. He lost, and
his response was to stop appearing at court — for something over two decades,
during which memorials went unanswered and vacant offices went unfilled because
only he could fill them.

A codified succession rule produced a constitutional crisis by being obeyed. It
is the strongest argument this corpus has for coding rule 7's insistence on the
rule rather than the outcome: the rule here is exactly what the outcome cannot
tell you.
