Mendoza wrote a whole chapter arguing that China had printing before Europe did,
and got the reason for it right without knowing any of the details that would later
prove him correct.

## The claim

He states the European case first and fairly: printing is generally held to have
begun at Mainz around 1458, credited to a German called Johann Gutenberg, and the
first printed book was Augustine's *City of God*.

Then he sets the Chinese claim against it.

> The Chinos do affirm, that the first beginning was in their country, and the
> inventor was a man whom they reverence for a saint... at this day there are
> found amongst them many books printed 500 years before the invention began in
> Germany.

His evidence is not argument but objects. Martín de Rada and his companions, on
their way back from Fujian to the Philippines, brought a chest of printed Chinese
books bought in Fuzhou — Mendoza says he owns one himself, and has seen others in
Spain, Italy and the Indies. They would have brought many more, he adds, but the
provincial governor grew suspicious that the books might teach foreigners the
secrets of his country, and sent word that he would give them books for nothing
rather than have them buy more — a promise, Mendoza notes drily, that was never
kept.

## What the editor could add three centuries later

The Hakluyt Society edition footnotes Mendoza's chapter with the scholarship
available in 1853, and it is worth setting beside the friar's claim because it
confirms the shape of it while correcting the specifics.

Printing without movable type in China does not go back past the early tenth
century — the Confucian classics were block-printed in Sichuan between about 890
and 925. Movable type, the editor notes citing Stanislas Julien's research, was
used far earlier than Gutenberg by a Chinese blacksmith named Bi Sheng, who worked
with baked clay characters between 1041 and 1048 — very nearly four hundred years
before Mainz. The technique was not widely adopted; Chinese printing settled
instead on carved wooden blocks, one per page, which suited a language of
thousands of characters far better than a case of movable type ever could.

The editor also records an actual surviving specimen: a copy of the *Thousand
Character Classic*, printed in 1348, examined in the British Museum's collection —
printed a full century before the Mainz Bible, in parallel columns of standard and
cursive script, meant to teach students to read both hands from the same page.

## Why Mendoza's version is worth keeping despite being wrong in the details

Because he had the causation right and the Europeans he was arguing against did
not.

His own guess at the mechanism — that the technique travelled from China to
Germany by way of Russia and Muscovy, or by sea and the Red Sea and Arabia — is
wrong, and he says so tentatively, offering it as the best account available to
him rather than as certainty. What he got right, on the evidence of a chest of
books and nothing else, was the conclusion every later scholar confirmed: that a
government run for six centuries on written examinations, published law codes and
a bureaucracy that had to be supplied with a canon of texts had built the means of
reproducing text at scale long before the country that would eventually claim the
invention as its own.

A friar who never went to China argued, from a box of books somebody else brought
back, against the settled opinion of his own continent — and turned out to be
righter than wrong.
