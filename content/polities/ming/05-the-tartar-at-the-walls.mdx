A century after Tumu, a Mongol chief did what the Oirat army that had
captured an emperor in 1449 never managed: he brought a Ming army to the
walls of Beijing itself, and this time there was no field battle to lose
first.

## Yenta's long war

Altan Khan - Yenta, in the record's own transliteration - spent most of the
Jiajing emperor's forty-six-year reign raiding the Shanxi frontier from his
base near Datong, sometimes in company with his brother, the Ordos chief
Kisiang. The Chinese court's own historian judged that the frontier had
never been more disturbed than it was by this one man. In 1541, guided by a
renegade Chinese monk with a personal grievance to settle, Yenta broke
through the Great Wall and marched on Taiyuan itself, returning with vast
plunder; the following year he inflicted an even heavier blow further south,
at Pingyang, ravaging thirty-eight districts and carrying off two hundred
thousand prisoners. A bounty of a thousand taels and an official
appointment was placed on his head. It changed nothing. Yenta kept his own
paid spies inside the capital - one of them, discovered only after the
fact, was among the Jiajing emperor's own most trusted ministers.

## The year he came for the capital

In 1550, after a first reverse near Datong the previous year, Yenta returned
with greater force than ever and marched directly on Beijing, intending to
lay siege to the capital itself. Only the arrival of fresh troops hurried in
from Liaodong and other provinces forced him to withdraw, and even then he
managed an orderly retreat with minimal losses. The raid is remembered by
its cyclical-calendar name, the Gengxu Incident, and it did to the mid-Ming
court what Tumu had done a century earlier without the added humiliation of
a captured emperor: it proved that a Mongol chieftain could reach the
dynasty's own capital more or less at will, a full century after the wall
and frontier system built in Tumu's aftermath was supposed to have made
that impossible.

## An old enemy, settled too late to matter much

The dispute with Yenta was not actually resolved until 1570, under the
following reign, when the defection of his own grandson to Chinese service
gave both sides an opening: Yenta demanded the boy's return, China required
the prior release of Chinese captives first, and once that condition was
met the exchange became the basis of a genuine peace - Yenta accepting a
Chinese princely title and a formal, if largely ceremonial, submission to
the throne. It was a real settlement, and it held. But it arrived twenty
years after Beijing itself had already been menaced, decades into a reign
that had spent most of its own energy simply surviving one chieftain's
campaigns rather than resolving the underlying problem the wall-building of
the following decades would still be trying to fix.
