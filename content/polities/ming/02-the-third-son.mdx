The founder's grandson was on the throne for four years. His fourth son took it
from him in a civil war, moved the capital, invaded Vietnam, campaigned into
Mongolia five times, and sent the fleets — and did all of it in twenty-two
years.

## The usurpation

Hongwu had given his sons military commands on the frontier. When he died in
1398 the throne went to a grandson, who began reducing the princes' power
immediately; the Prince of Yan, holding the northern command at Beiping,
revolted.

The war took three years. He won it, entered Nanjing in 1402, and the palace
burned; the reigning emperor's body was never satisfactorily identified, which
produced a rumour that he had escaped in disguise that outlasted the dynasty.

What followed the victory is worth stating because it is the least discussed
part. The senior official who refused to draft the accession announcement, Fang
Xiaoru, was executed along with — by the traditional account — his relatives to
the tenth degree, a category invented for the occasion by adding his students
and friends to the nine degrees of kin. The figure usually given is over eight
hundred people.

Then the Yongle emperor had the records of the preceding reign rewritten so that
his nephew's four years were absorbed into his father's, and his own succession
followed directly from the founder.

## Moving the capital

He moved the capital from Nanjing to Beiping, renamed Beijing, and the move is
the most consequential decision of the reign.

The logic was military: the threat was the Mongols, the emperor should be where
the armies were, and he had spent his adult life on that frontier. The
consequence was economic. Beijing cannot feed itself; the grain came up the
Grand Canal from the Yangtze, and the canal had to be rebuilt and dredged and
then maintained for ever, with a hereditary transport service of tens of
thousands of men, as a permanent charge on the state.

Every later Ming and Qing government paid that bill. The capital of China has
been in the north ever since, next to the frontier, at the end of a supply line.

## Everything at once

The scale of the Yongle reign is hard to convey without a list, so here is one.

Five personal campaigns into Mongolia, the last of which he died on. The
invasion and annexation of Vietnam in 1406 and a twenty-year occupation that
never stopped fighting insurgents. Seven maritime expeditions under Zheng He,
reaching Hormuz, Aden, Mecca and the East African coast. The rebuilding of the
Grand Canal. The construction of the Forbidden City. The *Yongle Encyclopedia*,
over eleven thousand volumes, copied by more than two thousand scholars, the
largest compilation of its kind before the twentieth century — never printed,
because printing it was beyond even this reign, and now surviving in about
four hundred scattered volumes.

## What it cost

Vietnam was abandoned in 1427, two years after his death, after a guerrilla war
that had bled the army continuously.

The fleets stopped in 1433 and the shipyards were shut down; the court reasoned
that the northern frontier was the real threat and that the voyages returned
prestige rather than revenue, which was true.

The Mongol campaigns achieved nothing durable. The Mongols dispersed when a
large army came and returned when it left, which is the permanent problem of
campaigning on a steppe, and in 1449 a Ming emperor took an army out to settle
it and lost both the army and himself.

What survived was the capital, the canal and the Forbidden City. A reign that
spent everything on projecting force in four directions left behind the three
things that faced inward.
