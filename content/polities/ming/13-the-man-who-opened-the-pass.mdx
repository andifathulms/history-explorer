In April 1644 a rebel army took Beijing and the last Ming emperor hanged himself
on the hill behind the palace. The Ming had a general still in the field: Wu
Sangui, commanding the frontier army at the Shanhai pass, the gate in the Great
Wall where it meets the sea.

He was between a rebel who had killed his emperor and a Manchu army on the other
side of the wall. He opened the gate to the Manchus, and together they destroyed
the rebel and took Beijing.

The Qing then kept it, and Wu Sangui spent the next thirty years conquering
southern China for them.

## The manifesto

In 1673 he rebelled against the dynasty he had let in, and published a statement
of his reasons. Palafox's continuator prints it from a Jesuit manuscript, and
whatever its exact wording owes to translation through three languages, its
argument is the thing itself:

> When I summoned the Tartars to the assistance of the emperor my master,
> against the rebels who attacked him, and who endeavoured to usurp the
> monarchy, I gave an opportunity without intending it to these nations to seize
> upon it themselves. It now causes me the most painful regret, and my
> conscience reproaches me continually for the evil I have brought down upon my
> country, by reducing it beneath this tyrannical yoke. I am afraid that heaven
> will be irritated against me, and will punish me severely, if I do not make
> every effort to repair my fault.

The account is careful to record the price. The court held his only son and two
grandchildren as hostages against exactly this, and he knew what raising the
standard would do to them. He raised it anyway, and the narrator does not let
him off: the liberty of his country, he writes, served as *a decent pretext for
saving his own life in preference to those of his children*.

The revolt failed. It took eight years, ruined the south a second time, and
ended with Wu Sangui dead and the Qing more securely in possession than before.

## What the Qing kept

Almost everything.

They kept the six ministries, the provincial structure, the county magistrates,
the tax registers, the Grand Canal and its grain fleet, the capital at Beijing
and the palace in it. They kept the examinations, on the same canon, and
expanded them. They kept the Ming law code with amendments. They compiled the
Ming History, at enormous length, as every Chinese dynasty compiles its
predecessor's — which is itself the strongest statement of continuity a Chinese
state can make.

What they added was the thing the Liao had invented and the Yuan had used: a
separate structure for the conquerors. The banner system, Manchu and Mongol and
Chinese-martial, sat alongside the civil administration; senior posts were
doubled between a Manchu and a Chinese holder; Manchuria and Mongolia and later
Tibet and Xinjiang were governed through a different office entirely.

That is the northern frontier's oldest administrative idea, arriving at the
capital for the last time, and it held the largest Chinese empire there has
ever been for two hundred and fifty years.

## The queue

The one thing the Qing did not take over was appearance. In 1645 they ordered
every Chinese man to shave the front of his head and wear the Manchu queue, on
a deadline, on pain of death.

It was resisted more fiercely than the conquest itself in some places —
Jiangyin held out for eighty-one days over it — and it was enforced, and within
two generations it was simply what Chinese men looked like. When the Qing fell
in 1912, cutting the queue was the act by which people announced that the
dynasty was over.

A hairstyle imposed by a conquering minority in a single edict became, in three
hundred years, the thing Chinese men had to cut off to be modern. Wu Sangui, who
had shaved his own head in 1644 before he rode through the pass, would have
understood the arithmetic exactly.
