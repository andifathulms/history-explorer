The Wanli emperor's decades-long refusal to appear at court get remembered
as the reign's defining failure. Less remembered is what the same reign
actually paid for while its emperor was refusing to show up: two major wars,
fought back to back by the same general, that did more to empty the
treasury than the paralysis at court ever managed on its own.

## Ningxia

Wanli had not been long on the throne when a revolt broke out among the
military colonies of the northwest. Pübei, a soldier of Tartar origin who
had risen high in Chinese service, found himself and his family targeted by
jealous Chinese officers resentful of his standing; a personal insult to his
son escalated into an armed rising, and Pübei's forces overran the upper
Yellow River garrisons before holing up in the walled city of Ningxia. The
siege that followed was long and costly on both sides - Chinese assaults
repeatedly repulsed with heavy losses - until the general Li Rusong solved
it with engineering rather than storm, diverting the Yellow River itself
against the city's own walls until the flooded defences finally gave way.
Pübei threw himself into the flames of his own residence rather than be
taken; his body was pulled out and beheaded anyway; his head was carried to
Li Rusong as proof the rebellion was over.

## Korea

The same army that had just taken Ningxia had barely stood down when a
second crisis broke, this one from across the sea. Toyotomi Hideyoshi -
Fashiba, in the record's own rendering - used the pretext of Korea's own
internal weakness to seize the port of Fushan in 1592 and pour troops
through it, overrunning the peninsula so completely that the Korean king
fled to China itself and begged the Ming court for help against what both
courts recognised as a common threat. Beijing did not hesitate: an army was
raised and sent through Liaodong, and when the war's early fighting went
against China - a Chinese brigade all but destroyed after an overeager
commander crossed a river ahead of his own support - the court chose to
escalate rather than negotiate a peace on Japanese terms.

It was the Ningxia army that answered the call, Li Rusong once again in
command, crossing the Yalu to meet a Japanese force operating under
Hideyoshi's own direct authority. The same general who had flooded one
rebel stronghold now had to fight a war on foreign soil against Japan's
most capable commander, in a campaign that dragged on with the same kind of
grinding cost the Ningxia siege had already imposed on the treasury once.

## What both wars actually bought

Neither campaign was fought for nothing - Ningxia kept the northwest
garrisons from fragmenting further, and the Korea campaign kept a hostile,
battle-hardened Japanese army off the Chinese mainland it might otherwise
have reached. But the two wars together, fought within a few years of each
other by the same overstretched military establishment, are exactly the
kind of expense a court paralysed by its own emperor's absence from
government was least equipped to manage carefully. The fiscal exhaustion
that the dynasty's silver economy would spend its final decades trying to
outrun did not start with the wall-building of the following century. It
started here, with two wars that had to be paid for while the man who was
supposed to be minding the accounts had stopped coming to work.
