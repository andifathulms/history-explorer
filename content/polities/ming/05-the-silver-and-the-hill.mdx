Zhang Juzheng's reform of 1581 consolidated the accumulated tangle of land taxes,
labour services and surcharges into a single payment, assessed on land and
collected in silver. It is called the single whip, and it is the last major
fiscal reform of imperial China before the twentieth century.

It also made the Ming state's revenue depend on a metal China did not mine.

This record's second chapter is about where the silver came from: Japan, and then
Spanish America by way of Manila, in quantities large enough that the price of
silver in China and the output of a mine in Peru were connected facts. A
government that collects in silver is fine while the silver arrives. In the
1630s the flow was interrupted — the Tokugawa restricted Japanese exports, the
Manila trade was disrupted — at the same time as the coldest decades of the
period, crop failure across the north-west, and epidemic.

Taxes assessed in silver in a country where silver had become scarce are a tax
increase nobody voted for.

## 1644

A rebel army out of the famine districts took Beijing in April. The last Ming
emperor walked up the hill behind the palace and hanged himself from a tree.

The general holding Shanhai pass against the Manchus then opened it and invited
them through to destroy the rebels, which they did, and then stayed for two
hundred and sixty-eight years.

## The field, and the reference set

`ended.by` names the Qing, and the Qing is not a record on this site. It is a
backdrop entry in `reference-set.yaml` — one of about fifty polities carried for
their numbers, so that the percentile columns are computed against something
wider than the corpus — and `ended.by` is allowed to point there.

Two records on this site do it: this one, and the Mughal record, which names the
British empire. In both cases the polity that ended a very large empire is
present as a row of measurements and nothing else.

That is a different kind of absence from the fourteen empty `ended.by` fields
this corpus's Maritime Asia region carries, and arguably a worse one, because the
field looks full. A reader who follows the link finds a name, a span and a reach
figure, and no page.
