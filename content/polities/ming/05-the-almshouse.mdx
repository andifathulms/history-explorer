Galeote Pereira went to China as a smuggler and stayed as a prisoner, and what he
wrote down about the system that held him is the most detailed outside account of
Ming local government that exists from the sixteenth century.

## How he got there

He was one of a party of Portuguese traders caught by the coastguard off the
Fujian coast in 1549, in a period when direct trade was illegal and everyone did
it anyway. He and his companions were marched inland, tried, and held — first as
prisoners under sentence, then, when the sentence was never carried out, as a kind
of permanent registered population, for years, before he finally got home and wrote
it up.

He had every reason to hate the country that held him and instead wrote an account
that is admiring in places to the point of astonishment.

## The whipping

He watched punishment carried out and described the instrument precisely, because
he had reason to look closely at it.

> Their whips be certain pieces of canes, cleft in the middle, in such sort that
> they seem rather plain than sharp. He that is to be whipped lieth grovelong on
> the ground. Upon his thighs the hangman layeth on blows mightily with these
> canes, that the standers by tremble at their cruelty. Ten strypes draw a great
> deal of blood, twenty or thirty spoil the flesh altogether, fifty or threescore
> will require long time to be healed, and if they come to the number of one
> hundred, then are they incurable.

Thieves taken near the coast, he says, were whipped first and then cast in prison,
where they died of hunger and cold — *at that time when we were in prison, there
died of them above threescore and ten.*

## The almshouse

Then, in the same breath, the thing that made him marvel.

> They have moreover one thing very good, and that which made us all to marvel at
> them, being Gentiles: namely, that there be hospitals in all their cities,
> always full of people; we never saw any poor body beg.

He asked why, and was told: in every city there is a great enclosure containing
houses for the blind, the lame, and the old who cannot work and have no other
means to live. They are admitted on a written petition to the provincial
authority, proving their condition, and once admitted they have rice for as long
as they live — nothing else, but that for life. The houses also keep swine and
hens, so that the poor are provided for without going out to beg.

A state that whipped a man to permanent injury for theft and fed every blind and
crippled pauper in its cities for the rest of their lives, as a matter of ordinary
administration, and did both without apparent contradiction. Pereira records the
two facts one after the other because that is the order he encountered them in,
and does not try to reconcile them.

## The post

He also describes, from the inside, the machine that kept the court in Beijing
informed of a country too large to see.

Every province sends monthly intelligence of everything of note — war, the state
of the country, the king's revenues — to the capital, and it is done, he says,
*without fault*: a post five hundred leagues from court still keeps its appointed
day. Riders carry a horn or a belt of bells so the next post-house can have a
fresh horse ready before they arrive; where the road is water, the boatmen have
their barks ready the same way. Those whose news must travel the furthest send
their couriers first, so that reports converge on the capital together rather than
in the order the news occurred.

A prisoner with every reason to want this government to fail is describing, in
close technical detail, an information system more reliable than anything in the
Portugal or the Spain he had come from.

## The cormorants

And, because he was a curious man and had a great deal of enforced leisure, the
detail that has nothing to do with government at all.

The king kept barges on the rivers stocked with tame sea-crows — cormorants —
bred, fed, and kept in cages, allotted a monthly ration of rice, and given out to
the greatest magistrates, two or three birds to a man, to fish with. At the
appointed hour the barges gather in a ring in shallow water; the birds, tied
loosely under the wing, are let down; each fisherman empties his bird's throat into
his own barge as it fills and lets it dive again; when enough is taken, the birds
are freed to fish for their own pleasure.

There were twenty such barges, he says, in the city where he was held, and he went
almost daily to watch them, and was never satisfied he had seen enough of so
strange a way of fishing.

## Why a prisoner's account is worth more than an ambassador's

Because he had no audience to perform for and no host to flatter.

An envoy sees what he is shown. Pereira saw what happened to a man the system had
no further use for — the whip, the prison, the seventy dead of cold and hunger
in a single term — and also saw, because he was left with nothing to do but look,
the almshouses, the post riders and the fishing birds that nobody had arranged for
his benefit. Both halves of the account come from the same pen and neither
softens the other.
