---
title: The instrument that did not walk away
drafted_from: jackson-1999
phase: peak

This site has told the slave-soldier story several times and it always ends the
same way. The Abbasids bought Turks to escape provincial armies and were being
deposed by them at Samarra within thirty years. The Samanids raised *ghulams*
who had no local landholding and no claim of their own, and one of them walked
off with Ghazna in 962 and founded the dynasty that replaced them. The Ayyubids'
purchased Bahriyya took the sultanate in 1250. The Safavids broke the Qizilbash
with a slave guard four centuries later and this site's Roman page observes that
the same failure mode runs through the whole corpus.

The Ghurids ran the same instrument and got a different result, and the
difference is worth setting out because it is the largest consequence any polity
in this collection produced.

Mu'izz al-Din had no son. His Indian conquests — Delhi, the Gangetic plain,
Bihar, Bengal — were held by Turkish slaves he had bought, trained and raised
to command: Qutb al-Din Aibak at Delhi and Lahore, Bakhtiyar Khalji in Bengal,
Yildiz at Ghazna, Qabacha in Sind. When he was assassinated near the Indus in
1206, the instrument did exactly what the instrument always does. It took the
state.

What makes India different is what it took the state *into*. Aibak did not
carve a province off a surviving Ghurid empire; the empire went under within a
decade behind him, and there was nothing to secede from. What he held was a
conquest a thousand miles from the mountains that had launched it, with no
metropole left to answer to, a local revenue base, and an army of men in the
same position as himself. So the succession did not fragment the state. It
relocated it.

Aibak's line and then Iltutmish's became the Delhi Sultanate, which ruled
northern India for three centuries and traced its authority to a Ghurid
appointment — the single successor claim counted on this page. The slave system
that dissolved every other polity that used it is, here, the mechanism by which
a thirty-year empire produced a three-hundred-year one.

The reason is not that the Ghurids managed their ghulams better. It is that
they died at the right moment, in the wrong place, and left their army holding
somewhere worth keeping.
