In 1150 or 1151 Ala al-Din Husayn of Ghur sacked Ghazna. The chronicles say the
city burned for seven days, and he was called *Jahansuz*, world-burner,
afterwards.

The stated cause was vengeance: two of his brothers had been killed by the
Ghaznavids. The structural cause is duller and more decisive. Ghur was a
Ghaznavid client, and the Ghaznavid capacity to discipline its clients had
been going since Dandanaqan in 1040. A hundred and ten years of a vassal getting
stronger while its overlord got weaker produced exactly one outcome, and the
only surprising thing is how long it took.

Ala al-Din did not take Ghazna in the sense of keeping it — the Ghaznavids
returned, and then lost the city to Ghuzz Turkmen in 1163. What he did was
demonstrate that it could be done.

<Figure id="gr-lashkari" />

The generation after him did the rest. Ghiyath al-Din Muhammad held Firuzkuh and
Khurasan; his brother Mu'izz al-Din took Ghazna in 1173 and used it as the base
for India. Two brothers, one senior and one operational, neither of whom
supplanted the other — which is worth noting in a corpus where fraternal
arrangements almost always end the way the Buyids' did.
