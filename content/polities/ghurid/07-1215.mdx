Ghiyath al-Din died in 1203 and Mu'izz al-Din in 1206, and the state did not
survive both of them.

It had already lost the argument in the field. The Khwarazmians were by then the
strongest power in the east and the Qara Khitai still the protectors of
everything north of the Oxus, and after the two of them shut the road at
Andkhud the Ghurid claim to Khurasan was a claim and nothing else.

Then Mu'izz al-Din was assassinated near the Indus, with no son.

## What went where

The Indian provinces went to the slave-generals within months and never came
back. Ghazna went to Yildiz, Sind to Qabacha, Delhi and Lahore to Aibak, Bengal
to the Khalji.

What was left was Ghur itself: the mountains the dynasty had started in, held by
a succession of short-reigned sultans whom the sources record so
inconsistently that the name of the last one cannot be established. Firuzkuh
fell to Ala al-Din Muhammad of Khwarazm in 1215, and the summer capital, its
palace and its archive went with it.

## The shape of it

Roughly thirty years elapsed between the Ghurids taking Ghazna and losing
Firuzkuh. It is one of the shortest imperial careers anybody has had, and it
produced one of the longest-lasting successor states.

The reason those two facts belong in the same sentence is that they have the
same cause. An empire held together by one man's appointments, in country that
could not support it, at the far end of a march from mountains that produced
nothing, was never going to outlive the man. What it could do — and what it did
— was put able men in charge of a rich country a thousand miles away and then
die before it could recall them.
