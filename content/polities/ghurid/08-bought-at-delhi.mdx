The Ghurid empire lasted about thirty years. The state its slaves founded lasted
three hundred, and the fullest account of how that happened was written by a man
who had grown up in Ghurid country and ended his life at the court of a Ghurid
slave's descendant.

## The transaction

Juzjani tells the story of Iltutmish's purchase in some detail, because by the
time he was writing it had become the origin story of the dynasty he served.

Shams al-Din Iltutmish was a Turkish slave who had been taken to Ghazna and
could not be sold there, because an order had been issued that nobody was to buy
him. He was moved to Bukhara for three years, brought back to Ghazna for
another, and still could not be purchased.

Then Qutb al-Din Aibak — who was himself, at that point, still a slave — came to
Ghazna after the Gujarat campaign, heard the story, and asked Sultan Mu'izz
al-Din for permission to buy him.

The sultan's answer is the sentence the whole afterlife turns on. Since a
command has been issued that he should not be purchased at Ghazna, let them take
him to the city of Delhi, and there he can be purchased.

So Aibak left an officer behind with instructions to bring the two slaves down
to Delhi on his return, and bought Iltutmish there.

## And then the recommendation

It gets better. Some years later Mu'izz al-Din sent for Iltutmish, gave him a
special robe of honour, ordered his deed of manumission drawn up — and told
Aibak, in Juzjani's rendering: treat Iltutmish well, for he will distinguish
himself.

Set that beside what happened next. Mu'izz al-Din was assassinated in 1206.
Aibak took Delhi and Lahore and reigned until he died at Lahore, whereupon the
commander of troops and the lord justice of Delhi wrote to Iltutmish. Iltutmish
took the sultanate and is, by common consent, the real founder of the Delhi
Sultanate — the man who consolidated it, obtained investiture from Baghdad for
it, and made it a state rather than a garrison.

The Ghurid sultan personally authorised the sale of that man to that buyer, in
that city, and then recommended him.

## The historian

Minhaj al-Din Juzjani was from a family of scholars in the highlands of Herat.
His maternal ancestors had held the qadi-ship of the fortified town of Tulak,
in the Ghurid mountains, by diploma from the caliph, and he kept the documents.
He was, in every sense that mattered to him, a man of Ghur.

He wrote the *Tabaqat-i Nasiri* at Delhi and dedicated it to Sultan Nasir al-Din
Mahmud Shah, a grandson of Iltutmish — who gave him a cloak, a turban and a
caparisoned horse for it, and a stipend afterwards for a verse account of a
campaign. The commander of that campaign, Balban, gave him the revenues of a
village.

So the standard history of the Ghurids was written by a Ghurid, in India, for
the grandson of a slave his sultan had once given orders about, and paid for
with a village in the Punjab.

## What that is an afterlife of

Nothing else the Ghurids did survives in this way. The dynasty produced no
literature anyone kept, no administrative tradition, no line of successors in
its own country. Its capital is a tower in a gorge that Europe could not locate
for seven hundred years.

What it produced was three or four capable men whom it had bought as boys, and a
country to put them in. That turned out to be enough — and the man who wrote it
all down had to move two thousand kilometres to be somewhere it still mattered.
