title: A tower nobody could find
drafted_from: flood-2009
phase: aside
---

Almost nothing written by the Ghurids survives. Firuzkuh was destroyed, the
dynasty's own records went with it, and this page's record is visibly thin as a
result: no cited extent, no cited population, and a last sultan the sources
cannot agree on. What the Ghurids left instead is buildings, and the buildings
say a good deal.

The largest is a brick tower sixty-five metres high standing in a river gorge
in the mountains of Ghur, at the confluence of the Hari Rud and the Jam,
surrounded by nothing. It was raised in the 1190s under Ghiyath al-Din, and it
is covered from base to summit in geometric brickwork, glazed turquoise tile
and Kufic and naskhi inscription — including, running around it, most of the
nineteenth sura, Maryam, which is the sura about the mother of Jesus.

Europe did not know it was there. It was reported in 1886 and then effectively
forgotten, and only came to general notice in 1957, when two French
archaeologists reached it. The site around it — a palace, fortifications, a
pottery kiln, a Jewish cemetery — is very probably Firuzkuh itself, the summer
capital this page's chapters keep referring to and which nobody could point at
for seven hundred years. It leans, the river undercuts it, an earthquake in
2022 brought bricks down, and it has been on the list of world heritage in
danger since the year it was inscribed.

The other end of the empire built too, and built differently. At Delhi, from
1193, the Ghurid governor raised a congregational mosque and beside it the
minaret now called the Qutb Minar, and both were constructed substantially out
of the dismantled fabric of temples — columns, brackets and carved panels
reused as columns, brackets and carved panels. Flood's argument, which this
chapter follows rather than the usual one, is that reuse of this kind is not
adequately described either as pure iconoclasm or as pure economy: it was a
common medieval practice of appropriating the prestige of what was taken, done
by rulers on all sides of the period's religious lines, and reading it only as
a statement about religion imports a later quarrel into a twelfth-century
building site.

Two towers, then, two thousand kilometres apart, put up within a few years of
each other by the same family: one in an empty gorge that nobody visited for
seven centuries, one in a city that became the capital of northern India for
three. The dynasty that built them lasted about thirty years as an empire, and
this is the part of it still standing.
