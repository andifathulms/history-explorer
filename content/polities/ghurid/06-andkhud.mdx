---
title: The road back from Khwarazm
drafted_from: juzjani-raverty
phase: contraction
---

In 1204 Mu'izz al-Din went north to finish the Khwarazmshah. It was the one war
the Ghurids fought against an equal, and it took them about six months to stop
being an imperial power in Khurasan.

Muhammad Khwarazmshah heard the preparations coming and went home ahead of
them, calling in horse and foot from Khurasan and sending to the Gur Khan of the
Qara Khitai for help. Juzjani, who wrote for the Delhi sultans and remembered
the Ghurids fondly, gives the Ghurid army enough elephants to drink the Oxus
dry; his translator, who argues with him in the footnotes for a page at a time,
notes that the sober figures are large enough without the poetry. Mu'izz al-Din
reached the east bank of the river, arranged his elephants, and gave orders to
find a ferry for the morning.

The news that arrived that night was that Muhammad had come back, that the
sultan of Samarkand was with him, and that the Qara Khitai were on the road.
Mu'izz al-Din burnt his heavy baggage in the dark and started back along the
Oxus. He had already sat in front of Khwarazm itself for five days and given it
up — not enough siege equipment, not enough forage, a campaign that had gone on
too long. The Khwarazmians came after him at dawn. At Hazarasp the Ghurids
turned and fought; their right wing went first and the rest went with it, and
more of the elephants and the war material was lost in the pursuit.

They were still retreating when they reached Andkhud and found the road shut.
The Qara Khitai under Banjko of Taraz were across it, and closing from every
side. The Ghurids fought from dawn until the sun went down and darkness stopped
it. One account puts their dead at fifty thousand; another says they broke on
the first charge, which the translator thinks likelier than his author's version
and says so.

What happened during the night decided the rest. Husain ibn Kharmil, who
commanded the vanguard and had spent the afternoon urging the sultan to attack
again in the morning, looked at the fresh and countless enemy and at horses that
had walked from Khwarazm, and rode off towards Juzarwan with five thousand of
his own, and most of the men whose mounts were finished went too. At dawn Mu'izz
al-Din drew up what was left of the centre and his own slaves and started the
battle anyway. Juzjani says he stood until about a hundred horsemen remained,
that the arrows sticking in his canopy made it look like a porcupine, and that
he would not turn his head, until a Turkish slave called Ayaz Juki took hold of
his bridle and dragged him to the fort of Andkhud.

The Qara Khitai invested the fort and began cutting through the walls. What got
him out was the sultan of Samarkand, riding with the besiegers, who sent in word
that he did not wish a Muslim sovereign to be killed by unbelievers and that if
Mu'izz al-Din would surrender what was left of his elephants, his animals, his
treasure, his arms and his armour, that could be made to buy his life. He
agreed, and reached his own territory alive.

He had marched north to annex Khwarazm. He came back with a hundred horsemen,
having paid the contents of his baggage train for permission to leave. Khurasan
was gone, and it was not coming back; two years remained, and they were spent in
India.
