---
title: Three lines, two gaps
drafted_from: chi-4
phase: peak
---

The dynasty is conventionally divided into three, and the divisions are not a
historian's tidiness — they are two interruptions during which the family did
not rule.

The **Kayusiyya** held the mountains from the seventh century until 1006, when
the Ziyarid ruler Qabus ibn Vushmgir ended them. That is the first gap: sixty-seven years in which Tabaristan was
administered by other people.

The **Ispahbadhiyya** began in 1073, moved the seat down out of the highest
country to Sari, and became something larger than their predecessors had been —
holding Gilan, Rayy and Qumis as well as Tabaristan, as vassals first of the
Seljuks and then of the Khwarazmshahs. Shah Ghazi Rustam, in the middle of the
twelfth century, is the one the sources treat as the peak: he fought the Nizari
Isma'ilis in the neighbouring valleys, held the
Caspian provinces against Seljuk claimants, and struck his own coin. The line
ended in 1210 with the murder of its last ispahbadh. That is the second gap.

The **Kinkhwariyya** appear in 1237, after the Mongols, and ruled Tabaristan as
Mongol vassals for a further hundred and twelve years, until a local rival
killed the last of the main line in 1349 and took the province.

Dynasties are usually ended once. The Bavands were ended in 1006 and came back;
ended in 1210 and came back; ended in 1349 and did not.

What kept being available across those gaps was not a state. It was a claim — a
family in known valleys, with a genealogy nobody local disputed, which could be
put back together whenever the power on the plateau was distracted for long
enough. That is a durable thing to be, and it is not the same thing as a durable
state, which the chapter after next is about.
