---
title: What six hundred and ninety-eight years means
drafted_from: chi-4
phase: aside
---

This record gives 651 to 1349, which is six hundred and ninety-eight years and
puts this dynasty among the four longest-lived polities on the site — behind
the Zaydi imamate and Byzantium, ahead of the Ottomans.

The figure encloses two periods, of sixty-seven and twenty-seven years, in
which the family ruled nothing.

This site has now met the same problem three times and answered it two
different ways, and the difference is worth setting out because a reader
otherwise finds an inconsistency and no reason for it.

The **Zaydi imamate** on the Arabian page runs 897 to 1962 as a single span,
with a chapter arguing that the number measures an office rather than a state.
Yemeni historiography treats the imamate as one institution with interruptions,
and the record follows it.

The **Ibadi imamate of Oman** stops in 893 even though the same office was
reconstituted in the same mountains repeatedly for another millennium, because
Omani historiography counts imamates in the plural, as distinct constitutions
rather than one thing with gaps.

This page follows the Zaydi pattern, for a reason that is about the sources and
not about tidiness: the three Bavand lines are one family, with a continuously
asserted descent, named collectively as one dynasty by the works this site
reads. Three lines of the same house are not three polities in the way two
Omani imamates constituted by separate elections are.

But the honest position is that all three cases are the same historical
phenomenon — an institution or a family that recurs — and that a data model
built on `start` and `end` cannot express recurrence at all. The site's span
fields carry a range where two sources disagree about a *date*. They have no
way to say that the thing between the dates was intermittent, so the caveat
gets written in prose on each page and the rankings show a number that means
something slightly different for these three than for everyone else.

That is a limitation worth stating rather than smoothing over, and it is the
second such limitation this corpus has run into. Reach cannot describe
Srivijaya, which held a strait rather than an area, or the Nizaris, who held
scattered castles. Longevity cannot describe a polity that stops and starts.
Both axes were built for territorial states with one beginning and one end, and
the site would rather publish the number with the argument attached than
quietly drop the polities that break it.
