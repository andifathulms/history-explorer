title: The mountains nobody wanted enough
drafted_from: chi-4

The Elburz range runs east to west between the Iranian plateau and the Caspian,
and the country on its northern face is unlike anywhere else in Iran: heavy
rainfall, forest, rice, and valleys reachable through passes that a small
number of determined people can hold. Every empire in this site's Iranian
Intermezzo held the plateau. None of them ever securely held this.

The Bavands are what lived there. The family claimed descent from Kawus, a son
of the Sasanian king Kavadh — a claim modern scholarship treats with caution,
since it may compress several figures of the great Parthian house of Ispahbudhan
into one convenient ancestor — and they were still asserting it seven hundred
years later. They used the title *ispahbadh*, which is a Sasanian military
office, and continued to use it four centuries after there was no Sasanian
state to hold an office in.

They also stayed Zoroastrian for a long time. The Arab conquest took the
Caspian plains in the seventh century and stopped at the mountains; Tabaristan
kept its own princes, its own calendar and its own religion into the ninth. The
Bavand ruler Qarin I accepted Islam around 842, which is two centuries after
al-Qadisiyya on this site's Rashidun page, and the dynasty carried on using the
Sasanian title and the Sasanian descent claim regardless. By the tenth century
the family was Twelver Shi'i, which in the Caspian was the ordinary confession
and not a statement.

What they did politically for seven centuries was acknowledge whoever was
strongest and remain in the mountains. The list is a table of contents for this
region: Abbasid governors, then Tahirids, then Samanids, then the local Alid
imams, then Ziyarids, then Buyids, then Ghaznavids, then Seljuks, then
Khwarazmshahs, then Mongols. Each of them received recognition and, when
convenient, tribute. None of them replaced the family, because taking the
Elburz was expensive and holding it was worse, and the Bavands never gave
anybody a sufficient reason.
