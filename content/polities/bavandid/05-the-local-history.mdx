---
title: A province writes itself down
drafted_from: chi-4
phase: aside
---

Around 1210 a man named Ibn Isfandiyar, whose father had been a senior official
of this dynasty and who had been at the Bavand court himself under Ardashir I,
started collecting material for a history of Tabaristan. He finished it a few
years later, in Persian, and it survives.

The *Tarikh-i Tabaristan* is a local history in the strict sense: not a
dynasty's chronicle and not a universal history with a province in it, but an
account of one region from its legendary origins through its Sasanian
princes, its resistance to the Arab conquest, its conversion, its Alid and
Zaydi imams and its ruling families, to the writer's own time. It preserves
material found nowhere else, including a Persian translation of a letter
attributed to a Sasanian-era Tabaristani ruler, and it is the principal source
for most of what has been said here so far.

That is worth stating directly. Almost everything a reader can know about the
Bavands comes through a book written by a courtier of the Bavands, at the end
of their second line, about his own province.

There are three positions a dynasty can be in with respect to its own record,
and this is the third and much the commonest.

The first is to have a friendly local source that nobody else can check: the
*Tarikh-i Sistan* does that for the Saffarids, who are bandits in every other
account and are not bandits in that one. The second is to have no source of your
own at all, so that the whole account is left to your enemies, which is what
happened to the Nizaris when their library at Alamut was destroyed.

Ibn Isfandiyar is neither. He is not hostile and he is not independent, and he
is the only witness there is. Almost everything anybody can know about the
Bavands comes through a book written by a courtier of the Bavands, at the end of
their second line, about the province he had grown up in — including the descent
from Kawus, and the seven-century span, and most of what the earlier chapters
here assert.

That is not a reason to disbelieve him. It is a reason to say so.
