A mountain dynasty in Tabaristan spent the late twelfth century being courted by
larger powers on both sides of it, and Ibn Isfandiyar — who was writing in
Tabaristan, for its ruling house — preserves two episodes that show what the
courting was worth.

## The feast on the plain

When Il-Arslan Khwarazmshah died in 1172 and his son Tekish took the throne from
his brother Sultan Shah Mahmud, the losing brother and his mother came to
Tabaristan for refuge.

The ispahbad Ala al-Dawla Hasan went out to Tammisha to meet them, sent his
governors and officers from Gilan and the districts of Ray ahead with presents,
and on the plain of Ganjina caused a feast to be spread *as far as Ispid
Daristan, for a distance of a parasang* — such a feast, Ibn Isfandiyar says, as
no man ever saw before or since.

Three miles of tables for a defeated claimant to somebody else's throne. It is a
statement about the host rather than the guest: a ruler who can provision that
much hospitality at short notice in a mountain province is telling the whole
region what his revenues are.

## The offer

The next ispahbad, Husam al-Dawla Ardashir, who reigned thirty-five years, was
put to a harder test.

The Seljuk sultan Tughril, quarrelling with his brother Qizil Arslan, wrote from
Iraq asking for help. Ardashir sent his amirs and nobles to Ray to meet the
Seljuk king and went forward himself to Lar — where, on meeting him, *he alighted
from his horse*, brought his royal guest to Fulul, and there enthroned and
entertained him royally.

Qizil Arslan heard about it and sent an envoy to make Ardashir an offer. He was
reminded of the favours he had received from Qizil Arslan's father and brother,
and asked to arrest Tughril, bind him and hand him over.

The price offered for that was:

> the rule of Ray, Sawa, Qum, Kashan and Qazwin, and full authority over Iraq and
> Adharbayjan.

That is not a bribe. It is an offer to multiply the Bavandid state several times
over — the whole of the Jibal and the north-west, handed to a dynasty whose
territory was a strip of forest and mountain between the Elburz and the Caspian.

Ardashir *indignantly rejected the proposal that he should betray his guest*.
After a while he sent Tughril on to Damghan and Bistam, with instructions to his
officers there to supply everything the sultan needed, day by day, until he
reached his own capital.

## The letter from India

The other episode is stranger and says more about how the dynasty was seen from
outside than any campaign could.

In 1183-4 an ambassador arrived from the Maharaja of India. A Shi'i missionary,
he reported, had come to that country arguing for his doctrine and had beaten
their own scholars in debate. So the Maharaja had written for a ruling — and the
address on the letter is the point. It was directed to

> the just and upright king in Tabaristan, who is descended from the Kisras, and
> who holds this doctrine.

*Descended from the Kisras* — the house of Sasan. The Bavandids claimed descent
from the pre-Islamic Persian kings and here is an Indian court using that claim,
unprompted, as the reason to write to them rather than to Baghdad or Cairo.

The embassy had set out forty strong. Thirty-eight died on the road; two arrived.

Ardashir had the reply composed at length, in florid Arabic, by a sayyid of Amul,
and it grew into a treatise with a title of its own — *the Epistle to the Indians
in reply to the pretensions of the obdurate*.

## What the dynasty actually had

No Bavandid ever ruled much. The whole point of this house is that it held one
defensible province for six hundred and ninety-eight years while everything
around it was conquered repeatedly.

These two episodes show what that bought. The ispahbads were rich enough to feed
an army for entertainment, independent enough that a Seljuk sultan fleeing his
brother thought Tabaristan a safe place to be, honourable enough that refusing to
sell a guest was worth more to them than seven provinces — and old enough that a
king in India believed the Sasanian line still held a throne in the mountains
south of the Caspian, and wrote to it for a ruling on doctrine.
