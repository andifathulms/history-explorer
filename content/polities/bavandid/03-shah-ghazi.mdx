---
title: Shah Ghazi and the neighbours in the next valley
drafted_from: chi-4
phase: peak
---

The second Bavand line reached its height under Shah Ghazi Rustam, who ruled
from about 1142 to 1165 and is the one member of this dynasty the wider sources
treat as a figure in his own right rather than as a name in a list of vassals.

He held the Caspian provinces against Seljuk claimants at the point when the
Great Seljuk sultanate was coming apart into family possessions administered by
atabegs, and he extended Bavand authority over Gilan and into Rayy and
Qumis. He struck his own
coin. The Seljuk sultan Mas'ud campaigned against him and did not finish him.

What he is remembered for is the Isma'ilis.

The Nizari Isma'ili state held Alamut and a network of fortresses in the
Elburz — the same mountains, a few valleys away.
The two were neighbours in the most literal sense available, and the
relationship was murderous. Nizari agents killed Shah Ghazi's son. He responded
with a campaign of extermination against the Isma'ili settlements he could
reach, and the sources report him building a tower of their heads, which is the
kind of detail medieval chroniclers report about a ruler they approve of.

That is worth dwelling on, because the Nizari state is normally described from
a great distance — a network of scattered castles that no imperial army could
ever finish, which is true. This is the same thing seen from the next valley,
and it looks nothing like that. It looks like a neighbour whose method is to
kill your son, against whom a local ruler with local knowledge could do
considerably more damage than a sultan arriving from the plateau with an
army.

Shah Ghazi died around 1165. His line lasted another forty-five years and ended
in 1210 with a murder, and the province passed to the Khwarazmshahs — briefly,
because the Mongols were nine years away.
