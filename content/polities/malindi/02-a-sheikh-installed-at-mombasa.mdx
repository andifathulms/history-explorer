Mombasa's own people gave Malindi its opening. Portuguese soldiers,
Malindi's own men and their allies the Wasegeju took Kilifi together,
and when Mombasa marched out to answer it, the army met the Wasegeju
on the way and was broken - opening Mombasa itself to occupation.
Rather than install a Portuguese garrison alone to hold a town that
had rebelled and burned before, Portugal turned to the ally who had
never once given it cause for doubt: the Sheikh of Malindi was
installed as ruler of Mombasa as well, with Portuguese soldiers behind
him rather than in place of him.

It was the high point of a century of loyalty repaid in full. Malindi
now governed, through the same ruling house, both its own town and
the harbour that had spent a hundred years trying to burn Portuguese
ships. In 1594 a new Portuguese fortress rose at Mombasa on the
Viceroy's own order - not to replace Malindi's rule there but to
secure it, an outward sign that Portugal now trusted this stretch of
coast enough to build in stone rather than simply defend it in wood
and men.

## A garrison, not a gift

The arrangement was never quite equal, and both sides knew it. The
fort's Portuguese captain answered to Goa, not to Malindi's Sheikh,
and the same decade that saw Malindi's ruling house installed at
Mombasa also saw the first Dutch and English ships probing the coast
- rivals Portugal would expect its client to help resist, whatever the
cost to Malindi's own men and resources. The Sheikh who had inherited
Mombasa had inherited, along with it, every enemy Portugal had ever
made on this coast, with none of the independence to refuse the
inheritance.
