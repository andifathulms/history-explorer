Ahmad held Mombasa for two decades on the strength of an alliance his
own house had spent a century earning. It did not protect him from
the men Portugal actually sent to govern beside him. In 1614 Mombasa
was handed to a new Portuguese governor, Melo Pereira, who treated
Ahmad with an open contempt none of his predecessors had risked - and
Ahmad, rather than accept it, fled the fort for Babai, a settlement of
his own slaves where he might at least be safe from the man Portugal
itself had put over him.

He was not safe. The Portuguese governor is said to have had him
murdered there and sent his head to Goa - proof, if any was needed,
of how completely the arrangement Portugal had made with Malindi's
ruling house had come to depend on Portugal's own restraint rather
than on any real security for the family that had kept faith with it
longer than any other on the coast.

## What Portugal was teaching its own allies

Stigand's account does not treat Ahmad's murder as an isolated act of
one bad governor, but as symptomatic of a wider change: "the
Portuguese, who had formerly established friendly relations with the
natives, at this epoch treated them with such brutality that rebellion
was rife in all their East African possessions." A hundred years of
Malindi's own loyalty had built the very inheritance that made Ahmad
worth murdering, and his son would grow up understanding exactly what
lesson his father's death was meant to teach.
