When Vasco da Gama reached the Swahili coast in 1498, most of its
towns mistook the Portuguese for hostile intruders and refused them
help. Malindi did not. Its Sheikh received da Gama warmly, supplied
him with pilots to replace the ones he had taken hostile from Kilwa,
and sent an ambassador back to Portugal aboard da Gama's own return
voyage. It was the beginning of a partnership neither side would let
go of for more than a century, built on a foundation neither side
ever hid: Malindi's oldest and most powerful rival was Mombasa, and
Portugal was the one power capable of keeping Mombasa in check.

The partnership paid off in both directions for generations. When
Mombasa "sprang into prominence" against Portuguese interests in the
1520s, Malindi sent a hundred and fifty men to help put it down. When
the Zimba - a raiding people out of the Zambezi valley who had already
devastated Kilwa and killed and eaten thousands at Mombasa - turned
north toward Malindi in 1589, they found a defence waiting: Malindi's
own people, fighting alongside a Portuguese garrison and Malindi's
native allies the Wasegeju, drove them off before they could do to
Malindi what they had done to every other town in their path.

## What loyalty bought

No other Swahili town on the coast could claim what Malindi could by
1589: a hundred years of Portuguese friendship that had cost it
nothing but occasional soldiers, and had paid it back, when the worst
threat in living memory came ashore, with the one defence strong
enough to turn it back. Mombasa had been burned, besieged and
occupied by turns since da Gama's first voyage. Malindi had been left
standing every time - and its own alliance with Portugal was about to
be worth a great deal more than a defended town.
