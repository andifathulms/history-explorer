Greek kingship in India ended without a battle and without a chronicle, and the
fullest surviving portrait of any of these kings was written by a Buddhist, in
Pali, in a work of religious argument, some generations after he was dead.

Everything the Greek and Roman writers say about Menander can be set out in
four sentences, and Rhys Davids sets them out. Strabo names him as one of the
two Bactrian kings who carried Greek rule furthest east, across the Hypanis and
as far as the Isamos — the Sutlej, and probably the Jumna. The lost forty-first
book of Justin carries his name in its title, together with that of Apollodotus,
calling them Indian kings. And Plutarch reports that he was noted for justice,
that he died in camp, and that the cities divided his ashes.

That is the whole classical record of the most important Greek king in India.
Rhys Davids notes that it is more than survives for any of the others, which
tells you something about the rest.

## The dialogue

The *Milinda-pañha*, the Questions of King Milinda, is a long conversation
between a Greek king and a Buddhist elder called Nagasena, on the self, on
rebirth without a soul, on merit, on what a chariot is when you have taken it
to pieces and on what it is that continues when a person does. It is a work of
didactic ethics and religious controversy cast in the form of a historical
romance, and it became a classic — quoted by Buddhaghosa, carried across
Buddhist Asia, treated as near-canonical in Burma, still read.

Its Milinda is not a foil. He is quick, well-read, sceptical and hard to
satisfy, and the whole method of the book depends on his being difficult. That
is a strange compliment to pay a foreign king who had been dead for generations
before the author was born.

At the end of the text as it stands, the king is convinced, hands the kingdom to
his son, enters the Order and attains arahatship.

## Rhys Davids does not believe it

His reasons are worth having, because they are a model of how to handle a source
that says what you would like it to say.

The passage sits exactly where a lost final leaf would sit. The Sinhalese
manuscripts flag its context as coming from a copy brought from Siam. None of
Menander's coins — twenty-two types, eight with his portrait — shows any
decisive sign of a conversion. And the coin epithets that do point towards
Buddhism prove something narrower than they appear to: that at least six Greek
kings of the region wanted to be known to their subjects as righteous, in a
formula answering to *dhammika dhamma-raja*. That is firm evidence of Buddhist
subjects whose views the kings were meeting. It is no evidence at all of a
Buddhist king.

His conclusion is that a writer composing in north-western India, at a distance
from the events, converted Menander because conversion was the fitting close to
the argument — and that the author would have been astonished at later critics
who took his history literally while treating his ethics as optional.

## What is left of a kingdom

Set the two records side by side.

From the Greek side: a name, two rivers crossed, a death in camp and a disputed
funeral. From the Buddhist side: a character with a speaking voice, a
philosophical position, a named attendant, a birthplace, and an argument he
loses over several hundred pages.

Nobody in these kingdoms wrote a history of them. The coins are magnificent and
they are mute about everything except who was king and who was expected to
spend the money. What preserved one of these men as a person rather than a
profile was a religion his subjects held and he probably did not, in a language
he probably could not read, in a book composed to win an argument he was not
present for.
