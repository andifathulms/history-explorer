title: Two faces of a coin

North of the Hindu Kush, the Bactrian coins are Greek objects: round, on the
Attic weight standard, with a Greek portrait, a Greek legend and a Greek god.

South of the Hindu Kush they change. The flan becomes square, in the Indian
manner. The weight standard becomes Indian. And the reverse carries the king's
titles in Prakrit, written in Kharoshthi — the Aramaic-derived script of
Gandhara — while the obverse keeps them in Greek.

That is a small change standing in for a very large one. A coin is a public document produced by a government in
quantity for people to hold, and a bilingual coin is a statement about who is
expected to hold it. The Bactrian kingdom's money addressed a Greek-speaking
settler and administrative class. This kingdom's money addressed the country.

The Ptolemies in Egypt were doing something that looks similar and turns out not
to be. That dynasty was divine in Greek and pharaonic in Egyptian, and published
the Rosetta decree in three scripts because three audiences had to be able to
read it — a state making different claims to different people, deliberately and
in parallel.

The difference is the direction of travel. The Ptolemies ran the two
vocabularies side by side for three centuries and the Greek one stayed on top;
Cleopatra was reportedly the first of them to learn Egyptian. Here the movement
runs the other way, and it is visible in the objects. Greek names give way to
local ones. The Greek legends get smaller and eventually get misspelt. Indian
deities appear. And the dynasties that took these kingdoms — Indo-Scythian,
Indo-Parthian, Kushan — went on striking coins in the same idiom for centuries
after the last Greek king, the Kushans using the Greek alphabet to write an
Iranian language for four hundred years. That is this coinage's grammar
outliving the people who brought it by three centuries and more.

Whether the men producing these coins still thought of themselves as Greeks is
Mairs' central question, and her answer is that the question is badly posed.
Identity in Bactria and Gandhara was situational and plural; the evidence is
objects rather than statements; and asking for one label for the people who made
them is asking the material for something it does not contain.
