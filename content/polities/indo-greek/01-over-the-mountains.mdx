Around 180 BC Demetrius, son of the Greco-Bactrian king Euthydemus, took an army
south through the passes of the Hindu Kush into Gandhara and the Punjab, and the
Greek kingdoms of India begin there.

The timing was not accidental. The Mauryan empire had ended in 185 BC when its
last king was killed by his own general, and north-western India was
consequently a set of successor kingdoms rather than a single power. A Greek army coming down from Bactria found the same kind of
opening the Bactrians had found in the Seleucid east forty years earlier.

What followed is difficult to narrate and there is no point pretending
otherwise. The classical sources have almost nothing; the Indian sources mention
the
*Yavanas* — Ionians, meaning Greeks — in passing and hostilely; and the
reconstruction is again from coins. What the coins show is that after about 130
BC there were several kings issuing at once in overlapping territories, and
whether they were branches of one family, rivals, or a sequence that has been
misordered is the argument rather than the background.

That is why the kingdoms are named in the plural, and why nobody can say how
one of these kings was supposed to be succeeded by the next.

Two things are clear enough to build on. The kingdoms lasted: the last
Indo-Greek issues are around the turn of the era, which is nearly two centuries
after Demetrius crossed and a century and a half after the Bactrian half was
gone. And they were not a garrison holding a foreign country at arm's length,
which is what the coins show and what the third chapter is about.
