Around 180 BC Demetrius, son of the Greco-Bactrian king Euthydemus, took an army
south through the passes of the Hindu Kush into Gandhara and the Punjab. This
site's Greco-Bactrian record carries that as its only turning point, and this
page is the other half of it.

The timing was not accidental. The Mauryan empire on this corpus's neighbouring
record had ended in 185 BC when its last king was killed by his own general, and
north-western India was consequently a set of successor kingdoms rather than a
single power. A Greek army coming down from Bactria found the same kind of
opening the Bactrians had found in the Seleucid east forty years earlier.

What followed is difficult to narrate and this page will not pretend otherwise.
The classical sources have almost nothing; the Indian sources mention the
*Yavanas* — Ionians, meaning Greeks — in passing and hostilely; and the
reconstruction is again from coins. What the coins show is that after about 130
BC there were several kings issuing at once in overlapping territories, and
whether they were branches of one family, rivals, or a sequence that has been
misordered is the argument rather than the background.

That is why this record's name is plural and why its `succession_rule` is null.

Two things are clear enough to build a page on. The kingdoms lasted: the last
Indo-Greek issues are around the turn of the era, which is nearly two centuries
after Demetrius crossed and a century and a half after the Bactrian half was
gone. And they were not a garrison holding a foreign country at arm's length,
which is what the coins in the third chapter are about.
