Menander I ruled somewhere in the middle of the second century BC and is the
only Indo-Greek king who appears substantially in more than one tradition.

The coins are the most numerous of any Indo-Greek ruler and are found over a
very wide area, from the Kabul valley to Mathura, which is the basis for saying
his kingdom was the largest. Strabo, quoting Apollodorus, says he crossed more
peoples than Alexander. Plutarch records that when he died the cities disputed
possession of his ashes and divided them, and built monuments over the portions
— which is what Buddhist sources say was done with the Buddha's relics, and is
either a striking coincidence or evidence that Plutarch's source knew what the
practice meant.

And he is the king in the *Milindapañha* — the Questions of Milinda — a Pali
text in which a Greek king called Milinda debates the monk Nagasena on the
nature of the self, of continuity, of what a chariot is when you have taken it
apart, and is convinced. The text is scripture in the Theravada tradition in
Burma and is still read.

How much of that is history is exactly the question. The *Milindapañha* is a
work of doctrine composed over a long period; it is not a transcript, and a
debate in which a Greek king is instructed and converted is the sort of thing a
Buddhist tradition would compose whether or not it happened. What it does
establish, and what the coins corroborate, is that a Greek king of the Punjab
was a plausible figure to put in that role — known, remembered, and associated
with Buddhism by people who had no reason to invent a Greek at all.

Some of Menander's coins carry a wheel on the reverse, which is a Buddhist
symbol and is also a general Indian one. This site's `religions_carried` count
on this record reads null rather than 1, and the reason is coding rule 2's
requirement: the count measures propagation, and being associated with a
tradition is not carrying it. Whether these kings were Buddhists, patrons of
Buddhists, or rulers who put a locally legible symbol on a coin is not settled,
and the corpus does not settle it with a number.
