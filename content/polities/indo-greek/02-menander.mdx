Menander I ruled somewhere in the middle of the second century BC, and by every
measure that survives he was the largest of these kings.

His coins are the most numerous of any Indo-Greek ruler and are found across a
very wide area, from the Kabul valley in the west to Mathura in the east, which
is the basis for saying his kingdom was the biggest of them. Strabo, quoting
Apollodorus, says he crossed more peoples than Alexander did. His portraits show
him young on some issues and old on others, which points to a long reign, and
the coins were still in circulation on the Gujarat coast generations after he
was dead.

## What being the largest of them amounted to

Not, on the evidence, an empire in any centralised sense.

The Indo-Greek kingdoms were a set of overlapping royal authorities in Gandhara,
the Kabul valley and the Punjab, several of them issuing at once, some of them
certainly contemporaries who have been read as a sequence. Menander is the one
whose issues cover the most ground, and even for him there is no capital that
can be named with confidence, no administrative document, no account of how a
province was governed or a tax collected. There is a very large amount of
excellent silver and nothing that explains it.

What the silver does show is what he was addressing. His coins carry his titles
in Greek on one face and in Prakrit, in Kharoshthi script, on the other, and
some of them use the formula that answers to the Pali *dhammika dhamma-raja* —
the king who rules by the Dhamma. A number of the Greek kings of this region did
the same. That is a statement made in public, in quantity, in a vocabulary his
subjects rather than his soldiers would recognise.

Some of his issues put a wheel on the reverse. It is a Buddhist symbol and it is
also a general Indian one, and the difference matters: being surrounded by
Buddhist subjects whose views you want to meet is not the same as being a
Buddhist, and nothing on the coins settles which of the two this was.

## The reason we know his name at all

He died on campaign, and Plutarch preserves what happened next: the cities
disputed possession of his ashes, and the quarrel was settled by dividing the
relics among them so that each could raise a monument.

That is precisely what the Buddhist tradition says was done with the remains of
the Buddha.

It may be a coincidence, and the classical writers who report it certainly had
no idea they were describing a Buddhist funeral. But it is the first sign of
what happened to Menander afterwards, which turned out to be more consequential
than anything he did while alive.
