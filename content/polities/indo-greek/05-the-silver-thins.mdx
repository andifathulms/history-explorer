The end of Greek rule in India can be watched happening in the metal.

## What debasement records

The early Indo-Greek coinage is good silver, struck on the Attic standard, with
portraits of a quality that stands comparison with anything from the
Mediterranean.

The later coinage is not. The silver content falls; the weight standard shifts
to a lighter Indian one; the flans get smaller; base-metal issues multiply; and
the portraiture, on the last kings, becomes formulaic — the same face with a
different name.

A coinage does that when the state issuing it has lost either its bullion or its
territory or both. In this case it is both, and the sequence is legible in the
find-spots: kings whose coins circulate over a wide area are succeeded by kings
whose coins are found in a district.

## Squeezed from two sides

North of the Hindu Kush, the Greco-Bactrian kingdom was destroyed by the Yuezhi
and the other steppe peoples moving south-west in the 130s BC. That removed the
Greek homeland, the recruiting ground and the source of everything the southern
kings had depended on.

From the west and south-west came the Sakas — Scythians, pushed along by the
same movement — who took Arachosia and then moved into the Indus valley and
Gujarat, and later the Indo-Parthians after them.

From the east, the Indian dynasties of the plains had recovered from the
Mauryan collapse and had no reason to leave Greek princes in the Punjab.

So a Greek aristocracy that was already dividing its own territory among forty
claimants lost its reinforcement, its hinterland and its market at roughly the
same time.

## The last of them

Strato II was minting in the eastern Punjab around the beginning of the first
century AD, on debased flans, in a small territory, in a script and language his
soldiers by then probably spoke better than they spoke Greek.

He is the last. After him there is no Greek-legend coinage struck by a Greek
king anywhere in Asia.

## What did not stop

Greek did not disappear from the region when Greek rule did. It goes on being
used.

The Sakas and then the Kushans kept striking coins with Greek legends and Greek
letters, because that was what money looked like there. Kanishka's coinage names
Iranian and Indian and Buddhist deities in Greek script. The Bactrian language
— an Iranian language — was written in the Greek alphabet, with an extra letter
added for a sound Greek did not have, for the next seven hundred years.

Ashoka's Kandahar edicts had been cut in Greek and Aramaic a century and a half
before all this, for populations already living there.

The kingdom that went out with a thin silver drachm in the eastern Punjab left
an alphabet in use on the Oxus until the Islamic conquests, which is a longer
survival than most empires manage and not one that any of the forty kings could
have intended.
