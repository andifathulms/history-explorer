title: The same movement, later

The chain of steppe displacements that ended the Bactrian kingdom did not stop
at the Hindu Kush. The Sakas — the Scythians of the Iranian world — had been
pushed south and west by the Yuezhi, who had been pushed west by the Xiongnu;
some of them went into Iran, where they gave their name to Sistan, and some came
down into the Indus valley and the Punjab.

They took the Indo-Greek kingdoms piece by piece over about a century. Maues was
ruling in Taxila by the 80s BC; Azes and his successors held most of the Punjab
by the 50s; the last Greek issues, of a king called Strato, are around the turn
of the era in a small territory in the eastern Punjab.

There is no last battle and no year that can be given as the year the Greek
kingdoms of India ended. There is a decade in which the coins stop.

What is worth saying at the end is what did not stop. The Sakas struck coins on
the Indo-Greek model, bilingual, with Greek legends they eventually stopped
being able to spell correctly. So did the Indo-Parthians after them. So did the
Kushans, who used the Greek alphabet to write Bactrian — an Iranian language —
for four hundred years, and in whose territory the Buddha was first shown in
human form, in drapery that had come east from Hellenistic Greece by way of
these kingdoms.

The political project failed completely. A Greek ruling class governing Central
Asia and north-west India from Greek cities did not survive, left no
institutions anybody kept, and produced no successor state of its own. The
visual and monetary vocabulary it carried east outlived it by half a millennium
and was taken up by everyone who replaced it, none of whom were Greek and none
of whom appear to have thought about it much.
