There is no narrative history of the Indo-Greek kingdoms. There is no king
list, no chronicle, no inscription from any of their courts recording a reign.
What there is, is coins — and from the coins, something like forty rulers have
been recovered whose names are otherwise entirely unknown.

## How a kingdom is reconstructed from money

The method is worth setting out because it is nearly all there is.

Each king struck coins with his own name and portrait, and often with an epithet
— Soter, saviour; Nikephoros, bringer of victory; Dikaios, the just; Aniketos,
the invincible. The names are Greek and the portraits are individual enough to
be recognisable as different men.

From that, and from the find-spots, the monograms of the mints, the metals, the
weight standards and the way one king's types are copied or overstruck by
another, the sequence and the geography can be partly reconstructed. Two
overlapping series emerge: kings in Bactria north of the Hindu Kush, and kings
in the Kabul valley, Gandhara and the Punjab south of it.

The result is a political history with an unusual shape. We know roughly how
many rulers there were, roughly where each of them minted, and something about
who followed whom. We know almost nothing about what any of them did.

## Bilingual by necessity

The southern kings struck bilingual coins: Greek legend on the obverse, the same
titles in Prakrit in Kharosthi script on the reverse.

That is not a cultural gesture. It is what a Greek-speaking dynasty does when
its taxpayers do not read Greek, and it is the single most useful thing the
Indo-Greeks did for anybody afterwards — because a coin with the same text in
two scripts is a bilingual key, and it is how Kharosthi and then Brahmi were
eventually approached.

The Indo-Greek coinage is also technically the best of the ancient world outside
the Mediterranean. The portraits are individual, unflattering and superb;
Eucratides struck the largest gold coin surviving from antiquity.

## Expansion into what

The push south through the passes in the 180s BC took Greek rule from Bactria
into the Kabul valley, Gandhara, the Punjab and — on the strength of a
statement in a much later Sanskrit grammatical text and a Greek geographer's
notice — as far east as Mathura and possibly Pataliputra.

That eastern raid is one of the most argued-over claims in the field. Patanjali,
writing on Sanskrit grammar, gives as an example of a past-tense construction a
sentence about the Yavana besieging Saketa and Madhyamika; the *Yuga Purana*
describes Greeks reaching Pataliputra. Strabo says the Greeks of Bactria took
more of India than Alexander.

None of it is a campaign narrative and all of it is compatible with a deep raid
rather than an occupation. What is not in doubt is that a Greek-speaking
dynasty was minting coins in the Punjab a century and a half after Alexander
had left.

## Why there are so many of them

The forty kings are the diagnosis. A territory that produces that many
sovereigns in about a hundred and fifty years, several of them minting
simultaneously in overlapping regions, is not a kingdom passing peacefully from
father to son.

The epithets tell the same story: too many saviours and invincibles, too many
men asserting on their money that they had won. A dynasty confident of its
position does not need to.

What the coins record, in other words, is not an empire expanding. It is a Greek
military aristocracy in the Punjab, dividing, quarrelling and re-dividing, on
increasingly small parcels of a country it had taken and could not agree how to
hold.
