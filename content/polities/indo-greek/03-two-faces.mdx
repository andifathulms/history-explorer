title: Two faces of a coin

The Bactrian coins on this record's parent page are Greek objects: round, on the
Attic weight standard, with a Greek portrait and a Greek legend and a Greek god.

South of the Hindu Kush they change. The flan becomes square, in the Indian
manner. The weight standard becomes Indian. And the reverse carries the king's
titles in Prakrit, written in Kharoshthi — the Aramaic-derived script of
Gandhara — while the obverse keeps them in Greek.

That is this record's only turning point, and it is a small thing standing in
for a large one. A coin is a public document produced by a government in
quantity for people to hold, and a bilingual coin is a statement about who is
expected to hold it. The Bactrian kingdom's money addressed a Greek-speaking
settler and administrative class. This kingdom's money addressed the country.

The corpus has the same move in Egypt. This site's Ptolemaic record describes a
dynasty that was divine in Greek and pharaonic in Egyptian, publishing the
Rosetta decree in three scripts because three audiences had to read it, and
carries a chapter arguing that the institutions field cannot express a polity
making different claims to different people. The Indo-Greek coinage is that
argument in miniature and in metal.

There is a difference and it favours this page. The Ptolemies ran two
vocabularies in parallel for three centuries and the Greek one stayed on top;
Cleopatra was reportedly the first of them to learn Egyptian. Here the direction
of travel is visible in the objects: Greek names give way to local ones, Greek
legends get smaller, Indian deities appear, and the successor dynasties —
Indo-Scythian, Indo-Parthian, Kushan — go on striking coins in the same idiom for
centuries after the last Greek king. The Kushan record two entries over uses the
Greek alphabet to write an Iranian language, which is this coinage's grammar
outliving its authors by three hundred years.

Whether the people producing these coins still thought of themselves as Greeks
is Mairs' central question across both of these records, and her answer is that
the question is badly posed. Identity in Bactria and Gandhara was situational and
plural, the evidence is objects rather than statements, and a corpus that wants a
single label for the people who made them is asking for something the material
does not contain.
