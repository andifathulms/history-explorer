title: The same movement, later

The chain of steppe displacements that ended the Bactrian kingdom did not stop
at the Hindu Kush. The Sakas — the Scythians of the Iranian world — had been
pushed south and west by the Yuezhi, who had been pushed west by the Xiongnu;
some of them went into Iran, where they gave their name to Sistan, and some came
down into the Indus valley and the Punjab.

They took the Indo-Greek kingdoms piece by piece over about a century. Maues was
ruling in Taxila by the 80s BC; Azes and his successors held most of the Punjab
by the 50s; the last Greek issues, of a king called Strato, are around the turn
of the era in a small territory in the eastern Punjab.

There is no last battle and no date this site will print as certain, which is
why this record's span closes as a range and why `ended.by` is empty. The
Indo-Scythians are not in this corpus.

What is worth saying at the end is what did not end. The Sakas struck coins on
the Indo-Greek model, bilingual, with Greek legends they eventually stopped
being able to spell. So did the Indo-Parthians after them. So did the Kushans,
this corpus's next record in the region, who used the Greek alphabet to write
Bactrian — an Iranian language — for four hundred years, and whose sculpture is
the Gandharan style in which the Buddha is first shown in human form, in
drapery that came from Hellenistic Greece by way of these kingdoms.

This site's Seleucid record has a chapter on Ai-Khanoum that calls the Gandharan
sculpture the residue of a political failure. That is right and it is worth
completing. The political project — a Greek ruling class governing Central Asia
and north-west India from Greek cities — failed comprehensively and left almost
no institutions. The visual and monetary vocabulary it carried east outlived it
by half a millennium and was adopted by everyone who took its place, none of
whom were Greek and none of whom cared.
